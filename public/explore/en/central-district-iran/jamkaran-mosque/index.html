The guide

The story

Listen first. A low murmur rises from the courtyard, the sound of thousands of quiet voices. Under your feet, the stone is smooth and cool, polished by countless steps. Somewhere a loudspeaker carries a recitation across the open space. Above you, turquoise and deep blue tiles catch the light on the domes. Welcome to Jamkaran Mosque, on the edge of the city of Qom, in the heart of Iran. This place is not mainly a monument. It is a working centre of devotion, and the people around you are here to pray.

To understand Jamkaran, start with one figure. Twelver Shia Muslims, the largest branch of Shia Islam and the faith of most Iranians, believe in a line of twelve Imams. The twelfth, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is believed to have gone into hiding more than a thousand years ago. Believers hold that he is still alive, concealed, and will one day return to bring justice to the world. Jamkaran is dedicated to him. Many pilgrims call him simply the Imam of the Age.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Most great Shia pilgrimage sites in Iran are shrines built over a tomb. In Qom itself, the famous shrine of Fatima Masumeh holds the grave of a revered holy woman. Jamkaran is different. No saint is buried here. People come not to honour the dead, but to feel close to someone they believe is living. That idea gives the whole place its particular mood — a mood of waiting, and of hope.

The origin of the mosque comes from tradition rather than documented history. According to the story told by pilgrims, a local man from the village of Jamkaran had a vision about a thousand years ago. In it, he met the Hidden Imam, who told him to build a mosque on this exact spot. The tale is old and loved, and it is repeated in devotional books. Historians treat it as a sacred legend, not a verified record. For believers, it is the reason the ground here feels chosen.

For most of its life, Jamkaran was a modest village mosque in the countryside outside Qom. That changed in recent decades. From the late twentieth century onward, interest in the mosque grew enormously across Iran. The site was expanded again and again, with large new prayer halls, courtyards, and minarets. What you see today is mostly modern construction, built on a vast scale to hold huge crowds. So the tiles around you are new, even if the devotion they frame is old.

Look at the decoration. The surfaces follow classic Persian traditions. Glazed tiles cover the domes and the arched entrances in blues, turquoise, white, and touches of yellow. Calligraphy runs in long bands along the walls, quoting the Quran and religious texts. Inside, many Iranian mosques use mirror mosaic, thousands of small glass pieces set into ceilings and walls. When the lamps are lit, these surfaces scatter light in every direction. Even if you cannot read the script, you can follow its rhythm with your eyes.

Now, a second surprise. Ask any regular visitor about Tuesday night. In local custom, the evening before Wednesday is the most important night at Jamkaran. Families drive in from Qom, from Tehran, and from towns much farther away. The courtyards fill late into the night with people praying, talking quietly, and sharing food on blankets. Tradition links this night to the mosque's founding story. If you visit on another evening, the place feels calm. On Tuesday night, it can feel like a small city.

There is also a ritual linked to written wishes. Pilgrims have long written short letters addressed to the Hidden Imam — requests for health, for work, for a child, for a family problem to be solved. By popular custom, these notes are dropped into a well on the grounds. Locals call it the well of petitions. Practice around the well has changed over the years as the site has been rebuilt, so watch what other visitors do. Notice how private these hopes are, even in such a crowd.

The mosque also has its own form of prayer. Visitors often perform a special set of prayers associated with Jamkaran, which tradition says the Hidden Imam taught in the founding story. You may see people standing, bowing, and kneeling in rows across the carpets, each at their own pace. Some hold small prayer stones made of pressed clay, which Shia Muslims touch with their foreheads. Others sit quietly with a book of supplications. Nobody expects you to join, but quiet attention is always welcome.

If you are here in the middle of the Islamic month of Shaban, you will see a very different scene. That date marks the birthday of the Hidden Imam, and it is the biggest celebration of the year at Jamkaran. Strings of lights cover the buildings and the roads around them. Volunteers hand out sweets, tea, and drinks to passing pilgrims. The crowds can be immense. Because the Islamic calendar moves against the solar one, this festival falls at a different time each year.

A few words on behaving well here. This is an active religious site, so modest dress matters. Women are usually expected to cover their hair, and a chador, the loose full-length cloak, is often required inside. Some entrances lend chadors to visitors. Shoes come off before the prayer halls, and there are counters where attendants keep them for you. Men and women often use separate entrances and sections. Ask before photographing people, especially anyone in prayer.

Step back and think about what Jamkaran means in Iran today. It shows how a quiet village story grew into one of the country's busiest places of pilgrimage. It also shows how faith, family life, and community all meet in one courtyard. Children run between the pillars while grandparents pray nearby. Young couples sit on the steps. Traders outside sell prayer beads and religious books. The mosque is a place of worship, but it is also a place where ordinary life continues.

Before you leave, pause for a moment in the open courtyard. Look up at the blue domes and the tall minarets against the wide sky over the plain. Listen again to the soft sound of voices around you. Many of the people here travelled a long way, carrying a single wish. As you walk back toward the gate, notice the folded notes, the quiet faces, the families settling on their blankets. Each of them has come to wait, just a little longer.

Practical tips

Visit on Tuesday evening to see the largest weekly gathering.
Women should bring a headscarf and expect to wear a chador.
Leave your shoes at the attended counters before entering halls.
Ask permission before photographing people, especially those praying.
Come in daylight on weekdays for a calmer, quieter visit.
