The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Pine needles shift overhead, and somewhere a bicycle bell rings on a quiet path. The air here smells of resin and cut grass. In front of you, a small bronze figure sits very still, bent over its work. It is a mouse. She wears spectacles, and she is knitting. This is Akademgorodok, the science town of Novosibirsk, and you are standing beside one of the most unusual monuments in Russia.

Look closely at what the mouse is knitting. It is not a scarf or a sock. It is a twisted ladder, two strands winding around each other — the double helix of DNA. Her needles hold the molecule of life itself. The joke is gentle, and it works on two levels. Anyone can enjoy a mouse with glasses. A biologist sees something deeper: the tiny animal that helped scientists read, and even rewrite, the code of heredity.

The monument was unveiled in 2013. Its home is a small square near the Institute of Cytology and Genetics, part of the Siberian Branch of the Russian Academy of Sciences. The idea came from the scientific community here. The message was simple. For decades, laboratory mice made countless discoveries possible, yet few people ever stopped to thank them. This bronze figure is that thank you, set in the open air where anyone can see it.

So why a mouse, and not a rat, a fruit fly, or a monkey? Mice are small and they breed quickly. Their genes are also remarkably similar to ours. For more than a century, researchers have used them to study cancer, immunity, inherited disease, and the effects of new medicines. Many drugs on pharmacy shelves today were tested in mice before any human took them. When you look at this figure, you are looking at a symbol of that quiet, enormous contribution.

Notice the expression the sculptor gave her. She is not a cartoon. She is not sad, and she is not a victim. She looks focused, almost scholarly, like a researcher at a bench late in the evening. The sculptor was Andrew Kharkevich, an artist known for his playful work. His choice matters. This mouse is shown as a partner in science — a colleague, not just an instrument. That small shift in tone is the heart of the whole monument.

Now think about the place around you. Akademgorodok means "academic town" in Russian. It was founded in the late 1950s, during the Soviet era, as a planned city for scientists. The mathematician Mikhail Lavrentyev led the project. The idea was bold. Gather some of the country's best minds into one forested community, far from Moscow, and give them room to think. Institutes, housing, and schools rose together among the birch and pine trees.

The Institute of Cytology and Genetics was one of the first institutes here. Its timing is important. In the Soviet Union of the mid-twentieth century, genetics was politically dangerous. An official doctrine, linked to the agronomist Trofim Lysenko, rejected the science of genes. Many geneticists lost their jobs, and some lost far more. Opening a genetics institute in distant Siberia was, in part, a way to protect a science that Moscow had pushed aside.

Here is something most visitors miss. This same institute is home to one of the most famous experiments in modern biology. Under the geneticist Dmitry Belyaev, researchers began breeding silver foxes, selecting only the calmest and friendliest animals in each generation. Over the decades, the foxes changed. Some began to wag their tails and seek human company, much like dogs. The work helped scientists understand how domestication happens. The mouse in front of you shares a neighbourhood with that legacy.

That is the second surprise of this square. A monument that looks like a light-hearted joke stands at the end of a serious story. It honours the animals of the laboratory, yes. But it also stands in the town where Soviet genetics found shelter and grew again. When scientists here chose to celebrate their work with a knitting mouse, they did it with a sense of humour that comes from long experience. Science can be patient, and science can also smile.

The monument also raises honest questions, and it is fair to name them. Animal research involves suffering, and debates about it continue around the world. Many researchers today work to reduce the number of animals they use, and to replace them where possible. This sculpture does not settle that debate. What it offers is respect. It asks you to remember that real lives stand behind medical progress, and that gratitude is the right response.

Take a slow walk around the figure. From the side, you can follow the curve of the helix as it rises from the needles. From the front, the spectacles catch the light. The bronze has worn smoother in some places than in others, where visitors have touched it. Like many small monuments, the mouse has become something people want to greet with their hands. Students from the nearby university sometimes stop here before exams, hoping for a little luck.

The setting itself is worth your attention. Akademgorodok was designed to sit inside the forest, not to replace it. Paths wind between tall pines, and squirrels often run across the lawns. Low institute buildings stand behind the trees, many in the restrained style of the Soviet period. In winter, snow covers the ground for months, and the mouse may wear a white cap on her head. In summer, the square is green and shaded, and the light falls in soft patches.

If you have time, walk a little further. The town centre, the university, and the shore of the Ob Sea are all within reach. The Ob Sea is a large reservoir formed when a dam was built on the Ob River, and locals go there to swim and walk in warm weather. Each part of Akademgorodok shows the same idea in a different form: a community built around curiosity, quiet streets, and trees.

Before you leave, look at the mouse one more time. Her needles are still. The helix is unfinished, as science always is. Behind her, in the buildings among the pines, people are still at work on questions no one has answered yet. Listen again to the wind in the branches and the distant bell of a bicycle. Then walk on slowly, and carry this small bronze thank you with you.
