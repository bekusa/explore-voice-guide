The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the open stone of Lenin Square. Listen. Traffic moves around the edges, but the centre feels wide and calm. In front of you, a long, pale facade rests behind a row of columns. Above it, a great grey dome curves against the Siberian sky. In winter, the air here can bite your cheeks within minutes. In summer, the square fills with long evening light and the sound of children chasing pigeons.

This is the Novosibirsk State Academic Opera and Ballet Theatre. Locals often give it a simpler name: the Siberian Colosseum. It is widely described as the largest theatre building in Russia. That is a remarkable claim for a city that barely existed a little over a century ago. Novosibirsk grew up fast, around a railway bridge over the Ob River. The theatre is how that young city chose to announce itself.

To understand the building, picture Novosibirsk in the early 1930s. The Soviet state was pushing industry deep into Siberia. Factories, workers and engineers poured into the city. Planners wanted a cultural centre to match this new energy. Construction began in the early 1930s. The first idea was not a classical opera house at all. It was something far more experimental, a hall for huge public spectacles.

The early plans described a kind of House of Science and Culture. The idea was a vast space where parades, mass performances and even scenes with vehicles or horses could take place. The stage was meant to merge with the audience. It was a bold, almost utopian dream of theatre for the masses. As the decade went on, official taste in architecture changed. The project was reworked into a more traditional form, with columns and a formal opera hall.

The dome above you is the part engineers still talk about. It is very wide, roughly sixty metres across, and it was built without internal columns to hold it up. The shell is made of thin reinforced concrete. For its time, that was daring work. Look up at the curve and consider the weight it carries. Then remember that it was built in Siberia, far from the main construction centres of the country.

Then came the war. When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, the building was not yet finished. Work slowed, and the half-built theatre found a different purpose. Here is a detail many visitors miss. During the war, these walls sheltered treasures evacuated from western museums. Collections from places such as the Tretyakov Gallery and the Hermitage were stored here, far from the front line. Paintings, sculptures and precious objects waited out the war inside an unfinished opera house.

Think about that for a moment. Thousands of kilometres to the west, cities were under siege. Here, in the cold quiet of Siberia, some of the most treasured art in the country sat in crates and storerooms. The building protected culture before it ever staged a single performance. When the danger passed, those collections returned home. The theatre kept the memory, and today staff and guides often tell this story with clear pride.

The theatre finally opened in May 1945, in the last days of the war in Europe. The opening production was Ivan Susanin, an opera by Mikhail Glinka. It tells the story of a peasant who sacrifices himself to save the young Tsar of Russia. The timing gave that first night a heavy emotional weight. A nation exhausted by war gathered in a brand-new hall to hear music about loyalty and sacrifice.

Now look again at the facade. The columns give the front a calm, classical rhythm. The building is long and low compared with its dome, so the dome seems to rise even higher. The pale walls change colour with the weather. On a grey day they look almost silver. At sunset they warm to a soft gold. In winter, snow settles along the ledges and makes every line of the building sharper.

Inside, the theatre feels like a world apart from the square. Visitors usually describe red velvet, gilded details and great chandeliers above the main hall. The space is large but the sound carries well. The building went through a long restoration in the early twenty-first century. When it reopened, much of its interior had been carefully renewed. If you go inside, take a moment in the foyer before the hall. Notice how quiet the thick walls make it.

The companies matter as much as the architecture. The ballet troupe in particular has a strong reputation. Over the decades, many dancers trained or performed here before moving to larger stages in Moscow, Saint Petersburg and abroad. The repertoire mixes classic Russian works, such as ballets by Tchaikovsky, with opera from across Europe. For people in Siberia, this stage has long been a doorway to the wider world of music and dance.

Now turn around and look at the square itself. In front of the theatre stands a large monument to Lenin, with sculpted figures beside him. Locals use this square as a meeting point, a place for festivals, and a place for winter ice sculptures in some years. The metro station below carries thousands of people through the city each day. The theatre is not a museum piece. It sits in the middle of ordinary daily life.

There is also a quiet joke that locals enjoy. Novosibirsk is a city of research institutes, factories and long winters. Yet its most recognised building is a house for opera and ballet. That says something about how the city sees itself. Hard work and science built Novosibirsk, but people wanted beauty and art at its centre. The dome is the answer to the question of what the city chose to celebrate.

Before you leave, stand back near the edge of the square. Let your eyes travel from the steps, up the columns, to the long curve of the dome. Behind those walls, there were once wartime crates of paintings. Today, rehearsal music may be playing in a hall you cannot see. Listen to the square around you, the footsteps and voices. Then walk on slowly, and look back once more at the grey dome over the city.
