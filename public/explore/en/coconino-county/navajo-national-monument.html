The guide

The story

Stand still at the rim for a moment. The wind moves through the pinyon pines and juniper, and the air carries the dry, resin smell of the high desert. Under your boots the ground is pale sand and broken sandstone. Then the land drops away. Below you lies a deep canyon of red and orange rock, and somewhere in its shadows, people once built a village inside the cliff itself.

This is Navajo National Monument, on the high Shonto Plateau of northern Arizona. It sits inside the Navajo Nation, the largest Native American reservation in the United States. The monument protects three cliff villages built by the Ancestral Puebloans, the people archaeologists sometimes call the Anasazi. Today many descendants prefer the term Ancestral Puebloan. Two of the villages, Betatakin and Keet Seel, can still be visited. The third, Inscription House, has been closed to the public for decades to protect it.

Here is the first surprise. The place you are standing is high — more than two thousand metres above sea level. Many visitors arrive expecting desert heat and find cool air instead, even in summer. In winter, snow can dust the canyon rim. That altitude shapes everything here. It shaped the plants, the water, and the choices of the people who lived in these canyons more than seven hundred years ago.

The canyon below is part of the Tsegi Canyon system. The name Tsegi comes from the Navajo language and refers to a rock canyon. Over a very long time, water cut through layers of Navajo Sandstone, the same kind of rock you see across much of this region. The sandstone is soft enough for water to carve deep alcoves into the cliff walls. Those natural shelters became the setting for the villages.

The village most visitors see is Betatakin. In Navajo, the name means something close to ledge house. It fills a huge, curved alcove in the canyon wall, like a village tucked inside a giant stone shell. The overhang shelters the rooms from rain and snow. It also gathers the sun in winter, when the low light reaches deep into the alcove, and gives shade in summer, when the sun rides high.

The builders were part of what archaeologists call the Kayenta tradition, a regional branch of Ancestral Puebloan culture. Their villages here are remarkably young compared with their long history in the region. Studies of tree rings in the wooden roof beams show that the cliff villages were built and occupied mainly in the thirteenth century. By around 1300, the people had left. A whole village rose and emptied in roughly two generations.

That is the second surprise. Tree-ring dating, also called dendrochronology, lets researchers read the exact years when trees were cut. Each year a tree adds one ring, wide in wet years and narrow in dry ones. By matching those patterns, scientists can date a roof beam to the very season it was felled. So the timeline of these villages is not a guess. It is written in the wood.

Why did people leave? Researchers point to several pressures together. The late thirteenth century brought a long drought across the Southwest. Changing streams and eroding fields made farming harder. Social and political changes across the region also played a part. The people did not vanish. They moved, joining communities to the south and east. Hopi people today trace clan histories to these canyons, and the Navajo, Zuni, and San Juan Southern Paiute also hold deep ties to this land.

The second village, Keet Seel, lies deeper in the canyon system. Its Navajo name refers to broken pottery, and for good reason — shards of old pots once covered the ground. Keet Seel is considered one of the best-preserved cliff villages in the Southwest. Reaching it is a serious undertaking. It is a long, rugged hike on sand and through streams, open only in certain seasons and only with a permit from the park.

The outside world learned about these villages in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Members of the Wetherill family, ranchers and traders who explored many Ancestral Puebloan sites, were among the early outsiders guided to them. Navajo families living nearby had known the ruins long before. In 1909, President William Howard Taft set aside the area as a national monument, one of the early sites protected under the Antiquities Act.

Now look at the plants around you. In the canyon bottom, below Betatakin, grow aspen and Douglas fir. These trees usually belong to much higher, colder mountains. They survive here because the deep canyon holds cold air and moisture. They are a living leftover from a cooler past. The short Aspen Trail drops toward them, and on a quiet day you can hear the leaves move.

For most visitors, the main experience is the Sandal Trail. It is a paved path from the visitor center to an overlook facing Betatakin across the canyon. Along the way, small signs name the plants that the Ancestral Puebloans and the Navajo used for food, medicine, and building. At the overlook, give your eyes time to adjust. The village blends into the rock, and the rooms appear slowly, one by one, as shadows shift.

When you are ready to leave, look once more across the canyon. The walls of the rooms are the same colour as the cliff, because they were made from the cliff. Listen for the wind and for the birds that call across the gap. Seven centuries ago, families stood at the edge of this same alcove and watched this same light move over the stone. As you walk back, notice the quiet. It is part of what this place still carries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the paved Sandal Trail to the Betatakin overlook.
Bring binoculars to pick out rooms across the canyon.
Pack layers; the high plateau stays cool most of the year.
Check Navajo Nation time; it differs from Arizona in summer.
Apply ahead for the permit needed to hike to Keet Seel.
