The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close, the Tasman Sea is breaking on a grey shingle beach. Behind you, the wind moves through the fronds of nīkau palms with a dry, papery rattle. The air smells of salt and wet leaves. Underfoot, the ground is soft and dark with fallen bark. This is the western edge of Kahurangi National Park, where the forest comes almost all the way down to the waves.

Kahurangi is a Māori name. It is often translated as treasured possession, and sometimes linked with the idea of clear blue sky. Both meanings fit. The park covers a huge area of the northwest corner of the South Island of New Zealand. It stretches from Golden Bay in the north, down through mountain ranges, to the coast here in the Buller District. It is the second-largest national park in the country.

The park itself is young. It was created in 1996, joining together older protected areas and wild land that had been managed separately for decades. But the landscape inside it is very old. Some of the rocks here are among the oldest in New Zealand. In the high country, geologists have found fossils of ancient sea creatures, including trilobites — small, armoured animals that lived on the ocean floor hundreds of millions of years ago, long before any dinosaurs.

Look at those palms around you. The nīkau is the only palm native to mainland New Zealand. It grows further south than almost any other palm in the world. Here, on the West Coast, it forms groves that feel almost tropical. That feeling is a little surprising, because this coast is cool, wet, and exposed to strong westerly winds. The heavy rainfall is exactly what keeps the forest so green and dense.

Notice the colour of the rivers and streams. The water often looks like strong tea. This is not pollution. The water runs through thick forest and peat, and it picks up tannins from decaying leaves and roots. In clear pools, the water can look golden. Where it is deep, it turns almost black. When a river meets the sea, you can sometimes see a line where the brown fresh water and the blue-green ocean meet.

Many visitors know Kahurangi through one long path — the Heaphy Track. It is one of New Zealand's Great Walks, a set of tracks with huts that walkers book in advance. The track crosses the park from the Golden Bay side to Kohaihai, near the small town of Karamea, in the Buller District. Most walkers take several days. Along the way they pass beech forest, open tussock country, river valleys, and finally this wild coastline.

The track is named after Charles Heaphy, an explorer and artist of the nineteenth century. In the 1840s, he travelled along this coast with another explorer, Thomas Brunner, guided by Māori who already knew the land well. That detail matters. Māori used routes through this region long before European explorers arrived. They travelled to the West Coast for pounamu — the green stone, also called greenstone or jade, which was valued for tools and ornaments.

Now, a surprise that most people miss. Kahurangi is home to giant snails. They belong to a group called Powelliphanta, and some have shells larger than a child's palm. They are not gentle grazers. These snails are carnivores. They hunt earthworms at night on the damp forest floor, and they swallow them like spaghetti. You are unlikely to see one, but their empty shells, with glossy bands of brown and amber, sometimes appear beside the track.

The park is also a place for rare birds. Great spotted kiwi live in the higher, colder parts of Kahurangi. Blue ducks, called whio, live on fast, clean rivers. And in recent years, conservation workers released takahē into the open tussock land of the Gouland Downs. The takahē is a heavy, flightless bird with blue and green feathers and a bright red beak. It was once believed to be extinct, until it was found again in the twentieth century.

The bird you are more likely to meet is the weka. It is brown, flightless, and very curious. Weka walk boldly around picnic areas and campsites. They are famous among trampers — the New Zealand word for hikers — for stealing shiny things, food, and even socks. If one comes close, keep your bag closed. Feeding wildlife is not good for the birds, however friendly they seem.

North of Karamea, inside the park, lies the Oparara Basin. Here the land is limestone, and water has carved it into enormous natural arches and caves. Rivers run straight through the rock. The forest grows over the top of the arches, so moss and ferns hang from the stone ceilings. Deep in the cave systems of this area, researchers have found the bones of moa and other birds that disappeared long ago. Some of these caves are protected and closed to casual visitors.

This is a wet place, and the weather changes fast. Rain can arrive in minutes, and rivers can rise quickly after heavy rainfall. Locals treat the forest with respect. Good boots, warm layers, and a rain jacket matter here, even on a bright morning. You will also meet sandflies — tiny biting insects common on the West Coast. They are most active near water and in still air. Repellent and long sleeves make the experience much more pleasant.

As you stand here, think about the scale of what lies behind you. Beyond these palms, the land rises into forest, then into ridges, open downs, and high marble country. Very few roads enter the park. Most of it can only be reached on foot. That is part of why it feels so quiet. The sounds you hear are mostly water, wind, and birds.

Before you leave, take one slow look around. Watch the light on the palm fronds, and the dark water moving towards the sea. Listen for the sharp call of a weka, or the hollow knock of waves rolling stones on the beach. This edge of Kahurangi is only a small doorway into a very large wilderness. As you walk away, notice how the forest closes behind you — green, damp, and unhurried, exactly as it has been for a very long time.
