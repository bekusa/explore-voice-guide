The guide

The story

Start with your feet. Look down at the stones below the low railing. Some are smooth, pale, and neatly fitted. Others, lower down, are rough, uneven, and dark from rain. They are two layers of history, one above the other. The rougher layer is the one that matters most here. Around you, traffic hums along the avenue and a light rail tram glides past. The open sky above this sunken square can feel strangely quiet.

You are standing at Valongo Wharf, in the port district of Rio de Janeiro. It does not look like much at first. It is an open excavation, a rectangle of old paving set below street level, with a few signs and benches nearby. But UNESCO considers this the most important physical trace of the arrival of enslaved Africans in the Americas. In 2017, the wharf was added to the World Heritage List for exactly that reason.

To understand why, go back to the late eighteenth century. Rio was growing into a major port of the Portuguese Empire. Ships carrying enslaved people from Africa had long landed closer to the city centre. The colonial authorities moved this trade out here, to the Valongo area, partly to keep it away from the eyes of the city's elite. A whole district grew around it. There were warehouses where captives were held, shops where they were sold, and the streets that led inland.

The stone wharf itself was built in 1811. From then until the trade was outlawed, this was the main landing point. Historians estimate that hundreds of thousands of enslaved Africans arrived through the Valongo region. Some estimates put the figure close to one million people. They came mostly from Central and West Africa, from regions that today include Angola, the Congo, and Mozambique. Brazil received more enslaved Africans than any other country in the Americas.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The rough stones under your gaze are the original landing surface. People stepped onto these stones after weeks at sea in brutal conditions. Many were sick. Many were children. The paving is called pé de moleque in Portuguese, after a peanut sweet with an uneven, lumpy surface. The name is homely and almost playful... and that contrast is part of what makes standing here so heavy.

Now look at the smoother, more regular stones. They belong to a second wharf, built on top of the first in 1843. That year, a royal bride arrived in Rio. Teresa Cristina, a princess from Naples, came to marry the young Emperor Pedro the Second. The old wharf was rebuilt and renamed the Empress's Wharf to welcome her. So a site of enslavement was quite literally paved over by a celebration of the monarchy.

Brazil passed a law against the transatlantic slave trade in 1831. It was widely ignored. Ships kept arriving, often landing at quieter beaches to avoid patrols. Only after a stronger law in 1850 did the trade truly decline. Slavery itself lasted much longer. Brazil was the last country in the Americas to abolish it, in 1888. That long timeline shaped the city around you in ways that are still visible.

In the early twentieth century, Rio went through a wave of modernisation. The port was expanded, and land was reclaimed from the bay. The waterline moved further out. Both wharves disappeared beneath layers of landfill and new roads. For roughly a hundred years, the site was simply gone. People walked and drove over it without knowing it existed. The water you might expect beside a wharf is now well beyond the buildings to the east.

Then came the second surprise. In 2011, workers were renovating the port zone ahead of the 2016 Olympic Games. Archaeologists working alongside them uncovered the old stones. They also found thousands of small objects. There were glass beads, cowrie shells, rings, amulets, clay pipes, and pieces of everyday life. Many of these items are linked to African religious and spiritual practices. They are small, personal things, carried across an ocean by people who had almost nothing else.

The discovery changed how the city sees this neighbourhood. Black community groups and researchers had long called this area Little Africa. After abolition, many freed people and their descendants settled in these streets. Nearby, the steep rock called Pedra do Sal became a gathering place for music and worship. Many people consider it one of the cradles of samba. Today, Afro-Brazilian religious groups hold ceremonies at the wharf, including a ritual washing of the stones.

If you have time, walk a few streets inland. In 1996, a family renovating their house in this district found human remains beneath the floor. It turned out to be a burial ground for newly arrived Africans who died soon after landing. That site is now a memorial and research institute. Together with the wharf, it gives you the beginning and, for many, the end of a journey.

It is worth noticing how simple the presentation here is. There is no grand building and no dramatic sculpture over the stones. The excavation is left open to the sky, the rain, and the noise of the city. Some visitors find that disappointing at first. Others find it more honest. You see the actual ground, not a reconstruction. Information panels around the edge explain the layers in Portuguese and English.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the railing. Look at where the two layers of stone meet. Above is the polished welcome built for one young woman. Below is the rough surface that received so many others. Then lift your eyes to the modern port district, the tram line, the office towers. As you walk away, notice the street names and the old buildings of Little Africa around you. The history did not end at this wharf. It walked out into these streets, and it is still here.
