The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves softly along the pools at your feet, and gulls call over Guanabara Bay. The breeze carries salt and warm concrete. Above you, a long white structure stretches toward the water, ribbed like the skeleton of some great creature. Its pale surfaces take the bright tropical light and turn it almost silver. This is the Museum of Tomorrow, and it was built on a simple idea. A museum does not have to look backward. It can ask what kind of world we are building next.

The building sits at the end of Pier Mauá, in Rio's old port district. It opened in December 2015, a few months before the city hosted the Olympic Games. The Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava designed it. You may know his work from other cities, where white bridges and stations rise like birds or bones. Here the shape follows the long, narrow pier. The museum reaches out over the bay, almost as if it is leaning toward the horizon.

Look up at the roof. Those long, fin-like elements along the top are not only decoration. They carry solar panels, and they were designed to move and follow the sun through the day. So the shape you see is also a working machine. This is the first surprise for many visitors. The building that looks like sculpture is also trying to reduce its own energy use. The form and the function are the same thing.

Now look at the pools around the base. They are not simply ornamental either. The museum draws water from Guanabara Bay to help cool the building. The water is filtered and then returned to the bay. Next time you see the reflecting pools shine in the sun, remember that they are part of a larger system. The bay that surrounds the museum is also helping it breathe.

To understand why this museum stands here, it helps to know this neighbourhood. For a long time, Rio's port area was a place many residents avoided. A heavy elevated highway, called the Perimetral, ran along the waterfront and cut the city off from the bay. In the years before the Olympics, the city launched a large renewal project known as Porto Maravilha, which means Marvellous Port. The highway came down. The waterfront opened up again. The Museum of Tomorrow became the symbol of that change.

Walk around and you will feel how the space has returned to people. Families walk along the promenade. Cyclists pass. Office workers eat lunch in the shade. The wide square nearby, Praça Mauá, connects the museum to the older city behind it. Across the square stands the Rio Art Museum, often called MAR, which joins a historic building with a modern one under a wavy roof. Together the two museums frame this corner of the port.

Inside, the museum is not about objects in glass cases. It is about questions. The exhibition is arranged as a journey with five main parts: Cosmos, Earth, Anthropocene, Tomorrows, and Us. The visit begins with the scale of the universe. Then it moves to our planet and its living systems. Step by step, the story narrows from the stars down to the choices that each of us makes today.

The Cosmos section usually starts inside a dark, dome-shaped space where a film plays all around you. It is a moment to slow down. You lie back or stand still as images of space, matter and life wash across the curved screen. Many visitors say this is the moment they stop thinking about photos and start simply watching. It sets the mood for everything that follows.

The Anthropocene is the centre of the museum's argument. The word comes from Greek, and it means roughly the age of humans. Scientists use it to describe a period when human activity has become a major force on the climate, the oceans and the land. In this section, tall screens surround you with images of cities, factories, forests and crowds. The goal is not to frighten. It is to show, very directly, how large our footprint has become.

Here is a second detail that many people miss. The museum does not claim to know the future. The section called Tomorrows is plural on purpose. It presents several possible paths, depending on how humans choose to live, consume and share. Interactive games and displays invite you to make choices and see their consequences. The message is gentle but clear. Tomorrow is not one fixed thing. It is something we are still deciding.

The final section, Us, is quieter. After all the data and the screens, the space asks a more personal question. What do we owe to one another, and to those who will come after us? It is a fitting end for a museum built in a city like Rio. This is a place of great natural beauty, set beside deep social contrasts. Standing near the bay, the question does not feel abstract at all.

When you step back outside, take a moment to look around the wider bay. Boats cross the water toward Niterói, the city on the far shore. On a clear day, you can follow the long line of the bridge that connects the two cities. Behind you, the old port warehouses and hillsides of central Rio rise in layers. Along the nearby waterfront, large street murals add colour to the walls of the renewed district. The modern museum and the working port share the same view.

Now look again at the white ribs of the roof. Notice how their shadows shift as the sun moves. Listen once more to the water at the edge of the pools, and to the voices of people walking along the pier. This building was designed to make you think about time, and the future. But for this moment, it simply asks you to stand here, in the present, beside the bay... and notice what is around you as you walk back toward the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book tickets ahead on weekends to avoid long queues.
Visit the Rio Art Museum across Praça Mauá too.
Bring sun protection for the open waterfront promenade.
Allow extra time for the dome film in Cosmos.
Walk the waterfront boulevard to see the street murals.
