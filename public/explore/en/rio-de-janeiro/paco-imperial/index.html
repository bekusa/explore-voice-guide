The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the open stones of Praça XV. Traffic hums somewhere behind you. A ferry horn sounds from the bay, and pigeons scatter across the square. In front of you is a long, low building with whitewashed walls, framed in grey granite. It looks almost modest. Yet more of Brazil's story passed through these doors than through almost any other building in the country.

This is the Paço Imperial, the Imperial Palace. It began life in the eighteenth century, not as a palace at all, but as the house of the colonial governors of Rio de Janeiro. The Portuguese crown ruled Brazil from across the Atlantic, and its local representatives needed a solid, practical seat. They built it right at the water's edge, where ships unloaded and the colony did its business.

Look at the materials. The walls are thick and plastered white. The doors and windows are framed in dressed stone, cut granite of the kind that Rio's hills provide in abundance. This is Portuguese colonial architecture at its most sober. There are no towers and no domes. Instead, the building relies on rhythm. Window after window runs along the facade, each one set into its stone frame, like a line of soldiers at attention.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. When this house was built, the sea came almost to its doorstep. Today the waterline sits some distance away, because later generations filled in the shore to make new land. But picture it. Small boats tied up just across the square. Sailors, merchants and enslaved workers carried goods up from the water. This square was the front door of the city, and this building stood beside it.

In 1763, Rio replaced Salvador as the capital of colonial Brazil. The governors' house became the residence of the viceroys, the king's direct representatives in the colony. Power moved south, and gold from the interior mines flowed out through this port. The building grew in importance, even if its walls stayed plain. Then, at the start of the nineteenth century, something happened that no one in the colony could have predicted.

In 1808, the Portuguese royal court arrived in Rio. Napoleon's armies had invaded Portugal, and the Prince Regent, Dom João, fled across the ocean with his family, his ministers and thousands of courtiers. It was an extraordinary reversal. For the first time, a European monarchy ruled its empire from a colony. The royal party came ashore near this square, and this building became the Royal Palace, the working heart of government.

That is the second surprise. For more than a decade, Rio was effectively the capital of the whole Portuguese empire. Decisions about Lisbon, Africa and Asia were made in these rooms. The royal family later lived mostly in a palace further from the centre, at São Cristóvão. But this building remained the place for ceremony, audiences and the daily business of the state. A sleepy colonial port changed almost overnight.

Dom João eventually returned to Portugal, but he left his son Pedro behind. In January 1822, Lisbon demanded that the young prince come home too. Brazilians feared a return to colonial rule. Pedro refused to leave. He announced that he would stay, and that day is still remembered in Brazil as the Dia do Fico, the Day of I Stay. That announcement was made here. A few months later, Brazil declared its independence, and Pedro became its first emperor.

So the Royal Palace became the Imperial Palace, the name it carries today. Coronations, receptions and great state occasions took place within these walls. Through much of the nineteenth century, the emperors of Brazil used it as a ceremonial seat. Imagine carriages pulling into the square, soldiers in uniform, crowds pressing close to see who stepped down at the door.

The most important moment came in 1888. Princess Isabel, daughter of Emperor Pedro the Second, was acting as regent. On the thirteenth of May, she signed a short law known as the Golden Law. It abolished slavery in Brazil, the last country in the Americas to do so. Crowds gathered outside to celebrate. It is worth pausing on this. Enslaved people had been landed and sold near this waterfront for centuries. The law that ended slavery was signed only steps away.

The empire did not last much longer. In November 1889, a military coup proclaimed the Republic. The square in front of you takes its name from that date, the fifteenth of November. The palace lost its purpose overnight. For much of the twentieth century it served as a central post office, a working building full of sorting tables and counters. Its grand history sat quietly under layers of everyday use.

In the twentieth century, Brazil's national heritage institute listed the building for protection. Later, a careful restoration stripped away many later changes and brought back its colonial character. In the 1980s it reopened as a cultural centre. Today you can walk through its rooms and courtyard, where exhibitions of art and history change through the year. The thick walls keep the inside cool, and the noise of the square drops away as you enter.

When you step back outside, look around the square. Just nearby stands an old stone fountain, once a water source for ships and residents. Across the square, a narrow arch leads into a lane of old houses. The church where the royal family worshipped rises close by. And the ferries still leave for Niterói across Guanabara Bay, as boats have left this shore for centuries.

Before you go, look once more at that long white facade. It is a plain building, and that is the point. Governors, viceroys, a prince regent, two emperors and a princess all worked behind these windows. Colony, kingdom, empire and republic each left their mark here. As you walk away across the stones, listen to the ferry horns and the traffic. The city kept moving, and this quiet house watched it change.
