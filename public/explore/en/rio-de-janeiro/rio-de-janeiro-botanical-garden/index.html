The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the traffic noise from the avenue outside, there is a soft, steady rustle. It comes from far above your head, where the crowns of tall palms move in the sea breeze. The air here is thick and green. It smells of wet soil, crushed leaves and, now and then, something sweet you cannot quite name. Welcome to the Rio de Janeiro Botanical Garden, known locally as the Jardim Botânico.

This garden began with a royal emergency. In the early nineteenth century, Napoleon's armies invaded Portugal. The Portuguese royal family fled across the Atlantic, and in 1808 they set up court here in Rio. The colony suddenly became the centre of an empire. That same year, the Prince Regent, Dom João, who later became King João the Sixth, ordered the creation of a garden on this land beside the lagoon.

Its first purpose was not beauty. It was business. The Portuguese wanted to grow valuable spices from the East Indies on Brazilian soil. Nutmeg, cinnamon, pepper and clove were the targets. These plants had made other empires rich, and the crown hoped to break that grip. So this was, at first, an acclimatisation garden — a place to test whether foreign plants could survive and thrive in a new climate.

Now look along the main avenue. Two straight lines of royal palms run toward the mountains, their grey trunks smooth and pale like concrete columns. This avenue gives the garden its most recognised view. The story of these trees goes back to a single palm. Dom João is said to have planted it himself in 1809. People called it the Palma Mater, the mother palm, and many of the palms around you descend from its seeds.

Here is the first surprise. According to a popular tale, the garden's directors once burned the palms' seeds to keep them rare and exclusive to the crown. Then, legend has it, enslaved workers climbed the trees at night, collected the seeds, and sold them. That, locals say, is how royal palms spread across Rio and beyond. It is a good story, but treat it as folklore. The original mother palm did not survive. It was struck by lightning in 1972.

As you walk, notice how the garden changes in texture. Some areas feel open and formal, with lawns and straight paths. Others feel almost wild. Large trees spread their roots across the ground in thick, twisting ridges. Look up and you may see vines climbing trunks, ferns and small plants growing directly on branches, and bromeliads sitting in the forks of trees like cups. Many of these come from the Atlantic Forest, the coastal rainforest that once covered much of this region.

That forest is still very close. Turn toward the mountains. Behind the garden rise the steep green slopes of the Tijuca range. On the highest point you can see the statue of Christ the Redeemer on Corcovado mountain. The garden sits right at the foot of this landscape. In 2012, UNESCO listed the wider Carioca landscape between the mountains and the sea as World Heritage, and this garden is part of that recognition.

Now for the second surprise. Keep your eyes on the treetops and the higher branches. The garden is not only for plants. Small marmosets, monkeys about the size of a squirrel, often move through the trees in quick, jerky jumps. You may hear their high, thin calls before you see them. Toucans visit too, with their huge colourful bills. Many visitors walk past without looking up. Take a moment, and the canopy often shows you something alive.

Water runs through this place in several forms. Look for the ponds and the lake. In the right season, giant water lilies from the Amazon float on the surface. Their round leaves can grow wider than a person is tall, with raised edges like enormous trays. Elsewhere you will find small streams, stone channels and old fountains. The sound of moving water mixes with birdsong and gives the garden its calm, steady background.

The garden also holds pieces of the city's past. Look for an old stone gateway standing by itself, a little out of place among the trees. It once belonged to the Imperial Academy of Fine Arts in central Rio. When that building was demolished, the portal was moved here and kept. You may also find ruins linked to the royal gunpowder factory, which operated in this area in the early days of the court.

The garden is organised into collections and themed areas. Depending on what is open during your visit, you may find a house of orchids, a greenhouse of bromeliads, a cactus garden, a Japanese garden, and a house for carnivorous plants. There is also a sensory garden, designed so that people can touch and smell plants, including visitors with limited sight. Each area feels like a different small world inside the larger one.

Behind the public paths, serious science continues. The garden is a research institution. Botanists here study Brazilian plants, keep seed and specimen collections, and work on the conservation of threatened species, especially from the Atlantic Forest. So the place that began as a royal spice experiment now helps protect the plants that were growing here long before any king arrived. That shift, from profit to protection, tells you a lot about the last two centuries.

Before you leave, find a bench or a quiet spot under the palms. Notice how the light falls in narrow beams through the leaves. Notice the temperature, a little cooler than the street outside. Listen again for that soft rustle high above. As you walk back toward the gate, look once more down the long avenue of palms toward the mountain. Each tall trunk carries a small piece of this garden's story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning for cooler air and active wildlife.
Bring insect repellent for the shaded, humid paths.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks on gravel.
Carry water; the garden covers a large area.
Check which greenhouses are open before planning your route.
