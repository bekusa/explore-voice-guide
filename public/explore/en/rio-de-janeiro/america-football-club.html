The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere nearby a bus groans uphill, a vendor calls out, and a ball thuds against a wall. This is Tijuca, a working neighbourhood in Rio de Janeiro's North Zone. Here, red and white are not just colours. They belong to a club that has been part of these streets for well over a century. Welcome to America Football Club.

The name surprises most visitors. Why would a Brazilian football club call itself America? The answer belongs to the early years of football in Rio. At the start of the twentieth century the game was new and fashionable. Young men from the city formed clubs with names that sounded modern and open to the world. America was founded in 1904, which makes it one of the older football clubs in the city.

Those early decades were a different world. Football in Rio began as a pastime of the wealthy, played in starched shirts in front of well-dressed crowds. Over the following decades the game spread through the whole city. It moved from private grounds into factories, beaches and back streets. America grew with that change. It became a neighbourhood club in the truest sense, rooted in Tijuca and the North Zone around it.

Here is the first surprise. America is not a small club that never mattered. It won the Rio state championship, the Campeonato Carioca, several times. Its last title came in 1960. For a time, it stood beside the clubs that most of the world now knows. Rio football is often told as the story of four giants. America is a reminder that the story once had more voices.

Look at the colours around you. Red dominates. The club's mascot is the devil, and fans often call the team simply the Diabo. Another affectionate nickname is Mecão, a playful way of saying the big America. The devil is not meant as anything sinister. In Brazilian football, mascots are cheeky and theatrical. The red devil fits a club that likes to see itself as proud, stubborn and a little mischievous.

Now the second surprise, and it involves music. The composer Lamartine Babo is one of the great names of Brazilian popular song. Around the middle of the twentieth century he wrote anthems for many of Rio's football clubs. Those songs are still sung in stadiums across the city today. Lamartine had a personal loyalty, though. He was an America supporter. The club's anthem, by his own heart's choice, was the one written from the inside.

That anthem carries a promise that every America fan understands. In simple words, it says the fan will keep cheering for the club until the very end of life. For a club whose great trophies are decades old, that line means a great deal. Supporting America is not about easy glory. It is about loyalty. Locals sometimes say that America is every carioca's second club — the team people quietly wish well, even when their first love wears another shirt.

The modern era has asked hard questions of clubs like this one. Brazilian football became a national business, with television money and huge squads. Neighbourhood clubs struggled to keep up. America spent long periods outside the top divisions. Yet it never vanished. Its identity survived in Tijuca, in family memories, and in the red shirts worn by fans who know the club's story by heart.

The club also looked beyond Tijuca for a home ground. Its stadium today stands in Mesquita, in the Baixada Fluminense, the wide urban region to the north of the city. That move tells its own story about Rio. Space is scarce in the old neighbourhoods. Land, crowds and money shifted outward. Many traditional clubs have had to balance where they play with where their memories live.

Famous supporters have kept the club in the public eye. The striker Romário, one of the greatest Brazilian players of his generation, grew up supporting America. Late in his career he even pulled on the red shirt for the club. It was a gesture of affection more than a career move. For fans, it confirmed something they already believed: America may be modest in size, but it holds a real place in Rio's football heart.

As you stand here, notice how football sits inside everyday life in this part of the city. Tijuca is a neighbourhood of apartment blocks, corner bars, bakeries and steep streets climbing toward green hills. Behind them rises the Tijuca massif, part of one of the largest urban forests in the world. On match days, conversations in the bars turn quickly to the team. On other days, the club is simply part of the furniture of local life.

Take a moment to look for the club's crest and colours wherever they appear — on walls, flags, shirts, or the entrance itself. Notice the red against the pale concrete of the street. Listen to how people talk about the team, if you hear them. Even if you do not speak Portuguese, the tone is clear. It is the tone of people discussing a family member: proud, frustrated, and fiercely loyal all at once.

If you visit on a match day, the experience changes. Supporters gather early, often in small groups who have known each other for years. Songs start before the players appear. The crowds are smaller than at the giant stadiums, which brings you closer to the noise and the people. Many visitors find that a smaller club tells them more about Brazilian football culture than the famous arenas do.

Before you move on, look around once more. The street, the traffic, the hills behind the rooftops — this is the setting where a club has lived through more than a century of change. Trophies came and went. Divisions rose and fell. The red shirt stayed. As you walk away, listen for a ball against a wall, or a snatch of song from a doorway. In Tijuca, the devil is never very far away.
