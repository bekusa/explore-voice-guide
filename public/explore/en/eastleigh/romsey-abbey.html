The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Inside, the abbey holds a deep, cool quiet. Your footsteps fall on old stone and the sound travels up into the arches and comes back softer. Light enters through tall windows and lands in pale bands across the pillars. The air smells faintly of stone, wax and old wood. You are in Romsey, a small market town a short drive west of Eastleigh, inside one of the finest Norman churches in England.

Before we look at any dates, look at the shapes around you. Heavy round columns rise in steady rows. Above them sit round-headed arches, one level stacked over another. This is Norman architecture, the style the conquerors brought to England after 1066. It feels solid and calm. Notice how the arches repeat down the length of the nave, like a slow drumbeat. The builders wanted a church that would last, and it has.

The story here begins well before the Normans. A community of Benedictine nuns was founded on this site in the early tenth century, under the Anglo-Saxon kings of Wessex. Benedictines followed the Rule of Saint Benedict, a pattern of prayer, work and study through each day. Royal women often lived and studied here. For centuries, this was a house of women, run by an abbess, with real power and land in the surrounding valley.

Here is the first surprise. Under your feet are traces of that older Saxon church. In the floor, look for a hatch or covered opening. Beneath it, you can see the curved stone foundations of an earlier building that stood here before the present abbey. Most visitors walk right past it. It is a small window into the ground, and it shows how one church was built on top of another, layer upon layer.

The church you see now was mostly built in the twelfth century. Work moved slowly, over many decades, from the east end toward the west. If you walk the length of the building, you may notice small changes in style as you go. The arches near the west end become a little more pointed. That is the early Gothic style arriving, as fashions shifted during the long years of building. The stone carries the record of that slow change.

Romsey had royal connections. The young Scottish princess Matilda was educated here by her aunt, Christina, who was a nun at the abbey. Matilda later married King Henry the First and became queen of England. Later in the twelfth century, Mary of Blois, a daughter of King Stephen, became abbess here. These were not quiet, distant figures. The abbey sat close to the centre of royal life in medieval England.

The abbey also has its own saint, Ethelflaeda, an early abbess. Legend has it that she would slip out at night and stand in the cold waters of the river, chanting the psalms. Locals still tell the story. Whether or not it is true, it says something about how the town remembered her: as a woman of fierce devotion. The River Test, famous for its clear chalk water, runs close by the town today.

Now for the second surprise, and it is outside. Walk around to the south side of the church, near the south transept. On the wall, you will find a large stone carving of Christ on the cross. This is the Romsey Rood, an Anglo-Saxon sculpture. A rood is simply an old word for a cross or crucifix. Look at the figure carefully. Christ stands upright, arms straight out, with a hand reaching down from a cloud above.

Inside the church, there is a second, smaller Saxon carving of the crucifixion, in one of the side chapels. It is more crowded, with other figures around the cross. Compare the two if you can. Together they give you a rare chance to see Anglo-Saxon sculpture, work made before the Norman Conquest, still in the building it was made for. Very little of this kind of carving survives in England.

In 1539, King Henry the Eighth closed the monasteries of England. This was the Dissolution, when religious houses were shut and their buildings often sold or torn down. Many great abbeys fell into ruin. Romsey was different. The townspeople bought the abbey church from the king for one hundred pounds, to use as their parish church. Look for the document of that sale on display inside. It is the reason the building still stands complete.

That purchase is the heart of this place. The nuns' living quarters and cloisters are gone. But the church itself passed into the hands of ordinary local people, and they kept it. For nearly five centuries since, it has served the town as a working church. Services, weddings, choir practice and quiet prayer still happen here. You are not in a museum. You are in a parish church that happens to be very old.

The abbey also holds a modern story. Earl Mountbatten of Burma, a senior naval commander and a member of the royal family, lived nearby at the Broadlands estate. He was killed in Ireland in 1979, and he is buried here in the abbey. Look for his memorial stone. For many visitors, it is a reminder that this building is still part of the living history of the country, not only the distant past.

Before you leave, take one more slow look down the nave. Follow the rows of round arches into the distance. Notice the colour of the stone, warm in some places and grey in others. Listen to the quiet again. Then step outside into the town, where shops and cafés sit around the abbey on every side. The church has watched over these streets for close to nine hundred years... and the people of Romsey still come in through its doors.
