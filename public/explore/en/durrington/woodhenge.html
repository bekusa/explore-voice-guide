The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across open chalk downland, and somewhere a skylark is singing high above the grass. Under your feet the turf is short and springy. In front of you stand rings of low concrete posts, oddly modern, like the pieces of an unfinished game. They look plain at first. But each one marks the exact spot where a great timber post once stood, more than four thousand years ago.

This is Woodhenge, on the edge of Durrington in Wiltshire. It sits about three kilometres north-east of Stonehenge, in the same ancient landscape. The name is modern. It was chosen because the plan of the site reminded early archaeologists of its famous stone neighbour. But this monument was built from oak, not stone. The wood rotted away long ago. What survives is the pattern that the posts left behind in the chalk.

Here is the first surprise. For thousands of years, nobody knew Woodhenge was here at all. Above ground there was very little to see. Then, in 1925, a pilot named Gilbert Insall flew over these fields. From the air he noticed dark rings of spots showing in a crop. Below the surface, the old post holes held deeper, richer soil. The plants above them grew differently, and the hidden plan appeared from the sky.

That discovery made Woodhenge one of the early success stories of aerial archaeology. Soon afterwards, in the late 1920s, the archaeologist Maud Cunnington and her husband Benjamin excavated the site. They found six rings of post holes, set one inside another. The rings are not perfect circles. They are oval, stretched slightly in one direction. The Cunningtons then marked each post hole with a concrete marker, so visitors could read the plan on the ground.

Take a slow walk around the rings now. Notice how the markers are not all the same. Some are thicker than others. They reflect the size of the original post holes, so you can sense which rings held the heaviest timbers. The posts in the middle rings were especially large. Picture tall oak trunks standing here, perhaps several times your own height. Whether they carried a roof, or stood open to the sky, is still debated.

Now look along the long axis of the oval. It points roughly towards the north-east, the direction of sunrise at midsummer. That is the same alignment found at Stonehenge. It is hard to believe this is a coincidence. The people who raised these posts were watching the sky closely. They built the turning of the year into the shape of the place, using timber, rope, and a great deal of shared labour.

Around the rings you can still trace a low bank and a ditch, softened by thousands of years of weather and ploughing. This kind of enclosure is called a henge. That is a circular earthwork where the ditch usually lies inside the bank, not outside it as in a defensive fort. So the ditch was not built to keep enemies out. It seems instead to mark a boundary between ordinary ground and a special space inside.

Archaeologists generally date the main timber monument to the later Neolithic, the New Stone Age, around the middle of the third millennium before Christ. That places it in the same broad era as the big stone phases at Stonehenge. The builders used antler picks to dig into the chalk. They had no metal tools for this work. Every post hole you see marked here was dug out by hand, one piece of chalk at a time.

Near the centre of the rings, the Cunningtons found the burial of a young child. They believed the child had been a dedication, perhaps a sacrifice, made when the monument was built. That interpretation is still uncertain. Many researchers today treat it with caution. And there is a sad footnote. The remains were later kept in London, and they were destroyed during bombing in the Second World War. So the question can no longer be tested.

Here is the second surprise. Woodhenge is not alone. Just to the north lies Durrington Walls, an enormous henge many times larger than this one. Its bank is so broad that a road now runs through it. Excavations there in the early twenty-first century found traces of houses, hearths, and large quantities of pig bones. Many researchers think people gathered there for great feasts, possibly at midwinter.

One influential theory links the whole landscape together. In this view, timber monuments like Woodhenge belonged to the world of the living. Stonehenge, built from lasting stone, belonged to the ancestors and the dead. The River Avon, flowing nearby, may have joined the two places in a ceremonial journey. It is an idea, not a certainty. But standing here, it helps to imagine people moving between wood and stone, between life and memory.

If you have time, look out across the fields to the west. In one of them stands the Cuckoo Stone, a single, rough sarsen stone lying close to the ground. Sarsen is the hard local sandstone also used for the largest stones at Stonehenge. It is easy to miss. Yet it is part of the same ancient story, and it is a quiet walk from here across open country.

The setting today is simple and very English. There is a small parking area, a fence, a gate, and an information board. Traffic passes on the nearby road. Houses of modern Durrington stand not far away. That closeness is part of the experience. Here, a monument older than the pyramids at Giza shares a view with garden fences and bus stops. Ordinary life carries on all around it.

Before you leave, stand at the centre of the rings for a moment. Look towards the north-east, where the summer sun would rise. Feel the wind, and listen again to the larks above the grass. The great oaks are gone. But the plan they left is still here, held in the chalk under your feet. As you walk back to the gate, notice how small the markers look, and how large the idea behind them still feels.
