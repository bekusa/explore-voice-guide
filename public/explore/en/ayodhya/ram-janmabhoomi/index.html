The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead, a bell rings, then another, then a chorus of voices calling a single name: Ram. Bare feet move across cool stone, and the air carries marigold, incense and the faint smoke of camphor. Ahead of you rises a temple of pale pink sandstone, carved so densely that the surface seems to ripple in the light. You are standing at Ram Janmabhoomi in Ayodhya, a place that millions of Hindus believe marks the birthplace of Lord Rama.

To understand why this ground matters, start with a story. Rama is the hero of the Ramayana, one of the great epics of India. In it, a prince of Ayodhya is exiled to the forest, loses his wife Sita to a demon king, and wins her back with the help of an army of monkeys and bears. For centuries, Indian families have told this story to their children. For many devotees, Rama is not only a hero. He is a form of the god Vishnu, and an ideal of how a just ruler should live.

Ayodhya itself sits on the banks of the Sarayu River, in the state of Uttar Pradesh in northern India. It is an old pilgrimage town. Its lanes hold hundreds of temples, monasteries and bathing steps called ghats. Pilgrims have walked here for generations to bathe in the river and to visit the shrines connected with Rama's life. The temple in front of you is the newest of them, but the devotion that surrounds it is very old.

This site also carries a long and painful dispute, and an honest guide must mention it. In the early sixteenth century, during the time of the first Mughal emperor Babur, a mosque was built here. It became known as the Babri Masjid. Many Hindus believed it stood on the exact spot of Rama's birth, and on the ruins of an earlier temple. Many Muslims saw it as a place of worship like any other. For generations, both claims were argued in courts and in the streets.

On 6 December 1992, a large crowd of activists tore down the mosque. Communal violence followed in many parts of India, and many people lost their lives. The question of who owned this land then moved slowly through the courts for decades. In November 2019, the Supreme Court of India gave the disputed site for the construction of a temple. It also directed that a separate plot of land elsewhere in Ayodhya be given for a new mosque. For many people, the ruling closed a chapter. For others, the memory remains difficult.

Construction moved quickly after the verdict. A foundation ceremony was held in August 2020. Then, on 22 January 2024, the temple held its consecration ceremony, known in Hindi as pran pratishtha — the ritual that, in Hindu belief, brings life into a sacred image. Prime Minister Narendra Modi took part, and the event was broadcast across the country. Some parts of the wider complex have continued to take shape after that date, so you may notice work still underway around the edges.

Now look at the building itself. The temple follows the Nagara style, the classical temple tradition of northern India. In this style, a tall curved tower, called a shikhara, rises above the inner sanctum like a mountain peak. Mountains matter here. In Hindu thought, the temple tower recalls Mount Meru, the sacred mountain at the centre of the universe. The design is associated with the Sompura family of Gujarat, who have designed temples in this tradition for generations.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The pink sandstone comes from Bansi Paharpur in Rajasthan, a quarry known for this warm, rosy stone. And according to the temple's builders, the main structure was raised without steel reinforcement in the stone. Instead, it relies on the old method of carefully cut blocks that lock together. The idea was to build something that could last for many centuries, in the way that India's oldest stone temples have lasted.

As you move closer, slow down and study the columns. Almost every surface carries carving. You will see gods and goddesses, dancers, musicians, flowers and scrolling vines. Many figures come from Hindu stories, so a single pillar can feel like a page from a picture book in stone. Because the stone is new, the edges are still crisp. In a hundred years, rain and touch will soften them. Today, you can see the carvers' work almost exactly as they left it.

Inside the sanctum stands the central image of Ram Lalla, which means Rama as a small child. Here is the second surprise. The figure is not pale like the walls around it. It is carved from dark stone, almost black, by the sculptor Arun Yogiraj from Mysuru in southern India. Rama is often shown with dark skin in Hindu art, and the sculptor followed that tradition. The child stands with a calm face and holds a bow and arrow. He wears gold ornaments and fresh garlands.

Watch the people around you as much as the building. You may see an elderly couple who have saved for years to make this journey. You may hear a group from a distant state singing devotional songs in their own language. Some visitors cry. Some simply stand still with folded hands. Temple staff and volunteers keep the lines moving, and the crowds can be very large, especially on festival days such as Ram Navami, Rama's birthday, and around Diwali.

A few practical words. Security here is strict, and checks are thorough. Mobile phones, cameras and many personal items are usually not allowed inside, so plan to deposit them before you enter. You will remove your shoes, and the stone can feel hot under bare feet at midday. Modest clothing is wise. After your visit, many pilgrims also walk to Hanuman Garhi, a hilltop temple to the monkey god Hanuman, and down to the ghats of the Sarayu River at sunset.

Before you leave, pause once more. Look up at the tower against the sky, and then down at the stone beneath your feet. This ground means joy to some people and grief to others, and both feelings are part of its story. Listen again to the bells and the chanting voices. As you walk back toward the town, notice the flower sellers, the scent of incense in the lanes, and the river nearby — running quietly, as it did long before any of these walls were built.

Before you go

Practical tips

Deposit your phone and camera before entering the complex.
Arrive early in the morning to avoid the largest crowds.
Wear modest clothing and expect to remove your shoes.
Expect very heavy crowds around Ram Navami and Diwali.
Walk to the Sarayu River ghats afterwards for sunset.
