The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Out here on the plain of Takhar, it moves across low ground, lifts a fine pale dust, and carries it toward the river. The soil underfoot is soft and powdery, the kind that holds a footprint for a moment and then lets it go. Look around and you may see very little: a couple of low rises in the land, some fields, a wide sky. Yet beneath these mounds lie the remains of one of the most unusual Bronze Age settlements in Asia.

This is Shortugai. It sits in the far north of Afghanistan, close to where the Kokcha River meets the Amu Darya, the great river the ancient Greeks called the Oxus. Across that water lies Central Asia. Behind you, to the south and east, rise the mountains of Badakhshan. The location matters enormously. It tells most of the story before a single trowel goes into the ground.

Here is the first surprise. The people who founded this settlement were connected to the Indus Valley Civilisation, also called the Harappan civilisation. That was the Bronze Age world of great planned cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, in what is now Pakistan and northwest India. Those cities lay very far to the south, across mountain ranges. And yet, here on the banks of the Oxus, archaeologists found a small outpost of that same culture.

The site was identified and excavated in the late 1970s by a French archaeological team working in Afghanistan, led by the archaeologist Henri-Paul Francfort. They uncovered pottery made in the unmistakable Harappan style, with familiar shapes and painted designs. They found beads, small objects, and a carved seal. For specialists, these finds were remarkable. They showed that the Indus world reached much further north than anyone had expected.

Why would a community travel so far to settle in this place? The most widely accepted answer is a single brilliant blue stone: lapis lazuli. The mountains of Badakhshan, not far from here, are one of the most important ancient sources of lapis in the world. In the Bronze Age, this deep blue stone was prized from the Indus to Mesopotamia and Egypt. A settlement here could sit close to the source and control part of that trade.

Scholars also suggest other reasons. The region may have offered access to tin, a metal needed to make bronze, and possibly to gold from the rivers. So Shortugai is often described as a trading colony, a kind of frontier station. Picture merchants and craftspeople living far from home, keeping their own pottery styles and habits, while goods moved along routes through the mountains and valleys around you.

The seal found here deserts a moment of attention. Indus seals are small, usually square, and carved from soft stone. They often show an animal and a short line of signs from the Indus script, a writing system that no one has yet deciphered. The Shortugai seal carries the image of a rhinoceros. It is a striking choice of animal for a place with no rhinoceroses, a quiet reminder of a homeland far to the south.

The second surprise is about water. This plain does not receive much rain, so farming depends on rivers. The excavators found evidence that the early settlers built irrigation canals to bring water to their fields. These ancient channels show careful planning and real engineering skill. In a sense, the people of Shortugai were doing what farmers in this valley still do today: leading river water across dry ground to grow crops.

The settlement did not stay purely Harappan forever. In its later phases, the pottery and objects change. They begin to look more like the local Bronze Age cultures of Central Asia, the world archaeologists call the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, named after two ancient regions on either side of the Oxus. Over time, the outpost seems to have blended into its neighbours. That shift is part of what makes this site so valuable to researchers.

Now, an honest word about what you will see. Shortugai is not a city of standing walls or grand monuments. The houses here were built mainly of mud brick, which slowly returns to earth once a roof is gone. What remains on the surface is mostly the shape of the mounds themselves, perhaps some scattered pottery fragments, and the open landscape. The real treasures were excavated and recorded decades ago.

Because of this, the best way to experience the site is with your imagination and your eyes on the horizon. Notice how the land falls gently toward the rivers. Notice the line of mountains, which once held the blue stone that drew people here. Think about the distances involved, travelled on foot or with pack animals. Standing on these modest rises, you are on one of the northern edges of an entire ancient civilisation.

This corner of Takhar has drawn settlers many times. Not far away, at the same meeting of rivers, lies Ai Khanoum, a Greek city founded much later, after the campaigns of Alexander the Great. So within a short distance there are traces of two very different worlds, separated by well over a thousand years. Both chose this ground for similar reasons: water, fertile soil, and a position on routes between south and north.

Before you leave, take one more slow look around. Watch the dust move across the fields, and listen to the wind again. The mounds in front of you look like ordinary earth. But they hold the memory of people who lived very far from home, trading a blue stone across the mountains. As you walk away, notice the river in the distance. It still runs where those first canals once began.
