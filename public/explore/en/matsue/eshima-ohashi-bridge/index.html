The guide

The story

Stand at the water's edge and listen for a moment. Small waves slap against the stone embankment. A car engine rises in pitch, climbs, and then fades into the sky. Above you, a pale concrete curve lifts off the shoreline and arcs over Lake Nakaumi. From down here it looks less like a road and more like a ramp aimed at the clouds.

This is the Eshima Ohashi Bridge. It links two prefectures: Shimane, where you are in the wider city of Matsue, and Tottori, on the far shore at the port town of Sakaiminato. Opened in 2004, it carries traffic between the small island of Eshima and the Sakaiminato waterfront. For years it was just a useful local crossing. Then the rest of Japan suddenly noticed it.

The reason was a television commercial. In the early 2010s, a Japanese carmaker filmed a small car climbing the bridge. The camera used a long telephoto lens from far away. In the footage the road appeared to rise almost straight up, like a wall of asphalt. Viewers were amazed. Many people asked the same question: could a car really drive up something that steep?

Here is the first surprise. The bridge is not that steep at all. The gradient on the Shimane side is about six percent, and the Tottori side is a little gentler. A six percent slope is noticeable on a bicycle, but it is ordinary for a mountain road. The dramatic look comes from the lens, not the engineering. A telephoto lens squeezes distance together, so the long, gradual climb looks compressed into one sharp rise.

Locals gave the bridge a playful nickname: Beta-fumi Zaka. It means roughly the slope where you press the pedal all the way to the floor. The name is a joke about the commercial. In reality, most drivers cross without any special effort. Still, the nickname stuck, and today you will hear it from taxi drivers, shopkeepers, and tour guides around the lake.

So why is the bridge so tall in the first place? Look at the water below. Lake Nakaumi is a brackish lagoon, meaning its water is a mix of fresh and salt. It connects to the Sea of Japan through a narrow channel near Sakaiminato. Ships travel through this area, and the bridge needed to be high enough for vessels to pass underneath. The engineers chose to raise the road in a long hump rather than build a bridge that opens and closes.

The design is a type called a rigid-frame bridge. In simple terms, the deck and the supporting piers are joined into one continuous structure, rather than resting on separate bearings. This allows a long central span without cables or towers. It is often described as one of the largest bridges of its type in Japan. Notice how clean the outline is. There are no suspension cables and no arches overhead. The whole form is just one smooth, rising line.

Now turn your attention to the island beneath your feet. Eshima is a flat, low piece of land in the lake, and it belongs to Matsue. Nearby is Daikonshima, another island linked by road. Daikonshima is known across Japan for growing peonies and ginseng. If you visit in spring, you may see peony fields and gardens in bloom. Together, these islands give this corner of the lake a quiet, rural feeling, very different from the castle town of central Matsue.

Here is the second surprise. You do not have to stay in a car to experience the bridge. A pedestrian walkway runs alongside the road. Walking up the slope takes some time, and the wind can be strong near the top. But the reward is a wide view. On a clear day you can look across the lake toward the mountains of Tottori. Often the outline of Mount Daisen appears in the distance, its slopes changing colour with the seasons.

If you want to see the famous steep view for yourself, distance is the key. The illusion works best from far along the shoreline, looking at the bridge almost head-on. Bring a camera with a long zoom, or use the zoom on your phone. The more you zoom in, the steeper the road appears. Many visitors try both angles: a wide shot that shows the gentle truth, and a zoomed shot that shows the dramatic illusion.

The light here changes a great deal through the day. In the morning, the lake is often calm and grey-blue, and the concrete looks soft and pale. In the late afternoon, the sun drops toward the west and paints the water gold. Fishing boats sometimes move slowly across the lagoon. Birds gather on the shallows, because Lake Nakaumi is an important wetland for migrating waterfowl, especially in the colder months.

Across the water, Sakaiminato is a working fishing port. It is also the hometown of Mizuki Shigeru, the manga artist who created the much-loved yokai stories. Yokai are creatures from Japanese folklore, such as spirits and goblins. The town has a street lined with small bronze statues of his characters. Many travellers combine the bridge with a walk there. In that way, one short drive takes you from a quiet island to a busy harbour full of folklore.

Before you leave, look at the bridge one more time. Watch a car begin the climb, slow slightly, and then drop out of sight over the crest. Listen to the wind moving across the open water and the soft sound of the waves at the shore. This structure became famous because a camera made it look impossible. Standing here, you can see the quieter truth: a long, patient curve that lets ships pass below and lets two prefectures meet above the lake.
