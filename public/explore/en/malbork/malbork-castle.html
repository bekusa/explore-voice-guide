The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, the ground is brick, and above you, the walls are brick too. Millions of red clay bricks, laid by hand, row after row, in shades from deep rust to faded orange. On a sunny afternoon the walls feel warm to the touch. On a grey Baltic morning they look almost purple. Somewhere nearby, the river Nogat moves slowly past the outer walls. This is Malbork Castle, and almost everything you can see was shaped from mud and fire.

By land area, this is often called the largest castle in the world. That claim is easier to believe once you start walking. The complex spreads along the riverbank in layers, with courtyards inside courtyards and gates behind gates. It is also one of the largest brick buildings ever raised in Europe. Stone was scarce on this flat northern plain. So the builders used what the river delta gave them. That was clay, and plenty of it.

The people who built this place were the Teutonic Knights. They were a German military and religious order, born during the Crusades in the Holy Land. They were warrior monks. They took vows of poverty, chastity and obedience, and they also carried swords. In the thirteenth century they came to this Baltic region to conquer and convert the pagan Prussians, the people who lived here then. The knights began this fortress in the late thirteenth century. They named it Marienburg — the Castle of Mary — after the Virgin Mary, their patron.

At first it was a modest convent fortress. Then, in 1309, everything changed. The Grand Master, the head of the whole order, moved his seat here from Venice. Overnight, this riverside stronghold became the capital of a powerful state. The knights controlled trade in grain, timber and amber across the Baltic. Money flowed in, and the castle grew to match their ambition. Over the following decades, builders added wing after wing, until the fortress reached roughly the shape you see around you today.

The castle has three main parts, and it helps to know them as you walk. The High Castle is the oldest and the most private. It was the monastery itself, where the knights prayed, ate and slept. The Middle Castle held guests, offices and the Grand Master's own palace. The Outer Castle, sometimes called the Low Castle, held the workshops, stables and storehouses. Each part had its own defences. An enemy who broke through one gate would face another wall, and then another.

Inside the Middle Castle, look for the Grand Master's Palace. Many visitors consider it the finest room sequence in the whole complex. The Summer Refectory, a great dining hall, has a ribbed vault that spreads out from a single slender column, like the leaves of a palm tree. It looks almost too light to hold the ceiling. Legend has it that during a siege, an enemy cannonball was aimed at that column through a window, to bring the whole roof down. According to the tale, it missed.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. The knights had a central heating system. Furnaces below certain rooms heated stones, and hot air then rose through openings in the floor. Small vents still mark the floors in parts of the palace. In a cold Prussian winter, this was a real luxury. It also tells you something about the order. These were monks under vows of poverty, yet their Grand Master lived with warm floors.

The great turning point came in 1410. At the Battle of Grunwald, a joint army of Poland and Lithuania crushed the Teutonic Knights. The victors then marched here and laid siege to Malbork. But the walls held, and the siege failed. The order survived, though badly weakened. Decades of war and debt followed. During the Thirteen Years' War, the knights could no longer pay the mercenaries who defended the castle. In 1457, those unpaid soldiers handed the fortress to the King of Poland.

That is the second surprise in Malbork's story. The largest fortress of the order was not stormed. It changed hands over unpaid wages. For roughly three centuries after that, the castle served as a royal residence of the Polish kings. Royal guests stayed here, and the buildings were used for state business. Then, in 1772, the First Partition of Poland gave this region to Prussia. Under Prussian rule, parts of the castle became barracks and storehouses, and many fine interiors were damaged.

In the nineteenth century, attitudes changed. Romantic writers and historians in Germany began to treat the castle as a national monument. Long restoration campaigns followed, carried on into the early twentieth century. Restorers repaired walls, rebuilt vaults and recreated lost decorations. Some of what you see is medieval work. Some is careful nineteenth-century reconstruction. Telling the two apart is part of the fun. Look for changes in brick colour and texture — newer bricks often look smoother and more even.

Then came 1945. In the final months of the Second World War, fierce fighting around Malbork left the castle badly damaged. Large sections were in ruins, including parts of the High Castle and its church. After the war, the region became part of Poland, and Polish conservators began a long restoration. A museum was established here, and the work continued for decades. In 1997, UNESCO added the castle to its World Heritage List, recognising it as an outstanding example of medieval brick Gothic architecture.

On the outside of the castle church, look for the large figure of the Virgin Mary. It is covered in a bright mosaic of coloured glass. The medieval statue was destroyed in 1945, and for many years the niche stood empty. After careful reconstruction, Mary returned to her place in 2016. Her colours stand out sharply against the red brick. For the knights, she was the true owner of this castle. Their whole fortress, in a sense, was built in her name.

Before you leave, walk across to the far bank of the Nogat if you can. From there the whole complex lines up in one long view — towers, gables and walls reflected in the slow water. In the late afternoon, the bricks catch the low sun and turn a deep copper colour. Take a moment there. You are looking at a monastery, a fortress, a royal home and a war ruin, all in one building. And all of it, from foundation to rooftop, made of baked river clay.
