The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The air here smells of pine resin and damp earth, and somewhere below you a mountain stream runs over stones. The path climbs gently through tall fir trees. Then, between the branches, a house appears. It has steep roofs, dark timber beams, and pale plaster walls. It looks less like a fortress and more like a large family home in a fairy tale. This is Pelișor Castle, and its name means simply little Peleș.

That name tells you its place in the story. A short walk away stands Peleș Castle, the large and heavily decorated summer residence of King Carol I, Romania's first king. Carol had no surviving children of his own. His heir was his nephew, Ferdinand. When Ferdinand married a young British princess named Marie, the old king decided the couple needed a home of their own. He chose a site on the same royal estate, close by but separate.

Construction began at the very end of the nineteenth century and finished in the first years of the twentieth. The architect was Karel Liman, a Czech designer who had already worked on Peleș itself. From the outside, the two buildings share a family look. Both use a German Renaissance style, with half-timbered upper storeys, which means a frame of wooden beams filled with plaster. Look up at the roofline, and you will see turrets and pointed gables that echo the larger castle.

But step inside, and the mood changes completely. Peleș is dark wood, heavy carving, and grand ceremony. Pelișor is lighter, softer, and far more personal. The reason is Marie. She was a granddaughter of Queen Victoria of Britain, and she had strong opinions about art and decoration. She did not want to live inside her uncle-in-law's taste. She wanted something modern for her time — and for her, modern meant Art Nouveau.

Art Nouveau was a style that spread across Europe around 1900. It turned away from strict symmetry and copied the shapes of nature instead. Think of curving stems, flowing lines, flowers, and leaves. As you walk through these rooms, watch for those organic forms in the furniture, the lamps, and the carved panels. Much of the interior work was carried out by the Viennese designer Bernhard Ludwig, who shaped the rooms around Marie's vision.

Marie mixed influences freely. She loved Celtic designs, the interlaced knots and crosses of Ireland and Scotland that reminded her of her British childhood. She also loved Byzantine art, the golden religious style of the old Eastern Roman Empire, which connected her to her adopted Orthodox homeland. Here you will find both, side by side. It is an unusual combination. Most visitors notice the colours first, but the real surprise is how many different worlds Marie gathered into a single house.

The room that people remember most is the Golden Room. This was Marie's bedroom. Its walls are covered with gilded decoration in the shape of thistles and leaves. The thistle is the emblem of Scotland, and Marie had spent part of her youth in Britain, so the plant carried personal meaning for her. Stand still in the doorway for a moment. Notice how the gold catches whatever light comes through the windows and spreads it softly across the room.

This room holds a quieter story as well. Queen Marie died here at Pelișor in 1938. During her life she had become one of the most admired figures in Romania. In the First World War she worked with the wounded and visited soldiers near the front. After the war she travelled to the peace talks in Paris and argued for her country's borders. Romanians still remember her with real affection, and you may sense that respect among the local visitors around you.

Here is something many visitors miss. Marie asked that her heart be buried apart from her body, in a place she loved by the Black Sea. That wish began a long journey. The political changes of the twentieth century moved the heart more than once. In 2015, it finally returned here, to Pelișor, and it now rests in the Golden Room. So this bedroom is not only a museum space. For many people it is also a place of remembrance.

As you move through the other rooms, look at the scale. The ceilings are lower than at Peleș, and the spaces feel closer to a real home. There are studies, sitting rooms, and small chapels. You may notice painted wall decorations, stained glass, and wooden furniture with gentle curves. Many pieces were designed for these exact rooms. It helps to imagine the house in use, with letters on the desks, children's voices on the stairs, and mountain light through the windows.

The setting matters as much as the building. Sinaia sits in the Prahova Valley, at the foot of the Bucegi Mountains, part of the Carpathian range. The Romanian royal family came here in summer to escape the heat of Bucharest. The cool air, the forests, and the views made the valley a fashionable retreat. A small resort town grew up around the royal estate, with villas, hotels, and a railway line that brought visitors from the capital.

When you leave, take your time on the path. Turn around once and look back at the house among the trees. From here, the timber frame and the steep roofs look almost modest compared with the larger castle nearby. That modesty was partly the point. Pelișor was built as a home for a young couple who would one day become king and queen. Marie made it her own, room by room, with her own ideas.

Now listen again to the forest. The stream is still running, and the fir trees are still moving in the wind. Marie walked these same paths more than a century ago. As you walk back toward Peleș, notice how the light changes between the branches, and how the mountain air feels on your face. The house behind you is quiet now. But it still carries the taste, the hopes, and the memory of the woman who lived in it.
