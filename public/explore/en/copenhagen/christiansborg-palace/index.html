The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Bicycle bells chime from the bridges, and water laps against old stone embankments. Above you, a tall green copper tower rises over the rooftops. You are standing on Slotsholmen, a small island in the heart of Copenhagen. For more than eight centuries, this island has been the place where Denmark is governed. The building in front of you is Christiansborg Palace. In its current form, it is the third palace of that name on this exact spot.

The story begins long before any palace. In the twelfth century, this was a marshy islet beside a small fishing settlement. In 1167, Bishop Absalon, a warrior and churchman, built a fortress here. Its job was to protect the harbour from pirates. That fortress helped turn a village into a trading town. In time, the town became Copenhagen, which means "merchants' harbour" in old Danish. So when you stand here, you stand close to the city's first foundation stone.

Here is something many visitors walk straight past. Absalon's fortress did not vanish completely. Its remains lie beneath the palace you see today. Workers found the ruins in the early twentieth century, while they prepared the ground for the current building. Today you can walk underground among these foundations. You will see rough limestone walls, the base of a later castle, and old wells. The air down there is cool, and it smells of damp stone.

Absalon's castle was destroyed in the fourteenth century. A new stronghold called Copenhagen Castle replaced it, and it served the Danish kings for centuries. Then, in the eighteenth century, King Christian the Sixth wanted something grander. He wanted a palace that matched the great courts of Europe. He tore down the old castle and built the first Christiansborg. It was a large baroque palace, rich with ornament, and it took many years to complete.

This first palace did not last long. In 1794, a fire broke out and destroyed most of it. The royal family moved across town to Amalienborg, and the monarchs have lived there ever since. A second Christiansborg rose on the ruins in the early nineteenth century. The architect Christian Frederik Hansen designed it in a calm, classical style. Then, in 1884, fire struck again. Once more, most of the palace was lost.

Not everything burned, though. Look around the island, and you can find survivors from earlier palaces. The Palace Chapel, designed by Hansen, came through the 1884 fire. Its pale classical front and dome still stand beside the main building. Behind the palace, the riding ground and the royal stables survive from the first Christiansborg. That means some of the oldest parts of this complex are not grand halls. They are places built for horses.

The palace you see now was finished in the late 1920s. The architect Thorvald Jørgensen gave it a neo-baroque design. That means a modern building that borrows the heavy, dramatic style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Look at the walls. The lower parts are clad in rough granite, and the stone feels solid and almost raw. Higher up, the surfaces become smoother. The building looks less like a fairy-tale palace and more like a fortress of the state.

Now comes the detail that surprises most people. Christiansborg houses all three branches of Danish power under one roof. The Folketing, the Danish parliament, meets here. The Prime Minister's Office is here. The Supreme Court is here too. Many people describe it as the only building in the world that holds all three branches together. On a weekday, you may see politicians walking in with coffee cups, as if this were any office.

The monarchy still has a place here as well. The Royal Reception Rooms are used for state banquets, official visits, and ceremonies. When the rooms are not in use, visitors can walk through them. The Great Hall holds a series of large, colourful tapestries by the artist Bjørn Nørgaard. They were a gift to Queen Margrethe the Second. The tapestries tell Danish history in bold, modern images, and they reach all the way to the present age.

Look up again at the tower. It is the tallest tower in Copenhagen, and it has a public viewing room near the top. From there, the old city spreads out below you. You can see the canals, the copper spires, and the harbour that Absalon once defended. On a clear day, you can look across the water toward Sweden. The view helps you understand why someone chose this small island long ago.

Walk around the outside, and the island shows its many layers. Thorvaldsen's Museum stands beside the palace, painted in warm ochre with bright decorations. Across the canal, you can see the old Stock Exchange, known as Børsen. Fire struck that historic building in 2024, and the city watched in shock. For an island that has lost two palaces to flames, it was a painful echo. Restoration work continues there today.

In front of the main entrance, a statue of King Frederik the Seventh sits on horseback. During his reign, in 1849, Denmark adopted its first democratic constitution. That moved power from an absolute king toward an elected parliament. It seems fitting that he faces the building where that parliament now meets. Behind the palace, in the riding ground, a second equestrian statue shows King Christian the Ninth. Kings on horseback guard both sides of this democratic house.

Before you leave, take a moment to stand still. Feel the cobblestones under your feet, and listen to the water in the canals. Fortresses, castles, and palaces have risen and fallen on this spot. Each time, the island stayed the centre of Danish life. As you walk away, look back once at the green tower above the rooftops. It marks the place where Copenhagen began, and where Denmark still makes its decisions.
