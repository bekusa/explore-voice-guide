The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water laps against the stone edge of the canal, and a cyclist rattles over the cobbles behind you. Now look up at the long wall in front of you. It is not grey or white like so many museums. It is a warm ochre, almost the colour of baked bread. Running along it is a long painted procession of tiny figures, in green, blue and terracotta. This is the Thorvaldsen Museum, and it does not look like anything else in Copenhagen.

The building tells one man's story. Bertel Thorvaldsen was born in Copenhagen in 1770, the son of an Icelandic woodcarver. He trained at the Royal Academy here. Then, in 1797, he went south to Rome. He planned a short study trip. Instead, he stayed for about forty years. In Rome he became one of the most sought-after sculptors in Europe. Popes, princes and wealthy collectors all wanted his marble.

His style was neoclassical. That simply means he looked back to ancient Greek and Roman sculpture for his ideals. Think calm faces, balanced poses, smooth white surfaces and figures from myth. His breakthrough came in Rome with a statue of Jason, the Greek hero who carried off the Golden Fleece. A wealthy English collector saw the model and placed an order. From that moment, Thorvaldsen's workshop grew into a busy business with many assistants.

Now turn back to that painted wall. The frieze shows Thorvaldsen's homecoming in 1838. After decades abroad, he sailed back to Copenhagen on a Danish naval frigate. The city treated his arrival like a national celebration. Boats filled the harbour, and crowds lined the quays. The painter Jørgen Sonne turned that day into this long, colourful scene. Walk slowly along it. You can pick out the ship, the sailors, and people of every class pulling carts of sculpture.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Thorvaldsen gave his work and his collections to his home city. In return, Copenhagen promised to build a museum for them. The site was a royal coach house beside Christiansborg Palace, the seat of the Danish parliament today. The architect Michael Gottlieb Bindesbøll reshaped the old building into what you see now. The museum opened in 1848, and it is often described as the first public museum building in Denmark.

Bindesbøll took his ideas from ancient Greece, but also from Pompeii and from Egyptian temple doorways. Notice how the doorways along the facade narrow slightly towards the top. That shape comes from ancient Egypt and Greece. High above the main entrance stands a bronze goddess of victory driving a chariot pulled by four horses. It was cast after a design by Thorvaldsen himself. Strong colour, ancient forms, a bold profile against the sky... in the 1840s, this was a daring building.

Step inside, and the colour continues. The walls are painted in deep reds, blues, greens and yellows. The ceilings carry painted patterns, inspired by the houses that archaeologists were uncovering at Pompeii in that era. Under your feet, the floors are laid in patterned terrazzo, a mosaic of small stone chips set in mortar. The white sculptures stand out sharply against this colour. That contrast was the whole point. Bindesbøll wanted the marble and plaster to glow.

The ground floor is a ring of small rooms around a central courtyard. Each room holds just one or two works, almost like a chapel for a single statue. Take your time here. You will meet gods and goddesses from Greek myth, such as Mercury, Venus and Ganymede, alongside portrait busts of the famous people of his age. Look at the faces. They are calm and still. Yet the hands, the hair and the drape of the cloth show a very sure touch.

Another surprise is that many of the figures here are not marble at all. They are plaster. In Thorvaldsen's workshop, the original model was usually made in clay and then cast in plaster. Skilled carvers then copied that plaster into marble for the client. So these plaster models are, in a real sense, the closest thing to the artist's own hand. You can sometimes spot small metal points on their surfaces. Carvers used marks like these to measure and copy each form exactly.

At the end of the building is a long, high hall full of large works. Among them are plaster versions of Christ and the Twelve Apostles. The marble originals stand in the Church of Our Lady, Copenhagen's cathedral, just a short walk away. If you visit the cathedral later, you can compare the two. The figure of Christ, with open arms, became one of the most copied religious images of the nineteenth century, reproduced in churches and cemeteries across the world.

Now find the inner courtyard. In the middle, low and plain, lies Thorvaldsen's grave. He died in 1844, four years before the museum opened, and he was buried here at his own wish, surrounded by his work. The tomb is simple, and ivy grows over it. Above, the courtyard walls are painted with plants, palms and trees. It feels a little like an outdoor garden placed inside the building. It is a quiet place to pause.

Upstairs, the tone changes. Here you find the sculptor's private collections. He gathered ancient Egyptian, Greek and Roman objects, including coins, vases and small bronzes. He also collected paintings by artists working in Rome during his years there. You will also find some of his personal belongings. These rooms give a sense of the man behind the marble — a working artist, a collector, and a Dane who lived most of his life in Italy and never lost his ties to home.

Standing here, it is easy to see why this place matters so much to Copenhagen. A city welcomed back one of its own sons. It gave him a building full of colour, and it gave him a grave in the middle of his own art. The museum is less a store of objects and more a portrait of one life. Every room points back to the same man, the same years in Rome, the same long voyage home.

When you step back outside, stop by the canal once more. Look again at the painted frieze, and find the ship that carried him home. Behind you rise the brick and copper roofs of the palace island. Ahead, bicycles and boats move along the water, just as the crowds once did on that day in 1838. Let your eyes rest on that ochre wall for a moment. Then carry the calm of those white faces with you into the city.
