The guide

The story

Listen to your footsteps first. Outside, gravel and park paths. Inside, the sound changes. It becomes a soft echo under high ceilings, then a hush as you move into the galleries. This is the National Gallery of Denmark, known to Danes simply as SMK. It stands at the edge of a green park in central Copenhagen, and it holds the country's largest collection of art. Take a moment here before you rush in. The building itself is the first exhibit.

The story of this collection starts long before this building. In the seventeenth century, Danish kings gathered a royal cabinet of curiosities, called a Kunstkammer. It was a room of wonders, where paintings sat beside shells, scientific instruments, and objects from distant lands. Over generations, the paintings in that royal collection grew in number and in value. Slowly, the idea changed. Art was no longer only a private treasure for the king. It became something that belonged to the nation.

For many years, the royal paintings hung at Christiansborg Palace, in the heart of the city. Then, in the late nineteenth century, a fire swept through the palace. Much of the art was carried out and saved, but the paintings now needed a new home. The answer is the building you see today. It opened in 1896, designed by the architect Vilhelm Dahlerup. He chose a grand style that looks back to the palaces of the Italian Renaissance, the period of art and building that began in Italy around the fifteenth century.

Look at the old facade for a moment. It is long, symmetrical, and formal, in red brick with pale stone details. Arches, columns, and carved ornaments frame the entrance. It was built to tell visitors that this was a serious place, a temple for art. Inside the old wing, you will find a wide staircase, decorated walls, and tall rooms with daylight from above. Many museums of that era followed the same idea. Art should feel important, and so should the walk toward it.

Now walk to the back of the museum, and here comes the first surprise. Behind the nineteenth century building stands something completely different. In 1998, a large modern extension opened, designed by the architect Anna Maria Indrio. It is white, clean, and full of glass. Between the old wing and the new wing runs a long, tall hall that many people call the Sculpture Street. It is lined with sculptures, and light pours in from above. Glass walkways cross the space like bridges.

The extension also looks out toward the park, called Østre Anlæg. Large windows let you see trees, water, and walkers outside. This park sits on land that was once part of the old city defences, the ramparts and moats that protected Copenhagen. Today, the lakes are calm, and ducks move across them. Standing at those windows, you can feel how the museum connects the indoor world of art with the city outside. It is a pleasant place to rest your eyes between galleries.

The collection covers about seven centuries of art, from the late Middle Ages to the present day. In the European rooms, you can see works by Old Masters from Italy, Flanders, and the Netherlands. The museum holds paintings connected to famous names such as Rubens and Rembrandt. One of the treasures is a painting by the Italian artist Andrea Mantegna, showing Christ as the Suffering Redeemer, held up by angels. Look closely at the details in the background. Small figures and quiet scenes appear behind the main image.

For many visitors, though, the heart of the museum is Danish art. The first half of the nineteenth century is known in Denmark as the Golden Age. It was a time of economic hardship, yet artists produced calm, precise, and luminous paintings. Look for the work of Christoffer Wilhelm Eckersberg, often called the father of Danish painting. Look also for his students, such as Christen Købke. Their pictures show everyday Copenhagen, harbours, rooftops, and family portraits. The light in these paintings is soft and clear, very much like the northern light outside.

Here is a detail many people miss. Golden Age painters often worked from careful observation, studying the sky and the shapes of buildings with great patience. When you leave the museum later, look up at a Copenhagen sky on a clear day. You may recognise the same pale blue and the same gentle clouds. These artists did not paint dramatic storms or grand battles very often. They painted the quiet world around them, and they made it feel important.

Later in the galleries, you will meet Vilhelm Hammershøi, a Danish painter from around 1900. His interiors are almost silent. Grey walls, white doors, a woman seen from behind, a beam of light on a wooden floor. Very little happens in his pictures, and that is exactly the point. Stand in front of one for a full minute. Notice how the room around you seems to grow quieter too. Many visitors find these paintings strangely calming, and some find them a little haunting.

The second surprise waits in the French collection. The museum holds a significant group of works by Henri Matisse. One of the most famous is a portrait of his wife, often called The Green Line. A bold stripe of green runs straight down the middle of her face. When it was painted in the early twentieth century, many people found it shocking. Today, it is one of the most beloved works in the building. Look at how colour, not shading, creates the shape of her face.

The modern and contemporary rooms continue the story into our own time. Here, you will find installations, video, and large works that ask you to move around them. The museum also runs workshops for children and families, where young visitors can draw and build their own art. There are often temporary exhibitions as well, so the experience can be different each time you come. Check what is on before you plan your route, and give yourself time to wander without a fixed plan.

Before you leave, return to the Sculpture Street, or to one of the windows facing the park. Notice the change in light compared with the morning. Notice the sound of voices, softer here than in the city streets. Behind you hang centuries of paintings. In front of you, the trees of Østre Anlæg move in the wind. As you walk out, look once more at the brick facade, and then at the sky above Copenhagen. It is the same light those painters tried to catch.

Practical tips

Check temporary exhibitions online before planning your gallery route.
Rest at the park-facing windows between busy gallery rooms.
Combine your visit with a walk through Østre Anlæg park.
Spend a full quiet minute in front of a Hammershøi interior.
Bring children to the museum's family drawing workshops.
