The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead, a wooden roller coaster rattles over its tracks. Closer by, you hear horse hooves on gravel and wind moving through old beech trees. The air smells of candy floss, frying oil and damp forest floor. This is Dyrehavsbakken, which most Danes simply call Bakken, meaning the hill. It sits at the edge of a royal deer park, north of central Copenhagen, and it may be the oldest working amusement park in the world.

That claim surprises many visitors. They often assume Tivoli, in the city centre, must be older. Tivoli opened in 1843. Bakken's story starts more than two hundred and fifty years earlier, in the late sixteenth century. Tivoli was designed as a garden for pleasure from the start. Bakken grew slowly, almost by accident. It began with a crowd, and then the crowd needed to be fed, entertained and sheltered from the rain.

The crowd came for water. According to the traditional story, a woman named Kirsten Piil found a natural spring here in 1583. Spring water had a strong reputation in that era. People believed it could heal illness and give strength. Word spread, and visitors began to arrive every summer to drink from the source. The spring still carries her name today, Kirsten Piils Kilde. You can find it in the woods nearby, a short walk from the rides.

Where pilgrims gather, traders follow. Over time, the people who came to drink also wanted beer, food and music. Tents went up. Jugglers, fiddlers and fortune tellers set up near the paths. Slowly, a seasonal fair took shape on this slope beside the forest. Nobody planned an amusement park. It formed layer by layer, as each generation added its own entertainment. That is why Bakken still feels a little uneven and organic, rather than neatly designed.

Now look around you at the setting. The park sits inside Jægersborg Dyrehave, a large forest that was once a royal hunting ground. Danish kings hunted deer here on horseback. In the mid-eighteenth century, the forest was opened to ordinary people, and that changed Bakken's fortunes. Families from Copenhagen could now walk through the trees for a day out. In 2015, this forest became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognised for its historic hunting landscape.

The deer are still here. Hundreds of red deer and fallow deer live in the open woods around the park. On a quiet morning, you may see a group of them grazing just beyond the edge of the fairground. It is a strange and lovely contrast. On one side, there are flashing lights and screaming riders. On the other side, there are antlers moving slowly through the grass, as if nothing has changed for centuries.

Up on the high ground of the forest stands the Eremitage, a pale hunting lodge from the eighteenth century. Kings used it for meals during the royal hunts. It is not part of the amusement park, but it is worth the uphill walk. From the lodge, the land opens into wide grassy plains. Looking back, you can see how small Bakken really is — a cluster of colour at the edge of a great green space.

Back inside the park, find the old wooden roller coaster, known simply as Rutschebanen. It opened in 1932, and it still runs on a frame of timber. Wooden coasters feel different from modern steel ones. They shake, they creak, and they seem almost alive beneath you. For many Danish families, a ride on this coaster is a tradition passed from grandparents to grandchildren. Listen to it from the path, and you will hear why people love it.

Now meet the most important figure at Bakken. He is not a ride. He is a clown called Pjerrot, with a white face, a white costume and a pointed hat. Pjerrot has entertained children here since the nineteenth century. Different performers have played the role over the years, but the character stays the same. He tells jokes, sings and teases the crowd. For many Danes, Pjerrot is the true face of Bakken.

Bakken also has a grown-up side. A cabaret stage called Bakkens Hvile has hosted song and comedy for many decades. Its singers, known as the Bakke girls, perform old-fashioned revue songs in a warm, slightly cheeky style. Revue is a Danish theatre tradition of short songs and sketches about everyday life. Even if you do not understand the Danish lyrics, the mood is easy to follow. It is noisy, friendly and full of laughter.

Notice the people around you. Bakken has always been seen as the more down-to-earth cousin of Tivoli. Here you will find working families, school groups, students and retired couples sharing benches and beer. Many locals come back every season, almost like a ritual. The first visits of spring often feel like a celebration that winter is over. The park closes for the colder months, so its opening carries real meaning for people in the region.

The way you arrive is part of the experience. Most visitors take a train to Klampenborg station and then walk through the trees to the park entrance. The walk is short, but it changes your mood. You leave a suburban station behind and enter a forest. Then, suddenly, the music and lights appear between the trunks. Horse-drawn carriages also travel the forest paths, which adds to the old-world feeling of the day.

Take a final moment before you leave. Stand at the edge of the park, where the paved paths turn into forest tracks. Behind you, the rides keep spinning and the coaster keeps rattling. In front of you, the beech trees stand tall and silent. More than four hundred years ago, people walked into these woods looking for healing water. They found something else too — a place to gather, laugh and spend a summer afternoon together.

As you walk back toward the station, listen for how the sounds change. The music fades. The hooves and birdsong return. Perhaps you will pass a deer standing quietly between the trees. Bakken was never planned. It grew from a spring, a crowd and a forest. And it is still here, still noisy, still welcoming, at the edge of the king's old hunting ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the train to Klampenborg and walk in through the forest.
Come early morning to spot deer grazing near the park edge.
Wear comfortable shoes for the uphill walk to the Eremitage.
Check the season before visiting; the park closes in winter.
Bring a jacket, since the forest stays cool even in summer.
