The guide

The story

Start with the gravel. Listen to it shift and crunch under your feet as you climb the slope. Ahead, a long ochre-yellow façade catches the light, warm even on a grey Danish afternoon. Behind you, the land falls away in lawns, water and old trees. You are standing on Valby Hill, one of the higher points in this part of the city, and that height is the whole reason this palace exists.

First, a small surprise about where you are. Frederiksberg is not technically Copenhagen. It is its own municipality, an independent town completely surrounded by the capital. Locals sometimes call it an island on dry land. It has its own town hall, its own identity, and a certain quiet pride. This palace, sitting high above the gardens, is the heart of that identity. The district even takes its name from it.

The story begins with a king and a journey. In the late seventeenth century, the young Crown Prince Frederick travelled through Italy. He saw villas set on hillsides, with terraces and gardens running down towards the view. He came home with those images in his head. As King Frederick the Fourth, he chose this hill for a summer residence of his own. The first palace was finished in the early years of the eighteenth century.

That Italian dream is still visible if you know where to look. The first building was smaller and plainer than what you see now. It was built in the Baroque style, the grand, symmetrical manner of that era, where everything is arranged to impress and to frame the view. Over the following decades, later kings extended the palace. Wings were added, and the building grew into the long, formal shape in front of you today.

Take a moment with that colour. The warm yellow is typical of Danish royal and noble buildings, and you will see similar tones across Copenhagen. Against a blue sky, the façade glows. Under cloud, it seems to hold the light that is missing from the air. Look along the rows of tall windows. Their steady rhythm is part of the Baroque idea: order, balance, and a sense of calm control over nature.

For much of the eighteenth century and into the nineteenth, this was where the royal family came to escape the city. In summer, the court moved out here for fresh air and space. The palace was close enough to the capital for a short journey, yet it felt like the countryside. Imagine carriages on the road below, servants carrying supplies up the hill, and music drifting out of open windows on long summer evenings.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the early nineteenth century, King Frederick the Sixth was especially fond of this place. The gardens were open to ordinary people, and the royal family sometimes sailed along the canals in small boats. Townspeople gathered on the banks to watch. It was a rare moment of closeness between a monarch and his subjects. Several Danish paintings from that era show these relaxed scenes, with families picnicking under the trees.

Then the palace changed its purpose. In the second half of the nineteenth century, the royal family stopped using it as a residence. The building was handed to the army. Since then, it has been home to the Royal Danish Army Officers' Academy. That is the second surprise: this elegant summer palace is a working military school. Behind those windows, young officers study and train, much as cadets have done here for well over a century.

That is also why you cannot simply walk inside whenever you like. The palace is not a museum in the usual sense. Public access to the interior is limited to occasional guided tours, which must be arranged in advance. If you do get inside, look for the palace chapel and the decorated ceilings of the older rooms. If not, the exterior and the setting tell most of the story on their own.

Now turn around and face the slope. Below you lie Frederiksberg Gardens. The first gardens here followed the strict Baroque fashion, with straight lines and clipped hedges. Later, in the late eighteenth century, they were reshaped in the romantic English style. That style imitates nature itself: winding paths, open lawns, irregular lakes and carefully placed groups of trees. Nothing looks planned, yet almost everything was.

Walk down into those gardens and you will find small buildings hidden among the trees. Look for the Chinese pavilion, built when European courts were fascinated by Chinese art and design. There is also a small temple-like structure and a grotto. Listen for birds, too. A colony of grey herons nests in the tall trees, and in spring their large nests and harsh calls are hard to miss. On the canals, ducks and swans drift past the old bridges.

One more unexpected neighbour sits right beside the palace: Copenhagen Zoo. It shares the hill with the gardens. From certain paths, you can catch a glimpse of the zoo's elephant enclosure, which was designed to look out over the park. Hearing an elephant trumpet near a Baroque palace is a strange experience. Across the road, the wilder park called Søndermarken continues the green space, with large trees and quieter corners.

Today, the hill belongs mostly to locals. Joggers run the paths in the early morning. Families spread blankets on the lawns in summer. Older couples walk the same circuit they have walked for decades. In winter, frost settles on the grass and the bare trees open up the view. The palace above stays the same: steady, yellow, watching over it all.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the top of the slope. Look down the line of the gardens, the way an eighteenth-century king once did. Notice the sound of the gravel, the birds in the trees, the distant hum of the city that never quite reaches up here. As you walk away, glance back once. From below, the palace looks exactly as it was meant to look: calm, balanced, and quietly in charge of its hill.
