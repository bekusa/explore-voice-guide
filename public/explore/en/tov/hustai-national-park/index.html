The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across the grass in long, low waves. Somewhere a marmot gives a sharp whistle, then goes quiet. The air smells of crushed wild thyme and dry earth. Under your feet, the ground is firm, springy, and slightly uneven. This is the open steppe of central Mongolia. The hills roll away in soft folds of green, gold, or pale brown, depending on the season. Welcome to Hustai National Park.

The park lies a short drive west of Ulaanbaatar, in Töv Province, whose name simply means the central province. Yet the city feels very far away here. There are no fences across the horizon and no traffic noise. The sky is the largest thing in view. On a clear day, the light is so sharp that distant ridges look closer than they are. Many visitors say the silence takes some getting used to.

The name Hustai comes from the Mongolian word for birch. Look at the north-facing slopes of the hills, and you will understand why. Pale birch trunks gather there in small groves, along with darker larch. The south-facing slopes, which get more sun, stay open and grassy. Ecologists call this mix forest-steppe. It is a border zone between the great Siberian forests to the north and the dry grasslands that stretch south toward the Gobi.

But most people come here for one animal. It is a small, stocky wild horse. Mongolians call it the takhi. Much of the world knows it as Przewalski's horse, named after a nineteenth-century Russian explorer who described it to European scientists. It is the last true wild horse on Earth. It was never domesticated. That makes it very different from the herds of Mongolian riding horses you may have passed on the road.

Here is the first surprise. For several decades, this horse did not exist in the wild at all. The last confirmed sightings in Mongolia came in the late 1960s. Hunting, competition with livestock, and harsh winters had pushed the takhi out of its last refuges. The species survived only in zoos and private collections, mostly in Europe and North America. Every living takhi descended from a very small group of captive animals.

In the 1990s, a cooperation between Dutch conservationists and Mongolian partners began to bring the horses home. The first animals arrived at Hustai in 1992, flown from European breeding programmes. They were kept in large enclosures at first, so they could adjust to the climate and the grass. Then they were released onto the open steppe. Many people doubted that zoo-born horses could survive Mongolian winters. They did. Today, the takhi here live and breed fully in the wild.

The land around you was given stronger protection as the project grew, and it became a national park in the late 1990s. Success here helped change the species' global status. Conservation bodies no longer list the takhi as extinct in the wild. It is still rare, and still endangered. But Hustai became one of the places that proved reintroduction of a large animal can work. Other herds in western Mongolia and elsewhere followed.

So how do you recognise a takhi? Look for a compact, heavy body, a large head, and a sandy or reddish-brown coat. The belly and muzzle are pale. The mane stands straight up, short and stiff, like a brush. There is no long forelock falling over the eyes. A dark stripe often runs along the spine. Sometimes faint stripes show on the legs. And here is the second surprise. Takhi have a different number of chromosomes from domestic horses.

The horses live in family groups called harems. Each group has one stallion, several mares, and their foals. Young males without a harem often form loose bachelor groups. If you watch carefully, you can see the stallion keeping slightly apart, head up, watching. Mares stay close to the foals. When two groups meet, there may be a short display of snorting and stamping. These are not performances. This is how wild horses have organised their lives for a very long time.

The takhi do not live alone here. Red deer move through the birch groves. Mongolian gazelles sometimes cross the open plain in fast, light groups. Marmots dig burrows across the grassland, and their whistles carry far. Birds of prey circle overhead, hunting for small rodents. Wolves also live in the park. They are rarely seen, but they are part of the system. They sometimes take young foals, which is hard to hear, but it is what a real wild population faces.

People have lived on this land for thousands of years, too. Across the wider region, you can find ancient burial mounds and carved standing stones from earlier nomadic cultures, including the Turkic peoples of the early medieval period. Herding families still graze their animals in the buffer zone around the park. You may see a white felt ger, the round Mongolian tent, with smoke rising from its chimney. Livestock and wild horses share the same horizon here.

Timing matters if you want to see the horses well. In the middle of the day, they often rest on high ground, far from the tracks. In the early morning and in the evening, they tend to come down toward water and better grass. That is when the low sun turns their coats almost copper. Keep a respectful distance and stay near your vehicle or guide. Quiet voices help. The horses notice far more than you might think.

Before you leave, take one more slow look around. Notice the colour of the grass at your feet, and the pale line of birch trees on the nearest slope. Listen again for the wind and the marmots. Somewhere on these hills, a family of wild horses is grazing, as their ancestors did before they almost vanished. They are here because people decided to bring them back. As you walk away, let that quiet stay with you a little longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning or evening to see horses near water.
Bring binoculars; herds often graze on distant ridges.
Pack warm layers, as steppe weather changes quickly.
Stay near your vehicle or guide when approaching herds.
Keep voices low so the horses stay relaxed.
