The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of Rua dos Clérigos and tilt your head back. The street climbs steeply under your feet, its stones polished smooth by centuries of shoes. Above the rooftops, a grey granite tower rises into the sky, carved with garlands and scrolls. Around you, trams rattle, gulls call from the river, and the smell of roasted coffee drifts out of a doorway. This is Clérigos, the church and tower that Porto uses to find itself.

The name means 'of the clergy'. It comes from a brotherhood called the Irmandade dos Clérigos, a charitable fraternity of priests. In the early eighteenth century, this group cared for poor and sick members of the clergy. They wanted a church of their own, and a building that showed their standing in the city. So they chose a sloping plot on a hill above the old town. It was a difficult site — and it shaped everything that followed.

The man they hired was Nicolau Nasoni, an Italian from Tuscany. He was a painter and decorator before he was an architect. He came to Porto and stayed for the rest of his life. You can see the painter in his buildings. The stone is not simply cut — it is decorated, almost like a picture frame. Nasoni brought the Baroque style of Italy, a style of curves, drama and movement, and he worked it into the hard local granite.

That granite matters. Porto is a granite city, and the grey stone here is tough and coarse. It is much harder to carve than the soft marble of Rome or Florence. Look closely at the façade and you will see shells, flower garlands, flaming urns and curling scrolls. Every one of those shapes was cut from this difficult stone by hand. Against white plaster walls, the dark granite stands out sharply, like ink drawn on paper.

The church came first. Construction began in the 1730s and the main building was finished around the middle of the century. The famous tower came later and was completed in the 1760s. The two parts are joined by a long building at the back, which held rooms for the brotherhood. So when you walk around the site, you are walking around one long complex — church at one end, tower at the other, stretched along the slope.

Here is the first surprise. Step inside the church and look at the shape of the nave, the main space where people sit. It is not a long rectangle, as in most churches. It is an oval. This was an unusual and ambitious choice in Portugal at the time. The curved walls pull your eyes forward towards the altar. The space feels gathered and close, almost like a jewel box carved out of stone.

The interior is rich with colour. You will see coloured marble, gilded woodwork and carved detail around the main altar. Light comes in from high windows and moves slowly across the gold during the day. The room is often quiet, even when the street outside is loud. Take a moment to sit. Listen to the soft footsteps on the stone floor and the low voices of other visitors.

Now the second surprise. Nasoni loved this project so much that he asked to be buried here. He was a member of the brotherhood himself, and he was laid to rest in the church when he died. There is no grand tomb for the architect of one of Porto's best-known buildings. He simply lies within the work he made. Many visitors walk through without knowing that the designer is still here.

The tower is the part everyone remembers. It rises around seventy-five metres above the street, and for a long time it was one of the tallest structures in Portugal. Sailors coming up the Douro River could use it as a landmark. From the water, from the bridges, from the hills across the river, you can still pick it out. Locals sometimes give directions with it. If you are lost in Porto, look for the tower.

Climbing it is a real effort. A narrow stone staircase winds upward, with more than two hundred steps. The walls close in and the air grows cooler as you rise. Along the way, you pass the bells, which still ring above the old town. Then you step out onto a small, high balcony. The wind pushes against you, and the whole city spreads out below in orange roof tiles and grey stone.

From the top, take time to orient yourself. To the south, the Douro River curves between steep banks. Across the water are the port wine lodges of Vila Nova de Gaia, with their names painted on the roofs. Closer in, you can trace the cathedral on its hill and the long avenue of Aliados running down to the city hall. On a clear day, you may even see the Atlantic Ocean shining to the west.

The area around Clérigos is part of the historic centre of Porto, which UNESCO protects as World Heritage. Just behind the tower is a modern square with a grassy roof garden and olive trees, where people sit and eat lunch. A short walk away is the Lello bookshop, with its carved wooden staircase and long queues. Street musicians often play on the slope below the church. The old and the new sit very close together here.

Before you leave, walk a little way down the hill and turn around. From below, the tower looks taller and thinner, rising above the narrow street like a finger pointing at the sky. Notice how the light changes on the granite — silver in the morning, warm gold late in the afternoon. Notice the gulls circling the top. Two and a half centuries after Nasoni finished it, the tower still does its first job. It tells you where you are.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the tower early to avoid narrow staircase queues.
Wear grippy shoes for the steep street and stone steps.
Buy a separate ticket for the tower and museum.
Return near sunset to see warm light on the granite.
Pair your visit with the nearby roof garden behind the tower.
