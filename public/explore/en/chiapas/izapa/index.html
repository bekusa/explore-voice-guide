The guide

The story

Listen first. Insects hum in the long grass, and somewhere nearby a truck shifts gears on the highway. The air is thick and warm, heavy with the smell of wet earth and green leaves. Underfoot, the ground is soft and uneven, and here and there round river stones push up through the soil. You are standing in Izapa, in the far south of Chiapas. This is one of the oldest ceremonial centres in this corner of Mexico, and it is quieter than you might expect.

Izapa sits in the Soconusco, a narrow, fertile strip of land between the Sierra Madre mountains and the Pacific Ocean. The city of Tapachula is a short drive away, and the border with Guatemala is close. On a clear morning, look north. The Tacaná volcano rises above the green hills, its upper slopes often wrapped in cloud. That mountain is not just scenery. Many visitors never realise how closely this place and that peak may be connected... but we will come back to that.

People lived here for a very long time. Archaeologists believe the area was settled well over three thousand years ago, in what scholars call the Formative or Preclassic period — the long era before the great Maya cities of the Classic age. Izapa reached its height roughly in the centuries just before and after the start of the common era. During that time it was a major ceremonial and political centre for this coastal region.

Now look at the mounds around you. They may not look like the pyramids you have seen in photographs. There are no tall, sharp walls of cut stone here. Instead, the builders shaped platforms from packed earth and faced them with river cobbles. Over the centuries, rain and vegetation softened their edges. What remains are grassy hills arranged around open plazas. Try to picture them freshly finished, with plastered surfaces and wooden temples on top.

The site is spread out in several separate groups, scattered among farms and small villages. The group most visitors see first sits right beside the highway. Archaeologists have restored part of it, so the plaza and the platforms are easier to read. Here you can also see a long, narrow court between two parallel mounds. This is a ballcourt, where people played a ritual ball game that was known across ancient Mexico and Central America.

The ball game was much more than sport. Players struck a heavy rubber ball with their hips, not their hands. For many cultures in this region, the game carried religious meaning, linked to the movement of the sun and the struggle between life and death. Stand at one end of the court and look down its length. The space feels modest, almost intimate. Spectators would have stood very close to the action.

But the true treasure of Izapa is its sculpture. Archaeologists have recorded many dozens of carved stone monuments here. The most important are stelae — tall, flat standing stones carved with scenes — and they are often paired with low, round stones called altars. Together, these carvings form one of the largest collections of early sculpture anywhere in Mesoamerica, the cultural region that stretches from central Mexico into Central America.

The carvings at Izapa have their own recognisable style. Scholars call it simply the Izapan style. The scenes are crowded and full of movement. You see human figures, gods, serpents, birds, and water flowing along the edges. Many carvings place the action under a band that seems to represent the sky, and above a band that seems to represent the earth. Reading them is like reading a stage, with heaven above and the world below.

Here is the first surprise. Many experts see Izapa as a bridge between two great traditions. Some of its imagery recalls the older Olmec culture of the Gulf Coast. Other features look forward to the later Maya. Certain mythological figures carved here seem to echo stories that appear centuries later in Maya sacred writing. So this small, quiet field may hold an early chapter of a story that later filled whole cities.

The most famous carving is known as Stela 5. It shows a large tree at its centre, surrounded by seated figures and a dense web of smaller images. Many people call it a Tree of Life. Its meaning is still debated, and you will hear very different interpretations from scholars, guides, and religious groups. The honest answer is that no one can read it with certainty. That mystery is part of its pull.

Be aware that many of the original monuments are no longer standing in the field. Some were moved to museums for protection, including the regional museum in Tapachula and the national museum in Mexico City. In the groups you visit, you may find original stones, replicas, or empty spots where a carving once stood. Look closely at the surfaces that remain. Even weathered stone can still show the faint curve of a body or a coil of a serpent.

And now, the second surprise — the volcano. Some researchers have suggested that the builders aligned parts of Izapa with Tacaná and with the movement of the sun across the year. A few have even proposed that the ancient calendar of this region may have roots near here. These ideas remain theories, not settled fact. Still, stand in the plaza, face the mountain, and you can feel why people might have built their sacred spaces toward it.

There is a human side to Izapa today, too. Several of the groups sit on land worked by local families. Cacao, mango, and banana trees grow close to the ancient platforms. Cacao matters here. The Soconusco was famous for its cacao long before Europeans arrived, and the beans were valued so highly that they were traded and even used like money. The trees around you continue a very old story.

Before you leave, pause for a moment in the centre of the plaza. Feel the heat on your shoulders and the soft earth under your shoes. Notice how the grassy mounds frame the sky, and how the mountain watches from the north. The traffic on the road keeps moving, unaware. As you walk back, look down at the river stones in the path. People carried stones like these here more than two thousand years ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with the restored group beside the Tapachula highway.
Visit early morning for cooler air and clearer volcano views.
Bring water, sunscreen, and insect repellent for humid conditions.
See original stelae at the regional museum in Tapachula.
Wear sturdy shoes for soft, uneven ground between mounds.
