The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Far below the stone walls, the Caribbean breaks against the cliff with a slow, heavy sound. Wind moves through the rainforest behind you. Under your feet the ground is grey stone, rough and pitted, warm from the sun and green with moss in the shaded corners. This is Fort San Lorenzo. It sits high on a cliff where the Chagres River meets the sea.

Look out toward the water and you will see why the Spanish chose this spot. The river mouth lies directly below. For centuries, the Chagres River was a highway across the narrowest part of the Americas. Boats travelled upstream into the jungle, and from there mules carried goods overland to Panama City on the Pacific side. Whoever held this cliff controlled that door between two oceans.

That door carried enormous wealth. Silver from Peru and gold from across Spanish America crossed the isthmus on its way to Spain. Goods from Europe travelled the other way. Some of this trade moved along the Chagres, and the Spanish crown knew that pirates and rival powers were watching. So, in the late sixteenth century, the Spanish began to build defences here. The work is often linked to the Italian military engineer Bautista Antonelli, who designed fortifications across the Spanish Caribbean.

The first fort was not the stone structure you see today. Early defences here used wood and palm thatch, and that detail matters for the story that comes next. In 1671, the Welsh privateer Henry Morgan sent his men to take this fort. He needed the river to reach Panama City, the richest Spanish town on the Pacific coast. The defenders fought hard from the cliff top, and the attackers suffered heavy losses on the approach.

Here is a story most visitors never hear. According to an account written by a man who sailed with the buccaneers, one attacker was hit by an arrow. He pulled the arrow from his own body, wrapped it in cotton, set it alight, and fired it back. The burning arrow landed on the palm roofs inside the fort. Fire spread, gunpowder exploded, and the defence collapsed. Historians treat some details with caution, but the fire itself is part of the record.

After taking the fort, Morgan's men moved up the Chagres and crossed the jungle. They sacked Panama City soon after. The old city was destroyed, and the Spanish rebuilt it at a new site nearby. So the fall of this small fort on a cliff had consequences for the whole region. It changed the map of the Pacific capital itself.

The Spanish rebuilt San Lorenzo, this time with more stone. Then, in 1740, another attack came. The British admiral Edward Vernon, fresh from his capture of Portobelo along this same coast, turned his guns on the fort. Ships bombarded the walls from the sea, and San Lorenzo fell again. Once more, the Spanish returned and repaired it. Much of what you walk through now comes from later rebuilding in the eighteenth century.

Take a look at the building materials around you. Many of the walls are made from coral stone and local rock, cut into blocks and set with mortar. Over time, salt air, rain, and tropical heat have eaten into the surface. You can see small holes, soft edges, and dark stains where water runs during the rainy season. Plants push through the joints. The forest is always trying to take this place back.

Walk toward the dry moat, the deep trench cut into the rock. It separated the fort from the land behind it, so attackers from the jungle side faced a sudden drop. A bridge once crossed it. On the inner side, look for the vaulted rooms built into the walls. Soldiers stored gunpowder and supplies in spaces like these. Step inside one and notice how the temperature drops, and how the sound of the sea becomes a low hum.

On the walls facing the water, old iron cannons still point toward the river mouth. They are rusted, rough, and heavy-looking. Stand behind one and follow its line of fire. You can picture a sailing ship trying to enter the Chagres, and the gunners above with every advantage of height. The view from here explains the whole military logic of the fort better than any map.

The fort's story did not end with the Spanish. In the mid-nineteenth century, during the California Gold Rush, thousands of travellers came through the Chagres on their way west. They took boats up the river and crossed the isthmus rather than sail around South America. Later, a railway and then the Panama Canal changed everything. Upstream, the Gatun Dam turned part of the river into Gatun Lake, a key part of the canal.

In 1980, UNESCO listed San Lorenzo together with the fortifications of Portobelo as a World Heritage Site. In 2012, the site was added to the list of World Heritage in Danger, because of decay and the pressure of weather and time. The fort also lies inside a protected forest area, on land that the United States military once used. That is why the road here passes through such dense, quiet jungle. Howler monkeys, toucans, and many other birds live in these woods.

Before you leave, walk back to the edge of the cliff. Look down at the brown river water mixing with the blue of the sea. Listen to the waves again, and to the birds in the trees behind you. Many ships once came and went at this river mouth, along with soldiers, pirates, and gold seekers. Today it is mostly quiet. As you walk away, notice the moss on the stones, and the way the forest closes in around the old walls.
