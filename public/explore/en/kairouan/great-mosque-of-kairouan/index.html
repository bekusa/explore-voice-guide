The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The marble of the courtyard is cool and smooth under the soles, worn soft by centuries of bare and slippered steps. The light here is white and hard, and it bounces off the pale stone so strongly that you may need to narrow your eyes. Around you, rows of horseshoe arches throw clean bands of shadow. Beyond the walls, the city goes on with its scooters and voices. But inside, the loudest sound is often the wind... and the soft calls of pigeons on the ledges.

You are standing in the Great Mosque of Kairouan, also known as the Mosque of Uqba. It is one of the oldest mosques in North Africa, and one of the most important in the whole Islamic world. Its story begins in the year 670. An Arab general named Uqba ibn Nafi led his army into this flat, dry plain and founded a camp here. That camp became Kairouan. The first mosque on this site was part of that founding moment.

That first building was simple, and almost nothing of it survives. The mosque you see today is largely the work of the ninth century. At that time, a local dynasty called the Aghlabids ruled this region as governors for the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad. Around 836, the Aghlabid ruler Ziyadat Allah the First rebuilt the mosque on a grand scale. Later rulers added to it and repaired it. So the walls around you carry the work of many generations, layered one over another.

From outside, the mosque looks more like a fortress than a place of prayer. The outer walls are thick and heavy, supported by strong buttresses. That shape makes sense. Kairouan began as a military base on an open plain, and the mosque had to feel solid and permanent. The colour of the stone is a warm sandy brown, close to the colour of the land around the city. In the late afternoon, these walls turn almost golden.

Now look up at the minaret. It stands at one end of the courtyard, square and massive, rising in three stacked levels. Each level is a little smaller than the one below it. This tower is often described as one of the oldest standing minarets in the world. Its square, stepped form had a long influence. Many later minarets across North Africa and Andalusia, the Muslim lands of medieval Spain, follow a similar square plan. Here is the model, still standing.

Here is something many visitors walk straight past. Look closely at the lower parts of the minaret and parts of the walls. Some of the blocks are reused stones from older buildings, and a few carry carved Latin letters. These pieces came from Roman sites in the region. The builders of Kairouan did not have a quarry of fine marble nearby. So they took what the ruined Roman and Byzantine cities left behind, and set it into a new building with a new purpose.

That habit of reuse becomes even clearer when you look at the columns. The porticoes around the courtyard and the great prayer hall stand on a forest of columns. Look at them one by one. They do not match. Some are smooth marble, some are veined granite, some are porphyry with a deep reddish colour. The capitals at the top differ too. Many of these columns were brought from Roman and Byzantine ruins, including, it is said, ancient Carthage near modern Tunis.

There is a popular legend about these columns. Locals say that no one can count them all correctly. According to one version of the tale, anyone who tries too hard will lose their sight. It is folklore, of course, but it tells you something real. When you look into the prayer hall, the rows of columns and arches repeat and repeat, and the eye truly does lose its place. Counting them is harder than it sounds.

Now turn your attention back to the courtyard floor. It is not perfectly flat. The surface slopes gently toward the centre, where you will find a decorative drain. This is a clever water system. In a dry land, rain was precious. When it fell, the courtyard guided the water down into a large cistern below your feet. Look for the stone wellheads around the courtyard. Their rims show deep grooves, cut over centuries by ropes pulling up buckets.

The prayer hall is the heart of the mosque. Non-Muslim visitors are usually not allowed inside, but you can often look in through the open doors. If you can, let your eyes travel down the wide central aisle. At the far end is the mihrab, the niche that shows the direction of Mecca. Above it rises a ribbed dome. The mihrab is decorated with carved marble panels and with glazed tiles that shine with a metallic sheen. These lustre tiles are linked to ninth-century workshops in Iraq.

Beside the mihrab stands the minbar, the stepped pulpit from which the Friday sermon is given. It is made of carved wood and dates from the ninth century. Scholars consider it one of the oldest minbars in the world that still stands in its original place. Its panels are carved with dense patterns of plants and geometric shapes. For more than a thousand years, voices have spoken from these same wooden steps to the people gathered in this hall.

The mosque gave the city a special status. Kairouan became a major centre of Islamic learning in North Africa, and scholars came here from far away. There is also a well-known popular saying. According to this tradition, seven pilgrimages to Kairouan were once said to equal one pilgrimage to Mecca. It is a belief, not a rule of the faith. But it shows how deeply people across the region respected this place. In 1988, Kairouan was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list.

Before you leave, find a shaded spot under the arcade and simply sit for a moment. Watch the light move across the marble. Notice how the arches frame the minaret, and how the sky above the courtyard looks very large and very blue. Listen for the call to prayer if the time is right. It rises from this old tower as it has for centuries. This is a working mosque, not a museum, and that quiet continuity is part of its power.

When you step back out through the gate, look once more at the heavy brown walls. Behind them is a space of open light, reused Roman stone, and old wood. Around you, the streets of the medina lead away toward the markets and the city's other old mosques. As you walk, notice how often you can still see the minaret above the rooftops. For a long time, it was the fixed point of this city. In many ways, it still is.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear clothing that covers shoulders and knees.
Avoid Friday midday prayers when visitor access is limited.
Ask locally whether a combined medina sites ticket is required.
Bring sunglasses because the white marble courtyard reflects strongly.
Return in late afternoon to see the walls turn golden.
