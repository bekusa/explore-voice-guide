The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes in low across the water, and it carries salt from the Atlantic just beyond the dunes. Under your feet the concrete feels smooth and pale, warm in the sun or cool when the clouds roll over. Somewhere below, the lagoon laps at the pillars. A heron lifts off the shallows. Then a car passes, slowly, curving away from you along the ring. That curve is the reason you are standing here.

This is the Laguna Garzón Bridge, on Uruguay's Atlantic coast. It crosses the narrow neck of a coastal lagoon that separates the department of Maldonado from the department of Rocha. Most bridges try to get you across a gap as fast as possible. This one does the opposite. It bends the road into a circle over the water. Drivers have to slow down, turn, and look around them, whether they planned to or not.

The bridge was designed by Rafael Viñoly, one of Uruguay's best-known architects. He was born in Montevideo and built a long international career, with large projects in New York, London, Tokyo and elsewhere. This small coastal crossing is very different from his skyscrapers and convention centres. It carries no grand tower and no dramatic arch. Instead, it makes one simple gesture: a ring of concrete lying almost flat on the lagoon. The bridge opened in late 2015.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The ring is really two bridges in one. Traffic in each direction takes its own half of the circle. If you drive towards Rocha, you follow one arc. If you drive back towards Maldonado, you follow the other. The two halves meet at each shore, so the whole crossing forms an open loop. In the middle of the circle there is nothing at all — only open water, framed by the road.

Why build a circle? The idea was partly about speed. A straight bridge on a quiet coastal road invites people to drive fast. A curve forces them to ease off. But the curve also does something else. As you go around, the view keeps changing — first the open Atlantic side, then the long reach of the lagoon inland, then the far shore. The bridge turns a short crossing into a slow panorama.

Before the bridge, crossing here was a small adventure in its own right. For many years, cars crossed the lagoon on a small raft, a flat ferry that carried only a few vehicles at a time. Locals remember waiting at the edge of the water for their turn. In bad weather, the raft could stop altogether. For the coast of Rocha, that slow crossing kept the area quiet and somewhat cut off from the busier resorts to the west.

And that is the second surprise. This bridge did not only connect two shores — it opened a debate. Some people welcomed easier access between José Ignacio, the fashionable village on the Maldonado side, and the wilder coast of Rocha. Others worried about what easier access might bring: new houses, more traffic, more pressure on fragile dunes and wetlands. The bridge sits in the middle of a real question about how this coast should change.

The lagoon itself deserves your attention. Laguna Garzón is a coastal lagoon, a body of shallow water held behind a sandbar. At times the sandbar opens, and the lagoon connects directly with the ocean. At other times it closes again. So the water can shift between fresh, salty and in between, depending on rain, wind and tides. That changing mix supports fish, crabs and many kinds of birds. The area has gained recognition as a protected natural space in Uruguay.

Take a moment to scan the water and the reeds. Herons and egrets often stand motionless in the shallows. Gulls and terns work the air above the sandbar. In some seasons, visitors report flocks of swans and flamingos on the Uruguayan lagoons, though what you see depends very much on the time of year. Binoculars help. So does patience. The birds here care very little about the bridge — they watch the water, not the cars.

Now look at the bridge itself as an object. The structure is low and light, with slim pillars standing in the lagoon. The concrete is pale, almost the colour of the sand around it. There are no heavy towers or cables to block the horizon. From the shore, the ring can look like a thin line drawn on the water. From above, in photographs, it looks like a perfect circle. Standing on it, you only see part of the curve at a time.

The ring also has space for people on foot. A walkway follows the circle, so you can stroll the loop and stop wherever the view catches you. This is one of the quiet pleasures of the place. You walk, the horizon slides around you, and the road beside you stays calm because cars must move slowly. Families fish from the edges. Cyclists stop to rest. Couples wait here for the sun to drop low over the water.

The light changes the bridge more than anything else. At midday, the concrete glares white and the lagoon turns a hard, flat blue. In the late afternoon, the low sun turns the water silver and gold, and the curve of the bridge throws a long shadow. On grey days, the sky and the lagoon blend together, and the ring seems to float. Wind is almost always part of the experience, so bring a layer even in summer.

Around you, the landscape is open and low. On one side lie the dunes and the ocean. On the other, the lagoon stretches inland towards farmland and scrub. There are no tall buildings, no big signs. The road leads east into Rocha, a department known for long empty beaches, small fishing villages and wide skies. In a sense, the bridge works like a gate. Behind you, the polished resorts of Maldonado. Ahead, a rougher and quieter coast.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Follow the curve of the concrete with your eyes until it disappears behind you. Notice how slowly the cars move, and how the drivers glance out at the water. Listen for the wind, the birds, the small slap of waves on the pillars. A simple circle has changed how people cross this place... and, perhaps, how long they stay to look.
