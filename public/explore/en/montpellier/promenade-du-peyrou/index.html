Promenade du Peyrou

Climb the last steps and the view opens suddenly before you.

⏱ 5 min

The story

Climb the last steps and the view opens suddenly before you. You stand on a wide stone terrace, high above the rooftops of Montpellier. The wind moves a little freer up here. On a clear day the land rolls away toward distant hills, and sometimes toward the sea. This is the Promenade du Peyrou, the high garden terrace at the western edge of the old city.

The promenade was first laid out in 1689. From the beginning it was meant as a grand walk, a place where the people of the city could stroll and take the air. Its terraces step down the slope in calm, ordered levels. Even now, more than three centuries later, that simple purpose holds. People come here to walk slowly and to breathe. On warm evenings the whole terrace fills with the soft sound of footsteps.

At the entrance stands Montpellier's own triumphal arch, the Porte du Peyrou. It was built in 1691, designed by the architect Francois d'Orbay, who had worked on the great palace of Versailles. The arch is heavy and proud. In 1715, carved panels were added to its stone, showing scenes that glorified the long reign of King Louis the Fourteenth.

Walk through the arch and toward the center of the terrace. There you will find a bronze figure on horseback, the equestrian statue of Louis the Fourteenth. The king sits calm and commanding above the crowd. The statue was raised here in 1718, a clear sign of royal power set at the high point of the city for all to see.

This statue, however, has a dramatic past. During the French Revolution, on the first of October, 1792, the figure was pulled down. It was broken into pieces and sent away to Lyon, where the metal was melted to make cannon. For about half a century, the empty base waited. The proud king was simply gone from his hilltop.

The statue you see now is a later replacement. It was quietly set in place on the twenty-ninth of August, 1838, after that long absence. The return was handled with care, in calmer times. Stand before the horse and rider and remember that this bronze is a second act, a restoration of an image that the city once tore down in anger.

Now walk to the far end of the terrace, where an elegant little building waits. This is the water tower, a graceful stone pavilion ringed with columns. It is not just decoration. It marks the end of a long aqueduct that carried fresh water into the city, supplying the many fountains in the streets below. Beauty and use meet in one structure. The pavilion looks like a small temple, yet it served a very practical need.

Look out beyond the water tower and you can trace the line of the aqueduct itself. Its tall arches march across the low ground toward the horizon. The Saint-Clement aqueduct carries water from a source about fourteen kilometers away. Work on it began in 1753. The double row of arches is one of the finest sights at the Peyrou.

The garden reached its finished form in 1774, completed by the architect Jean Giral. That year also marked the start of a new reign in France. So the terrace, in its final shape, sits at a hinge of history. It was built to honor one age of kings, even as another was quietly beginning to slip away.

Take a slow walk along the upper level now. The plane trees give patches of shade. Benches line the path, inviting you to rest. Below, the city spreads out in tiled roofs and church towers. This is one of the best places in Montpellier to understand how the old town sits within its landscape, gathered on its gentle hill. From up here the church towers seem close enough to touch.

On many days the Peyrou is calm and unhurried. Couples walk arm in arm. Older residents sit and talk in the shade. Sometimes a market or a small event fills part of the terrace with voices. But even then the place keeps its dignity. It was built for the long, easy walk, and that spirit has never quite left it.

Before you go, find a spot at the railing and look out one last time. Feel the open air and the long view. Behind you stand the arch, the king, and the quiet water tower. Below you lies the city they were built to crown. The Promenade du Peyrou has watched over Montpellier for centuries, and for a moment, now, it lets you watch too. Take that wide view with you as you head back down into the streets.

Key facts

Promenade first laid out in 1689
Triumphal arch built in 1691 by François d'Orbay
Louis the Fourteenth statue first raised in 1718
Original statue destroyed in 1792, replaced in 1838
Saint-Clément aqueduct begun in 1753, fourteen kilometers long

What to look for

The columned water tower at the far end
Double row of arches crossing the lowland
Carved panels glorifying Louis the Fourteenth
The bronze king mounted on his horse

Practical tips

Come for sunset views over the city
Walk the lower terraces, not just the top
Rest on a bench beneath the plane trees
Bring a hat; the terrace is exposed
