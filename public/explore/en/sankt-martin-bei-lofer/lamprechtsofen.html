The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you hear water. It runs somewhere ahead of you in the dark, steady and heavy, the sound of a mountain draining itself. The air at the entrance turns cool on your skin, then cold. It smells of wet stone and something mineral and clean. The ground is damp underfoot, and every surface shines a little under the lamps. You are standing at the mouth of Lamprechtsofen, one of the deepest cave systems on Earth.

The cave sits in the Saalach valley, close to the village of Sankt Martin bei Lofer, in the Austrian state of Salzburg. Above you rise the Leoganger Steinberge, a range of pale limestone peaks. The border with Bavaria is not far away. From the road, the entrance looks modest, almost easy to miss. But behind it lies a vertical world. The passages climb up through the mountain, far above the place where you now stand.

That is the first surprise. Most deep caves are explored from the top, with cavers descending into the earth. Lamprechtsofen is usually entered from the bottom. Its main opening sits low on the valley side, close to the river. The system then rises through shafts, halls and narrow galleries towards high openings on the plateau above. In other words, the depth of this cave is measured upwards, from the summit entrances down to the door in front of you.

And the depth is enormous. In 1998, a team of Polish cavers connected a high entrance on the plateau with the lower system. With that link, Lamprechtsofen became, for a time, the deepest known cave in the world. The vertical difference is more than 1,600 metres. That is greater than the height of many mountains. Since then, other caves in other countries have taken the record. But this one still ranks among the deepest anywhere.

You will see only a small part of it. The show cave section is a lit path of several hundred metres. It runs along the lowest level of the system, with stairs, bridges and railings. Beyond the last lamp, the cave continues for many kilometres. Those passages belong to expert cavers with ropes, helmets and days of supplies. As you walk, keep in mind that the darkness at the edge of the light is not an ending. It is the start of something much larger.

Now the name. Lamprechtsofen means, roughly, the oven of Lamprecht. In the local German dialect, the word ofen can describe a cave or a rock hollow. As for Lamprecht, there is a story, and it is a legend rather than documented history. According to the popular tale, a knight named Lamprecht returned from the Crusades with a great treasure. He hid it deep inside this cave.

The tale continues in different versions. In one, his heirs came looking for the gold and went too far into the dark. Some say they never returned. Others say the treasure is still somewhere in the mountain. No one has found it. Locals enjoy the story, and you will hear it told with a smile. But the real treasure here is the cave itself, and the water that made it.

So think about that water. This is limestone country. Rain and melting snow fall on the high plateau. The water is slightly acidic, and over a very long time it dissolves the rock. It finds cracks and widens them into shafts and tunnels. Then it rushes down through the mountain to the valley. Every passage you walk through was shaped this way. And the process has not stopped. The cave is still being carved today.

You will feel this as you climb. The path follows the course of the water, and in places the water is loud. Look for streams falling over rock steps, and pools where the current slows and turns clear. After heavy rain or during snowmelt, the flow can rise sharply. That is why access to the cave can change with the weather. Respect for water is the oldest rule here.

Notice the rock around you. Unlike many show caves, Lamprechtsofen is not mainly a cave of decoration. You will not see endless forests of stalactites. Instead, you see raw shapes made by moving water. There are smooth, scooped walls, polished channels and great blocks fallen from above. The surfaces carry bands of grey, cream and darker stone. In the lamplight, wet rock turns almost silver. It is a cave that shows its structure honestly.

The temperature stays cold all year. Inside, it is only a few degrees above freezing, whatever the season outside. On a hot summer day, the change at the entrance is dramatic. Your breath may show in the air. Drips fall from the ceiling, and the sound of each drop carries. Stop for a moment, away from other visitors, and listen. The cave has a soundscape of its own: water, echoes, and a deep, steady quiet beneath it.

For cavers, Lamprechtsofen remains an active place of exploration. Expeditions have spent long periods underground here, mapping passages and pushing into new sections. Teams from several countries have worked in the system over the decades. Their work is slow and careful. They measure each passage, draw each chamber and note each connection. The map of this mountain is still growing. When you look into the dark beyond the railings, you look towards places that only a few people have ever seen.

The climb in the show section takes real effort. There are many stairs, and the steps are often wet. Take your time. Hold the railings. Look back sometimes, because the view down a lit shaft is very different from the view up. The scale is easier to understand when you see how far you have come. And remember, even the top of this path is still near the very bottom of the cave.

When you step back out into daylight, pause at the entrance. Feel the warmer air reach you, and notice how bright the valley looks. Look up at the grey limestone above the trees. Somewhere inside that mountain, water is still running down through the dark, still carving, still on its way to the river beside you. You walked only the first steps of its long journey... and the mountain kept the rest.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer, even on hot summer days.
Wear shoes with good grip for wet steps.
Check conditions after heavy rain or snowmelt.
Pause away from groups to hear the water clearly.
Allow energy for many stairs on the climb.
