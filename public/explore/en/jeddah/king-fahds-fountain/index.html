The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind the hum of traffic on the Corniche, there is a softer sound: a long, rushing hiss, like wind through a gap in a door. Then you feel it. On some evenings a fine salty mist drifts across the seafront and settles on your arms. Look up. A single white column of water climbs out of the Red Sea and keeps climbing, far higher than seems possible.

This is King Fahd's Fountain, in Jeddah, on the west coast of Saudi Arabia. It is often described as the tallest fountain of its kind in the world. The figure most commonly cited is a jet of around 312 metres. That is taller than most skyscrapers in the city behind you. From the water, the column rises as one straight line. Only near the top does it soften, open, and begin to fall.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. This is not fresh water. The fountain draws seawater straight from the Red Sea, pushes it skyward, and lets it fall back into the same sea. In a desert country, where fresh water is precious and often produced by desalination plants, that choice makes sense. The salt also explains the mist you may taste on your lips. A fresh-water fountain would not leave that faint, briny film on your sunglasses.

The fountain carries the name of King Fahd bin Abdulaziz, who ruled Saudi Arabia from 1982 until 2005. It was built in the 1980s and presented to the city as a gift. Those years were a period of fast growth for Jeddah. Oil wealth was reshaping the kingdom. New roads, new districts, and a long modern seafront were all taking shape. The fountain became a kind of signature for that new, outward-facing city.

Think about what it takes to throw water this high. The jet does not drift upward gently. It leaves the nozzle at enormous speed, driven by powerful pumps set low in the structure. Without that force, gravity would pull the water down long before it reached the sky. The engineering is hidden. You see only the result — a pale line that looks almost solid, as if someone had drawn it on the air with chalk.

Now watch the top of the column for a while. The wind off the Red Sea is rarely still, and the fountain shows it clearly. On a calm day the jet stands nearly upright. On a breezy day the plume bends, leans, and spreads into a wide veil of spray. Some locals read the fountain almost like a weather vane. Before you look at your phone, look at the water. It will tell you which way the wind is blowing.

The fountain also changes with the light. In the late afternoon the spray turns gold and amber as the sun drops toward the sea. Sometimes, with the sun behind you, a small rainbow appears in the falling mist. After dark, floodlights take over. The column glows bright white against the black sky. From many parts of Jeddah, people can see it rising above the rooftops, like a lighthouse made of water instead of stone.

The second surprise is about scale. Because the jet is so tall and so thin, your eyes struggle to judge it. Standing on the promenade, it may seem close. In fact the fountain stands out in the water, some distance from shore. Try this. Find a building of known height along the Corniche and compare it with the column. The comparison usually shocks people. The water rises well above anything around it.

The Corniche itself is part of the story. This long seafront stretches for many kilometres along the Red Sea. During the 1970s and 1980s, the city placed large works of public art along its roads and shores. Abstract sculptures, oversized geometric forms, even boats and bicycles cast in concrete and steel. The fountain belongs to the same idea: that a working port city could also be a place to walk, sit, and look.

And people do sit. In the evening, families spread carpets and blankets on the grass and along the waterfront. You may smell grilled meat, cardamom coffee, and sweet tea from thermoses. Children ride bicycles and chase each other between the benches. Fishermen cast lines from the rocks. For many Jeddah residents, the fountain is not a monument to visit once. It is simply the backdrop to ordinary nights out by the sea.

It helps to remember where you are. For centuries, Jeddah has been the main gateway for pilgrims travelling to Mecca, which lies inland to the east. Ships and later planes brought travellers from across the Muslim world through this city. The old quarter, known as Al-Balad, still holds coral-stone houses with carved wooden balconies. The fountain sits at the other end of that long timeline — a modern image for a city that has always faced the sea.

If you want the best view, give yourself time and move around. From directly in front, the column looks straight and simple. From an angle along the shore, you can see the plume curve and drift with the wind. From higher ground or from a building nearby, the fountain and the dark water together make a clean, graphic shape. Each viewpoint gives you a different fountain, even though nothing about it has changed.

Before you leave, turn back to the water once more. Notice the sound again — that steady hiss under the city noise. Notice the mist, if the wind is carrying it toward you. Watch a single moment of the column, rising and falling at the same time. As you walk away along the Corniche, keep glancing over your shoulder. The fountain will stay in view for a long while, a pale line above the Red Sea, still climbing.

Practical tips

Arrive before sunset to see daylight and floodlit views.
Walk along the Corniche to compare different viewing angles.
Bring a blanket and join families picnicking on the seafront.
Protect camera lenses from salty spray on windy evenings.
