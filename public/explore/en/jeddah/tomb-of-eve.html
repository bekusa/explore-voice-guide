The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the wall, Jeddah's traffic hums and honks. Inside, the sound drops away. You hear the scrape of sandals on sandy ground and perhaps a bird in a dusty tree. The light here is flat and bright. It bleaches the low stones and the pale earth until everything looks almost the same colour. This is a cemetery, and a working one. Yet for centuries, people have come here because of one name: Eve, the first woman.

You are standing in a burial ground that locals call the cemetery of Our Mother Eve. It sits a short distance from Al-Balad, the old heart of Jeddah, where coral-stone houses still lean over narrow lanes. Around you, the city has grown in every direction. Apartment blocks, shops, and busy roads press against the walls. Inside, the space feels older and quieter, as if the city has agreed to leave this one patch alone.

Let us be clear about what this place is. According to a long-standing tradition, Eve was buried here after her life on earth. This is a belief, not a documented historical fact. Islamic scholarship does not confirm it, and many religious authorities have openly rejected it. But the story has shaped how people see this ground for many hundreds of years. And it has shaped something even larger: the way some people explain the name of the city itself.

Here is the first surprise. In Arabic, the word jaddah means grandmother. One popular explanation says the city is named after Eve, the grandmother of all humanity. Others prefer a different reading of the name, from a word linked to the seashore. Scholars still debate the true origin. So when you say the word Jeddah, you may be repeating a legend without knowing it. The tale and the city have travelled together for a very long time.

The tradition is not new. Medieval travellers who passed through Jeddah on the way to Mecca wrote about a site linked to Eve. Jeddah has long been the main sea gateway for pilgrims heading inland to Mecca. Ships arrived from Egypt, India, Africa, and beyond. Many pilgrims stopped here before the journey to the holy city. For some of them, a visit to the place of Eve became part of the route.

Later visitors described something remarkable. Travellers in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries wrote about a very long, narrow enclosure. It was said to mark the body of Eve, stretched out across a great length of ground. Some accounts placed small structures at the head, the middle, and the feet. The sheer size was part of the legend. People said the first woman must have been a giant. Pilgrims touched the walls, prayed, and left offerings.

Now comes the second surprise. If you look for that long enclosure today, you will not find it. In the twentieth century, the Saudi religious authorities removed the structure. Their view was that venerating a grave in this way went against the teachings of Islam. Later, the site was sealed so that visitors could not gather at a particular spot. The tomb of legend was, in effect, erased from the landscape on purpose.

This matters for how you see the ground in front of you. In the strict tradition followed in Saudi Arabia, graves are kept simple. There are no grand monuments, no marble, no names carved in gold. A grave is often marked only by a low mound of earth or a plain stone at each end. So the quietness here is not neglect. It is a deliberate choice about how the dead should be remembered — modestly, and equally.

Take a slow look around. The rows of small stones run across the sandy ground. Some look recent. Others are worn and half sunk into the earth. Families still bury their loved ones here, so you may see fresh mounds or people standing in silence. This is the most important thing to understand about the cemetery. It is not a museum. It is a place of grief and prayer for people who live in this city today.

Because of this, access can change. At times, the gates are locked, and visitors can only look in from outside. Rules about who may enter can also vary, and they are not always posted. If you are unsure, ask politely at the entrance or ask a local guide. Dress modestly, speak quietly, and step back if a funeral arrives. A respectful visitor is almost always welcomed more warmly than a curious one.

Step outside the walls for a moment and notice the contrast. The streets around the cemetery are ordinary and alive. You may smell cardamom coffee, car exhaust, and grilled meat from a nearby shop. Shop signs flash in Arabic. This mix of sacred ground and everyday traffic is very typical of old Jeddah. The city has always been a place of passage, where pilgrims, traders, and residents share the same crowded streets.

It helps to visit this site together with Al-Balad. In the old town, you can see tall houses built from coral stone taken from the Red Sea. Their wooden window screens, called rawasheen, let air move through while keeping the rooms private. Walking from those lanes to this cemetery, you move between two kinds of memory. One is built in stone and timber. The other is kept almost entirely in stories.

So what remains of Eve here? Perhaps not a body, and not a monument. What remains is an idea that has survived demolition, debate, and the growth of a modern city. People still ask taxi drivers for the tomb of Eve. Visitors still come to stand at the wall. The legend has outlived the stones that once marked it, and that may be the most interesting fact of all.

Before you leave, pause once more at the gate. Look at the plain stones and the pale earth under the strong sun. Listen again to the gap between the noise outside and the stillness inside. Whatever you believe about the first woman, you are standing in a place where many generations came to think about beginnings and endings. As you walk back into the city, notice how quickly the traffic sound returns.
