The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against a pebble beach. A raven calls from somewhere high in the cedars, and the sound carries across the inlet. Underfoot, the moss is so thick that each step sinks a little, soft and cool and damp. The air smells of salt, wet bark, and kelp drying on the rocks. This is Gwaii Haanas, at the southern end of the Haida Gwaii archipelago, off the north coast of British Columbia. The Haida name is often translated as Islands of Beauty. Standing here, the translation feels modest.

This is not a place you reach by accident. There are no roads into Gwaii Haanas. Every visitor arrives by boat, by kayak, or by floatplane, across open water that can turn rough very quickly. That distance is part of what protects it. The archipelago here holds well over a hundred islands, from large forested ridges to small rocks covered in seabirds. Most of the coastline you can see has no buildings, no docks, and no lights at night. What you hear is mostly wind, water, and birds.

The Haida people have lived on these islands for many thousands of years. This is their homeland, and that fact shapes everything about the place. The second half of the official name — Haida Heritage Site — matters as much as the first half. Gwaii Haanas is not only a national park reserve in the Canadian sense. It is a cultural landscape, full of old village sites, fishing camps, and sacred places. The forest you are looking at is also a record of human lives.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. Gwaii Haanas is managed jointly. The Council of the Haida Nation and the Government of Canada share decisions through a body called the Archipelago Management Board. This arrangement comes from the Gwaii Haanas Agreement of 1993. Both sides kept their own view of who owns the land, but they agreed to protect it together. Many people around the world now study this model of shared management between an Indigenous nation and a national government.

That agreement did not appear out of nowhere. In the 1980s, commercial logging was moving across these islands. In 1985, Haida people stood on a logging road on Lyell Island, called Athlii Gwaii in Haida, and blocked the trucks. Elders came forward, and some Haida were arrested. The protest drew attention across Canada. It became one of the important moments in the country's environmental and Indigenous rights history. The forests you see today stand partly because of those people on that road.

The protection does not stop at the shoreline. In 2010, the waters around these islands became the Gwaii Haanas National Marine Conservation Area Reserve. So the area is protected from the mountaintops down to the seafloor. That matters, because here the land and sea depend on each other. Salmon return up the streams, and bears and eagles carry their remains into the forest. The nutrients from the ocean feed the trees. Look at the giant cedars and spruce, and you are looking at a forest fed partly by the sea.

Now look closely at that forest. Sitka spruce and western red cedar rise straight and tall. Their trunks are wide, and the lower branches often carry heavy coats of moss. Fallen logs lie across the ground, and young trees grow along their backs in neat lines. These are called nurse logs. Because the islands sat apart from the mainland for so long, some animals here developed differently. The black bears of Haida Gwaii, for example, are a distinct subspecies, larger than many of their mainland cousins. Some naturalists call the archipelago the Galapagos of the North.

At several old village sites, you may meet the Haida Gwaii Watchmen. These are Haida guardians who live at key sites during the visitor season. They watch over the places, welcome visitors, and share knowledge about their history. Their presence is a reminder — you are a guest here. The Watchmen often decide how many people can come ashore at once and where they can walk. Listening to them is one of the best parts of any visit, because they carry stories you will not find on any sign.

The most famous of these sites is SGang Gwaay, an old village on a small island at the southern edge of the archipelago. It is also known by the name Ninstints. In 1981, SGang Gwaay was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Here, carved poles still stand in a line facing the sea, beside the outlines of old longhouses. Many are memorial and mortuary poles — poles raised to honour the dead, and sometimes to hold their remains. They are weathered grey and silver, and some lean toward the ground.

This is the second surprise for many visitors. The Haida chose to let these old poles stand, and slowly return to the earth. They are not restored, varnished, or moved into museums. Moss grows in the carved eyes. Small plants take root in the cracks. To an outsider, this can look like neglect. For many Haida, it is the right way for a pole to end its life. The decay is part of the meaning. So please look, but never touch or lean on them.

The village sites also hold a painful history. In the nineteenth century, diseases brought by outsiders, especially smallpox, spread through Haida communities. The death toll was enormous. Many southern villages were abandoned, and survivors moved north to larger communities. When you stand among the quiet longhouse pits, you are standing in a place that was once full of families, voices, and smoke from cooking fires. The silence here has a history.

But Gwaii Haanas is not only about the past. In 2013, the Haida raised a new carved pole at Windy Bay on Lyell Island, known in Haida as Hlk'yah GaawGa. It is called the Gwaii Haanas Legacy Pole. It marked twenty years of the agreement between the Haida Nation and Canada. Its carvings tell the story of the land, the sea, and the people who stood to protect them. It was the first new monumental pole raised in Gwaii Haanas in well over a century.

As you move through these islands, take your time. Watch the tide line, where small crabs move under the rocks. Look for eagles sitting high in dead snags along the shore. Notice how fast the weather changes, from bright sun to grey mist in minutes. Bring patience, and bring respect. Everything here asks you to slow down — to listen to the Watchmen, to stay on the paths, and to leave no trace of your visit.

So, before you leave this spot, stop for a moment. Listen again to the water on the stones, and the raven somewhere above you. Feel the moss under your boots, and the cool air coming off the sea. These islands have been watched over for thousands of years. Today, for a short time, you are part of that long line of people who have stood here and paid attention. Carry that quiet with you as you walk back to the shore.

Good to know

Key facts

Gwaii Haanas is co-managed by the Haida Nation and Canada.
The Gwaii Haanas Agreement between both governments was signed in 1993.
Haida protesters blocked logging on Lyell Island in 1985.
SGang Gwaay village became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981.
The surrounding waters became a marine conservation reserve in 2010.

What to look for

Weathered grey memorial poles standing in a row at SGang Gwaay.
Young trees growing in straight lines along fallen nurse logs.
Shallow rectangular pits marking where old longhouses once stood.
Moss and small plants growing inside the carved poles' features.
Bald eagles perched on dead snags above the shoreline.
Before you go

Practical tips

Book your trip ahead, since a visitor reservation is required.
Attend the mandatory visitor orientation before entering the park reserve.
Pack waterproof layers because weather changes within minutes.
Follow Haida Gwaii Watchmen directions at every village site.
Go with a licensed operator if you lack open-water experience.
