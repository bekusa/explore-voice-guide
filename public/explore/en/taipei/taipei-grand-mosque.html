The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along a wide city avenue. Scooters whine past. Then your eye lands on something you may not expect in Taipei. A white dome rises above a quiet courtyard, framed by two slender towers. The air smells of warm asphalt and the green trees of the park across the road. This is the Taipei Grand Mosque, and it holds one of the most surprising stories in the city.

The mosque sits on Xinsheng South Road in the Da'an District. Daan Forest Park, one of the largest green spaces in central Taipei, lies just across the street. That contrast matters. On one side, joggers, dog walkers and families on bicycles. On the other side, a building shaped by a faith that most people in Taiwan do not practise. The two sit side by side without fuss. That calm coexistence is part of what you have come to see.

To understand why a mosque stands here, go back to the late 1940s. At the end of the Chinese Civil War, the Nationalist government retreated from mainland China to Taiwan. Hundreds of thousands of people came with it. Among them were Chinese Muslims, many of them Hui. The Hui are Chinese-speaking Muslims whose ancestors converted centuries ago. Some served as soldiers. Others worked as officials, cooks, teachers or traders. They arrived on an island with almost no established Muslim community.

These newcomers needed a place to pray. At first, they gathered in modest rented spaces. Over the following years, community leaders worked to build something permanent. Diplomacy also played a part. Taiwan was seeking friends abroad, and several Muslim-majority countries offered support for the project. The building you see today was completed around 1960. It became a religious home for local Muslims. It also became a symbol in the government's relations with the wider Islamic world.

Look at the facade now. The design is generally credited to the Taiwanese architect Yang Cho-cheng. His name also appears on several of Taipei's larger public buildings from that period. Here he worked in a style drawn from Middle Eastern and Islamic traditions. Notice the pointed arches and the clean white surfaces. Notice how the central dome sits above the main prayer hall. The two minarets stand like sentries on either side. A minaret is the tower from which the call to prayer was traditionally sung.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The building is not huge. Photographs often make it look grand and remote. In person, it feels almost domestic in scale. The dome is broad rather than towering. The courtyard is compact and swept clean. That modest size tells you something honest. This mosque served a small community of exiles. They were building for themselves, and for the future, not for spectacle.

The city of Taipei has since recognised the mosque as a historic site. That status protects the building and its appearance. It also marks something less obvious. A structure built by a small religious minority is now treated as part of the city's shared heritage. For a place that so many people simply drive past, that is quite a quiet statement.

Now think about who prays here today. The original congregation of mainland exiles has aged. Their children and grandchildren are part of Taiwanese society. But the mosque has found a new life. Over recent decades, many workers and students have come to Taiwan from Indonesia, Malaysia and other countries. Many of them are Muslim. On Fridays, the midday congregational prayer can fill the hall and spill toward the courtyard.

That is the second surprise. You might imagine a quiet relic of the 1960s. Instead, on a busy Friday, you may hear several languages in one courtyard. Indonesian, Mandarin, Arabic and English mix together. Shoes line up neatly near the entrance. Friends greet each other after the prayer. For many migrant workers far from home, this is a place of familiar rhythm in an unfamiliar city.

If you visit, a little care goes a long way. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. You will remove your shoes before entering the prayer hall. Women may be asked to cover their hair, and the mosque often has scarves available. Move quietly, and avoid walking in front of anyone who is praying. Photography of the building is usually fine. Photographing worshippers is another matter. Ask first, or simply don't.

Inside the prayer hall, notice what is absent. There are no statues and no paintings of people. Islamic tradition avoids images of living beings in places of worship. Instead, the eye moves to calligraphy, geometric patterns, and the open floor covered with carpet. Look for the mihrab. It is a niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca, the direction everyone faces in prayer. The whole room is turned toward a city thousands of kilometres away.

Step back outside and look at the neighbourhood around you. Over the years, small halal restaurants and grocers have appeared in parts of Taipei. Halal means food prepared according to Islamic rules. The mosque has helped anchor that growth. Some visitors come for the architecture, then leave hungry and curious about Indonesian or Chinese Muslim cooking. It is a gentle way to carry the visit forward into the rest of your day.

Before you go, turn once more toward the dome. Watch how the light sits on the white surface. Then look across the road at the trees of Daan Forest Park. A community of exiles built this place far from where they began. Today, people from other distant homes keep it alive. As you walk away, listen again to the traffic and the scooters. Somewhere behind that noise, the courtyard stays still, waiting for the next prayer.
