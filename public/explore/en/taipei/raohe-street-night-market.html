Raohe Street Night Market

The smell reaches you first.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

The smell reaches you first. Black pepper, charcoal smoke, and the sweet edge of grilling sausage drift down the street. You hear the hiss of hot griddles and the steady calls of vendors. A tall gate rises ahead, painted with dragons and lit from inside. You have arrived at one of the oldest night markets in Taipei.

Raohe Street runs straight and narrow for about six hundred meters. There are no side lanes to lose yourself in. One path leads in, and the same path leads back out. This simple shape makes the market easy to walk. You join the slow river of people and move with it, stall by stall, smell by smell.

The market sits in the Songshan District, in the eastern part of the city. Long ago this area was a small river port. Boats carried goods along the Keelung River, which still runs close by. Trade brought workers, and workers needed feeding. In this way the street has fed people for many generations, long before the lights and signs arrived. Some of the family stalls you pass tonight have been run by the same households for decades.

In 1987 the city set Raohe Street aside as a night market for visitors. The vendors who had gathered here for years were finally given a fixed home. Today more than one hundred stalls open each evening. Many sell food. Others sell clothing, toys, phone cases, and small games where you toss rings to win a prize.

At the western end of the street stands a temple. Its name is Ciyou Temple. It honors Mazu, the goddess of the sea, who watches over sailors and travelers. The temple is far older than the market, dating to the eighteenth century. Red lanterns hang at its gate. People step inside to light incense and pray, then turn back toward the food.

The dish that draws the longest line is the pepper bun. Vendors here call it hujiao bing. They wrap a ball of dough around pork, green onion, and a heavy pinch of black pepper. Then they press the buns against the inside wall of a deep clay oven. The oven works much like the tandoor ovens of South Asia. A good one is brushed with syrup and scattered with white sesame seeds before it goes in. The buns bake until their bottoms turn crisp and golden.

You wait your turn and take one. The vendor lifts it out with a long metal tool. It is too hot to hold at first, so you pass it from hand to hand. You bite through the crisp shell, and the juice inside runs free. The pepper warms the back of your mouth. This single bun explains much of why people come here.

There is far more to taste. Try the medicinal spare rib soup, simmered slowly with herbs until the meat slips from the bone. Try a Taiwanese sausage, sweet and grilled over glowing coals. Braver eaters seek out stinky tofu, which smells sharp but tastes mild and soft inside. For something sweet, look for egg waffles and shaved ice piled high with fresh fruit. Many stalls let you point at what you want, so you can order without sharing a language.

Eating here follows an easy rhythm. You buy one small thing. You eat it as you walk. Then you stop at the next stall and begin again. Most vendors take only cash, so keep small bills within reach. Many portions are sized for sharing, which lets a group taste a wider spread without filling up too soon.

Remember to look up as you move. Two tall gates mark the ends of the street, each shaped like a temple roof and traced in bright light. The signs above the stalls are stacked three rows deep. Steam rises into the glow and softens every edge. The whole street begins to feel like one long, bright room with the night sky for a ceiling.

A few simple notes will help your visit. The market opens in the late afternoon and stays busy until close to midnight. The crowd grows thickest after eight in the evening. The Houshanpi metro station sits only a short walk away. Wear shoes you do not mind getting a little greasy, carry tissues, and above all, come hungry. An empty stomach is the only ticket this place asks for.

When you have eaten your fill, drift back toward the temple gate. Watch the smoke rise and the crowd flow past in the lamplight. A night market is not only a place to eat. It is a place where a city gathers after dark, the way it has done here for a very long time. Stand still a moment, and let the evening move around you.

Key facts

Opened as a designated tourist night market in 1987.
The street runs about six hundred meters, single lane.
More than one hundred vendors open each evening.
Nearby Ciyou Temple honors Mazu, dating to eighteenth century.
Pepper buns bake inside a hot clay oven.

What to look for

Tall lit gates shaped like temple roofs.
Vendors pressing buns onto the oven's inner wall.
Red lanterns glowing at the temple gate.
Steam rising into the layered, bright signs above.

Practical tips

Carry small cash bills, since most stalls accept only cash.
Arrive after eight in the evening for the fullest scene.
Take the metro to Houshanpi station, then walk over.
Share portions with your group to taste more dishes.
