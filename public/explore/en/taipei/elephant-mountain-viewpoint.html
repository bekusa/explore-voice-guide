Elephant Mountain Viewpoint

The climb begins in shade.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

The climb begins in shade. Tall trees close over the path, and the air smells of warm leaves and damp stone. Insects hum in the green. Ahead, stone steps rise steeply and disappear around a bend. Behind and above you, though you cannot see it yet, waits one of the finest views of Taipei. Take the first steps slowly. The mountain rewards patience more than speed.

This hill is called Elephant Mountain. Its Chinese name is Xiangshan. It belongs to a group known as the Four Beasts Mountains, named for an elephant, a tiger, a leopard, and a lion. The elephant is the most visited of the four. From the city below, the long ridge does look a little like a resting elephant.

The mountain is not high. The summit reaches only about 180 meters. But the path is steep, made mostly of stone steps that rise without much rest. Take your time on the way up. There is no shame in pausing. Older walkers and young children climb it daily, one slow step after another. Handrails run along the steeper sections, so you always have something to hold.

The trail begins right at the edge of the city. From Xiangshan metro station, the last stop on its line, signs point you toward the hill within a few minutes' walk. The change is quick. One moment you are among apartment blocks and shops. The next, you are under forest, with the city humming somewhere below. Vendors near the trailhead sell cold drinks and ice cream, a good idea for the way back.

After about twenty minutes of climbing, the trees open. You reach the spot most people come to find. Before you stands Taipei 101, the tall tower that for years ranked among the highest in the world. It rises straight from the district below, far above every roof around it, close enough to feel within reach. On a clear day you can pick out the smaller towers and the green hills beyond the city.

The most famous perch here is a cluster of large boulders. People call them the Six Giant Rocks. Visitors climb onto them and pose with the tower behind. If a line has formed, wait your turn and be careful on the stone. The reward is a clean photograph with the whole skyline spread out below you.

Try to time your climb for the hour before sunset. The light turns soft and golden across the glass towers. Then the sun drops, the sky deepens, and the city begins to glow. Window by window, the buildings light up. Taipei 101 shines against the dark. Many say this is the finest hour on the mountain. Arrive a little early to claim a good spot, because the best places fill quickly near dusk.

Bring water with you. The steps are steep, and the air is often warm and damp. A small towel helps as well. If you plan to stay for the night view, carry a light for the walk down, since the forest grows dark quickly once the sun is gone. Good shoes make the descent safer.

Along the way you will find places to rest. Wooden pavilions stand beside the path at intervals. Sit for a while and let your breath settle. From some of these shelters you already catch glimpses of the city through gaps in the trees, a small preview of the wider view waiting above. These pavilions also offer cover if a sudden shower passes through, which happens often in summer.

The mountain is more than a viewpoint. It is a true pocket of forest inside a large city. Listen, and you may hear birds and the rustle of squirrels in the branches. Green ferns crowd the edges of the steps. Within minutes of leaving the streets, you are surrounded by living, breathing woods. In the warmer months, the calls of frogs and insects rise from the undergrowth after dark.

The trail does not end at the first viewpoint. Paths continue deeper into the Four Beasts hills, linking to longer walks for those who wish to go on. But most visitors are content to stop at the rocks, take in the sight, and rest before turning back down toward the lights.

When the view has filled your eyes, find a quiet step and simply sit. Below you, a city of millions hums and shines. Up here, there is only the wind and the dark shapes of the trees. It is a rare thing to stand so close to a great city and feel, for a moment, gently apart from it.

Key facts

Part of Taipei's Four Beasts Mountains group.
The summit rises about 180 meters high.
A steep stone-step trail leads to the top.
The main viewpoint is about twenty minutes up.
It faces Taipei 101 across the Xinyi district.

What to look for

Six large boulders used as a photo platform.
The city skyline spreading toward distant hills.
Wooden rest pavilions placed along the climb.
Dense green forest pressing close to the steps.

Practical tips

Start about an hour before sunset for best light.
Bring water; the stone steps are steep throughout.
Take the metro to Xiangshan, the line's last stop.
Bring a small light for the walk down.
