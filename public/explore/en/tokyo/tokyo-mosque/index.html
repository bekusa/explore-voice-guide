The guide

The story

Leave your shoes at the door, and the first thing you notice is the carpet. It is soft and thick under your feet, and it feels warm after the hard pavement outside. Look up, and the space opens into a bright dome of white, blue, and gold. Outside, trains rattle past Yoyogi-Uehara Station. In here the sound drops away. Light from tall windows falls in coloured patches across the floor. You are standing inside Tokyo Mosque, known locally as Tokyo Camii, on a quiet residential street in Shibuya.

For many visitors, the surprise is simply that this building exists here at all. Tokyo is a city of shrines and temples, of wooden gates and paper lanterns. Then, on an ordinary corner, a slender minaret rises above the rooftops, with a pale stone dome beside it. The contrast is part of the experience. Neighbours walk their dogs past the entrance. Delivery bikes hum along the road. And above them stands a building that would look perfectly at home on the shores of the Bosphorus in Istanbul.

The story begins not in Turkey but in Russia. After the Russian Revolution, a community of Muslim Tatars left their homeland. The Tatars are a Turkic people whose historic heartland lies along the Volga River. Some of them travelled east across Siberia and Manchuria, and a number eventually settled in Japan. They opened shops, ran businesses, and built a small community in Tokyo. What they lacked was a proper place to pray together.

In 1938, that changed. The Tatar community opened a mosque on this site, alongside a school for their children. It was a modest building, but it was a landmark for Muslims in Japan at the time. For decades it served worshippers from many countries, as diplomats, students, and traders passed through the capital. Over the years, however, the original structure aged badly. By the 1980s it was considered unsafe, and in 1986 it was demolished.

For more than a decade, the site waited. Then a new chapter began with support from Turkey. Many of the original Tatar families had ties to the Turkish world, and Turkish religious authorities took on the project of rebuilding. The new mosque was completed in 2000. It was designed in the classical Ottoman style... the style of the great imperial mosques built in Istanbul from the fifteenth century onward, with a central dome and slim, pencil-shaped minarets.

What many visitors do not realise is how much of this building came from far away. Turkish craftsmen worked on the decoration, and a large share of the materials were brought from Turkey. Marble, tiles, and fittings travelled thousands of kilometres to reach this Tokyo street. Take a moment to look closely at the stonework and the finishes. You are looking at the work of artisans trained in a tradition that stretches back many centuries.

Now look upward again. The main dome rests above the prayer hall, and smaller half-domes and arches support it. This layered arrangement is a signature of Ottoman design. It spreads the weight of the dome and creates a feeling of one large, open space. Inside, the inner surface of the dome carries painted patterns and lines of Arabic calligraphy. In Islamic art, writing itself is a form of decoration, and verses are often arranged in circles or bands.

Below the dome, a large chandelier hangs low over the carpet. In traditional Ottoman mosques, lamps were hung low so that worshippers could see clearly, while the dome above stayed open and airy. Around the walls, look for the coloured glass windows. On a sunny day, they throw small patches of red, blue, and green across the floor. The effect changes through the day, so the room you see in the morning feels different by late afternoon.

Facing the front of the hall, you will see the mihrab. This is a niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca, the direction Muslims face when they pray. Beside it stands the minbar, a tall staircase-like pulpit. The imam climbs it to give the sermon during Friday prayers. Both pieces are carefully decorated. Their placement tells you how the whole room is oriented, even if the street outside runs at a different angle.

This is a working mosque, not a museum. Daily prayers take place here, and Friday midday prayers often bring the largest crowd. If you visit during a prayer time, you may hear the call to prayer, and you will see people gathering in rows on the carpet. Visitors are generally welcome to watch quietly from the back. Women are asked to cover their hair, and the mosque usually has scarves available to borrow. Modest clothing is expected for everyone.

Downstairs, on the ground floor, you will find the Turkish Culture Center. This part of the building is designed for exchange between communities. It hosts exhibitions, events, and guided tours, and it often has information about Islam and Turkish culture. Many Japanese schoolchildren and curious residents come here for their first look inside a mosque. That role, as a meeting point, is now as central to the building as its religious one.

The mosque is often described as the largest in Japan, and the Muslim community it serves is now very diverse. On any given day you might hear Turkish, Arabic, Urdu, Indonesian, Bengali, and Japanese in the entrance hall. Small shops and restaurants nearby reflect this mix. Walking back toward the station, you may pass places selling halal food, a quiet sign of how this community has grown since the first Tatar families arrived.

Before you leave, step back outside and look at the building once more from across the street. Notice how the minaret rises above the power lines and apartment blocks. Notice the trains sliding past, the shopfronts, the ordinary rhythm of a Tokyo neighbourhood. A community that crossed a continent built a home here. Others rebuilt it with stone from another sea. As you put your shoes back on, the carpet's softness stays with you for a few steps... and then the city takes over again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Remove your shoes before entering the prayer hall.
Women should bring or borrow a headscarf.
Wear modest clothing covering shoulders and knees.
Avoid Friday midday prayers if you want quiet viewing.
Walk from Yoyogi-Uehara Station, just a few minutes away.
