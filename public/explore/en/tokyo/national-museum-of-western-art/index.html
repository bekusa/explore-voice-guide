The guide

The story

Walk in from the Ueno Park side and the ground changes under your feet. The gravel paths and tree roots give way to a flat, open forecourt. In front of you stands a low, grey-green box that seems to float on thick round columns. Close up, the wall panels are not smooth. They are studded with small pebbles set into the concrete, rough enough to catch the light. The building looks modest. That is part of the story.

This is the National Museum of Western Art, and its main building was designed by Le Corbusier, the Swiss-French architect who shaped much of twentieth-century modern architecture. It opened in 1959. It is the only building by Le Corbusier in East Asia. In 2016, it was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of a group of seventeen of his works spread across seven countries. So you are standing beside one link in a chain that runs from France to India to Argentina.

But the museum exists because of a collector, not an architect. His name was Kojiro Matsukata, a Japanese industrialist who led the Kawasaki shipbuilding company in Kobe. In the early twentieth century, during long stays in Europe, he bought art on a remarkable scale. Paintings, sculptures, prints, and decorative objects. His goal was not only private pleasure. He wanted Japanese people, most of whom would never travel to Europe, to see Western art in person, in their own country.

History did not cooperate. A financial crisis in the late 1920s hit Matsukata's business hard, and much of the collection was scattered or sold. A large part stayed in storage in Europe. Some works kept in London were lost in a fire. The group of works that remained in France was seized by the French state during the Second World War, as the property of an enemy nation. For years, it looked as though Matsukata's dream had simply ended.

Here is the first surprise. After the war, Japan and France negotiated the return of the collection. France agreed to send most of it back, but on one condition. Japan had to build a proper museum to house it. Even then, France kept some important pieces. A famous painting by Vincent van Gogh of his bedroom in Arles, once owned by Matsukata, still hangs in Paris today. So the building in front of you is, in a real sense, the price of a diplomatic agreement.

Le Corbusier came to Tokyo, studied the site, and produced the design. The detailed work and construction were carried out by three Japanese architects who had trained in his Paris studio: Kunio Maekawa, Junzo Sakakura, and Takamasa Yoshizaka. Look across the plaza and you will see a larger concrete hall with a dramatic curved roof edge. That is Tokyo Bunka Kaikan, a concert hall designed by Maekawa. Teacher and student face each other across the same open space.

Now look again at the columns lifting the building off the ground. Le Corbusier called these pilotis. They free the ground floor and let space flow underneath. The whole plan follows an idea he called the museum of unlimited growth. The galleries wind outward in a square spiral, like a shell. In theory, new rooms could be added to the outer edge forever, as the collection grew. In practice, the building never expanded that way. Later additions were built as separate wings instead.

Before you go inside, spend a moment in the forecourt. Several bronze sculptures by Auguste Rodin stand here, the French sculptor of the late nineteenth century. The tall, dark panel crowded with twisting bodies is The Gates of Hell, inspired by Dante's poem about the underworld. Look near the top and you will find a small seated figure, chin on his hand. That figure later became famous on its own, as The Thinker. A larger cast of The Thinker sits nearby, so you can compare the two.

Step inside and the mood shifts. You enter a tall central hall lit from above. Daylight falls through a skylight shaped like a long triangle, crossing the room over a single concrete column. This space was meant to display nineteenth-century works, and it is the heart of Le Corbusier's spiral. From here, a gentle ramp carries you up to the galleries. Walking up it slowly is part of the design. The view of the hall turns and opens as you climb.

Upstairs, pay attention to the ceilings. In some galleries they drop low, then rise again. Le Corbusier worked with a system of proportions he called the Modulor, based on the measurements of a standing human body. The low ceilings were not cheap construction. They were deliberate, meant to bring the art close and keep the rooms at a human scale. Narrow balconies also look down into the central hall, linking the floors together.

The collection itself covers a long arc of European art. It begins with religious paintings from the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance and runs through the Dutch and Flemish masters to the French painters of the nineteenth century. Matsukata knew Claude Monet personally and visited him at his garden in Giverny. That friendship is why Monet is so strongly represented here, including his paintings of water lilies. The collection continues into the early twentieth century, and the museum has kept adding works over the decades.

Here is the second surprise. One huge Monet water lily canvas from Matsukata's collection disappeared for decades. In 2016, it was found rolled up in storage at the Louvre in Paris, badly damaged, with roughly half of the painted surface lost. It came back to Tokyo. After careful conservation work, it went on display in 2019. Look for it, and do not expect perfection. The missing upper section is part of what the canvas carries now, a record of war, neglect, and rescue.

As you leave, take one last look at the building from the edge of the forecourt. Notice how low it sits among the trees of Ueno Park, and how the pebbled panels shift from grey to green as the light changes. Listen to the crows in the trees and the families walking toward the zoo and the other museums. A collector dreamed of bringing Europe to Japan. A war nearly erased that dream. And an architect left one quiet concrete spiral here, still holding the art it was built for.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the JR Ueno Station park exit for the shortest walk.
Walk the ramp slowly to watch the central hall unfold.
Check current exhibitions online, special shows need separate tickets.
Circle the forecourt sculptures before entering the galleries.
Pair your visit with Tokyo Bunka Kaikan across the plaza.
