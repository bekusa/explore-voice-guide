The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Trains hum and clatter somewhere below you. Footsteps hurry toward the stations. Car horns sound now and then. Above all of that, a pale tower rises in narrow steps, each level a little slimmer than the one below. At the very top, a slender spire points into the sky. You may already have seen this building from across Tokyo without knowing its name. Now you are close enough to look straight up at it.

This is the NTT Docomo Yoyogi Building. Docomo is one of Japan's largest mobile phone companies. The tower stands where the edges of Shinjuku, Yoyogi and Sendagaya meet. It was completed around the turn of the millennium, at the height of Japan's mobile phone boom. At that time, millions of people were carrying their first handsets. Telephone networks needed new infrastructure, and this tower was part of that answer.

Here is the first surprise. Many visitors assume this is a busy office tower, full of desks and meeting rooms. Much of it is not. The building is widely reported to hold telecommunications equipment for the Docomo network. Large parts of its interior serve machines rather than people. So this tall tower is not mainly a place where people work. It works mainly for the phones in thousands of pockets on the streets around you.

Look at the shape again. Many visitors notice a likeness to New York. The stepped profile, the strong vertical lines and the tall pointed crown remind many people of the Empire State Building. That tower is an Art Deco skyscraper from the 1930s. Art Deco was a style of bold geometry and decorative setbacks. The Tokyo tower is far younger. It is also built with modern materials and was designed with earthquakes in mind. Still, at dusk, the resemblance to the American tower is clear.

The setbacks are worth a closer look. They are the places where the tower steps inward as it climbs. They make the building look lighter as it rises. Your eye moves upward naturally, from one narrowing level to the next. They also give the top a crown-like shape. Without them, it would be a plain box. From the street, you get a sense of great height without a flat, heavy wall in front of you.

Now for the second surprise. High on the tower, below the spire, there is a large clock. It is set into the upper part of the building, and many people say it can be read from far away. The clock was not part of the tower from the beginning. It was added a little later. So a building that holds communication equipment also shows the time to the whole district. Many commuters check it as they pass, often without thinking about it.

The height is hard to judge from directly below. The tower rises more than two hundred metres to its roof. The spire adds even more height. For some years it ranked among the tallest structures in central Tokyo. Today, newer towers in other districts have passed it. Still, it remains one of the clearest landmarks on this side of the city. You can see the pointed top from many places across western Tokyo.

One practical point matters here. This is not an observation tower. There is no public viewing deck at the top. Visitors are not invited inside to ride the lifts or look out over the city. The best way to enjoy it is from the outside, at different distances. In this way, the tower is a part of the skyline more than a destination. You enjoy it as a shape against the sky, not as a room with a view.

The neighbourhood around it changes quickly within a few minutes' walk. In one direction is the Shinjuku station area, with its department stores, crowds and large crossings. In another direction is Yoyogi, with quieter streets, small restaurants and language schools. To the south are the wide green spaces of Shinjuku Gyoen, a large national garden. The tower stands between these different worlds, linking them in one view.

The best views often come from a short distance away. From the green lawns of Shinjuku Gyoen, the tower rises above the treetops. Garden and skyscraper appear together in one frame. Old pines and modern stone stand side by side. From the elevated walkways around the south side of Shinjuku Station, it fills the space between other buildings. Walk a little and watch how the tower moves between the other buildings. From some angles it is in full view, and from others only the spire shows.

The light changes the building throughout the day. In the morning, the pale facade looks cool and quiet. At midday, strong sun brings out every line and setback. In the late afternoon, the stone warms to soft gold and pink. After dark, the crown is lit, and the tower becomes a calm marker above the flashing signs of the streets below. If you can, come back at a different hour and compare.

It is also worth thinking about what the tower means. When it was built, mobile phones were becoming part of daily life in Japan. Over the following years, Japanese phones gained cameras, email and internet services early. That was a moment when the country set new trends in mobile technology. This tower stands from that era. It looks back to old New York skyscrapers, but it was built for the future of mobile communication.

Before you move on, look up once more. Find the clock and read the time. Notice the narrow steps of the crown, and the spire above them. Then lower your eyes to the street. The trains are still humming, and people are walking past with phones in their hands. Some of their calls and messages may pass through this tower. As you walk away, look back now and then. The tower will appear between buildings, a steady point in the city around you.

Before you go

Practical tips

View the tower from the lawns of Shinjuku Gyoen.
Use the walkways at Shinjuku Station's south side.
Return at dusk to see the illuminated crown.
Plan to view it outside; there is no public observation deck.
Combine your visit with a walk through quiet Yoyogi streets.
