The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the trees, Tokyo hums — trains, taxis, the low rush of a city that rarely rests. In here the sound softens. Gravel crunches under your shoes, a crow calls from a high branch, and water splashes somewhere ahead. Office towers rise on every side, glass and steel catching the light. Yet the air under these old trees feels cooler and slower. You are standing in Hibiya Park, one of the first places where modern Tokyo learned how to take a breath.

Hibiya Park opened in 1903, and it is widely described as Japan's first Western-style park. That idea was new. Japan had beautiful gardens long before this, but most were private. They belonged to temples, nobles, or warlords, and they were made for quiet looking. This park was different. It was made for the public — for walking, for music, for meeting friends. It was a statement that a modern city needed shared green space.

The ground under your feet has changed hands many times. During the Edo period, when the shoguns ruled from the castle nearby, this area held the large residences of regional lords, called daimyo. Their estates stood close to the castle walls, a sign of their rank and their duty. After the Meiji Restoration in the late nineteenth century, when power returned to the emperor, the estates were cleared. For a time the land served the army as a parade ground.

The man most closely linked with the park's design is Honda Seiroku, a forestry scholar who is often called the father of Japanese parks. He looked to Europe, especially Germany, for ideas. You can see that influence in the wide paths, the open lawns, and the formal flower beds. But Japanese touches remain everywhere — in the ponds, the stone lanterns, and the careful shaping of trees. The result sits between two worlds, much like Tokyo itself at the start of the twentieth century.

Find the pond called Shinji-ike, with its stone edges and its fountain in the shape of a crane. The crane stands with its beak raised, and water rises from it into the air. It is often said to be one of the earliest decorative fountains in Japan. In Japanese culture the crane is a symbol of long life and good fortune. Look closely at the stonework around the pond, too. Some visitors say these banks recall the old castle moats that once ran through this district.

Now for a story many people walk past without knowing. Near the restaurant called Matsumoto-ro stands a huge ginkgo tree, its trunk thick and creased with age. Locals call it the Kubikake Icho, which means the neck-wager ginkgo. According to a popular tale, the tree was marked for cutting during road work. Honda Seiroku insisted it could be moved and saved. He is said to have bet his own neck — his job, his reputation — on its survival. The tree lived.

Matsumoto-ro itself opened around the same time as the park, and it has become part of the place's memory. For generations, Tokyo families came here for Western-style dishes, which were still a novelty in the early twentieth century. The restaurant is especially known for its curry rice. It also holds a well-known charity curry event each year, a tradition that many regulars treasure. Sitting on its terrace under the trees, you can feel how the park mixed old Japan with new tastes.

The park also carries darker memories. In 1905, after the war between Japan and Russia, a large crowd gathered here to protest the peace treaty. Many people felt the terms gave Japan too little after so much sacrifice. The rally turned into rioting that spread across the city. Historians call it the Hibiya Incendiary Incident. It showed that this open space was more than a garden. It had become a place where ordinary people could gather and make their voices heard.

That role as a public stage continues today. In the park you will find an open-air concert hall, known in Tokyo simply as Yaon. Generations of musicians have played here, from rock bands to folk singers, and the venue holds a special place in Japanese music history. There is also Hibiya Public Hall, a handsome older building used for concerts, lectures, and meetings. On a busy evening you may hear music drifting through the trees before you see the stage.

The seasons shape the park's character. In spring, flower beds fill with tulips and other blooms, and families spread out on the lawns. In summer the deep shade of the trees becomes a refuge from Tokyo's heavy heat. Autumn brings the most famous show — the ginkgo trees turn a bright, buttery yellow, and their fan-shaped leaves cover the paths. In November the park often hosts a chrysanthemum exhibition, a flower closely tied to Japan's imperial family.

Step toward the edges and the park shows its position in the city. To one side lie the moats and walls of the Imperial Palace grounds, where the emperor's residence stands. To another side, the government district of Kasumigaseki begins, with its ministries and offices. Ginza, the shopping district, is a short walk away. Across the road stands the Imperial Hotel, a name with a long history of its own. Few parks sit so close to so many centres of power and daily life.

Notice who shares the park with you. At lunchtime, office workers in dark suits sit on benches with boxed lunches. Older people walk slowly along the paths, some with cameras pointed at the flowers. Children chase pigeons near the fountains. On weekends, festivals and food events sometimes fill the open plazas with tents and long tables. This is the human rhythm that the park's founders imagined — a common ground where very different people can rest side by side.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the water. Watch the crane fountain lift its spray, and follow the line of the trees up to the towers beyond them. Listen again to the city at the edges, and to the softer sounds in here — leaves, footsteps, birds. As you walk out, look back once at the old ginkgo. It has watched more than a century of Tokyo pass by, and it is still standing.
