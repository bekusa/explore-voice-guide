The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the water here and listen. The fjord laps against stone. A gull calls somewhere overhead. The light in northern Norway has a way of lying flat and silver across the sea, even at midday. Somewhere beneath that surface, in the cold, quiet waters near Harstad, rests the wreck of a small wooden ship. You cannot see her from the shore. But her story reaches from this harbour all the way to the bottom of the world.

The ship began life in Norway in 1884. She was built as a whaler and sealer, a working vessel made for hard northern seas. Her original name was Patria. Ships like her were built tough, with thick timbers to push through pack ice. Nobody at her launch could have guessed that she would one day carry one of the most important scientific expeditions of her century. For her first years, she simply worked... hunting in cold water, like many Norwegian boats of her kind.

Then, in the late nineteenth century, a Belgian naval officer named Adrien de Gerlache went looking for a ship. He had a bold plan — to lead a scientific expedition to Antarctica. At that time, the southern continent was still almost unknown. De Gerlache bought the Norwegian whaler, refitted her for science, and gave her a new name: Belgica. She sailed from Belgium in 1897. It was the beginning of what history now calls the Belgian Antarctic Expedition.

Here is the first surprise. The crew of this modest ship included names that would later become famous. The first mate was a young Norwegian called Roald Amundsen. Years later, he would become the first person to reach the South Pole. The ship's doctor was an American, Frederick Cook, who later made his own controversial claims about the North Pole. Two of the great polar figures of the next generation learned their trade together, on these same wooden decks.

The crew was also remarkably international for its time. Alongside Belgians and Norwegians, there were scientists from other countries. Henryk Arctowski, a Polish scientist, studied the sea, the weather and the ice. Emil Racoviță, a Romanian biologist, collected plants and animals that few people had ever recorded. In an age of national rivalries, this was a small floating community of many languages. Their work became a foundation for later Antarctic science.

In early 1898, the Belgica pushed deep into the ice off the Antarctic Peninsula, in the area of the Bellingshausen Sea. And then the ice closed around her. Some historians believe de Gerlache took this risk on purpose. Others think he was simply caught. Either way, the result was the same. The ship was frozen in place, and she could not escape. The men aboard became the first people known to spend a full winter in Antarctica.

Think about what that meant. For weeks, the sun did not rise at all. The cold was constant. The ship creaked as the ice pressed against her hull. The men ate tinned food and grew weak. Many began to show signs of scurvy, a disease caused by a lack of vitamin C. Some also suffered badly in their minds. The long darkness, the isolation and the silence were almost too much to bear.

The expedition paid a real price. Before the ship even reached the ice, a young Norwegian sailor, Carl August Wiencke, was lost overboard in a storm. During the winter, the Belgian geophysicist Émile Danco died aboard the trapped ship. His name survives today on the Antarctic map, on a stretch of coast named in his memory. These losses are a quiet reminder that early polar science was paid for with human lives.

Now comes the second surprise. Doctor Cook and Amundsen found that fresh meat helped the sick men recover. They began to eat seal and penguin meat, often lightly cooked. At first many of the crew refused. But slowly their health improved. It was an early, practical lesson in polar survival. Amundsen remembered it well. Fresh food, good planning and respect for the cold later became central to his own expeditions.

In early 1899, after about a year in the ice, the crew finally cut and blasted a channel through the floes. The Belgica broke free and returned north. She came home with scientific samples, weather records and observations that scientists studied for many years. After that, the ship had a second career in the Arctic. A French prince, the Duke of Orléans, used her for several northern voyages in the early twentieth century, around Greenland and the Arctic seas.

Later, the ship came back to Norway, the country where she was born. Her days of exploration were over. She was renamed and returned to ordinary working life along the coast, much like her first years as a whaler. For a ship with such a remarkable past, this was a modest ending. But it was not yet the final chapter. War was coming to northern Norway.

In the spring of 1940, the fighting of the Second World War reached this region. Harstad and the waters around it became important to the Allied forces during the campaign for Narvik. The old ship was taken into service as a supply vessel. During that spring she was lost, and she sank in the waters near Harstad. She has rested there on the seabed ever since. Divers have visited and studied the wreck, and in recent years Belgian groups have shown strong interest in her remains.

There is a modern echo too. In Belgium, a project has worked to build a new ship inspired by the Belgica, as a way to keep her story alive. So the old wooden hull lies here in the north, while her memory sails on far to the south. It is a strange and fitting link. A Norwegian ship carried a Belgian dream to Antarctica, and then came home to Norway to rest.

Before you walk on, look out once more across the water. Notice the colour of the sea, the islands and the mountains along the fjord. The same cold northern water once shaped the timbers of that small ship. Somewhere down there, in the dark and stillness, lies a hull that once stood frozen in Antarctic ice. As you leave, carry that picture with you... a quiet harbour, and a ship that went to the end of the world and came home.
