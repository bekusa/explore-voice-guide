The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the wide stone steps and look up. The facade in front of you is brick, a warm yellow-brown that turns almost golden in low sun. Tall, narrow openings rise across the front like a row of open doors. Behind you, traffic and footsteps hum along the avenue. In front, everything seems to slow down. This is the Gothenburg Museum of Art, and it sits at the very top of the city's main boulevard.

That boulevard is Kungsportsavenyen, which locals simply call the Avenyn. It runs uphill from the old canal toward this square, Götaplatsen. The museum closes the view like a full stop at the end of a long sentence. City planners wanted exactly that effect. Walk down the Avenyn later and turn around, and you will see how the building anchors the whole street.

The museum opened in 1923, for a large exhibition that marked three hundred years since the founding of Gothenburg. The city wanted a monumental square for the occasion, a place of culture rather than commerce. The architects Sigfrid Ericson and Arvid Bjerke designed the building. Their style is often called Nordic Classicism, or Swedish Grace. This was a 1920s movement that took the calm lines of ancient temples and simplified them, with less ornament and more quiet proportion.

Notice how the building avoids the usual marble and gleaming white stone. The brick gives it a grounded, almost humble feeling, even at this scale. Look closely at the walls and you can see the texture of individual bricks and the fine lines of mortar between them. Up close, the material looks handmade and human. From a distance, it reads as one solid, glowing mass. That balance between grand and modest is typical of Swedish design from this period.

Now turn to the fountain in front of the steps. The large bronze figure rising from the water is Poseidon, the Greek god of the sea, by the Swedish sculptor Carl Milles. It was installed in the early 1930s, and it has become a symbol of the city. That makes sense for Gothenburg, a port town that grew rich on shipping and trade. Poseidon holds a fish in one hand and a shell in the other, and he looks out over the avenue toward the harbour.

Here is a story that locals enjoy telling. According to a popular tale, the city was uneasy about the statue's original anatomy. Milles reportedly had to make one part of the god rather more modest. Whether every detail of the story is true is hard to say. But you will often hear people in Gothenburg laugh about it, and some visitors look up at the statue with new curiosity once they know. It is a small reminder that even serious public art has a playful side here.

The square around you is a cultural stage in its own right. To one side stands the concert hall, home to the Gothenburg Symphony Orchestra. On the other side is the city theatre. Nearby you will also find the city library. Together, these buildings make Götaplatsen a place where music, drama, books, and art share one open space. On a summer evening, the steps fill with people sitting, talking, and watching the light change across the brick.

Inside, the museum is best known for its Nordic art, especially work from around the turn of the twentieth century. The heart of this is the Fürstenberg Gallery. It is named after Pontus and Göthilda Fürstenberg, a Gothenburg couple who supported and collected the artists of their own time. They donated their collection to the city. Because of their gift, the museum holds one of the strongest groups of Scandinavian art from that period anywhere.

In these rooms you can meet painters who shaped how Scandinavians saw themselves. Carl Larsson painted bright, intimate scenes of family and home. Anders Zorn captured light on water and skin with quick, confident brushwork. Ernst Josephson pushed toward something stranger and more personal. And the Norwegian Edvard Munch, best known for The Scream, is represented here too. Walking through, you can follow Nordic painting as it moved from careful realism toward mood, memory, and emotion.

Here is something many visitors miss. The collection reaches far beyond Scandinavia. The museum also holds work by European names such as Rembrandt and Rubens from the Dutch and Flemish traditions. There are pieces by French artists like Claude Monet, and later work by Pablo Picasso and Vincent van Gogh. Many people come for the Nordic rooms and are surprised to find these artists in a museum of this size. It is worth leaving time to wander.

The building itself also rewards attention inside. Watch how daylight enters the galleries. Many Nordic museums of this era were designed around soft, even light, which matters in a country where winter days are short and summer evenings are very long. The rooms change in mood with the seasons. A painting of a pale summer night can feel very different when you see it on a dark November afternoon, with rain on the windows outside.

Attached to the museum is a separate exhibition hall for changing shows, often contemporary art. So the same building holds two kinds of time. In one room you find a painter from more than a century ago. A short walk away, you might find work made in the last few years. That mix keeps the place connected to the present city, not only to its past.

Before you go inside, or after you come out, take a moment on the top step. Look down the long, straight line of the Avenyn toward the canal. Listen to the fountain behind Poseidon, the trams in the distance, and the voices of people crossing the square. The brick at your back is warm if the sun has been on it. This is the view the planners imagined in 1923 — a city looking up toward its art, and its art looking back down at the city.
