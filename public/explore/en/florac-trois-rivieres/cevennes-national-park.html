The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere nearby, water is running over stone. In Florac, that sound never quite goes away. Springs, streams and small rivers thread through the town, and the air smells of wet rock, wood smoke and, in autumn, fallen chestnut leaves. Above the rooftops, grey limestone cliffs rise like a wall. You are standing at a doorway between two very different worlds, and both of them belong to Cévennes National Park.

The park was created in 1970, and it is unusual among France's national parks. Most protect wild land where almost nobody lives. Here, people never left. Farmers, shepherds and villagers still live and work inside the protected heart of the park. The idea was not to freeze a wilderness but to look after a landscape that humans and animals have shaped together for thousands of years. That makes this a living park... one with kitchens, sheepdogs and school buses.

Florac is the natural place to begin. The park's headquarters sits in the town's château, a solid stone building with round towers that stands near the centre. It was rebuilt in the seventeenth century after earlier conflicts, and later served many purposes before the park moved in. Today it houses offices and an information space. If you want to understand the shape of the territory before you head out, this is where the maps, the staff and the exhibits are.

Now look around and notice the ground itself. Florac sits on a geological seam. To one side rise the causses, which are high limestone plateaus, dry and open, covered in short grass and scattered pines. To the other side lie the Cévennes proper — steep ridges of granite and schist, a dark, flaky rock, cut by narrow valleys. Limestone drains water away underground. Granite and schist hold it on the surface. That single difference explains much of what you will see.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. Florac's water does not only come from rivers. A powerful spring called the Source du Pêcher bursts out of the limestone cliff at the edge of town. Rain that falls on the plateau above sinks through cracks in the rock, travels underground, and finally emerges here. After heavy rain it can roar. In a dry spell it quietens almost to a whisper of water. The town grew up around this reliable supply.

The name of the town also tells a story about water. Florac joined with neighbouring communes in 2016 to form Florac-Trois-Rivières, meaning Florac of the Three Rivers. The Tarn, the Tarnon and the Mimente all meet in this area. The Tarn flows on from here into its famous gorges, a deep canyon carved between two limestone plateaus. Follow it downstream and the valley narrows into steep walls of pale and orange rock.

The landscape around you is more than a national park. In 2011, UNESCO added the Causses and the Cévennes to its World Heritage list as a Mediterranean agro-pastoral cultural landscape. In plain words, that means land shaped by farming and grazing. The honour is not for a single monument. It is for stone terraces, sheep tracks, shepherds' huts, barns and fields — the ordinary work of generations who made a living from difficult ground.

Grazing is the key. For centuries, flocks of sheep moved each summer from the hot lowlands near the Mediterranean up to the cool mountain pastures. This seasonal journey is called transhumance. The animals followed ancient routes known locally as drailles, broad tracks marked by low stone walls and worn into the hillsides. Some of these routes are still used. If you are here at the right season, you may meet a flock on the move, bells ringing, dogs working the edges.

On the granite and schist slopes, another tree shaped daily life — the sweet chestnut. Locals once called it the bread tree, because its nuts fed families through long winters. They were dried in small stone buildings over slow fires, then peeled and stored. Look for old chestnut groves on the hillsides, with thick, twisted trunks. Look also for the dry-stone terraces that hold the soil in place. Building them by hand took enormous patience.

The Cévennes also carry a hard history. This was a stronghold of French Protestants. After the king outlawed their faith in the late seventeenth century, many worshipped in secret, in forests and ravines. In the early eighteenth century, Protestant peasants rose in an armed revolt known as the war of the Camisards. The fighting was brutal, and villages were burned. Memory of that time still runs deep in the region, and you will find small museums and memorials across the hills.

In 1878, a young Scottish writer walked through these hills with a stubborn donkey named Modestine. He was Robert Louis Stevenson, later the author of Treasure Island. His book, Travels with a Donkey in the Cévennes, describes the landscape, the people and the Protestant history he found here. His route passed through Florac. Today a long-distance walking trail follows his footsteps, and some hikers still travel it with a donkey carrying their bags.

The park is also a place for wildlife. On the cliffs of the gorges to the west, griffon vultures were reintroduced in the late twentieth century after disappearing from the area. Now you can watch these huge birds circling on warm air above the canyons. Beavers have returned to some rivers, and deer move through the forests. Look up at night as well. The park was recognised as a dark sky reserve, and on a clear evening the Milky Way spreads across the sky.

Two high summits frame the park. To the north is Mont Lozère, a broad granite mountain with open moorland and scattered boulders. To the south is Mont Aigoual, where a weather observatory stands on the summit. On a clear day, people say you can see from the Alps to the Mediterranean. Both are easy to reach from Florac by road, and both feel very different from the sheltered streets of the town below.

Before you move on, pause by the water again. Watch how it slides over the stones, clear and cold, coming down from plateaus you cannot see from here. Notice the walls of the houses, built from the rock around them. Notice the cliffs above, grey and patient. This park is not behind a fence or a gate. You are already inside it, and every path out of Florac leads deeper in.
