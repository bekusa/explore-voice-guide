The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is wind moving across the grass, and perhaps the sound of a car on the road behind you. Then there is the quiet. Row after row of slender white stone markers runs up the green slope ahead. They are close together, like a crowd standing still. The ground under your feet is soft and carefully kept. This place asks you to slow down, and most people do without being told.

You are standing at the Srebrenica-Potočari Memorial and Cemetery. It lies in the village of Potočari, a few kilometres north of the town of Srebrenica itself, in eastern Bosnia and Herzegovina. The hills here are steep and wooded. The valley is narrow. Before the war, this was a mining and spa region, known for its mineral springs. Today, its name stands for something else. This guide will walk with you gently through what happened, and what this place holds now.

In the early 1990s, as Yugoslavia broke apart, war spread across Bosnia. Srebrenica was a town with a largely Bosniak population. Bosniaks are the Bosnian Muslims, one of the country's main communities. As Bosnian Serb forces took territory across the east, thousands of Bosniak refugees crowded into the Srebrenica area. In 1993, the United Nations declared it a safe area, a zone that was meant to be protected from attack. A small battalion of Dutch peacekeepers was later stationed here.

In July 1995, that protection collapsed. Bosnian Serb forces under the command of General Ratko Mladić advanced on the enclave and took the town. Tens of thousands of people fled. Many women, children and elderly people came here, to Potočari, to the Dutch base, hoping for safety. Thousands of men tried a different route. They set off on foot through the forests, in a long column, toward territory held by the Bosnian government.

What followed over the next several days was the worst mass killing in Europe since the Second World War. Bosnian Serb forces separated men and boys from the women here at Potočari. They captured many others from the column in the woods. More than eight thousand Bosniak men and boys were killed. Their bodies were buried in mass graves. Later, many of those graves were dug up with machines and the remains moved to other sites, to hide the evidence.

That last detail explains something many visitors do not expect. Because the remains were moved, a single person's bones could be scattered across two, three, or even more separate graves. Identification became slow and painstaking work. Forensic teams used DNA, matching fragments of bone with blood samples given by surviving relatives. The International Commission on Missing Persons led much of this effort. Even now, decades later, some families are still waiting for news.

This is why the cemetery keeps growing. Each year, on the eleventh of July, the anniversary of the fall of Srebrenica, newly identified victims are buried here. Thousands of people gather on this hillside. Coffins wrapped in green cloth pass from hand to hand across the crowd. Some families have buried only a few bones, because that is all that has been found. Some have chosen to wait, hoping more will be identified.

Look now at the grave markers themselves. Most are white stone pillars in the traditional Bosnian Muslim style, called nišani. Their shape is simple and slightly tapered. Each carries a name, a year of birth, and the year 1995. Take a moment to read a few of the birth years. You will notice that some belong to teenagers. A small number of graves have different markers, because not every victim was Muslim, and the cemetery reflects that.

Near the entrance is an open-air prayer space, called a musalla, where communal prayers are held. Nearby stands a curved stone wall engraved with the names of victims, arranged by surname. Families come here to find a name and touch it. Often the same surname repeats again and again, down a long column of letters. Fathers, sons, brothers and cousins were taken together. One family name can fill a whole section of stone.

Across the road are the old buildings of a former battery factory. During the war, this was the base of the Dutch peacekeepers. Today, part of it holds the memorial exhibition. Inside, the space is bare and industrial, with concrete floors and high ceilings. Personal belongings recovered from the graves are on display. A watch, a pair of glasses, a small notebook. These ordinary objects often stay with visitors longer than any number.

The question of justice has been long and difficult. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, a United Nations court in The Hague, ruled that the killings at Srebrenica were genocide. The International Court of Justice reached the same finding about the crime itself. Ratko Mladić and the Bosnian Serb political leader Radovan Karadžić were both convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment. In 2024, the United Nations General Assembly designated the eleventh of July as an international day of remembrance for Srebrenica.

Remembrance here is also something people do with their feet. Every summer, thousands of walkers join the March of Peace. Over several days, they walk the forest route of the 1995 column, but in reverse, from the free territory back toward Potočari. They arrive in time for the July burials. Survivors walk beside young people from Bosnia and from abroad. Many say the hills feel very different when you have climbed them yourself.

The memorial was formally opened in 2003. It is not a museum of the distant past. It is a living cemetery and a place of mourning for families who still live in this region. Some of the mothers of Srebrenica, women who lost husbands and sons, still come here regularly. If you meet a survivor or a guide from the memorial, listen. Their voices are the heart of this place.

Before you leave, turn and look back up the slope. Notice how the white markers catch the light, and how the rows seem to continue past where your eye can follow. Notice the hills around you, the same hills the column walked through that July. As you walk back toward the road, you might carry one name with you. Say it quietly. That small act of memory is exactly what this place was built for.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly and keep voices low among the graves.
Ask before photographing mourners or family members at graves.
Visit the exhibition in the former battery factory across the road.
Expect large crowds and road closures around the eleventh of July.
Join a guided visit led by memorial staff or survivors.
