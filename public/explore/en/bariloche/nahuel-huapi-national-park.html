The guide

The story

Start with the air. It is cold and clean, and it carries the sharp, green smell of wet forest and old leaves. Somewhere close, water moves over stone. Above you, the light falls through the beech trees in thin, shifting strips. The mountains here hold snow for much of the year. This is northern Patagonia, where the Andes meet a long chain of glacial lakes. You are on the edge of Nahuel Huapi National Park, the oldest national park in Argentina.

Many travellers reach the park the way you may have come. They drive south from San Martín de los Andes, the main town of the Lácar Department, along the famous Seven Lakes Road. The road curves past one lake after another. Each one has its own colour, from deep navy to bright turquoise. Slowly, the forest closes in, and the peaks grow taller. The landscape you see now is the result of that same journey, repeated by ice, water and time.

The park takes its name from its great central lake. Nahuel Huapi comes from Mapudungun, the language of the Mapuche people, who have lived in this region for many centuries. The name is usually translated as island of the jaguar. Nahuel means jaguar, and huapi means island. Jaguars no longer live this far south. But the name stays on the map, a small trace of a time when the land and its animals looked very different.

Here is the first surprise. This park began as a gift. In 1903, the explorer and scientist Francisco Moreno, known in Argentina as Perito Moreno, donated land near the lake to the nation. He asked for one thing in return. The land had to be protected for everyone, forever. That donation became the seed of the Argentine national park system. Over the following decades, the protected area grew, and in the 1930s it took the name it carries today.

The scale is hard to feel from a single viewpoint. The park covers roughly seven thousand square kilometres. It stretches across two provinces, Neuquén and Río Negro, and runs west to the border with Chile. Inside it are forests, rivers, waterfalls, high lakes and glaciers. If you could fly over it, you would see a maze of blue fingers of water reaching between the mountains. Those long, narrow lakes are the work of glaciers that carved these valleys during the ice ages.

Look at the water closely. Its colour changes with the sky and the depth. On a calm morning, the lake is a sheet of glass that holds a perfect copy of the peaks. By afternoon, the wind often rises, and the surface breaks into short, white waves. Locals know that Patagonian weather can change fast. A clear sky can turn grey within an hour. Then the sun returns, and the whole valley seems freshly washed.

Now turn your attention to the trees. Much of the forest here is southern beech. Three types are common: coihue, lenga and ñire. The coihue grows tall and straight in the wetter, lower forests. The lenga climbs higher up the slopes. In autumn, the lenga and ñire turn red, orange and gold, and the mountainsides look as if they are on fire. Underneath, bamboo-like cane called caña colihue grows in dense green walls.

The second surprise is a small forest with a big reputation. On the Quetrihué Peninsula, near the town of Villa La Angostura, there is a rare grove of arrayán trees, a kind of myrtle. Their bark is a bright cinnamon colour, smooth and cool to the touch, with pale patches where old bark has peeled away. The trunks twist slowly upward. Visitors walk among them on wooden boardwalks that protect the shallow roots.

A popular story follows this grove. According to a tale that many guides repeat, the arrayán forest inspired the woods in the Disney film Bambi. It is a lovely idea. But there is no solid evidence for it, and the claim is widely treated as legend. The truth may be better anyway. These trees are real, they are slow-growing, and some of them are very old. They need no film to be remarkable.

Above the forests, the high mountains rule the horizon. The tallest is Cerro Tronador, an old volcano that is no longer active. Its name means the Thunderer. It does not come from storms. It comes from the deep booming sound of ice breaking off its glaciers and crashing down the slopes. On a quiet day, hikers near its base can sometimes hear this sound echo across the valley. It is one of the most memorable sounds in Patagonia.

The park is also home to wildlife that many visitors never see. The huemul, a stocky Andean deer, is endangered and very shy. The pudú, one of the smallest deer in the world, hides in thick undergrowth. Look up, and you may spot an Andean condor riding the warm air above the ridges. Along the shores, you may hear the loud call of the chucao, a small forest bird. Locals say its song can bring good or bad luck, depending on which side you hear it from.

People have shaped this landscape too. The town of Bariloche grew on the southern shore of the great lake, and it is now the main gateway to the park. Villa La Angostura sits on the northern shore, quieter and greener. Mountain refuges, trails and boat routes connect the wild interior to these towns. Yet large parts of the park remain hard to reach. That distance is part of what keeps them whole.

Take a moment now, before you move on. Notice the sound of the wind in the beech leaves. Notice the cold edge in the air, even in summer. Look at the line where the forest meets the water, and then at the line where the rock meets the snow. Somewhere beyond those ridges, ice is still slowly moving. A century ago, one man asked that this land be kept for everyone. You are standing inside that promise.

Before you go

Practical tips

Drive the Seven Lakes Road from San Martín de los Andes.
Pack waterproof layers because Patagonian weather shifts quickly.
Visit lakes early in the morning for calm, mirror-like water.
Come in autumn to see the beech forests turn red and gold.
Stay on boardwalks to protect the shallow arrayán roots.
