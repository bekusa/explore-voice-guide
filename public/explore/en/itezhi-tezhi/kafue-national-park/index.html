The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere out on the water, a fish eagle throws back its head and calls, a high, ringing cry that carries across the lake. The air smells of dust, warm grass, and wet reeds. Underfoot, the ground is pale sand mixed with grey earth, dotted with the tracks of animals that passed here before dawn. Welcome to the southern edge of Kafue National Park, Zambia's oldest national park, and one of the largest wild places in Africa.

Size is the first thing to understand here. The park covers around 22,400 square kilometres. That is larger than some entire countries. A drive from this southern gateway near Itezhi Tezhi to the far northern plains can take most of a day on rough tracks. Most visitors only ever see a small part of it. And that is part of its character... Kafue is not a place you finish. It is a place you sample.

The park takes its name from the Kafue River. The river runs through the park for a long stretch before it turns east and eventually joins the Zambezi, the great river of southern Africa. Along its banks, tall trees cast deep shade, and hippos rest in the slow pools. Crocodiles lie on the sandbars with their mouths open to cool down. The river is the spine of the park. Almost everything here depends on it.

The lake in front of you is not natural. Lake Itezhi Tezhi formed after engineers built a dam across the Kafue River in the 1970s. The dam stores water for hydroelectric power stations further downstream, which supply a large share of Zambia's electricity. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The drowned trees you may see standing in the shallows are the remains of the old woodland that the rising water flooded. Their pale, bare trunks now serve as perches for cormorants and fish eagles.

Kafue was declared a national park in 1950, at a time when much of southern Africa was still under colonial rule. Long before that, people lived and hunted across this landscape. Communities such as the Ila, known for their long tradition of cattle herding on the plains downstream, and other groups in the region shaped the land around the park. Today, villages lie close to the boundaries, and conservation here is closely tied to the lives of the people who share this country with wildlife.

Most of the park is covered by miombo woodland. Miombo is a type of dry open forest that stretches across much of central and southern Africa. In the dry months, the leaves turn shades of copper, red, and gold. Then, just before the rains, many trees put out fresh new leaves in pink and bronze, before they turn green. It is one of the few places in the world where spring colour looks like autumn colour.

Between the woodlands, you will find dambos. A dambo is a shallow, grassy valley that holds water after the rains. These open strips are where grazing animals gather, so they are some of the best places to look for wildlife. Watch also for termite mounds. Some rise taller than a person. Mongooses, monitor lizards, and even leopards use them as lookout points and shelters.

Now, the animals. Kafue holds one of the widest ranges of antelope species in Africa. You may see puku, a golden-brown antelope that favours river edges. There are graceful roan and sable antelope, with their curved horns. There is the shy oribi, small and quick in the grass. Defassa waterbuck stand near the water with shaggy coats. In the wetlands of the north live red lechwe and the elusive sitatunga, an antelope with splayed hooves built for walking on soft, swampy ground.

The predators are harder to find, but they are here. Kafue is well known among guides for its leopards, which hunt mostly at night and rest in trees by day. Lions move across the plains and woodland. The park is also one of Zambia's important strongholds for the African wild dog, a painted, highly social hunter that is endangered across much of the continent. Cheetahs are present too, though sightings are rare. Patience matters more here than speed.

Far to the north lie the Busanga Plains. This is a vast floodplain that fills with water during the rainy season and slowly drains through the dry months. When the land dries out, huge herds of red lechwe and puku graze across the open grass. The plains feel completely different from the woodland around you now. Flat, wide, and silver with water early in the season, they are reachable only for part of the year.

The rhythm of the year shapes everything in Kafue. The rains usually arrive around November and continue for several months. Many tracks become impassable, and parts of the park close to vehicles. In the dry season, the grass dries and thins, animals move closer to permanent water, and viewing becomes easier. Birdlife is rewarding at any time. Hundreds of bird species have been recorded here, from carmine bee-eaters to wattled cranes.

One more surprise. Kafue is part of something much bigger. It lies within the Kavango-Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area, a network of protected land that crosses the borders of five southern African countries. The idea is simple. Elephants and other animals do not recognise national borders, so neighbouring countries try to protect the routes they use. When you stand here, you are on the edge of one of the largest conservation landscapes on Earth.

So take a last look around you. Notice the line where the water meets the trees. Notice the way the light sits on the grass, and the small sounds — a bird in the reeds, the wind in dry leaves, perhaps a hippo grunting somewhere out of sight. Kafue does not show everything at once. As you move on, keep your eyes on the shade beneath the trees. Something may be watching you, just as quietly as you are watching it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the dry season for easier wildlife viewing.
Avoid dark blue and black clothing near tsetse flies.
Head out at dawn when predators are most active.
Book a guided game drive for the best sightings.
Carry binoculars to scan dambos and distant riverbanks.
