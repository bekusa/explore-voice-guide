The guide

The story

The air changes the moment you pass inside. It turns cool and damp, and it smells faintly of wet concrete and old machine oil. Your footsteps echo down a long, straight gallery that seems to have no end. Above you sits a great mass of rock and earth. Outside, a few minutes from Maastricht, the Belgian countryside rolls on in quiet farmland. In here, you are standing inside one of the most ambitious defensive works of the twentieth century.

Fort Ében-Émael lies just across the Dutch border, in the Belgian municipality of Bassenge. From Maastricht it is a short drive south, close to where the Meuse River and the Albert Canal run side by side. Belgium built this fortress in the early 1930s. Construction began around 1932 and the main work finished in the middle of that decade. The goal was simple. The fort had to guard the canal and the river crossings against an invasion from the east.

The builders made clever use of the landscape. When engineers cut the Albert Canal through the hills here, they left a sheer wall of rock on one side. The fortress was built on top of that plateau, so the canal itself became a giant moat. On the other sides, engineers added ditches, walls and anti-tank obstacles. From above, the site has a rough triangle shape. Much of it is invisible from the ground, because most of the fort lies beneath your feet.

That underground world is enormous. Several kilometres of tunnels connect the gun positions to barracks, ammunition stores, kitchens, a hospital area and a power station. Soldiers could live down here for long periods without seeing daylight. Think about that as you walk. Hundreds of men slept in bunks along these corridors. They ate, trained and waited here, deep in the rock, listening to the steady hum of the generators.

On the surface, the fort showed only its teeth. Steel cupolas and thick concrete casemates — armoured gun positions — rose from the grassy plateau. Some pointed north towards Maastricht. Others covered the canal, the bridges and the approaches from the south. Many people at the time believed the fort could not be taken. Military writers described it as one of the strongest forts in Europe. And on paper, they were probably right.

Then came the morning of 10 May 1940. Germany invaded the Netherlands, Belgium and Luxembourg on that day. Before dawn, silent gliders crossed the border and dropped onto the roof of the fort. They carried a small team of German paratroopers — fewer than a hundred men. The garrison inside numbered many hundreds. Yet the attackers had planned for months, and they had a weapon almost nobody had seen in battle before.

That weapon was the shaped charge, also called the hollow charge. It is an explosive formed so that it focuses its blast into a narrow jet. Placed on top of a steel cupola, it could punch through armour or wreck the guns inside. Historians generally describe Ében-Émael as the first combat use of this technology. Within minutes, the paratroopers had disabled many of the surface positions. The defenders found themselves trapped beneath their own roof.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The commander of the German assault team was not on the roof when the battle began. The tow rope of his glider broke over Germany, and he had to find another aircraft. He arrived hours later. A sergeant led the first attack in his place. Meanwhile, other German glider teams struck the nearby bridges over the canal. Some bridges fell intact. The Belgians managed to blow up at least one.

The Belgian garrison under Major Jottrand did not give up at once. Defenders fought from inside the fort, and Belgian artillery fired on the plateau. But the attackers held the roof, and German ground troops soon crossed the canal to reinforce them. On 11 May, a little over a day after the first glider landed, the fort surrendered. The speed of its fall shocked military planners across Europe. It showed that concrete alone could no longer guarantee safety.

As you walk the galleries today, look closely at the walls and equipment. Volunteers care for much of the fort, and they have kept a great deal of the original fittings. You may see ventilation pipes, telephone lines, iron bunks and heavy blast doors. The rooms carry painted signs and numbers that once guided soldiers through the maze. In some places, the concrete still carries the marks and scorching of 1940.

The second surprise waits outside. If your visit includes the roof, climb up to the plateau. It looks peaceful now, almost like a meadow. Grass and trees grow over the slopes. But look for the cupolas and the damaged steel. Look for the hollows in the ground. This quiet field was the landing zone, and the whole battle for the roof happened on a stretch of land you can cross in a few minutes on foot.

Standing up there, the scale of the plan becomes clear. A few gliders, landing almost silently, defeated a fortress that took years to build. For the defenders, it was a bitter and confusing experience. For military history, it marked a turning point in how armies thought about surprise and air assault. And for Belgium, it was the start of a long, hard occupation. The memorials around the site honour the soldiers who served and died here.

Before you leave, pause for a moment, wherever you are standing. If you are underground, listen to the drip of water and the quiet of the rock. If you are on the roof, look north towards Maastricht and the canal below. The farmland, the river traffic and the birdsong all feel ordinary today. That ordinary calm is perhaps the most important thing this old fortress has to show. Take it with you as you walk back into the daylight.
