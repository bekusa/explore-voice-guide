Palazzo Altemps

Just off the crowds of Piazza Navona, a plain old door opens into one of the quietest great museums in Rome.

⏱ 5 min

The story

Just off the crowds of Piazza Navona, a plain old door opens into one of the quietest great museums in Rome. Inside a restored Renaissance palace, ancient marble gods and emperors stand in frescoed rooms around a graceful courtyard. This is Palazzo Altemps, where classical sculpture is shown in a setting almost as old as some of the art itself.

The palace dates from the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, built and added to by wealthy Roman families, and it is a fine example of a noble Renaissance house in its own right. Painted ceilings, a colonnaded courtyard, and a private chapel survive, so the building frames the sculpture rather than merely storing it.

It is one of several sites that together make up the National Roman Museum, the city's great collection of ancient art, split across a handful of historic buildings. Palazzo Altemps holds the sculpture, much of it gathered by noble collectors in past centuries and now reunited for the public.

The heart of the collection is a group of antiquities assembled by the Ludovisi family in the seventeenth century, prized statues that passed through generations of collectors before coming to the state. To see them here, in a palace of the same era, is to glimpse how such treasures were once displayed in private grandeur.

Among the most famous pieces is a carved marble panel known as the Ludovisi Throne, showing a delicate scene of a figure rising from the sea, its low relief long admired for its grace and mystery. Scholars still debate exactly what it is and where it came from, which only adds to its draw.

Another powerful work shows a defeated warrior choosing death over capture, a dramatic group full of movement and tragedy. Carved long ago and much restored over the centuries, it is the kind of object that stops visitors in their tracks even in a city full of ancient art.

The quiet is part of the experience. Unlike the great crowded sites nearby, Palazzo Altemps is often calm, so you can stand alone before a statue and take your time. For visitors tired of jostling for a clear view, it is a genuine relief.

The rooms reward looking beyond the sculpture. Faded frescoes, carved fireplaces, and the courtyard with its arches all show how a rich Roman family once lived, and the contrast between the ancient marble and the Renaissance setting is a quiet pleasure in itself.

Because it is small and rarely crowded, the palace makes an easy, restful stop rather than an exhausting one. An hour or so is enough to take it in, which makes it a good counterpoint to a long, tiring day among the bigger ruins and museums.

Its position helps. The palace sits a short walk from Piazza Navona and the Pantheon, in the dense old centre, so it slots easily into a day on foot. Many visitors pass within a few steps of it without ever realising it is there.

A few practical notes. Entry is ticketed, sometimes as part of a combined ticket covering the other branches of the National Roman Museum, which can be good value if you plan to see more than one. Opening days can vary, so it is worth checking before you go.

Stand for a while in the courtyard before you leave, with the ancient statues in the rooms around you and the Renaissance arches overhead. Few places let you feel the long chain of collectors and caretakers who kept this art safe across the centuries. When you step back out toward Piazza Navona, the noise of the crowd returns all at once.

Key facts

A Renaissance palace near Piazza Navona.
Part of the National Roman Museum.
Holds the Ludovisi collection of ancient sculpture.
Displays the carved marble Ludovisi Throne.
Far quieter than Rome's most crowded sites.

What to look for

The low-relief panel called the Ludovisi Throne.
The defeated warrior choosing death over capture.
Faded frescoes on the palace ceilings.
The colonnaded Renaissance courtyard at its centre.

Practical tips

Visit for a calm break from the crowds.
Consider a combined National Roman Museum ticket.
Allow about an hour for the rooms.
Check the opening days before you go.
