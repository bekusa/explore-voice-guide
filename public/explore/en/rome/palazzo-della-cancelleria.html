The guide

The story

Run your hand along the pale stone of the façade, and it feels cool and slightly rough. This is travertine, the creamy limestone that built much of Rome. Here it is laid in long, calm courses, with flat pilasters marching across the front in an even rhythm. The noise of Campo de' Fiori fades just around the corner. For a moment, the street feels quieter, as if the building sets its own pace.

You are standing before the Palazzo della Cancelleria, one of the first great Renaissance palaces in Rome. It rose in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. That was a time when the city was turning its face back toward ancient ideas of order and proportion. Look at how the long front avoids heavy ornament. It relies on balance, careful spacing, and the steady repetition of windows and pilasters.

The man behind it was Cardinal Raffaele Riario. He was a great-nephew of Pope Sixtus the Fourth, the pope who gave his name to the Sistine Chapel. Riario became a cardinal very young, and he gained wealth and influence quickly. A palace of this size was a statement. It told Rome that the Riario family had arrived, and that they intended to stay.

Here is the first story most visitors never hear. According to a popular tale, Riario paid for part of this palace with gambling winnings. Legend says he won an enormous sum at dice in a single night from Franceschetto Cybo, the son of another pope. Historians treat the story with caution. But it has stuck to these walls for centuries, and Romans still enjoy telling it.

Now look closely at the decoration around the windows and along the upper bands. You may spot small carved roses repeated in the stone. The rose was the emblem of the Riario family. It works almost like a signature, quietly stamped across the building. Once you notice it, you begin to see it again and again, a small flower pressed into a very serious façade.

The stone itself has a second life. Tradition holds that much of the travertine was taken from ancient Roman ruins, possibly including the Colosseum and other nearby monuments. This was normal in Renaissance Rome. The old city was treated as a quarry. So the smooth surface in front of you may once have belonged to a building that watched gladiators or crowds of ancient Romans.

If the courtyard is open when you visit, step inside. It is one of the most elegant spaces of its kind in the city. Two storeys of arches rest on slender granite columns, with a closed upper level above. The proportions feel light and airy, almost weightless. For a long time, the design was linked to the architect Donato Bramante, who later worked on Saint Peter's Basilica. Today scholars debate who was truly responsible, and the answer remains open.

Those granite columns carry their own surprise. They are ancient, reused from older Roman structures. A long-held tradition connects them with the area of the Theatre of Pompey, the great complex that once stood nearby. That theatre is also where Julius Caesar was killed, close to this neighbourhood. So the columns may link this calm courtyard to the busiest chapter of the ancient city.

The palace did not stay with Riario for long. In 1517 he was caught up in a plot against Pope Leo the Tenth, a member of the Medici family. The cardinal survived, but he lost his palace. It was taken over by the papal government and became the home of the Apostolic Chancery, the office that prepared official papal documents. That is where the building gets its name. Cancelleria simply means chancery.

Something else is folded inside these walls. The palace absorbed the ancient church of San Lorenzo in Damaso, one of the old churches of Rome. The church was rebuilt within the new structure, so its entrance sits in the same long façade. From the street, you might not even realise a church is there. Look for the doorway that seems slightly different from the others along the front.

The building also played a part in modern Italian history. In November 1848, Pellegrino Rossi, the chief minister of the Papal States, was stabbed to death on the stairs of this palace. Soon afterwards, the assembly of the short-lived Roman Republic met here. That republic lasted only a few months before foreign troops restored papal rule. Still, for a brief moment, these rooms held a new idea of how Rome might be governed.

Today the palace belongs to the Holy See, the government of the Catholic Church. Under the treaty of 1929 between Italy and the Vatican, it has a special extraterritorial status. In simple terms, it is Vatican property in the middle of Rome. It still houses church tribunals, so it remains a working building. That is why access to the inner rooms is limited, and why the doors are not always open.

Inside, one hall is famous for its painted walls. It is the Sala dei Cento Giorni, the Hall of One Hundred Days. Giorgio Vasari and his workshop painted it quickly, and the name refers to that speed. Locals tell a story that Michelangelo saw the result and said it looked like it was done in a hurry. Whether he said it or not, the anecdote shows how artists of the time judged each other.

Before you leave, step back across the piazza and take in the full length of the front once more. Notice how the light moves across the pale stone as the day changes. Listen to the scooters, the voices drifting from the market, the footsteps on the cobbles. Then, as you walk toward Campo de' Fiori, look down at the small roses one last time... a cardinal's quiet signature, still here after five centuries.
