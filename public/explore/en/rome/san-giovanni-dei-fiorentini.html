The guide

The story

Stand at the far end of Via Giulia and listen for a moment. The traffic of the river road hums just beyond the church. But here, the street feels long, straight, and quiet. Pale travertine glows in front of you, the porous limestone that Rome has quarried for centuries. Touch it, and you will feel tiny holes in the stone, like a dry sponge. This is San Giovanni dei Fiorentini, the church of the Florentines in Rome.

The name tells you its purpose. This is a national church, built for a community of foreigners living in the city. In Renaissance Rome, Florentines were bankers, merchants, and artists. Many of them lived and worked in this neighbourhood, close to the river bend. They wanted a church of their own, dedicated to Saint John the Baptist. He is the patron saint of Florence, so the choice was a matter of pride as much as faith.

Look back along the street behind you. Via Giulia is one of the straightest streets in old Rome. It was laid out in the early sixteenth century under Pope Julius the Second, the same pope who commissioned Michelangelo's Sistine ceiling. The street was meant to be a grand, orderly route through a tangled medieval city. This church stands at its head, like a full stop at the end of a long sentence.

The real push for the building came from a Florentine pope. Leo the Tenth was born into the Medici family, the great banking dynasty of Florence. Under his rule, the Florentines in Rome felt they had a powerful friend. A design competition was held. Some of the most gifted architects of the age took part. Jacopo Sansovino, a Florentine sculptor and architect, won the early commission.

Here is the first surprise. Michelangelo himself drew plans for this church. He imagined a building with a circular, central plan — a shape that radiates out from one point, like a flower. His drawings still survive. But money ran short, and his design was never built. So the church you see is not Michelangelo's vision. It is the work of several hands, spread across more than a century.

That long building process shows if you know where to look. Antonio da Sangallo the Younger, another leading Renaissance architect, worked on the project. Later, Giacomo della Porta continued it. Then, in the early seventeenth century, Carlo Maderno completed the dome. Maderno is the same architect who designed the façade of Saint Peter's Basilica. Each man inherited the problems of the one before, including the difficult ground so close to the river.

The façade in front of you came much later still. It was designed by Alessandro Galilei and finished in the 1730s. By then, the interior had stood without a proper front for generations. Galilei was a Florentine, which suited the church well. His façade is calm and controlled, with columns and a triangular pediment. It belongs to the eighteenth century, when architects were stepping back from the drama of the Baroque toward something more restrained.

Now step inside. The interior is wide and bright, with a nave flanked by side chapels. Each chapel was paid for by a wealthy family, often Florentine. These families competed quietly through marble, paintings, and gilded frames. Walk slowly and notice how the styles shift from one chapel to the next. You are looking at decades of taste, side by side, like pages in an album.

Walk toward the high altar. This is the chapel of the Falconieri family. Its design is linked to Pietro da Cortona, a painter and architect of the Roman Baroque, and it was completed by Francesco Borromini. At its centre is a marble group showing the Baptism of Christ, carved by Antonio Raggi. Watch how the white figures seem to move. Saint John lifts his hand, and Christ bends his head beneath the water.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it completely. Francesco Borromini is buried in this church. He was one of the most daring architects in Rome's history, famous for curving walls and restless geometry. He died in 1667, by his own hand, after a long struggle with dark moods. Because of the way he died, his burial was a delicate matter. He was laid to rest in the tomb of Carlo Maderno, his relative and early teacher.

Think about that for a moment. Two of the men who shaped Baroque Rome lie together beneath the floor of this church. Maderno finished the dome above you. Borromini helped finish the altar in front of you. Their work and their remains share the same space. For a building made by so many architects, it feels fitting that two of them chose to stay.

The church holds another curious object. It keeps a reliquary that tradition associates with Saint Mary Magdalene. Treat this as a matter of devotion rather than documented history, as with many relics in Rome. In recent years the parish has also become known for something gentle and modern. It has welcomed animals, and locals have brought their dogs to blessings here. It is a small, human detail in a grand Renaissance space.

Before you leave, walk back out onto the steps. Let your eyes adjust to the light. To one side, the Tiber runs green and slow behind its high stone embankments. Ahead, Via Giulia stretches away between old palaces and ivy-covered walls. Somewhere a scooter passes, and a bell may ring from another church across the water. Take a last look at the travertine — still warm, still full of small holes — and carry this quiet corner with you as you walk on.

Practical tips

Walk the full length of Via Giulia to arrive here.
Dress modestly; shoulders and knees should be covered.
Stay quiet near the altar during services or prayer.
Pair your visit with a stroll along the Tiber embankment.
Bring a small coin for lighting the chapel paintings.
