The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, there is traffic, a scooter, maybe a dog barking from a balcony. In front of you, a line of brick arches crosses the grass. The bricks are a warm, dusty orange, worn soft at the edges. Some are dark with damp. Others have little tufts of weed growing from the joints. Touch one if you can reach it. It feels rough and slightly warm in the sun. This is the Aqua Alexandrina, and it has stood in this valley for roughly eighteen centuries.

Here is the first surprise. This was the last of the great aqueducts built for ancient Rome. By the time it was finished, the city already had about ten major aqueducts. Some of them were already several centuries old. So this one did not arrive in a thirsty city. It arrived in a city that already had more water than almost anywhere else in the ancient world. It was a late addition, and that tells us something about the age that built it.

The aqueduct takes its name from the emperor Alexander Severus. He ruled in the early third century, roughly from 222 to 235. He came to power as a teenager, and historians often describe his mother, Julia Mamaea, as a powerful presence behind the throne. Most accounts date the aqueduct to around 226. In those years, Rome was still rich and confident. But the empire was close to a long period of crisis, with short reigns, civil wars and pressure on the frontiers. This brick line in front of you comes from just before that storm.

Why build another aqueduct at all? The main answer is baths. Alexander Severus rebuilt and enlarged an older bath complex in the Campus Martius, the flat district near the river where the Pantheon stands today. Those baths had first been built under Nero. After the rebuilding, people knew them as the Baths of Alexander. A large public bath needed huge, steady volumes of water for its pools, its steam rooms and its fountains. This aqueduct was built, at least in large part, to feed that complex.

Now think about where the water started. The springs lay to the east of Rome, out in the countryside towards the ancient town of Gabii. From there, the channel ran for more than twenty kilometres towards the city. Most of that distance was underground or close to the surface. Roman engineers did not use pumps for this kind of work. They used gravity alone. The channel had to fall very gently and very evenly, mile after mile, so the water kept moving but never rushed.

That is why the arches appear here. In this part of the route, the ground dips into a shallow valley. If the builders had followed the land, the water would have dropped and then had to climb again, and gravity does not allow that. So they lifted the channel onto arches. The arches carried the water level across the low ground. Look along the top of the line. That flat, steady crest is the whole point of the structure. The ground below goes up and down. The water above did not.

Look closely at the building method. The core is Roman concrete, a mix of lime, volcanic ash and rubble. The outside is faced with brick. This brick-faced concrete was the standard Roman technique by the third century. It was fast, strong and cheap compared with cut stone. In some places you can see where the facing bricks have fallen away. There, the rough grey core shows through, like the inside of a cake. That broken surface shows you how the whole thing was made.

Here is the second surprise. You are not standing in an archaeological zone fenced off from daily life. You are in a residential district of modern Rome. The neighbourhood around you is even called Alessandrino, after the aqueduct. Apartment blocks rise on both sides. Children kick footballs near the piers. People walk dogs along paths that follow the old water line. Few places show so clearly how Rome simply grew around its past, instead of clearing it away.

The arches also had a long afterlife. After the fall of the western empire, the great aqueducts of Rome slowly failed. Some were cut during sieges. Others fell into disrepair as the population shrank. For centuries, much of the city drew water from the river and from wells. Then, in the late sixteenth century, Pope Sixtus the Fifth built a new aqueduct, the Acqua Felice. Its engineers drew water from the same eastern spring area, and its course followed this ancient line in places. So in a sense, this old water route came back to life.

As you walk along the arches, notice how uneven they are. Some stand tall and nearly complete. Others are stumps, only a few courses of brick above the grass. In some stretches there are gaps where whole sections have gone. Over the centuries, people took bricks for building, and earthquakes and weather did the rest. Each missing arch is a small story of reuse. Somewhere in this part of Rome, there are probably farmhouses and walls that contain bricks from this aqueduct.

Take a moment to think about the water itself. Roman writers praised some aqueducts for cold, clear water, and criticised others for cloudy or warm supplies. This channel mainly served baths and fountains, not only drinking tables, so its quality mattered less than its volume. But imagine the sound. Inside the channel above your head, a steady stream moved day and night. It flowed for generations, quietly, without anyone in the baths ever seeing where it came from.

There is also a lesson in scale here. The Roman aqueduct system was one of the largest public works of the ancient world. Hundreds of kilometres of channels brought water into one city. This line is only one thread in that network, and not even the most famous one. Yet it still crosses a modern neighbourhood with confidence. The builders could not have imagined tram lines, satellite dishes or traffic lights. Still, their work has outlived almost everything that stood around it in their own time.

Before you leave, stand back a little so you can see several arches in a row. Notice how the light falls through the openings and lays rectangles of sun on the grass. Notice the new buildings framed inside the old curves. Listen again to the city around you — the cars, the voices, the birds in the trees. Eighteen centuries ago, this place was quiet farmland, and water ran above it towards the baths of an emperor. Today the water is gone, but the line is still here, and you are walking beside it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the park paths that follow the arch line.
Come in late afternoon for low light through the arches.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven grass and gravel.
Bring water, as shade near the arches is limited.
Combine your visit with a stroll through the Alessandrino quarter.
