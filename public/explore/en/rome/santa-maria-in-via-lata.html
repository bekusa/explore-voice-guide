The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the pavement of Via del Corso. Scooters hum past. Shop doors open and close. Footsteps move in both directions on the narrow sidewalk. Then look up, and the noise seems to drop away. In front of you rises a pale stone front with deep shadows behind its columns. This is Santa Maria in Via Lata. Many people walk past it every day without turning their heads. Today, you will not be one of them.

Start with the name, because it holds a clue. Via Lata means the broad street in Latin. In ancient Rome, this was the city stretch of the Via Flaminia, the great road that ran north out of the capital. The modern Corso follows almost the same line. So the church takes its name from the road under your feet. For roughly two thousand years, people have walked, ridden and marched along this route.

Later, the street gained a new name. In the Renaissance and after, the Roman Carnival held horse races along this long, straight road. The Italian word for race is corsa, and the street became the Corso. Picture riderless horses running down the stone, with crowds pressed against the buildings on both sides. Today the racing is gone. But the long, straight line of the street is still the same, and you can see it in both directions from here.

Now look closely at the front of the church. It was designed in the mid-seventeenth century by Pietro da Cortona. He was both a painter and an architect, and he was one of the leading figures of the Roman Baroque. The Baroque was a style of drama, movement and light, and it came after the calmer balance of the Renaissance. Cortona did not want a flat wall here. He built something closer to a stage set made of stone.

Notice the two levels. On the ground floor, there is a portico, a covered porch held up by columns. Above it, there is a second level that is also open, like a loggia, which is a gallery open to the air. Behind the columns, the space is dark. In front of them, the stone is bright. That contrast gives the façade its depth. On a sunny afternoon, the shadows move as the light changes, and the building seems to change with them.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Look at the very top, at the triangular pediment. In the middle of it, an arch pushes upward and breaks into the triangle. It is a small act of rule-breaking. A classical architect would keep the triangle whole. Cortona opens it up, so the arch seems to rise through the roofline. Once you see it, the whole façade feels as if it is reaching up.

This new front was part of a larger plan for the street. In the same period, the papacy worked to make the Corso straighter and wider. Pope Alexander the Seventh, who supported this façade, had a strong interest in shaping Rome's streets and squares. The church was meant to be seen as you moved along the road, not only from directly in front. Walk a few steps along the pavement and look back. You will notice how the columns line up and move as you walk.

Now for the second surprise. The church you see is only the top layer. Below street level, there are the remains of a large ancient Roman building. Archaeologists have studied these lower levels, and they belong to a great structure with long rows of arches. In the early Christian centuries, a diaconia was set up here. A diaconia was a church welfare centre that gave food and help to the poor of the city. So this place served the hungry long before it had a Baroque face.

The lower levels also carry a strong tradition. Locals say that Saint Paul lived here while he was in Rome under guard, waiting for his trial. Some versions of the story add that Saint Luke, the Gospel writer, stayed with him and wrote here. These are traditions, handed down over many centuries, and not proven history. The Bible says that Paul spent two years in Rome under guard. Whether those years passed in this exact spot, no one can say for certain. Still, the story has drawn pilgrims here for generations.

Step inside, if the doors are open. After the bright street, your eyes need a moment. The interior is calmer than the façade suggests. It has a central nave, which is the long main hall of the church, with an aisle on each side. Look for the coloured marble, the gilded details and the paintings in the side chapels. Listen for a moment as well. The traffic sound drops to a low murmur behind the thick walls.

Your eyes will move toward the high altar. Here the church keeps an old icon of the Virgin Mary, a painted image venerated by Romans for centuries. The altar around it is often attributed to Gian Lorenzo Bernini, the great sculptor and architect of Baroque Rome. Notice how the frame of stone and colour leads your gaze toward the small painted face at its centre. The Baroque artists used all that richness to focus attention on one sacred image.

As you come back out, look at the buildings on either side. The church stands right beside the great Palazzo Doria Pamphilj, one of the largest noble palaces in the city. It still holds a famous private art collection. Church and palace share this block of the Corso, a reminder of how closely faith, power and family wealth lived together in papal Rome. A short walk south takes you to Piazza Venezia. A short walk north takes you toward the shopping streets.

Before you move on, take one more look at the façade from across the street. See the shadows behind the columns, and the small arch breaking through the top. Under your feet, the ancient road still runs north, as it has for two thousand years. Below the church, ancient arches stay hidden in the dark. When you walk away down the Corso, glance back once. The building does not ask for attention. But now you know what it holds.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the afternoon when sun deepens the portico shadows.
Cross the Corso to see the whole façade at once.
Ask on site whether the underground levels are open.
Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees, before entering.
Combine with Palazzo Doria Pamphilj next door.
