The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The roar of water fills the small square, so loud that people lean close to hear each other. Coins click against stone. Someone laughs as a coin flies over a shoulder. Now lift your eyes above the foam and the marble figures. Behind all of that motion sits a tall, pale wall of windows and columns. That wall is Palazzo Poli, the palace that carries the Trevi Fountain on its face.

Most visitors never learn the building's name. They come for the fountain, take their photographs, and leave. But the fountain and the palace are one structure. Water and architecture were designed together. The fountain does not stand in front of the palace. It grows out of it. The central arch, the columns, and the statues all belong to the palace front. Palazzo Poli is the stage, and the fountain is the performance.

The palace takes its name from the Conti family of Poli, a noble Roman line who held it for a long period. Like many Roman palaces, it grew in stages. Older houses were joined, rebuilt, and refaced over time. When the city decided to give this water a grand new ending, the palace front became the chosen backdrop. Part of the older building was reshaped so the fountain could fill the centre of the facade. You can still see the plain palace wings on each side.

The water itself is far older than the palace. It arrives through the Aqua Virgo, an aqueduct built under Agrippa, the general and close ally of the emperor Augustus, in 19 BCE. The Aqua Virgo still feeds this fountain today. That means the water in front of you follows a route that Romans have used for about two thousand years. Few things in any city carry that kind of continuity.

The name Virgo, meaning maiden, comes from a story. According to tradition, a young girl showed thirsty Roman soldiers where to find the spring. Look high on the right side of the facade. A carved relief shows this scene, with the girl pointing the way. On the left, a matching relief shows Agrippa approving the plans for the aqueduct. Together, these two panels tell the origin story of the water below them.

The fountain you see belongs to the eighteenth century. In the 1730s, Pope Clement the Twelfth held a design competition. The architect Nicola Salvi won the commission. He spent the rest of his life on the project, but he died before it was finished. Other hands completed the work, and the fountain was finally inaugurated in 1762, under Pope Clement the Thirteenth. Three decades separate the first plans from the opening.

Now look at the centre. A powerful figure stands in a shell-shaped chariot inside the main arch. This is Oceanus, the god who personified the great sea surrounding the world. Below him, two Tritons, half man and half fish, guide two sea horses. One horse rears and struggles. The other moves more calmly. Many guides explain this pair as the two moods of the sea, wild and peaceful. Notice how different the two animals feel.

On either side of Oceanus, two quieter statues stand in niches. One represents Abundance. The other represents Health, sometimes called Salubrity. These figures speak to the purpose of the water. Clean water brought wealth and health to the city. In the eighteenth century, this was not decoration only. It was a public message from the popes about what they gave to Rome. Look up again and you will see the papal coat of arms crowning the top of the facade.

Here is a story many visitors miss. On the right side of the fountain, near the edge, sits a large carved stone vase, shaped a little like a cup. Romans call it the ace of cups, after the playing card. Legend has it that a barber with a shop nearby kept criticising Salvi's work. Salvi, the story goes, placed the vase there to block the barber's view. It is folklore, not documented history, but locals still enjoy telling it.

And there is a second surprise behind those windows. Palazzo Poli is not empty. Part of the palace is home to the Istituto Centrale per la Grafica, a national institute for prints and engraving. Its collections include historic copper plates, among them plates by Giovanni Battista Piranesi, the eighteenth century artist famous for his dramatic etchings of Roman ruins. The institute sometimes holds exhibitions inside the palace, so the building above the crowds is also a working home for art on paper.

The fountain has its own modern legend too. In 1960, Federico Fellini filmed a famous scene for his film La Dolce Vita here. The actress Anita Ekberg waded into the basin at night in a black evening dress. That image travelled around the world. Today, entering the water is strictly forbidden. The coin tradition is more relaxed. Locals say that one coin, thrown over the shoulder with your back to the water, means you will return to Rome.

Those coins do not stay in the basin. They are collected regularly, and the money goes to Caritas, a Catholic charity that supports people in need in the city. So a small private wish turns into public help. It is a gentle detail, and it fits the original spirit of the place. This palace front was built to celebrate a gift to the public, and it still collects gifts today.

Before you go, take one more look at the whole facade. Let your eyes travel from the churning water, up past Oceanus, past the reliefs and the windows, to the papal arms at the top. Feel the cool spray if the wind turns. Listen to the water rushing beneath the voices. As you walk away through the narrow streets, the sound will follow you for a while... and then fade, leaving the palace standing quietly behind its fountain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning to see the facade without dense crowds.
Walk to the side wings to see the plain palace walls.
Check for exhibitions inside the Istituto Centrale per la Grafica.
Throw your coin with your right hand over your left shoulder.
Keep a close eye on bags in the packed square.
