The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The noise of the city is far below you here. You hear wind moving through umbrella pines, gravel shifting under shoes, and somewhere a bird calling from a broken wall. The walls around you are made of thin, flat Roman bricks, laid in long patient rows. Many are the colour of dried apricots. Touch one if you can reach it safely. This brick once carried marble, painted plaster, and the private life of an emperor.

You are on the Palatine Hill, standing in what remains of the Domus Augustana. The name means, roughly, the House of the Augustus. That title belonged to every reigning emperor, not only to the first one. So this was not the home of Augustus himself. It was the private wing of a much larger palace, built for the emperor Domitian near the end of the first century. Later rulers lived here for generations.

Here is the first surprise. The English word palace comes from this hill. The Latin name Palatium became shorthand for the emperor's residence. That residence sat here for so long that the name travelled into many European languages. When people talk about a palace anywhere in the world today, they are echoing this exact patch of ground. You are standing at the root of the word.

Domitian's palace had distinct parts. To one side was the Domus Flavia, the official wing. There the emperor held audiences, gave banquets, and showed his power to visitors. The Domus Augustana, where you are now, was the more private side. Beyond it lies a long sunken garden shaped like a racetrack, often called the Stadium. Together these spaces covered much of the hilltop. They turned an old neighbourhood of aristocratic houses into one enormous imperial home.

The Roman poet Martial named the architect as Rabirius. Almost nothing else is known about him. Yet his work survives in the scale of these ruins. Look at how the builders cut into the hill. They did not simply sit a house on top. They carved downward and created two main levels. The upper level sits at the height of the official wing. The lower level drops dramatically toward the valley below, on the side facing the Circus Maximus.

The structure you see is mostly brick-faced concrete. This was the great Roman building technique. Workers poured a strong concrete core and faced it with brick on both sides. Then they covered the brick with marble panels or painted plaster. Today the decoration is almost all gone. Only the skeleton remains. That is why the ruins look warm and earthy, when the emperor saw cool, polished stone in many colours.

If you can look down into the lower level, find the sunken courtyard. Archaeologists call it the lower peristyle, meaning a courtyard surrounded by columns. At its centre is a large basin. It once held water, and its shapes are carefully designed, with curving forms around a central platform. Picture the light bouncing off that water onto the surrounding walls. On hot Roman afternoons, this was a cool and quiet heart for the household.

Rooms open off this courtyard in careful, symmetrical patterns. Some are rectangular. Some have curved walls or niches for statues. The builders loved geometry. They played with octagons, half-circles, and domed spaces, long before such forms became common elsewhere. Walking around the upper edge, notice how one room shape leads into another. It feels less like a house and more like a series of experiments in space and light.

Now a darker story. Domitian was a suspicious ruler, and his reign ended in 96 when he was assassinated in his palace. The Roman writer Suetonius tells a striking tale about his fear. He says the emperor had the walls of his walkways lined with a shining polished stone. In that reflective surface, Domitian could see anyone approaching from behind. Whether every detail is true, the story shows the mood of a man who trusted no one.

The palace did not die with him. Emperor after emperor lived on this hill for centuries. They altered rooms, added baths, and repaired what earlier rulers had built. That long use is part of why the ruins are so complicated to read. Walls from different periods sit side by side, sometimes on top of one another. Archaeologists still study these layers to separate one building phase from the next.

When the empire faded, the hill changed again. Gardens, vineyards, and private villas grew over the ancient remains. Much of the marble was carried away and reused in churches and houses across the city. In later centuries, collectors dug here for statues. Systematic excavation came much later. The quiet building near the upper level now houses the Palatine Museum, which holds sculpture and fragments found on this hill.

Take a moment at the edge facing the valley. Below lies the long, open shape of the Circus Maximus, where chariot races once drew huge crowds. The emperor's household could look down on that spectacle from these heights. Today it is a grassy oval with joggers and dog walkers. The distance between you and that field is the distance between ruler and people. Rome built it directly into the landscape.

Before you move on, look again at the brick under your hand or at your feet. Each small brick was made, carried, and laid by a worker whose name is lost. Above you, the pines move in the wind, just as they move in old paintings of this hill. As you walk away, listen for the city returning below. The traffic, the voices, the bells. Rome is still living at the foot of its first palace.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for cooler air and softer light.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and uneven ground.
Bring water, as shade on the hilltop is limited.
Combine your visit with the nearby Palatine Museum.
View the lower courtyard from the upper level railings.
