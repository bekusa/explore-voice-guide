The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Via delle Quattro Fontane hums behind you, but it fades as you walk up the gentle slope toward the palace. Gravel crunches underfoot. Ahead, a wide facade of warm, honey-coloured stone opens into rows of tall arched windows, stacked like a theatre set. In the early afternoon, the light falls straight into those arches. It makes the whole front look less like a wall and more like a screen of light and shadow.

This is Palazzo Barberini, one of the great family palaces of Baroque Rome. Baroque is the dramatic, theatrical style that shaped the city in the seventeenth century. Big gestures, curving lines, art designed to move you emotionally. This building was made to impress, and it still does. But behind its calm proportions there is a story of ambition, rivalry, and a pope who wanted the whole city to know his family name.

That pope was Maffeo Barberini, who took the name Urban the Eighth when he was elected in 1623. His family came from Tuscany and had grown rich through trade. Once Maffeo reached the papal throne, the Barberini rose fast. Nephews became cardinals. Money and land followed. In the late 1620s, the family acquired this site on the edge of the old city, and work on a new palace began. It was meant to be a home, but also a statement.

The project brought together three remarkable architects. Carlo Maderno, an older master who had worked on the front of Saint Peter's Basilica, drew up the first plans. When he died in 1629, the young Gian Lorenzo Bernini took charge. Working beside him was Francesco Borromini, Maderno's relative and assistant. Here is the surprise: these two men later became the most famous rivals in Roman architecture. In this building, for a few years, they worked side by side.

You can still see their two personalities in the staircases. Bernini's staircase, on the left side as you face the palace, is square and grand. It rises in broad, steady flights, with a strong sense of order. Borromini's staircase, on the right, is completely different. It is an oval spiral, winding upward around an open centre. Many visitors use only one of them. If you can, walk both, and feel how differently each one moves your body.

Stand at the bottom of Borromini's oval stair and look up. Paired columns curve around the opening, climbing like the inside of a shell. The light comes from above and slides down the pale stone. It is elegant, but also slightly restless, which is very typical of Borromini. Bernini preferred clarity and drama. Borromini loved geometry and surprise. Rome would soon be filled with their rival churches, but the argument started, quietly, here.

Now look for the bees. They are everywhere in this palace — carved in stone, painted on ceilings, worked into decoration. Bees were the emblem of the Barberini family. According to a popular family story, the original coat of arms showed horseflies, and the family later changed them to bees, which suggested hard work and sweetness. Whatever the exact history, the bees became a kind of signature. Once you notice them, you will find them across Rome, on fountains and churches too.

The greatest burst of family pride is upstairs, in the main salon. Lift your eyes to the ceiling. Pietro da Cortona painted this huge fresco in the 1630s. It shows Divine Providence seated high in the clouds, surrounded by figures who seem to fly beyond the frame. In the centre, a wreath carries three giant bees. The message is simple and bold: heaven itself has chosen the Barberini. Lie back on one of the benches, if there is space, and let your eye travel.

Not every Roman admired this family. The Barberini took bronze from the ancient portico of the Pantheon, and Romans responded with a famous joke. In Latin it runs: what the barbarians did not do, the Barberini did. The line was a play on the family name, and it spread through the city as street satire. It is a reminder that this beautiful palace was paid for with power, and that power always had critics.

The family fortunes did not last forever. Over the following centuries, the palace passed through heirs and changing times. In the twentieth century, the Italian state acquired the building, and it became home to the National Gallery of Ancient Art. Here, ancient means older art, roughly from the thirteenth to the eighteenth century. For decades, part of the palace was also used by the Italian army as an officers' club, so the full museum opened only gradually.

Today the rooms hold paintings that many people know from books. Look for Raphael's portrait known as La Fornarina, a young woman with a bare shoulder and an armband carrying the painter's name. Find Caravaggio's Judith Beheading Holofernes, shocking even now, with its dark background and cold, focused faces. There is also a portrait of King Henry the Eighth of England by Hans Holbein, broad and heavy, staring straight at you. Take time with just a few works rather than rushing through every room.

As you move through the galleries, notice the building itself between the paintings. The floors change from room to room. Windows frame views of the garden at the back, where trees soften the noise of the city. Doorways line up in long axes, so you can sometimes see through five or six rooms at once. That alignment was intentional. It let visitors feel the scale of the family's wealth with every step they took.

When you leave, walk down toward Piazza Barberini at the bottom of the hill. In the centre of the square stands Bernini's Triton Fountain, a sea god blowing water through a shell, held up by dolphins. Look closely at the base, and yes, there are bees again. The palace, the square, and the fountain were all part of the same family story, written into stone across this corner of Rome.

Before you go, turn back for one last look at the facade. Those arched windows are catching the light just as they did for the cardinals and princes who once climbed these stairs. The bees are still watching from the stone. The traffic is still humming below. Take a slow breath here, between the garden and the street, and notice how quiet it feels for a moment... before the city pulls you back in.
