The guide

The story

Stand in Piazza di Montecitorio and look up. A tall shaft of red granite rises from the paving, its sides cut with rows of small Egyptian figures. Birds, eyes, reeds, seated gods. Behind it, the pale facade of Palazzo Montecitorio fills one side of the square. Scooters hum past. Politicians and journalists gather on the steps. Notice the obelisk's shadow on the stones. Two thousand years ago, that shadow was the whole point.

This obelisk is the surviving heart of what Romans called the Solarium Augusti, or Horologium Augusti. In plain words, it was the sundial of Augustus, the first Roman emperor. It did not stand here originally. It stood a short walk to the north, in the open fields of the Campus Martius. The Campus Martius, or Field of Mars, was a wide plain beside the Tiber. Augustus turned much of it into a showcase of monuments.

The stone itself is far older than Rome's empire. It was carved in Egypt for a pharaoh named Psammetichus the Second, in the sixth century before Christ. It stood at Heliopolis, an ancient centre of sun worship near modern Cairo. Then history turned. In 31 BCE, Augustus defeated Antony and Cleopatra. The following year, Egypt became a Roman province. Soon afterwards, Egyptian obelisks began their long journey across the Mediterranean to Rome.

Moving one was a serious engineering problem. These are single blocks of stone, many tonnes in weight, more than twenty metres long. Special ships carried them. Teams of workers dragged them, raised them, and set them upright. Augustus brought this one to Rome and dedicated it to the Sun. The inscription on its base announced the reason clearly. Egypt had been brought under the power of the Roman people. The obelisk was a trophy of conquest — and also an offering to the sky.

Here is the part most visitors miss. Augustus did not simply display the obelisk. He used it as a gnomon. A gnomon is the upright part of a sundial, the part that casts the shadow. Around its base, builders laid a pavement of travertine stone. Into that pavement they set lines and letters of bronze. As the sun moved, the obelisk's long shadow travelled across these markings. A whole public square became a scientific instrument.

We know about this partly from Pliny the Elder, a Roman writer of the first century. In his Natural History, he describes the obelisk and its pavement. He names the mathematician behind the design, Novius Facundus. According to Pliny, Facundus placed a gilded ball on the obelisk's tip. The ball gave the shadow a sharp, clear point. Without it, the shadow of a pointed stone would blur at the edge.

Pliny adds a striking detail. By his own time, only a few decades later, the instrument no longer worked correctly. He offers several possible reasons. Perhaps the sun's course had shifted. Perhaps the ground had moved, through earthquakes or the floods of the Tiber. Perhaps the obelisk had settled on its foundations. Even in the ancient world, people noticed that their great clock had drifted.

In the twentieth century, a German archaeologist named Edmund Buchner proposed a bold idea. He argued that the sundial was vast, covering much of this district. He also linked it to the Ara Pacis, the Altar of Augustan Peace, which stands nearby. In his reconstruction, on the emperor's birthday in September, the shadow pointed toward the altar. Sun, emperor, and peace were tied together in one geometric gesture. It is an elegant theory.

But scholars still debate it. Excavations under buildings on Via di Campo Marzio uncovered part of the ancient pavement. There, archaeologists found bronze letters set in travertine. Some letters spell Greek words for signs of the zodiac. Others seem to refer to seasonal winds. Many experts now think the monument was mainly a meridian line, rather than a full clock for every hour. A meridian line marks the sun's position at midday through the year. So the shadow measured seasons and the length of the year itself.

Whatever its exact form, the obelisk eventually fell. As ancient Rome declined, the Campus Martius flooded, filled with earth, and slowly disappeared under new streets. The great shaft broke and lay buried for centuries. It was found again in the eighteenth century, cracked and damaged. Repairing it took time and skill. Restorers are said to have used red granite from the ruined Column of Antoninus Pius to patch the broken sections.

In 1792, under Pope Pius the Sixth, the obelisk rose again here, in Piazza di Montecitorio. It was a typical move for papal Rome. Popes liked to raise ancient obelisks in public squares, often topped with Christian or papal symbols. This one gained a bronze sphere at its tip. That sphere is a later addition, but it quietly recalls the gilded ball that once sharpened the ancient shadow. Look up and you can still see it catching the light.

Today the setting carries a new kind of power. The building behind the obelisk houses Italy's Chamber of Deputies, the lower house of parliament. So a monument to Roman conquest now stands before modern Italian democracy. Security staff watch the square. Television crews often film here. The Pantheon is only a few minutes' walk away, and the narrow streets between them follow paths that Romans have used for centuries.

As you walk north toward Via di Campo Marzio, remember what lies beneath your feet. Several metres down, below shops and apartments, bronze letters still sit in their ancient stone. Very few visitors ever see them, because the site is rarely open. Yet they are there. The ground level of ancient Rome is far below the modern city, sealed under layers of river silt and rebuilding.

Before you leave, look once more at the obelisk's shadow on the paving. It moves slowly, almost too slowly to notice. Watch for a minute and you will see it shift. That simple movement is what Augustus and his mathematician wanted to capture. A pharaoh's stone, an emperor's clock, a pope's monument... and now, quietly, a shadow crossing a modern Roman square in the afternoon light.
