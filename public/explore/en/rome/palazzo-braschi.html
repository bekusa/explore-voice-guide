The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the narrow corner where the street opens towards Piazza Navona. Feel the worn paving under your feet, rounded smooth by centuries of shoes and cart wheels. Listen to the fountains just beyond, the scrape of café chairs, the murmur of tour groups. And above you, a long, calm wall of pale stone and stucco rises, its windows set in steady rows. This is Palazzo Braschi... a palace that arrived late to the party, and stayed.

Most of the great family palaces in this part of Rome went up during the Renaissance and the Baroque. This one is different. It was built at the very end of the eighteenth century, for the family of Pope Pius the Sixth, whose family name was Braschi. The pope's nephew, Luigi Braschi Onesti, was the intended owner. Historians often call it the last palace in Rome built for a pope's relatives. It closes a long chapter in the city's story.

That chapter has a name — nepotism. The word comes from the Italian for nephew. For centuries, a new pope would often raise his relatives to wealth and power. They received titles, land, and grand houses near the centre of the city. Many of the palaces around you were born this way. Palazzo Braschi is the final example. Soon after it was built, Europe changed, and the old system began to fall apart.

In fact, history caught up with the building almost at once. In the late 1790s, French revolutionary armies entered Rome. Pius the Sixth was taken from the city as a prisoner, and he died in exile in France. The palace was still unfinished when the storm arrived. So this confident façade, with its orderly windows, carries a quiet irony. It was built for a family whose power was already ending.

The design is usually credited to the architect Cosimo Morelli. Look at the restraint of the exterior. There are no twisting Baroque curves here, no crowded sculpture. Instead you see clean lines, regular spacing, and a sense of balance. This reflects the taste of the late eighteenth century, when architects looked back towards the calm order of ancient Rome. The building fills an irregular plot, so its sides face several different streets and squares.

Now, before you go inside, turn to the corner of the building. There, on a pedestal, stands a battered marble figure. Its face is almost gone, its arms broken away. This is Pasquino, one of the famous talking statues of Rome. It is a fragment of an ancient sculpture, probably a Hellenistic work — that is, from the Greek tradition after Alexander the Great. It was found nearby and set up here around the start of the sixteenth century.

Here is the surprise. For hundreds of years, Romans attached anonymous notes to this statue. The notes mocked popes, cardinals, and powerful families, often in sharp, witty verse. Speaking openly against the authorities was dangerous, so people let the statue speak for them. These notes became known as pasquinades, and the word passed into other languages. Look closely at the base. Locals still leave paper messages here today, and the tradition continues.

There is something fitting about this pairing. A palace built to display a pope's family sits beside the statue that mocked popes for centuries. Pasquino was here long before the Braschi family. In a way, he has outlasted them too. When you hear the rustle of paper taped to his pedestal, you are hearing one of the oldest voices of free speech in the city.

After the Braschi family, the palace entered a new life. Once Rome became the capital of a united Italy in the later nineteenth century, the state acquired the building. It served as government offices for many decades. During the fascist period of the twentieth century, it was used by the party. After the Second World War, displaced families found shelter in its rooms. The grand halls became, for a time, ordinary homes.

Since the early 1950s, Palazzo Braschi has housed the Museo di Roma — the Museum of Rome. Its subject is the city itself. The collections focus on Rome from the Middle Ages to the twentieth century, through paintings, drawings, prints, sculpture, and furniture. It is a place to see how artists and residents pictured the streets you are walking through. Exhibitions change regularly, so the rooms you visit may differ from one season to the next.

Inside, one feature greets nearly every visitor — the grand staircase. It is wide and slow to climb, designed for ceremony rather than speed. Notice the dark, polished columns along the way, and the way light falls from above onto the stone. Take your time on the steps. The staircase was built to make arrival feel important, and it still does. Many people pause halfway up just to look around.

From the upper floors, find a window that faces Piazza Navona. This is one of the quiet pleasures of the visit. Below you, the long oval of the square stretches out. Its shape follows the ancient stadium of the Emperor Domitian, which once stood on this exact ground. From up here you can see the fountains, the church domes, and the crowds moving like a slow tide.

When you step back outside, take one last look. The pale wall of the palace, the broken marble face of Pasquino, the papers fluttering at his feet. Power built the walls, and ordinary Romans gave the corner its voice. Listen again to the fountains and the café chatter as you walk on. You are moving through a city where every corner holds layers... and this one holds more than most.
