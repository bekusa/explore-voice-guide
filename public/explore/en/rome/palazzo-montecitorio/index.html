The guide

The story

Stand in the open square and feel the smooth paving stones under your feet. Above you, a pale facade curves gently, almost like an open book. Pigeons settle on the cornices. Somewhere behind those tall windows, laws for an entire country are being argued over. This is Palazzo Montecitorio, home of the Italian Chamber of Deputies, the lower house of parliament. Before we go inside its story, take a moment to simply look at the shape of the building in front of you.

Notice that the facade is not flat. It bends outward, and it is broken into several angled sections, like panels of a folding screen. This was a deliberate trick. The designer wanted the long front to feel lively when seen from the square, instead of a single heavy wall. As you walk from one side to the other, the light falls differently on each panel. Shadows shift. The building seems to turn slowly with you.

The mind behind this design was Gian Lorenzo Bernini, the great sculptor and architect of Baroque Rome. Baroque, put simply, is the seventeenth-century style of drama, movement, and theatre in stone. Bernini began the palace in the 1650s for the Ludovisi family, who were connected by marriage to Pope Innocent the Tenth. It was meant to be a grand private residence. But history had other plans for it — and the palace waited a long time to become what it is today.

When Pope Innocent the Tenth died in the mid-1650s, the money and the political will disappeared. Work stopped. For decades the half-built palace stood unfinished in the heart of the city. Then, near the end of the seventeenth century, another pope, Innocent the Twelfth, took an interest. He gave the project to the architect Carlo Fontana, who had trained under Bernini. Fontana completed it, but with a new purpose. The private home would become a seat of papal justice.

So here is the first surprise. Long before it held a parliament, this building held courts of law. It became known as the Curia Innocenziana, named after the pope who finished it. Judges, lawyers, and petitioners climbed these steps for papal legal business. Fontana also added the small bell tower you can see on top of the facade. In other words, this square has been a place of public argument and official decisions for more than three centuries.

Now look closely at the lower parts of the facade, near the windows and along the base. You will see stone that looks rough and unfinished, almost like raw rock pushing out of the polished wall. Most visitors walk straight past it. This is one of Bernini's playful touches. It suggests that the palace grows directly out of natural stone, as if the building and the hill were one thing. It is a sculptor's idea, applied to architecture.

Speaking of the hill — the name Montecitorio means, roughly, the hill of Citorio. The origin of that word is uncertain. One popular explanation says the mound formed from debris and earth piled up in ancient times. Another tale links the name to an old Latin phrase about citizens being called to vote here. Scholars debate these stories, so treat them as theories rather than settled fact. Either way, the ground here is slightly raised above the surrounding streets.

The next big change came in the nineteenth century. Italy unified, and in 1871 Rome became the capital of the new kingdom. The government needed a home for its elected deputies, and this former courthouse was chosen. At first, the deputies met in a temporary hall built in the courtyard. It was uncomfortable, and reports describe it as poorly built for such an important role. A permanent solution was needed, and the palace was about to double in size.

In the early twentieth century, the Sicilian architect Ernesto Basile designed a large new extension behind Bernini's front. Basile worked in the Liberty style, which is the Italian name for Art Nouveau — a style of flowing lines, natural motifs, and decorative craft. His wing faces a different square, Piazza del Parlamento, on the far side of the building. Here is the second surprise. If you walk around the block, the palace changes character completely, from Baroque curves to brick and early modern elegance.

Inside Basile's wing is the chamber where the deputies sit, arranged in a semicircle. Above them runs a long painted frieze by the artist Giulio Aristide Sartorio, showing scenes and figures from Italian history and allegory. The hall is lit from above through a decorated glass ceiling. Most people only see this room on television, during heated debates or important votes. On the days when the palace opens to visitors, it is possible to see it with your own eyes.

Now turn your back to the palace and look at the tall red-granite obelisk in the centre of the square. It is far older than anything else around you. It was carved in ancient Egypt for a pharaoh, Psamtik the Second, and brought to Rome by the emperor Augustus. In ancient times it served as the pointer of an enormous sundial in this part of the city, its shadow marking the days and seasons across the ground.

Over the centuries the obelisk fell, broke, and lay buried. It was rediscovered and later raised here in the late eighteenth century, under Pope Pius the Sixth. Look at the paving near its base. A thin metal line runs across the stones, a modern reminder of the obelisk's old job as a measure of time. So, in one small square, you have Egyptian stone, Roman astronomy, Baroque architecture, and a working parliament — all within a few steps.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Watch the security staff at the doors, the journalists waiting with cameras, the officials crossing the square in dark suits. Then lift your eyes to Bernini's bending facade and the clock under its little bell tower. This building was planned as a family palace, finished as a courthouse, and became the house of the nation's voters. As you walk away toward the side streets, notice how quickly the noise of politics fades into ordinary Roman life.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book interior visits in advance through the Chamber's official channels.
Expect airport-style security checks before entering the palace.
Walk around to Piazza del Parlamento to see Basile's wing.
Combine your visit with the Pantheon, a short walk away.
Keep a respectful distance from guards and official entrances.
