The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along the river road behind you, and somewhere close by, water splashes into a stone basin. In front of you, a ring of pale columns rises from the grass. In the low afternoon sun the marble turns the colour of warm honey. This is the Temple of Hercules Victor. It is one of the oldest buildings still standing in Rome, and it is easy to walk past without a second look.

Look first at its shape. Most Roman temples were rectangles, with a grand front porch and steps leading up to a single entrance. This one is a circle. A solid round chamber, called the cella, sits at the centre. It is wrapped by a single ring of slender columns. The design follows Greek models. The effect is light and open, almost like a small pavilion in a garden rather than a house for a god.

Now for the first surprise. Look closely at the stone. Most of ancient Rome was built in brick, concrete, or local volcanic stone. This temple was built mainly in fine marble brought from Greece. Scholars generally date it to the late second century before Christ. That makes it the oldest surviving marble building in the city. The Rome of emperors and gleaming marble forums came later. This small round temple stood here first.

Why would anyone build in such costly imported stone at that time? Rome was expanding fast across the Greek world. Its generals and merchants came home with new wealth and new taste. Greek architecture, Greek art, and even Greek craftsmen followed them. Some historians link the temple to a Greek architect working in Rome in those years. That link is still debated. What is clear is that this building reflects a moment when Roman builders were learning from Greece.

The location matters too. You are standing in what the Romans called the Forum Boarium, the cattle market. In the ancient city this was a busy riverside zone. The Tiber is only a short walk away. Boats unloaded goods here, and livestock was traded on this ground. Merchants met, argued, and made deals. For a god of strength and success, there was no better neighbourhood.

And Hercules had a special bond with this place. According to Roman legend, the hero passed through here with a herd of cattle. A fire-breathing monster named Cacus lived in a cave on the nearby Aventine Hill. It stole some of the animals. Hercules found the hiding place and killed the thief. Romans told this story for centuries, and they honoured Hercules at several shrines around this market. The title Victor simply means the winner.

There is a more everyday side to the story as well. Some ancient evidence connects this temple to Hercules as a protector of olive oil merchants. Olive oil was central to Roman life. It was used for cooking, for lamps, and for washing at the baths. If that connection is correct, a successful trader may have paid for the temple as a thank-offering. The exact patron is still discussed by scholars, so treat this part as likely, not certain.

Now count the columns, if you like. The ring was designed with twenty. Nineteen still stand today. Look at the top of each one. The capitals are carved with curling acanthus leaves, a style called Corinthian, which was the most decorative of the classical orders. Some of these capitals and column sections are later replacements from ancient repairs. If you compare them carefully, you may notice small differences in carving and stone colour.

Here is the second surprise. Lift your eyes to the roof. The original stone beams and upper decoration above the columns are gone. The low, tiled roof you see sits directly on top of the capitals. It does not belong to the ancient design. It comes from much later use. That is why the building looks a little squat. You are seeing a Greek temple wearing a hat from another age.

That later use explains why the temple survived at all. In the Middle Ages it was turned into a Christian church. In later centuries it was known as Santa Maria del Sole, Saint Mary of the Sun. Local tradition tells of an image of the Virgin found in the river that seemed to give off light. As a church, the building was cared for, roofed, and kept in use. Many pagan temples were taken apart for their stone. This one was protected because people still prayed inside.

For a long time, visitors gave the temple the wrong name. Because it is round, many believed it was a temple of Vesta, the goddess of the hearth. Her famous temple in the Roman Forum was also circular. You will still see the old name in many paintings and engravings. Artists from across Europe sketched this building on their travels. Later research showed that it belonged to Hercules instead.

Now turn and take in the square around you. Just nearby stands a second ancient temple. It is rectangular, with a deep porch and Ionic columns, which have simple scroll-shaped tops. It is usually identified with Portunus, a god of harbours. Seeing the two together is rare. One temple is round and one is rectangular, both are from the Republic, and both have stood together beside the river for more than two thousand years. Across the road you can see the bell tower of Santa Maria in Cosmedin, home of the famous Mouth of Truth.

The interior is usually closed, so most visitors see the temple from outside its fence. That is no loss. Walk slowly around the full circle. The light changes on each side, and the columns seem to open and close against one another as you move. Morning sun falls on one side, and the evening sun warms the other. The temple looks different from every angle.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Notice the grass at the base, the soft grey streaks in the marble, and the bright tiles above. Listen again to the fountain and the traffic. Two thousand years ago, cattle, sailors, and oil merchants filled this ground with noise. Today it is quieter, but the ring of Greek stone still stands in the same place by the river.
