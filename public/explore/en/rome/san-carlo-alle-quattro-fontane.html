The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at this crossroads. Traffic hums along the narrow street, scooters slip past the curb, and the stone under your feet is worn smooth. At each of the four corners, water drips into a weathered basin. Now look up at the small church squeezed onto one corner. Its front seems to move. The wall bends inward, swells outward, then bends in again, like a sheet of stone caught in a breeze.

This is San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane. Romans often call it San Carlino, little Saint Charles, because it is so small. The name means Saint Charles at the Four Fountains. It honours Saint Charles Borromeo, a sixteenth-century archbishop of Milan who became a key figure of Catholic reform. And it was the first major independent work of one of the most daring architects Rome has ever known, Francesco Borromini.

First, the fountains themselves. They came before the church, in the late sixteenth century, when Pope Sixtus the Fifth cut long straight roads across the hills of Rome. This junction sits on the Quirinal Hill. Each corner holds a reclining stone figure. Two are river gods, and two are female figures. Look down each of the four streets and you may catch distant views of obelisks and churches. Sixtus planned those long sight lines on purpose.

The church was built for a small Spanish religious order, the Discalced Trinitarians. Discalced simply means barefoot, a sign of their simple life. The order was historically devoted to ransoming Christians held captive. They had little money and an awkward, cramped corner plot. Borromini began with the friars' living quarters and a small cloister in the 1630s. The church itself followed soon after, and the façade came decades later, completed only after the architect's death.

Here is the first surprise. The whole interior is tiny. It is often said that the entire church could fit inside one of the great piers that hold up the dome of Saint Peter's Basilica. Whether or not the comparison is exact, the point stands. Borromini had almost no room. Instead of fighting the cramped space, he turned it into the whole idea of the building.

Step inside, and notice the colour first, or rather the lack of it. Almost everything is white or pale grey. There is very little gold and very little coloured marble, nothing like the gilded churches across the city. Borromini worked mostly in plaster, stucco and light. The plain surfaces let your eye follow the shapes without distraction. This was partly a matter of a tight budget, and partly a matter of style.

Now look at the walls. They never run straight for long. They curve in and out, and the columns are packed closely together around the edge. The floor plan grows from simple geometry, built on triangles and circles combined into a stretched oval. For a religious order named after the Holy Trinity, the triangle is no accident. Three in one appears again and again in the design, if you look for it.

Then raise your eyes to the dome. This is the moment most visitors remember. The oval dome is covered in a honeycomb of carved shapes. Crosses, octagons and hexagons lock together across the surface. Each row of shapes grows smaller as it climbs toward the top. This is a trick of perspective. The shrinking pattern makes the dome look much higher and deeper than it really is.

At the very top sits the lantern, the small opening that lets daylight fall into the church. Look closely there, and you will find a dove, the Christian symbol of the Holy Spirit, set inside a triangle. Light comes down from above and spreads across the white coffers. Hidden windows around the base of the dome add more light. On a bright day the whole ceiling seems to float.

If the cloister is open, do not miss it. This is the second surprise. It is a small courtyard with two levels of columns, and it has no sharp corners. Where you would expect a corner, the walls curve outward instead. In the middle stands a well. It is a quiet, almost domestic space, and it shows the same restless geometry as the church, only gentler and plainer.

Now come back outside and look again at the façade. The lower level curves in, out, and in again. Above the door, in a niche, stands the statue of Saint Charles Borromeo. Look at the angels beside him. Their wings bend together to form an arch over his head. Higher up, angels hold a large oval medallion. The tall bell tower on the corner adds one more twist.

Borromini's life was not easy. He worked in the same years as Gian Lorenzo Bernini, the most celebrated artist in Rome, and the two men were rivals. Bernini was charming and well connected. Borromini was intense, private and difficult. He died in 1667, by his own hand. Just a short walk down the street stands Sant'Andrea al Quirinale, a small oval church by Bernini. Seeing both in one walk is a fine lesson in two different minds.

Before you leave the crossroads, pause on the corner once more. Feel the cool stone of the fountain basin. Listen to the water under the noise of the traffic. Then look back at that bending façade. Nearly four centuries ago, a stubborn architect took a cramped corner and very little money, and he made the walls move. As you walk on, notice how flat the other buildings suddenly seem.
