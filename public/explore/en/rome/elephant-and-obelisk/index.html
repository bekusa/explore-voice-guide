The guide

The story

Listen first. Scooters hum along the narrow lanes, footsteps echo off old stone, and somewhere a waiter calls across the square. Then look up. A small marble elephant stands on a pedestal, ears slightly lifted and trunk turned to one side. On its back it carries a slender Egyptian obelisk, much older than anything else around you. It is a strange pairing at first glance. Stay with it for a few minutes, and it starts to make a kind of quiet sense.

You are standing in Piazza della Minerva, a short walk behind the Pantheon. The square takes its name from the church at your back, Santa Maria sopra Minerva. The name means Saint Mary above Minerva. For centuries people believed the church stood on the ruins of a temple to Minerva, the Roman goddess of wisdom. Scholars now think the temple nearby was dedicated to a different goddess. The old name stayed anyway, and wisdom is the theme of the monument in front of you.

Start with the obelisk itself. It is a genuine ancient Egyptian monument, carved in red granite. Its hieroglyphs connect it to the pharaoh Apries, who ruled in the sixth century before Christ. It probably stood at Sais, a royal city in the Nile Delta. Like many Egyptian obelisks, it later travelled to Rome in antiquity. Romans often used such stones to decorate temples to Egyptian gods like Isis. Then the city changed, the temples fell, and the obelisk disappeared under the ground for many centuries.

It came back to light in the 1660s. Dominican friars were digging in the garden of their convent, right beside the church, and they uncovered it. The pope at that time was Alexander the Seventh, from the powerful Chigi family of Siena. He loved grand public projects, and he wanted the obelisk displayed in the square. For the design he turned to the most famous artist in Rome — Gian Lorenzo Bernini, the sculptor behind so many of the city's fountains and churches.

Bernini did not carve the elephant himself. By this point he was an old master with a large workshop. He produced the design, and his assistant Ercole Ferrata carried out the sculpture. The finished monument was unveiled in 1667. This was a common way of working in Baroque Rome. Baroque here means the dramatic, theatrical style of the seventeenth century, full of motion and surprise. The idea belonged to the master, and skilled hands in his studio shaped the stone.

Why an elephant? The image did not come from nowhere. A famous illustrated book printed in Venice in 1499, called the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili, shows an elephant carrying an obelisk. That strange dream romance was much admired by artists and scholars. For educated Romans, the elephant also had a clear meaning. It stood for strength, patience and intelligence. An animal strong enough to carry heavy wisdom on its back — that was exactly the message the pope wanted.

Now walk around to the pedestal and find the Latin inscription. In simple terms, it says that anyone who sees the carvings of Egyptian wisdom carried by an elephant, the strongest of animals, should understand something. A strong mind is needed to support solid wisdom. It is a sermon in stone, aimed at every passerby. In a square named for the goddess of wisdom, beside a convent of learned friars, the choice was very deliberate.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Look underneath the elephant's belly. You will see a solid stone block filling the space between its legs, under the saddlecloth. Bernini reportedly did not want it. According to a popular story, a Dominican friar insisted that the obelisk could not rest safely on four legs alone. The block was added. Bernini then hid it under a long, heavy saddlecloth, so the support looks like part of the costume.

And now the second surprise, which belongs to legend rather than to documented history. Walk around to the elephant's back end. Notice how its rear points toward the Dominican convent, and its tail sits slightly to one side. Locals say this was Bernini's revenge on the friar who criticised his design. The elephant, in other words, turns its backside to the Dominicans. No one can prove it was intentional. Romans have enjoyed telling the story for centuries all the same.

Romans also gave the statue a nickname. The elephant is small, rounded and a little stocky. People began calling it Pulcin della Minerva, which means something like the little chick of the Minerva. Some versions say the name was really porcino, the little pig, because of its plump body. Either way, the nickname shows real affection. Grand papal monuments in Rome often end up with teasing names from the people who live beside them.

Take a moment for the details on top. At the very peak of the obelisk, you can see a small cluster of mounds crowned by a star. These are symbols from the Chigi family coat of arms, the emblem of Alexander the Seventh. The pope placed his family's mark above the ancient Egyptian stone. It is a quiet reminder of how Roman popes reused the ancient past. They took pagan monuments and claimed them for the Christian city.

The church behind you holds its own weight of history. In 1633, in the Dominican convent next door, Galileo Galilei was made to formally reject his view that the Earth moves around the Sun. So the elephant's message about wisdom stands only steps away from one of history's best-known clashes between science and authority. The monument itself has had hard moments too. In 2016, the tip of one tusk was broken off, and it was later repaired.

Before you go, look once more at the elephant's face. The trunk curls, the eyes are small, and the expression is almost gentle. Behind it, the plain brick front of the church rises over the square. Around you, people sit on the steps, check their maps and drift toward the Pantheon. As you walk away, glance back over your shoulder. The little elephant is still carrying its ancient burden, steady and patient, exactly as Bernini imagined.
