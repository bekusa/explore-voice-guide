The guide

The story

Run your hand along cool white marble and you will feel it before you see it. The edges are soft, worn by almost two thousand years. Faces are chipped. Horses' legs end in rough stone. This block is all that survives of a monument to an emperor. It is a stone base, carved on every side, that once held up a tall column of red granite. That column no longer stands, and the base has travelled far from where it first stood.

Let us begin with the man it honoured. Antoninus Pius ruled Rome in the second century, between the reigns of Hadrian and Marcus Aurelius. His reign is remembered as calm. There were no great wars of conquest and no famous scandals. Historians often describe his time as one of the most peaceful stretches the Roman Empire ever knew. The Senate gave him the name Pius, meaning dutiful. One reason was his loyalty to the memory of Hadrian, the emperor who had adopted him.

When Antoninus died in 161, his two adopted sons took power together. They were Marcus Aurelius, later famous as the philosopher-emperor, and Lucius Verus. Together they raised this column in his memory. They placed it in the Campus Martius, the old Field of Mars. This was a flat stretch of land in the bend of the Tiber, used for military drills, public buildings, and imperial funerals. The column stood close to the place where the emperor's body had been burned on a funeral pyre.

Now picture the column itself. It was not carved with spiralling battle scenes like Trajan's Column. Its shaft was a single, smooth piece of red granite, quarried in Egypt and shipped across the Mediterranean. Moving one solid piece of stone that size was a serious feat of engineering. On top, most scholars believe, stood a statue of the emperor. The plain red shaft rose from the carved white base you see here. The contrast of colours would have been striking in the Roman sun.

Here is the first surprise. For more than a thousand years, the column vanished from view. As Rome shrank after antiquity, the ground level in the Campus Martius rose. Floods, rubble, and new buildings buried the old city layer by layer. The column fell and disappeared beneath the streets. Then, in 1703, builders working near what is now Piazza di Montecitorio struck something huge. They had found the granite shaft and its base, still deep underground.

The discovery excited the city. Popes and architects debated how to raise the shaft and display it again. Several attempts were planned, but the great stone was heavy, awkward, and already damaged. It lay on the ground for decades. A fire later harmed it further. In the end, the shaft was never raised. Its granite was cut up and used to repair the ancient Egyptian obelisk that now stands in Piazza di Montecitorio, in front of the Italian parliament.

That is the second surprise, and few visitors know it. If you walk past that obelisk, you are looking at an Egyptian monument mended with pieces of a Roman column. Stone from Egypt, carved for an emperor, finally returned to stand beside another Egyptian stone. The base, however, was saved as a single work of art. Restorers repaired it, and it was moved to the Vatican, where it is kept today in the Vatican Museums.

Look now at the main face of the base. This scene shows an apotheosis, which means the moment a human being becomes a god. A large winged figure rises upward, carrying the emperor and his wife, Faustina the Elder, into the sky. Faustina had died many years before her husband, but here the couple ascends together. Eagles fly beside them, the bird of Jupiter and a symbol of imperial power. Antoninus holds a sceptre topped with an eagle.

Below them, two figures watch the scene. On one side sits the goddess Roma, the personification of the city, wearing armour and leaning on a shield. On the other side reclines a young man holding an obelisk. He represents the Campus Martius itself, the very field where this monument stood. It is a quiet, clever detail. The sculptors placed the location of the funeral inside the artwork, as if the land itself were saying farewell.

Walk around to the two shorter sides. Here the mood changes completely. These panels show a decursio, a ritual parade performed at imperial funerals. Mounted soldiers ride in a circle around a group of standing soldiers with their banners. You can almost hear the hooves on the packed earth. The riders gallop in a loop, honouring the dead emperor as smoke rose from the pyre nearby.

Now look closely at how these riders are carved, because this is where art historians lean in. The figures do not stand on one shared ground line, as in most classical reliefs. Instead, each group sits on its own small ledge of stone. The scene seems to be viewed from above and from the side at the same time. It is less realistic and more like a diagram. Many scholars see this as an early sign of the flatter, more symbolic style of late Roman and medieval art.

The fourth side carries the dedication, carved in neat Roman capital letters. It records that the monument was set up for the deified Antoninus by his sons. Even if you do not read Latin, notice the spacing and the depth of the letters. Roman inscriptions were designed to be read from a distance, often painted in red to make them clearer. Here, as on so much ancient stone, the paint is long gone and only the shape of the letters remains.

It is worth remembering that what you see is only part of the original monument. The base was made to be seen from street level, beneath a tall red column, outdoors under an open sky. Today it rests in a quieter setting, framed by museum walls. The parade still circles. The emperor still rises toward the gods. But the field, the pyre, and the column that once towered above them are gone.

Before you move on, step back and take in the whole block once more. Follow the wings of the great figure upward, then let your eyes drop to the riders below. Two moods share one stone: the calm ascent of an emperor, and the noise of soldiers honouring him on the ground. As you walk away, listen to the sounds around you now — footsteps on stone, quiet voices. And picture, somewhere across the city, a slim red obelisk carrying a piece of this column still.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Vatican Museums to see the surviving carved base.
Walk all four sides to compare the different reliefs.
Pair your visit with the obelisk in Piazza di Montecitorio.
Do not confuse it with Marcus Aurelius's column nearby.
Book Vatican Museums entry in advance to avoid long queues.
