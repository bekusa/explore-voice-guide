The guide

The story

Start with the façade. Stand in the small square and look up. The front of this palace is covered almost edge to edge in pale stucco: garlands, ribbons, carved shields, and tall figures standing in niches between the windows. In the soft Roman light, the surface looks less like stone and more like icing on a cake. Behind you, the narrow streets near Campo de' Fiori carry the sound of scooters, footsteps, and café cups on saucers.

You are standing in front of Palazzo Spada. It is one of the more surprising buildings in this part of Rome. From the outside it looks like a wealthy family home of the Renaissance. Inside, it holds a small art gallery, a working government office, and one of the cleverest optical tricks in the city. That trick is tucked away in a courtyard and is only a few metres long.

The palace began in the mid-sixteenth century. It was built for Cardinal Girolamo Capodiferro. His name survives in the name of the square in front of you, Piazza Capodiferro. This was a time when powerful churchmen built grand homes near the Tiber to show their status. In a crowded city, a decorated façade worked as a public message. It told everyone who passed exactly how important the owner was.

Look again at the figures in the niches. They represent famous men of ancient Rome, the founders and heroes the Renaissance loved to admire. Around them, the stucco forms a dense pattern of decoration. Very few Roman palaces from this period carry this much ornament on the street front. Most chose plainer, heavier stone. Here the owner chose detail, and almost five centuries later the effect still stops people in the middle of the street.

In 1632, the palace passed to Cardinal Bernardino Spada. His family name has stayed with the building ever since. Spada was a collector and a man of taste. He enlarged the art collection and brought in the leading architects of his day. One of them was Francesco Borromini, a restless and brilliant designer of the Roman Baroque. Baroque is the dramatic style of seventeenth-century Rome, full of curves, movement, and theatrical effects.

Now for the first surprise. Walk through to the courtyard and look for a long, narrow colonnade. It looks like a gallery of columns that runs deep into the distance, towards a garden and a statue at the far end. Your eyes tell you it is a long passage. It is not. The real gallery is only about eight metres long. Most visitors guess it is four or five times longer.

This is Borromini's forced perspective gallery. He worked with a mathematician to make it. The floor rises slowly as it goes back. The ceiling drops. The columns become shorter and closer together. Even the floor pattern narrows. Each change is small, but together they fool the brain. Your mind reads the shrinking shapes as distance, because that is how distance normally looks.

Here is the second surprise. The statue at the end looks like a life-size figure standing far away. In fact, it is much smaller than a person, and it stands quite close. Sometimes a member of staff or another visitor walks into the gallery. As they move towards the end, they seem to grow into a giant. Their head comes close to the ceiling. Then the trick collapses all at once, and the whole space suddenly looks small.

Why build something like this? Part of the answer is fashion. Baroque patrons loved illusion, and a clever trick showed off wealth and learning. But there is also a quieter idea. The gallery reminds us that what looks large may be small. What looks far away may be close. In a palace built to impress, Borromini added a small lesson about appearances and the limits of human sight.

Upstairs, the Galleria Spada holds the family's painting collection. It is a museum, but it feels like a private home. Paintings hang close together, frame beside frame, in the way collectors displayed them centuries ago. The collection includes works by Guercino, Guido Reni, Titian, and both Orazio and Artemisia Gentileschi. Artemisia was a rare successful woman painter in a world run by men. Take your time, because many of the best pictures are small.

The rooms themselves deserve attention too. Look for painted ceilings, old furniture, and walls in deep colours. Natural light comes in from tall windows and falls across gilded frames. The rooms are quiet. You can hear your own steps on the floor. After the noise of the streets outside, the calm is part of the experience. Few large museums in Rome feel this personal.

The palace is not only a museum. Since 1927 it has belonged to the Italian state, and it is the seat of the Council of State. This is one of Italy's highest courts for administrative law. So, while you look at Baroque paintings, lawyers and judges work in other parts of the same building. Much of the palace is closed to visitors for this reason. One closed hall holds an ancient statue of Pompey. According to a popular tradition, Julius Caesar died at the foot of this very statue. Historians cannot prove the story, but it has followed the statue for centuries.

Before you leave, come back to the courtyard. Look once more down Borromini's little colonnade. Now that you know the truth, try to see it as it really is: short, narrow, and close. It is harder than you expect. Your eyes still want the long gallery. Then step out into the square, under the stucco heroes, and back into the narrow streets. Notice how the lanes curve and narrow around you... Rome still plays with your sense of distance, even outside the palace walls.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the courtyard illusion before the upstairs picture gallery.
Watch someone walk down the colonnade to see scale shift.
Combine your visit with nearby Campo de' Fiori market.
Allow extra time for the small, densely hung paintings.
Check ahead, since government use closes parts of the palace.
