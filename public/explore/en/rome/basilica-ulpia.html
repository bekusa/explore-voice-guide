The guide

The story

Stand at the railing and look down. The modern street hums behind you, scooters and buses and footsteps, but below your feet the ground drops away into an older Rome. Grey stone columns rise from a pale floor, some whole, some broken off at the knee. The light falls flat across them at midday and turns warm and gold toward evening. This is the Basilica Ulpia, and for a few generations it was one of the grandest rooms in the Roman world.

First, a small surprise. When you hear the word basilica, you probably picture a church. But this building was never a church. In ancient Rome, a basilica was a public hall — a covered space for law courts, business deals, and official meetings. It was a place for arguments, contracts, and crowds. Only centuries later did Christians borrow this long, aisled shape for their churches. So the word moved from the courtroom to the cathedral... and this hall is one of its grandest early examples.

The name comes from the man who built it. Ulpia was the family name of the emperor Trajan, who ruled in the early second century. Trajan was a soldier emperor. Under his rule, the empire reached its greatest size. He wanted to leave a mark on the city itself, and this hall was part of his answer. It sits inside the Forum of Trajan, the last and largest of the imperial forums that line this valley.

The money came from war. Trajan fought two campaigns against the Dacians, a kingdom roughly where Romania is today. The victories brought enormous wealth back to Rome, including gold and silver from Dacian mines. That treasure paid for this whole complex. The forum was dedicated in the year 112, and the famous column nearby followed a year later. So, in a real sense, the floor below you was paid for on a battlefield far to the north.

The design is usually credited to Apollodorus of Damascus, Trajan's favourite architect and military engineer. He had already built a great bridge across the Danube for the Dacian campaign. Here he worked on a different scale of problem. He had to make a huge public space feel ordered and calm. The plan he chose was long and symmetrical — a wide central nave with double aisles running along each side, and a curved apse at each end.

Try to picture it whole. The building ran across the forum, not along it, like a wall of columns closing off the open square. Inside, rows of columns carried a second storey of galleries. Light came in from high windows above the nave. The floor was laid with coloured marble — yellow stone from North Africa, purple-veined stone from Asia Minor. When the hall was full, the voices of lawyers and merchants must have carried up into the roof.

Now look closely at the grey columns below. Their colour is the clue. Many scholars connect this kind of grey granite to quarries in the Egyptian desert, far from the Nile. Each shaft had to be cut, dragged across sand to the river, shipped down the Nile, and carried across the Mediterranean. Every column in this room crossed a desert and a sea before it stood here. That journey is the second surprise.

The basilica was only one part of a carefully planned sequence. Visitors first entered a great open square with a statue of Trajan on horseback. Then they passed through this hall. Beyond it stood two libraries, one for Greek books and one for Latin books. Between those libraries rose Trajan's Column. You can see it from here — the tall spiral of carved marble, telling the story of the Dacian wars in a continuous band.

The column has its own quiet secret. After Trajan died, his ashes were placed in a chamber in its base. That was unusual. Romans normally kept burials outside the sacred city boundary. Trajan was honoured with an exception. So this whole district, built to celebrate his victories, also became his tomb. The courtroom and the grave stood only a few steps apart.

Some scholars suggest that one of the apses here was linked to the freeing of enslaved people. Under Roman law, a slave could be freed in a formal ceremony, and some ancient texts connect such ceremonies with this forum. The exact details are still debated. But it is worth holding the idea in mind. This hall may have been a place where some people walked in enslaved and walked out free.

The building lasted for centuries, but not forever. As the empire faded, the forum slowly fell apart. Marble was stripped and burned for lime. Columns were carried off for other buildings. Medieval houses and later neighbourhoods rose over the ruins. In the twentieth century, much of that later city was cleared, partly to build the broad avenue behind you, Via dei Fori Imperiali. Excavations since then have uncovered more of the ancient floor.

What you see today is a careful reconstruction of fragments. Several column shafts have been set upright again on their old positions. Pieces of marble paving lie where they fell or where archaeologists placed them. Beyond the ruins, the curved brick front of Trajan's Markets climbs the hillside. Two church domes rise near the column, built centuries after the forum. Layers of Rome sit side by side in one view, and the eye can move between them.

Take a moment before you move on. Look at the line of grey columns and imagine the roof above them, the noise, the marble glowing under the high windows. Then look at the column, and the domes, and the traffic. The same ground has held an emperor's hall, a medieval town, and a modern avenue. As you walk away, listen to the street around you. In its own way, this is still a place of crowds, business, and talk.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the railings along Via dei Fori Imperiali for wide views.
Visit late afternoon when low light warms the columns.
Bring binoculars to study the column's spiral carvings.
Combine with the museum inside Trajan's Markets nearby.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven cobbles around the forum.
