The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the Appian Way, the traffic of central Rome fades to a low hum. Cypress trees line the road, and the old paving stones are worn smooth and uneven underfoot. The air smells of dry grass and warm brick. In front of you stands a pale church with a calm, classical face. It looks simple, but it sits above one of the oldest Christian burial grounds in the world. You are standing at San Sebastiano fuori le mura, Saint Sebastian outside the walls.

That name tells you where you are. In ancient Rome, the law forbade burials inside the city walls. So the dead were carried out along the great roads, and the Appian Way became an avenue of tombs. Families built mausoleums beside the road for all travellers to see. Over time, Christians dug their own burial galleries into the soft volcanic rock here. This church grew out of that landscape of memory, on ground that was already full of graves.

Here is the first surprise. The word catacomb was born on this spot. In Roman times, this stretch of road ran past a dip in the ground, possibly old quarries. People called the area ad catacumbas, a phrase usually explained as meaning near the hollows. The underground cemetery here took that name. Centuries later, when other early Christian cemeteries were rediscovered around Rome, people used the same word for all of them. Every catacomb in the world borrows its name from this place.

The saint himself is a figure of both history and legend. Sebastian was a Christian in the Roman army, martyred during the persecutions around the year 300. According to the traditional story, he was tied to a post and shot with arrows. He survived, and a widow named Irene nursed him back to health. Then he confronted the emperor again and was beaten to death. Legend says his body was thrown into a sewer, and then recovered and buried in these catacombs.

But the church was not first dedicated to Sebastian at all. When it was built in the fourth century, people called it the Basilica of the Apostles. An old tradition held that the remains of Saint Peter and Saint Paul rested here for a time. Archaeologists working beneath the church found a room where early Christians met for funeral meals. Its walls carry hundreds of scratched messages asking Peter and Paul for their prayers. Those graffiti are real, and they are among the oldest evidence of devotion to the two apostles.

That first building was much larger than the church you see today. It was a funerary basilica, a hall built for burials and memorial meals rather than ordinary parish worship. Its plan was long, with a rounded end, a little like a Roman racetrack. The floor itself was packed with tombs. Several emperors and wealthy Romans built similar halls around the city in the same period. Only traces of that great structure survive now, partly inside the walls of the later church.

The church in front of you mostly belongs to the early seventeenth century. Cardinal Scipione Borghese, nephew of Pope Paul the Fifth, paid for a complete rebuilding. The architect Flaminio Ponzio began the work, and Giovanni Vasanzio finished it. The cardinal also built the famous villa and gallery that still carry his family name in Rome. Look at the facade. Its portico, the covered porch at the front, rests on ancient columns that were reused from older buildings.

Step inside, and the scale feels modest after the grand basilicas of the city centre. The nave is a single, bright hall. Above you hangs a carved and gilded wooden ceiling, golden against dark wood. On the left side is the chapel of Saint Sebastian himself, built over the place where tradition locates his tomb. Under its altar lies a white marble figure of the saint, stretched out in death, with the marks of arrows on his body.

That statue comes from the workshop world of baroque Rome. It was carved by Giuseppe Giorgetti, a sculptor of the late seventeenth century, and many sources link its design to the circle of Gian Lorenzo Bernini. The marble is polished to a soft sheen, so the body seems almost warm. Notice how the figure lies on its side. It looks less like a hero and more like a young man who has just stopped breathing. Many visitors find it the quietest moment in the church.

Now for the second surprise. In 2001, a marble bust of Christ was identified in the convent attached to this church. Many scholars believe it is the Salvator Mundi, the Saviour of the World, the last sculpture Bernini made before he died. It had stood here for a long time without much attention. Not every expert agrees on the attribution, but the bust is now displayed in the church. If it is Bernini, you are looking at the final work of the greatest sculptor of baroque Rome.

The chapel of relics holds objects that belong more to faith than to history. One is a stone with the impression of two feet. Popular tradition says they are the footprints of Christ, left when he met Saint Peter on the Appian Way. In that tale, Peter was fleeing Rome and asked, Quo vadis, Lord? Where are you going? Christ answered that he was going to Rome to be crucified again, and Peter turned back. A small church just up the road keeps the memory of that meeting.

The catacombs below spread through several levels of tunnels cut into the rock. They can only be visited with a guide, and the entrance is beside the church. Down there, the air is cool and still. You will see narrow niches in the walls, where bodies were laid and sealed. You will also see Roman mausoleums with painted and stucco decoration, built before this area became mainly Christian. Pagan and Christian burials sit almost side by side, which shows how slowly the old world changed.

San Sebastiano is also one of the traditional Seven Churches of Rome. In the sixteenth century, the priest Philip Neri led pilgrims on a walk to seven great churches in a single day. This was the most distant stop, out on the old road. Pilgrims still follow the route today, often on foot. That is why you may meet groups here with walking shoes and dusty trousers, pausing on the steps to rest before the long walk back.

Before you leave, stand for a moment on the road outside. Look along the Appian Way, past the walls and the dark cypress trees. Ancient travellers saw tombs on both sides of this road, and the dead were part of the journey. Beneath your feet, the tunnels run on in the darkness. Above them, birds call from the pines, and the light moves slowly across the pale stone of the facade. Take that quiet with you as you walk on.
