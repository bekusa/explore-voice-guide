The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the voices and the footsteps on the stone, there is a steady sound of falling water. It is soft and low, almost hidden by the crowd. Look down, and you will see why. The fountain in front of you does not rise up to greet you. It sits below the level of the street, as if the piazza had slowly swallowed it. A stone boat lies there, half sunk, with water spilling over its sides.

This is the Fontana della Barcaccia, at the foot of the Spanish Steps in Piazza di Spagna. The name is not exactly a compliment. In Italian, barcaccia means something like a battered old boat, a worthless tub. Romans have always had a sharp tongue for their monuments. Yet this modest little vessel is one of the most loved fountains in the city. And it has been here much longer than the famous staircase rising behind it.

The fountain dates from the early seventeenth century. It was commissioned by Pope Urban the Eighth, a member of the powerful Barberini family. The design is credited to the sculptor Pietro Bernini. His son was Gian Lorenzo Bernini — the man who later shaped much of Baroque Rome. Many historians believe the son helped his father on this project. The work was finished in the late 1620s, shortly before Pietro died.

Now, why a sinking boat? Here we move from history to legend. In 1598, the Tiber River flooded badly, and much of central Rome went under water. Locals say that when the flood went down, a small boat was left stranded right here in the square. According to the story, the image stayed with people, and the fountain recalls that strange sight. It is a lovely tale. Nobody can prove it, but Romans still tell it with pleasure.

There is a more practical explanation too, and it is the first surprise of this place. The fountain is fed by the Acqua Vergine, an ancient aqueduct. It was first built in 19 BCE by Marcus Agrippa, the right-hand man of the emperor Augustus. By the time it reaches this part of the city, the water has very little pressure. There was no way to throw a tall jet into the air. So the designer did the clever thing — he lowered the fountain into the ground.

That choice shaped everything you see. A grand vertical fountain was impossible here. Instead, the water simply overflows, gently, from the boat into the basin around it. A boat that is low in the water, almost sinking, fits that quiet flow perfectly. It turns a technical weakness into the whole idea of the sculpture. Stand at the edge for a moment and watch the water slide over the stone rim. It is a calm movement, not a show.

Look closely at the details, because they carry a family signature. At each end of the boat you will find a carved sun, with a face. Water pours from these faces. Search the stone and you will also find bees. The bee and the sun were symbols of the Barberini family, and Pope Urban put them on his buildings across Rome. Once you notice those bees, you will start to see them all over the city — on churches, palaces and other fountains.

Here is the second surprise. This fountain is not just for looking at. The Acqua Vergine has a long reputation for good, clean water. Romans have drunk from it for centuries. Small spouts on the fountain let people fill a bottle or cup their hands. On a hot afternoon, you will see visitors and locals leaning in to drink. Do keep your feet out of the basin, though. Wading and climbing on the fountain are not allowed.

Turn around now and look up the Spanish Steps. They are younger than the boat by about a century. The staircase was built in the 1720s to connect this square with the church of Trinità dei Monti at the top. So for roughly a hundred years, the Barcaccia sat here without its famous backdrop. The square takes its name from the Spanish embassy to the Holy See, which has stood nearby since the seventeenth century.

To your right, as you face the steps, is a pale building with a small plaque. This is where the English poet John Keats died in 1821, still a young man. Today it is a museum dedicated to him and to other Romantic poets. Keats heard this same water from his room. Some people like to imagine that the sound of the fountain stayed with him in his final weeks. That part is imagination, of course — but the water was certainly there.

The fountain has had a hard modern life as well. It went through a careful restoration in the early 2010s, which cleaned the stone and repaired the water systems. Then, in 2015, a group of football supporters visiting Rome threw bottles and damaged the newly restored sculpture. The event caused real anger across the city. Specialists repaired the damage, and the boat was saved. It was a reminder of how fragile this old stone really is.

Before you leave, look at the long, straight street that runs away from the fountain, opposite the steps. That is Via dei Condotti, today lined with fashion shops. Its name comes from the conduits, the water channels, of the same ancient aqueduct that feeds this fountain. So the water at your feet has quietly given its name to one of the most expensive streets in Rome. The old and the new sit right next to each other here.

So take one last look at the little stone boat. It is stuck forever in its sinking, low in the water, sun faces streaming. Hear the water again under the noise of the crowd. It has been running here for about four hundred years, through floods, wars and restorations. As you walk away, up the steps or down Via dei Condotti, that soft sound will follow you for a few metres. Then the city will take over again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to see the fountain without dense crowds.
Bring a refillable bottle to fill from the drinking spouts.
Do not sit on the Spanish Steps; sitting is now prohibited.
Visit the Keats-Shelley House beside the steps afterwards.
Walk down Via dei Condotti to follow the aqueduct's old name.
