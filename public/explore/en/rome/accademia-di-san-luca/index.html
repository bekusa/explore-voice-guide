The guide

The story

Step out of the crowd around the Trevi Fountain, and walk a few short streets uphill. The noise drops quickly. The sound of coins hitting water fades, and the stone under your feet turns quieter, older, a little uneven. In front of you is a calm palazzo façade on a small square. There is no grand sign shouting for attention. Yet behind these walls is one of the oldest academies of art in Europe: the Accademia di San Luca.

The name comes from Saint Luke the Evangelist. For centuries, painters across Europe treated Luke as their patron saint. The reason is a legend, not a historical record. According to an old Christian tradition, Luke painted a portrait of the Virgin Mary from life. Painters liked this story very much. It gave their craft a holy origin, and it made them part of a long and honourable line. Guilds of painters in many cities took Luke's name for that reason.

In Rome, painters had an old guild of their own. But in the late sixteenth century, some artists wanted something more ambitious than a trade guild. They wanted an academy, a place for teaching, debate, and the theory of art. The painter Federico Zuccari is closely linked to this new start. He became the first head of the reformed academy, with the title of principe, or prince. Papal support gave the institution its official standing in the city.

This was a quiet but real change in how people saw artists. A guild protected a craft and a market. An academy argued that painting, sculpture, and architecture were intellectual work, close to poetry and philosophy. Members gave lectures. Young artists drew from models and from ancient sculpture. They studied geometry and anatomy. The academy in Rome became a model, and later academies in other European capitals followed a similar idea.

Here is a first surprise. The academy has not always lived in this building. For a long time, its home was down near the Roman Forum, beside the church of Santi Luca e Martina. The painter and architect Pietro da Cortona, who also led the academy, rebuilt that church in the seventeenth century. In the twentieth century, large demolitions cleared the area for a new road along the ancient forums. The academy then moved here, to Palazzo Carpegna.

Palazzo Carpegna has its own story. The great Baroque architect Francesco Borromini worked on the palace in the seventeenth century for the Carpegna family. Borromini was famous for strange, inventive geometry — curves that bend where you expect straight lines, ovals where you expect circles. Many of his plans for this palace were never fully built. But one piece of his work here is still a favourite of architects.

That piece is the ramp. Inside, instead of an ordinary staircase, a gently sloping spiral ramp winds upward through the building. There are no steps. The floor simply climbs in a smooth curve, lit from above. People often say it was designed so that horses or carriages could go up, but treat that as a popular explanation rather than a proven fact. Whatever the reason, walking it feels unusual. Your body turns slowly as you rise, and the light changes with each turn.

The academy also holds a collection of art. Over the centuries, members gave works to the institution, and many students left their prize pieces here. The most famous painting is a picture of Saint Luke painting the Virgin, attributed to Raphael and his workshop. It brings us full circle to the legend. A painter shows the patron saint of painters at his easel. Look for the figure watching from the side; many viewers believe it is a portrait of Raphael himself.

A second surprise is about who belonged. Membership was an honour, and the academy elected painters, sculptors, and architects from across Europe. Some were women, at a time when this was rare. The Swiss-born painter Angelica Kauffman, one of the most successful artists of the eighteenth century, was a member. Her presence in these lists is a small reminder that the art world of the past was a little more open than many people assume.

Competition was part of life here. In the early eighteenth century, the academy organised regular prize contests for young artists, supported by the papacy. Students from many countries came to Rome to take part. They drew, painted, and designed buildings on set themes. Many of these competition drawings and models survive in the academy's archive. Together, they show how taste changed, decade after decade, in the hands of young people trying to impress their teachers.

The sculptor Antonio Canova also led the academy, in the early nineteenth century. By then, Rome was the place every serious artist wanted to visit. Students arrived from Paris, London, Copenhagen, and far beyond. They copied ancient statues, sketched ruins, and argued in studios across the city. The academy stood at the centre of this international world. Its rooms carry traces of those conversations, in portraits of members and in works donated by them.

Today the academy is still active. It is not only a museum. It holds exhibitions, conferences, and lectures, and it keeps a large library and archive used by researchers. Members are still elected from among living artists and architects. So when you stand here, you are not visiting a closed chapter of history. You are at the door of an institution that has been meeting, debating, and teaching for more than four hundred years.

Before you leave, take one more look at the square around you. Notice how quiet it is, only a few minutes from one of the busiest fountains in the world. Notice the warm colour of the plaster and the plain lines of the façade. Then think of that spiral ramp inside, turning slowly upward. As you walk back down toward the crowds, the city may look a little different — less like a postcard, and more like a working studio.
