The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Traffic hums along Via Veneto, a scooter whines past, and café cups clink on the pavement nearby. Then you climb a short double staircase toward a plain brick facade. Behind it is one of the strangest rooms in Rome... a crypt where the walls are made of human bone. Before we go down, let's take this place one layer at a time.

The church itself was built in the early seventeenth century, between 1626 and 1631. Its patron was Cardinal Antonio Barberini. He was a Capuchin friar, and he was also the brother of Pope Urban the Eighth. The Barberini family shaped this whole neighbourhood. Their name is on the square just downhill, and their palace stands close by. Look for their emblem, the bee, as you walk these streets.

The Capuchins are a branch of the Franciscan order. Franciscans follow Saint Francis of Assisi, who taught a life of poverty and simplicity. You can see that idea in the architecture here. The facade is restrained, almost severe, next to the grand Baroque churches elsewhere in Rome. There are no twisting columns, no marble explosions. This was a house for friars who owned very little, and the building looks the part.

Step inside the church, and look to your right. In the first chapel hangs a painting of Saint Michael the Archangel by Guido Reni, a celebrated painter of the period. Michael stands calmly, sword raised, one foot on the defeated devil. His face is serene, almost bored. Many visitors walk past it on their way to the crypt. It is worth a slow minute — the colours are soft and clear, and the composition is perfectly balanced.

Now the first surprise. Locals love a story about that painting. According to a popular tale, the face of the devil under Michael's foot resembles Giovanni Battista Pamphili, the man who later became Pope Innocent the Tenth. The Pamphili family were rivals of the Barberini. Was it a painter's private joke? Nobody can prove it. But look closely at the devil's face, and decide for yourself.

Another detail sits right in the floor, and most people step over it. Near the high altar is the tomb of Cardinal Antonio Barberini, the founder. A man of his rank could have ordered a grand monument. Instead his slab carries a short Latin inscription. It reads, in English: here lies dust, ashes, and nothing. No name in large letters, no list of honours. It is the perfect introduction to what waits below.

The way to the crypt now leads through a small museum. It tells the story of the Capuchin order — their work with the poor, their missions, their daily life. You will see simple habits, tools, and documents. The museum also holds a painting of Saint Francis in meditation, which some scholars attribute to Caravaggio. The attribution is debated, so treat it as an open question. Either way, the image of a friar holding a skull sets the mood perfectly.

And now, the crypt. It is a long, low corridor beside a row of small chapels. In each chapel, the bones of Capuchin friars are arranged into patterns. Skulls are stacked into arches. Pelvic bones form rosettes and fans. Vertebrae trace delicate lines along the ceiling. The remains of roughly 3,700 friars are here. The light is low and warm, and the air is cool and still.

Where did all these bones come from? When the friars moved to this new friary, they brought with them the remains of brothers buried at their older house in the city. Over the following generations, more friars were added. Tradition says that the earth in some chapels was brought from Jerusalem, from the Holy Land. That is a story the friars tell, not something you can verify by looking. But the soil is real, and some burials remain beneath it.

Each chapel has its own theme, often named for the bones that dominate it. There is a chapel of skulls, a chapel of pelvises, and a chapel of leg and thigh bones. One chapel holds a scene of the Resurrection. Some complete skeletons still wear brown Capuchin robes, tied with a rope belt. On one ceiling, a small skeleton holds a scythe and a set of scales — symbols of death and judgement. Local tradition links it to a child of the Barberini family.

Here is the second surprise. This is not meant to be horror. For the friars, the crypt was a meditation. A plaque in one chapel puts it simply: what you are now, we once were... what we are now, you will be. The message is not fear. It is humility. Life is short, so live it well. Many visitors arrive expecting something macabre and leave strangely calm.

Writers have been coming here for a long time. The American author Mark Twain visited in the nineteenth century and described the crypt in his travel book, The Innocents Abroad. He wrote about it with his usual dark humour, chatting with a friar about the former owners of the bones. Today the crypt is quiet. Most people whisper without being asked. The silence is part of the experience.

When you climb back up the stairs, notice how the street sounds return. The cafés, the taxis, the voices. Below you, thousands of friars lie in careful patterns. Above you, Rome carries on with its day. Walk down to the square at the bottom of the hill, and you will find a fountain by Bernini, sculpted for the same Barberini family. Look for the bees carved on it.

Take one last look at the plain brick front of the church. From here it seems so modest. Nothing on the outside tells you what lies underneath. Perhaps that is the whole lesson of this place. Carry it with you as you step back into the light and the noise of Via Veneto.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered.
Expect photography to be forbidden inside the crypt.
Visit the museum first to understand the crypt.
Keep voices low; this remains an active religious site.
Walk downhill afterward to see Piazza Barberini.
