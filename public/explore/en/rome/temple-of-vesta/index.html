The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under passing feet, and somewhere a guide raises a voice above the crowd. Then look at the curved white marble in front of you. It is only a fragment of a circle, a few columns joined by a carved band of stone, but the curve itself matters. In a forum of rectangles, this small building was round. For more than a thousand years, a fire burned inside it, day and night.

This is the Temple of Vesta, in the heart of the Roman Forum. Vesta was the goddess of the hearth. That was the fireplace at the centre of every Roman home, where families cooked, gathered and made small offerings. Here, Rome kept a hearth for the whole city. The Romans believed that while this flame burned, Rome itself was safe. If it went out, it was a terrible sign.

Here is the first surprise. Most Roman temples held a statue of their god, but this one had no statue of Vesta inside. The fire itself was the goddess. It was a living presence, not a carved figure. That idea is very old, and the round shape may be old too. Roman writers connected it to the simple round huts of the earliest people who lived on these hills. In other words, the temple kept the memory of a village hearth inside a great imperial city.

The flame was guarded by priestesses called the Vestal Virgins. There were usually six of them at a time. They were chosen as young girls, roughly between six and ten years old, from respectable families. Ancient sources describe a long term of service, about thirty years. They spent the first decade learning, the second performing the rites, and the third teaching the younger women. After that, a Vestal was free to leave and even to marry. Many chose to stay.

Their life carried real privilege. Vestals could own property and make their own wills, which most Roman women could not do. They had honoured seats at public games. According to ancient writers, if a Vestal happened to meet a condemned prisoner on the way to execution, he could be pardoned. Important documents were sometimes placed in their care, including the wills of powerful men. Roman sources say this was true even of Julius Caesar and the emperor Augustus.

But the privilege came with a dark side. If the sacred fire went out, the Vestal on duty could be whipped. A Vestal accused of breaking her vow of chastity faced something worse. She was not executed in the ordinary way, because her blood could not be spilled. Instead, she was sealed alive in an underground chamber near one of the city gates, with a little food and a lamp. Ancient historians record several such cases. It is a hard thing to imagine in this quiet, sunlit space.

The fire was not only kept alive. It was also renewed. On the first of March, which was the old Roman new year, the flame was ritually rekindled. In June came the Vestalia, the goddess's festival. During those days, ordinary Roman women could enter the temple barefoot to bring offerings. At other times, men were not allowed inside at all. The Vestals also prepared a special salted flour, used in sacrifices across the city.

Deep inside the temple was a closed storeroom called the penus. Only the Vestals could enter it. What was kept there was a secret, and secrets attract stories. One famous legend says the penus held the Palladium. This was a small, ancient image of the goddess Minerva, supposedly carried from burning Troy by the hero Aeneas, the legendary ancestor of the Romans. No one can confirm it. But Romans believed their city's fate was tied to whatever lay hidden here.

The building you see is not the original. Fire was the temple's purpose, and fire was also its enemy. It burned and was rebuilt several times. The last major rebuilding came around the end of the second century, under the empress Julia Domna, wife of the emperor Septimius Severus. Much later, after centuries of ruin, archaeologists put part of it back together in the 1930s. They used surviving original fragments and filled the gaps with new material. Look closely, and the difference between old and new stone is visible.

Now turn toward the long rectangular courtyard behind the temple. This was the House of the Vestals, where the priestesses lived. Today you can see pools of water and a row of marble statues on tall pedestals. These honoured the senior Vestals, each called the Virgo Vestalis Maxima, the chief Vestal. Many of the pedestals still carry inscriptions praising their virtue and devotion. Walk slowly along the line and read the shapes of the letters, even if you do not read Latin.

Here is the second surprise. On one pedestal, the name of the honoured Vestal has been deliberately chiselled away. Nobody knows for certain why. A popular theory suggests she converted to Christianity, and her name was removed in disgrace. It remains a theory, not a fact. It does remind us that this order ended in a changing world. In the late fourth century, as Christianity became the official religion, the emperor Theodosius closed the old temples. The fire of Vesta was put out, and the Vestals disappeared.

One last word, to avoid a common confusion. Across the city, near the river Tiber, stands another round temple with a complete ring of columns. For centuries people called it the Temple of Vesta too, simply because of its shape. Today it is identified as a temple of Hercules, and it is one of the oldest surviving marble buildings in Rome. If someone tells you they saw the Temple of Vesta by the river, now you know which one they mean.

Before you move on, stand still and look at the curve of those columns once more. Picture a thin line of smoke rising from the roof, and a woman in white walking the short path to the house behind you. Hundreds of years of Roman life were measured by this small flame. As you walk away along the paving, listen to the crowd and the gravel again. The fire is gone, but the circle where it burned is still here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the House of the Vestals right behind the temple.
Come early morning for softer light and fewer crowds.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven ancient paving.
Bring water and a hat for the shadeless forum.
Plan a separate walk to the round temple near the Tiber.
