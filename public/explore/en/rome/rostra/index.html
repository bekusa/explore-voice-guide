The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Gravel crunches under passing feet. Somewhere a guide is calling out in a language you may not know. In front of you is a long, low wall of brick and pale stone, rough at the edges, with a row of small holes along its face. It does not look like much at first. But this platform was once the place where Rome came to hear itself speak, argue, mourn, and decide.

This is the Rostra, the great speakers' platform of the Roman Forum. From here, magistrates and orators addressed the crowds packed into the open square below. Laws were announced. Elections were fought. Funerals turned into political rallies. If you wanted the Roman people to hear you, this was where you stood. For centuries, few spots in the ancient world held more spoken words than the few square metres in front of you.

The name holds the first surprise. In Latin, a rostrum is a beak, and the word also meant the bronze ram on the prow of a warship. After a naval victory at Antium in 338 BCE, the Romans took the prows of captured enemy ships and fixed them to the speakers' platform as trophies. The platform took its name from those ship beaks. Every time you hear a lecturer step up to a rostrum today, you are hearing an echo of that battle.

Now look again at those holes along the front of the wall. Archaeologists believe they held the fixings for bronze prows and other decorations. Imagine dark metal rams, each one taken from a real ship, jutting out over the heads of the crowd. A citizen standing where you stand would have looked up at a speaker framed by war trophies. The message was clear. Words spoken here carried the weight of Roman power.

The platform you see is not the first one. The older Rostra of the Republic stood a short distance away, near the Comitium, the open meeting place in front of the Senate House. That building, the Curia, is the tall brick hall you can see nearby. In the last years of the Republic, Julius Caesar began to move the speakers' platform to this new position. After his death, his heir Augustus completed the work.

The move was not only practical. It changed the shape of public life. The old platform faced the gathering place of the citizen assemblies. The new one sat at the end of the Forum, looking down its long open space. Some historians see this as a quiet statement about who now controlled the stage. The Republic of debating assemblies was giving way to a world shaped by one powerful family.

The Rostra also saw some of the darkest moments of that change. In 43 BCE, the orator and statesman Cicero was killed on the orders of Mark Antony, his bitter political enemy. Ancient writers record that Cicero's head and hands were then displayed on the Rostra. The man whose voice had filled this space was silenced and put on show at the very spot where he had spoken. Romans did not miss the cruel meaning.

The year before, the Forum had seen another famous scene. After Caesar was murdered in 44 BCE, his funeral took place here in the Forum, and Mark Antony delivered a speech that stirred the crowd into fury. Many visitors know this moment through Shakespeare's play, with its line about friends, Romans, and countrymen. Those exact words are Shakespeare's, not Antony's. But the anger in the crowd was real, and it shook the city.

Look around the platform and you will find more stories close by. To one side rises the triumphal Arch of Septimius Severus, a great marble gateway covered in carved battle scenes. Behind and above you, eight tall columns mark the Temple of Saturn, one of the oldest sacred sites in the Forum. Near this end of the platform, the Romans also placed markers that they saw as the symbolic centre of their city and its roads.

One of those markers was the Golden Milestone, set up under Augustus. It is often linked to the idea that all roads lead to Rome. A second, the Umbilicus Urbis, or navel of the city, stood here as well. Only fragments and foundations survive today, so they are easy to walk past. But it is worth pausing on the idea. Romans placed the centre of their world right beside the place where they spoke to the people.

The Rostra kept its role for centuries, long after real power had moved to the emperors. Emperors and officials still spoke here and still used it for ceremony. In late antiquity, scholars believe the platform was extended, and some link that work to a naval victory against the Vandals, a people who had attacked Rome. Even then, centuries after Antium, ships and victory at sea were still tied to this stage.

What you see now is a mix of ancient remains and careful modern restoration. Much of the original marble facing is long gone. Over the centuries, stone from the Forum was carried off and reused in churches and palaces across the city. In front of the platform stands a single tall column, the Column of Phocas, put up in the early seventh century. It was one of the last monuments added to the ancient Forum.

Stand here a little longer and try to picture the space full. The square in front of you packed with people, shoulder to shoulder. Voices rising and falling. A speaker on the platform above, the bronze prows below him catching the sunlight. Then let the picture fade, and notice what is really here. Broken stone, warm brick, tourists with phones, a lizard on a sunny ledge, swifts calling overhead.

As you walk on, glance back once at that plain line of brick with its small, empty holes. It is easy to miss. Yet the word rostrum still lives in many languages because of this wall. Every podium, every lectern, every person who stands up to speak to a crowd is part of a long line that runs back to this spot... and to a handful of captured ships.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stand in the open square below to see the orator's view.
Visit early morning, when the Forum is quieter and cooler.
A Forum ticket is required; consider booking ahead online.
Bring water and a hat; the Forum has very little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ancient paving and gravel.
