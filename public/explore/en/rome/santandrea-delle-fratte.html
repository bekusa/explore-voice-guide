The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in this narrow street, a few minutes' walk from the Spanish Steps. The noise of the shopping streets fades here. Look up, and you will see something unusual above the rooftops — a tower of pale stone and warm brick that seems to twist as it climbs. Small carved figures stand around its upper levels, and the top ends in shapes that look like a crown. This is Sant'Andrea delle Fratte, the church of Saint Andrew of the Thickets.

That name is the first small surprise. "Fratte" means thickets, or hedges, in Italian. Today this is the centre of Rome, packed with boutiques, cafés and hotels. But for centuries this area sat near the edge of the inhabited city. Gardens, vineyards and wild hedges filled the slopes here. The church kept the name long after the countryside disappeared under streets and palaces. So the name works like a small piece of old Roman geography, still painted on a modern street sign.

The building you see took shape mainly in the seventeenth century. A wealthy local family, the Del Bufalo, supported the rebuilding of an older church on this site. Their palace stood nearby, and the project was a statement of family pride as much as faith. Several architects worked on it over the years. But one name dominates the story — Francesco Borromini, one of the great architects of the Roman Baroque, the dramatic style of curves, movement and theatre that shaped so much of this city.

Borromini designed the drum of the dome and the bell tower above you. To see them properly, walk a few steps along the side street, Via di Capo le Case. From there, the whole composition comes into view. Notice that the dome's drum is mostly bare brick. It was never finished with the smooth finish Borromini planned. Many visitors walk past without noticing, but this unfinished state shows you the bones of Baroque construction — the raw material under the decoration.

Now look again at the bell tower. Borromini disliked simple, straight forms. Here, each level changes shape. The lower part is solid and square. Higher up, columns and curved surfaces push in and out. Near the top, carved figures with wings stand around the tower like guards. And the very top ends in curling forms, often compared to a crown or to flames. It is a small structure, but it carries all of Borromini's restless energy in one vertical line.

Borromini had a famous rival — Gian Lorenzo Bernini, the sculptor and architect behind Saint Peter's Square and many of Rome's great fountains. And here comes the second surprise. Inside this church, built partly by Borromini, stand two of Bernini's finest marble angels. The two men competed for decades, and their styles were very different. Yet today their work shares one roof. Rome has a way of placing old rivals side by side.

The angels have their own story. In the seventeenth century, Pope Clement the Ninth commissioned a series of angels for the Ponte Sant'Angelo, the bridge that leads to Castel Sant'Angelo. Each angel holds an object from the Passion of Christ — the story of his suffering and death. Bernini carved two of them with his own hands. According to the traditional account, they were judged too beautiful to leave outside in the rain and wind. Copies went onto the bridge instead.

The originals stayed in the Bernini family for decades. In 1729, they were moved here to Sant'Andrea delle Fratte. You will find them near the altar, one on each side. One angel holds the crown of thorns. The other holds the sign that was placed above the cross. Look closely at the drapery. The marble folds seem to move in a wind that does not exist. This is Bernini's great skill — turning hard stone into fabric, feathers and air.

The church has a third story, and for many visitors it is the most important. Walk to the side chapel dedicated to the Madonna del Miracolo, the Madonna of the Miracle. On 20 January 1842, a young Jewish banker from Strasbourg named Alphonse Ratisbonne entered this church while waiting for a friend. He later said that the Virgin Mary appeared to him in this chapel. He left the church a changed man, and soon converted to Catholicism.

Ratisbonne's experience became famous across Europe. He became a priest and dedicated his life to religious work, together with his brother. The chapel became a place of pilgrimage. Decades later, in 1918, a young Polish priest named Maximilian Kolbe celebrated his first Mass at this altar. Kolbe later died in Auschwitz, after offering his own life to save another prisoner. He was declared a saint. Plaques and images in the church remember both men, so look for them as you walk.

Because of this history, Sant'Andrea delle Fratte is not only an art destination. It is a living parish and a place of prayer. You may see people lighting candles at the chapel, or sitting in silence in the pews. Please move quietly, keep your voice low, and avoid photographing people at prayer. Shoulders and knees should be covered. If a Mass is in progress, wait at the back, or come back a little later.

As you walk through the nave, notice the contrast between the inside and the outside. The front of the church, facing the street, is calm and classical. It was completed much later, in the nineteenth century, long after Borromini's death. Inside, the decoration is richer — gilded details, painted ceilings and chapels added by different families over generations. The building is not one design. It is a layered record of many patrons, many decades, and many changes in taste.

Before you leave, step back into the street and look up one more time. Borromini's tower and Bernini's angels belong to the same church, but they speak in different voices — one in brick and geometry, the other in marble and movement. Together they show you two sides of Baroque Rome in a single visit. And very few of the crowds around the Spanish Steps ever come here to compare them.

As you walk away, listen to the change in sound. The quiet of this side street slowly gives way to traffic, voices and footsteps on the stones. Glance back once more toward the tower above the rooftops. The thickets that gave this church its name are long gone. But the brick, the marble and the candlelight inside still hold their place here, a short walk from one of the busiest corners of the city.
