The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, traffic hums along Via dei Fori Imperiali. Scooters buzz past, and tour groups drift towards the Colosseum. In front of you rises a heavy, square mass of brick. Its surface is rough and patched, in shades of rust, honey and grey. Some sections look almost new. Others are pitted and dark. This is the Torre dei Conti, one of the great family towers of medieval Rome.

To understand this tower, picture Rome around the year 1200. The ancient city had shrunk. Fields and vineyards covered much of the land inside the old walls. Powerful noble families competed for control of neighbourhoods, and they built towers to show it. A tower was a home, a fortress and a message all at once. Rome had many of them. Very few survive today, and this is one of the largest.

The tower is linked to one family in particular, the Conti di Segni. That is where its name comes from. The most famous member of the family was Lotario dei Conti. In 1198 he became Pope Innocent the Third, one of the most powerful popes of the Middle Ages. Construction is generally connected to him and his brother Riccardo, in the early thirteenth century. In short, a pope built a fortress for his family, in the middle of a city full of rivals.

Now look down towards the base. The tower does not stand on empty ground. Its builders placed it on top of ruins from ancient Rome. Below and around this spot lay the Temple of Peace, built by the Emperor Vespasian in the first century. It was a large complex of gardens, halls and colonnades. The medieval builders used the solid ancient remains as a foundation. So you are looking at two cities at once — a medieval tower resting on an imperial one.

Here is the first surprise. What you see today is only part of the original tower. In its prime, the Torre dei Conti was much taller. It was so tall that people in the Middle Ages counted it among the most impressive buildings in the city. Some sources called it the Torre Maggiore, which means the Greater Tower. The poet Petrarch, writing in the fourteenth century, praised it as a tower without equal anywhere in the world.

So what happened to the missing height? Earthquakes. A strong earthquake struck central Italy in 1348 and badly damaged the tower. Over the following centuries, further shocks and collapses brought down more of it. The upper floors were never rebuilt to their old height. As a result, the tower slowly became the wide, stubby block you see now. Its base still feels enormous, but its top seems cut short.

Look at the walls closely. You can see different types of brick and different patterns of repair. Some bricks are thin and laid in neat rows. Others are rougher and set more loosely. Each change shows a different moment in the tower's life — damage, repair, reinforcement, and more repair. Medieval Romans often reused bricks and stone from ancient buildings nearby. Some pieces in this wall may be far older than the tower itself.

Now turn your head and look around the skyline. On the slope of the Quirinal Hill, not far away, stands another tall medieval tower. That is the Torre delle Milizie, near Trajan's Markets. Once, towers like these rose across this whole area. They belonged to families who watched each other with deep suspicion. Today only a few remain, and they give you a sense of how crowded and tense this skyline once was.

The road beside you also has its own story. In the 1930s, Mussolini's government cut a wide, straight avenue through this part of the city. It was designed for parades, running from Piazza Venezia to the Colosseum. To build it, workers demolished a whole medieval and Renaissance neighbourhood. Houses, churches and small streets disappeared. The tower survived, but its old neighbours did not. That is why it now stands alone, cut off from the homes that once pressed against it.

This isolation changes how we see it. In the Middle Ages, the tower rose above a dense mass of rooftops and narrow lanes. People would have seen it in pieces, between buildings, from small squares. Today you can see almost the whole structure from the open avenue. It looks like a monument on display. But it was built to be part of a living, crowded neighbourhood, not an object in an open space.

The second surprise is much more recent, and it is a serious one. In November 2025, during restoration works, part of the tower collapsed. Workers were inside at the time. One worker was trapped under the rubble for many hours, and he later died in hospital. The event shocked the city. It also raised hard questions about how to care for old structures that have stood, damaged and repaired, for eight hundred years.

Because of this, you may find the area around the tower fenced off. You may see scaffolding, barriers or signs of ongoing engineering work. If so, treat the site with care and keep to the public paths. The tower is not only a historic monument. It is also a place where someone lost his life, and many Romans still remember that clearly. A quiet moment of respect here is appropriate.

Before you move on, take one last look at the brickwork. Notice how the light falls across the uneven surface. In the morning the wall can look pale and dusty. Late in the afternoon it turns a warmer orange. Above you, swifts and gulls often circle in the open sky. Around you, the ancient forums lie half buried below street level. As you walk away towards the Colosseum or Piazza Venezia, glance back once. You are leaving a tower that has survived popes, earthquakes, dictators and its own collapse — and it still stands, patched and stubborn, at the edge of the ancient city.
