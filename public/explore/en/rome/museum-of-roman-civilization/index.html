The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The traffic of central Rome is far away. Here the avenues are wide and straight, and the stone underfoot is pale and smooth. The light bounces off flat white walls and tall square columns. It feels quiet, almost formal. You are in EUR, a planned district south of the old city. In front of you stands the Museum of Roman Civilization, a building made to tell the whole story of ancient Rome in one place.

One honest note before we begin. For many years now, the museum has been closed for renovation. So you may be standing outside locked doors. That is fine. The building itself is part of the story. Its architecture, its setting and its collection all say something about how modern Italy chose to remember its ancient past. Let us walk through that story together, from the outside in.

First, the district around you. EUR stands for Esposizione Universale di Roma, the Universal Exhibition of Rome. The Fascist government planned a great world's fair here for 1942. It was meant to celebrate twenty years of the regime. Then the Second World War came, and the fair never opened. Many buildings were left half-finished. After the war, Rome completed them and turned EUR into a working neighbourhood of offices, museums and homes.

That explains the look of this museum. Notice the heavy, simplified columns. They have no carved capitals and no decoration. This is a stripped-down classical style. It borrows the grammar of ancient Rome, like the column, the portico and the symmetry, and removes the ornament. The result feels both ancient and modern at the same time. Some people find it cold. Others find it calm and strangely beautiful. Both reactions are reasonable.

Look at the shape of the complex. It is made of two large blocks facing each other. A long colonnade connects them across an open space. Walk into the middle and turn slowly. The columns frame the sky in tall vertical strips. Your footsteps echo. This was intentional. The architects wanted visitors to feel small and to feel that they were entering something serious, almost like a temple to history.

Now here is the first surprise. Most of the objects inside this museum are not ancient at all. They are copies. There are plaster casts of statues, inscriptions and reliefs, and scale models of buildings and machines. The originals stay in museums across Europe, North Africa and the Middle East. This collection gathered their likenesses under one roof, so that a visitor could see the whole Roman world in a single afternoon.

The collection has a long background. Its roots go back to a large archaeological exhibition held in Rome in 1911. That show marked fifty years since the unification of Italy. Then, in 1937, a much bigger exhibition opened. It celebrated two thousand years since the birth of Augustus, the first Roman emperor. Many of the casts and models made for that event later moved into this building.

That 1937 exhibition had a clear political purpose. Mussolini's government presented itself as the heir of the Roman Empire. Ancient Rome was used as propaganda for modern conquest. It is worth keeping that in mind as you look at this building. The museum was designed in that atmosphere. After the war, it opened as a place of study and education, but the walls still carry traces of the age that planned them.

The most loved object inside is a model of ancient Rome itself. The archaeologist Italo Gismondi led its creation, and work began for the 1937 exhibition. It shows the city in the time of Emperor Constantine, in the early fourth century. The model fills a large room. You can see the Colosseum, the Circus Maximus, the temples on the Capitoline Hill and thousands of houses packed between them.

Here is the second surprise. You may already know this model without realising it. Images of it appear in school textbooks, documentaries and films all over the world. When people picture the whole of ancient Rome from above, they very often picture this model. It was built from careful study of ruins, ancient maps and written sources. It is a scholar's best reconstruction, not a photograph, but few objects have shaped the public image of Rome so strongly.

The museum also holds plaster casts of the reliefs from Trajan's Column. The real column still stands in the city centre, near the Roman forums. Its carvings spiral upward in a long band, showing the emperor's wars across the Danube. On the real column, the upper scenes are almost impossible to see from the ground. Here, the casts were displayed at eye level. Visitors could follow soldiers, bridges and battles up close, one scene at a time.

The building has also held a planetarium and an astronomy museum in recent decades. It closed together with the rest of the complex. This mix says something about EUR as a whole. It was planned as a showcase of knowledge, science and culture. Today the district is a quiet business area during the week. At weekends the wide streets are calm, and families walk and cycle between the buildings.

If you have time, walk a little further through EUR. Look along the straight avenues. Many of them end at another large white building, placed like a stage set. The district was designed for these long views. You will notice the same pale stone, the same square columns and the same sense of order. Seen together, the buildings feel like one large idea, written in stone.

Before you leave, stand once more between the two halves of the museum. Look up at the columns against the sky. Listen to how quiet it is. Inside, a whole ancient city waits in miniature, made of plaster and patience. Outside, a modern city tried to dress itself in Roman clothing. As you walk away, notice how the shadows of the columns stretch across the stone, long and straight, just as the planners intended.
