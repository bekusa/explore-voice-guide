The guide

The story

Listen first. The traffic of Rome fades as you pass through the old gateway. In its place you hear wind moving through tall eucalyptus trees, a soft and papery sound. Breathe in, and you may catch their sharp, clean scent, almost like medicine. Gravel crunches under your shoes. This is the Abbey of the Three Fountains, and it feels further from the city centre than it really is.

The name comes from a story that has drawn pilgrims here for many centuries. Christian tradition holds that this is the place where Saint Paul the Apostle was executed. Paul was a Roman citizen, so he was not crucified. He was beheaded with a sword. In ancient times the area had another name, Aquae Salviae, which roughly means the waters of Salvius. Water has always been part of this valley's identity.

Now comes the legend that gives the abbey its name. According to the old tale, when Paul's head was struck from his body, it bounced three times on the ground. At each place it touched, a spring of water burst from the earth. Three springs... three fountains. This is folklore, not documented history. But it shaped the place completely, and the whole complex grew up around that story.

As you arrived, you probably walked under a medieval gatehouse with faded paintings inside its arch. Locals call it the Arch of Charlemagne. Legend says the Frankish emperor, who ruled much of western Europe around the year 800, gave gifts to the monks here. Historians treat that connection with caution. Still, look up at the vault as you pass. The colours are worn and patchy, but you can make out figures and patterns painted long ago.

Beyond the gate, there is not one church but three. The largest is the abbey church of Saints Vincent and Anastasius. Then there is a small church called Santa Maria Scala Coeli, which means Saint Mary of the Ladder of Heaven. Finally, at the far end of the lane, stands the church of Saint Paul at the Three Fountains. Each building has its own character. Together they make a quiet walk of a few minutes, from one doorway to the next.

Start with the abbey church. Its outside is plain brick, with very little decoration. That plainness is deliberate. In the twelfth century, the abbey passed to the Cistercians, a strict monastic order that rejected rich ornament. They believed simple walls and clear light helped monks focus on prayer. Inside, the nave is tall and bare. Your footsteps echo. On some of the great pillars, you can still see faded painted figures of the apostles.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Cistercian movement was led by Saint Bernard of Clairvaux, one of the most powerful religious voices of medieval Europe. Bernard sent monks to this abbey. Its first Cistercian abbot did not stay an abbot for long. He was elected pope in the 1140s and took the name Eugene the Third. So a man who walked these cloisters as a monk went on to lead the whole Western Church.

Bernard also appears in the story of the small church next door, Santa Maria Scala Coeli. According to tradition, Bernard was saying Mass here for the souls of the dead. During the service, he saw a vision of a ladder rising to heaven. Souls climbed it, released from suffering. That vision gave the church its name. Again, this is a pious legend. But it made the chapel a place where people came to pray for loved ones who had died.

The church of Saint Paul stands at the end of the lane, and it is the heart of the story. The present building dates from the late sixteenth century, when it was rebuilt in a more classical style. Step inside and look along the floor and walls. You will find three separate shrines, set at different levels. Each one marks the place of one of the three legendary springs. Tradition also points to a column here, said to be where Paul was bound.

Now for a second surprise. Those eucalyptus trees outside are not native to Italy. They come from Australia. In the nineteenth century, this low, damp valley was known for malaria, a deadly fever spread by mosquitoes. Trappist monks, a strict branch of the Cistercian family, took over the abbey in the late 1860s. They planted eucalyptus trees in large numbers, hoping the trees would help dry the marshy ground and make the area healthier.

The monks still live here, and the trees still shape daily life. The Trappists make a liqueur flavoured with eucalyptus, along with chocolate and other goods. You can find them in the small shop near the entrance. The products are part of the monastic tradition of supporting the community through work. If you visit at the right moment, you may hear the monks singing their prayers in the abbey church, a slow, steady chant that fills the plain brick space.

The area around the abbey has its own layers. Just beyond the trees lies the modern district of EUR, with wide boulevards and white stone buildings from the twentieth century. The contrast is striking. A short walk from the abbey there is also a grotto linked to a reported apparition of the Virgin Mary in 1947. Many pilgrims visit both places on the same day. So this valley holds ancient tradition, medieval monasticism, and modern devotion side by side.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the lane between the churches. Notice the light as it falls through the eucalyptus leaves onto the old brick walls. Notice how quiet it is, even though a major city surrounds you. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again for the wind in the trees. It is the same sound the monks hear every morning... a small, steady reminder that this place was built for stillness.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk all three churches in order, ending at Saint Paul's.
Dress modestly with covered shoulders and knees for the churches.
Keep voices low; this is a working monastery.
Visit the monks' shop for their eucalyptus liqueur.
Combine your visit with a walk through nearby EUR.
