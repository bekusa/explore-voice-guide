The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and let your eyes adjust. The white here is not soft. It is bright, almost hard, and on a sunny day it pushes back against the blue Roman sky. Under your feet is a plain, open paved square. Around you are apartment blocks, balconies hung with laundry, and the ordinary sounds of a residential neighbourhood. Then, rising out of all of this, three great curved walls stand like sails caught mid-turn.

This is the Jubilee Church. Its official name is Dio Padre Misericordioso, which means God the Merciful Father. Few visitors to Rome ever come out here. You are in Tor Tre Teste, a district on the eastern edge of the city, far from the Colosseum and the Vatican. That distance matters. It is a large part of the story of why this building exists at all.

In the 1990s, the Diocese of Rome looked at its outer suburbs. These districts had grown quickly after the Second World War, with tens of thousands of new residents in tower blocks. Many of these neighbourhoods had no proper church and no real centre. So the diocese launched a programme to build dozens of new parish churches before the year 2000. The Catholic Church called that year the Great Jubilee, a special holy year of pilgrimage and forgiveness.

This church was meant to be the flagship of that programme. The diocese held an international competition. The invited architects included some of the best-known names of the era. Tadao Ando from Japan was among them. So were Frank Gehry and Santiago Calatrava. The winner was the American architect Richard Meier, who is famous for his white, light-filled buildings. Here is the first surprise. Meier is Jewish, and he was chosen to design a Catholic church in the city of the popes.

The Jubilee date came and went before the building was ready. Construction took longer than planned, and the church was consecrated in 2003. Meier later designed another modern project in Rome, the museum pavilion that houses the Ara Pacis, an ancient Roman altar of peace near the Tiber. That museum caused loud public arguments in the city. This church, out in the suburbs, caused far fewer.

Now look again at those three curved walls. They rise in steps, each one taller than the one before it. Many people read them as a symbol of the Holy Trinity, the Christian idea of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as one God. The curves are sections of a sphere. Meier wanted shapes that feel like they belong to something larger than the building itself... a shell, a sail, a fragment of a globe.

Here is the second surprise, and it is one most visitors miss completely. Those white walls are made of huge precast concrete blocks, cast in a factory and lifted into place. The concrete contains titanium dioxide. When sunlight falls on it, a chemical reaction helps to break down dirt and some pollutants from the air. In other words, the walls were designed to help keep themselves white. In a city of traffic and dust, that is a practical answer to a real problem.

Building those curves was not simple. Each block had to fit a precise curved surface, and the walls lean and turn in ways that ordinary building methods could not easily handle. Engineers had to find ways to cast, transport, and set very heavy curved pieces with great accuracy. When you look closely, you can see the joints between the blocks. They form a quiet grid across the white surface.

On the other side of the shells, the building changes character. The north side is a straight, flat wall, more closed and more ordinary. It faces a smaller community wing with rooms for parish life. This contrast is deliberate. The church turns its curved, open face toward the sun and the square. Its practical side faces the work of the parish — meetings, classes, the everyday life of a neighbourhood.

If the church is open, step inside. The interior is simple and calm. Light is the main material here. It enters through tall glass between the curved walls and through glazing overhead. As the sun moves, the light slides across the white surfaces and the pale wood. The space feels very different at morning, at noon, and late in the afternoon. Please remember that this is a working parish church. Keep your voice low, and respect any service in progress.

Almost every surface is white, which can feel cold in photographs. In person, it often feels warm. The white walls reflect every change in the sky. A passing cloud dims the whole room. Then the sun returns and the space brightens again. Meier often said that white helps people see light, shadow, and form more clearly. Standing here, you can test that idea for yourself.

Think, too, about the people who live around this square. For them, this is not an architectural landmark. It is where children are baptised and where funerals are held. Older residents remember when there was no church here at all. Some neighbours found the design strange at first. Over time, it has become part of the daily view from their windows, as ordinary to them as the bus stop or the corner bar.

That tension is part of what makes the visit worthwhile. In central Rome, churches carry centuries of marble, gold, and painted ceilings. Here, the language is concrete, glass, and sunlight. Both kinds of building try to do the same thing. They try to give ordinary people a space that feels set apart from daily life. One uses gilding. The other uses light.

Before you leave, walk slowly around the outside once more. Watch how the three walls overlap and separate as you move, like pages turning. Notice the shadows they throw across the pavement. Listen to the neighbourhood around you — a scooter, a radio from a balcony, children in the square. Then look back one last time, and see how the white curves hold the light against the sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine the trip with public transport from central Rome.
Visit on a sunny day to see the light fully.
Check for services before entering the church interior.
Walk a full circle outside to see shifting curves.
Bring sunglasses; the white walls reflect strong sunlight.
