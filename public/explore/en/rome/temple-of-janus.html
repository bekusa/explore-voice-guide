The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Gravel shifts under shoes, a swift cuts across the sky, and somewhere a guide raises a voice in a language you may not know. The air smells of warm stone and dry grass. Around you lie broken columns, brick cores, and the long shadow of the Senate house. You are looking for a temple... and the first surprise is that you will not find one. The Temple of Janus is one of the most famous buildings in Roman history, and almost nothing of it survives.

So why does this place matter? Because the Romans gave this small shrine a job that no other building had. It was a measure of the whole state. Its doors stood open when Rome was at war, and they were closed only when Rome was at peace. Think about that for a moment. A citizen could walk past this spot and know, with one glance, whether the Republic was fighting somewhere in the world.

First, meet the god. Janus was one of the oldest and most Roman of the gods, with no clear match in the Greek world. He had two faces. One looked forward and one looked back. He watched over doorways, gates, passages, and beginnings of every kind. The Latin word for a door, janua, is related to his name. And every time you write the word January, you are using his name too — the month that opens the year, looking back at the old one and forward to the new.

The building was not a grand temple with a wide staircase and a forest of columns. Ancient writers describe something much smaller. It was a kind of covered gateway, with doors at each end, and inside stood a statue of the two-faced god. The Romans often called it Janus Geminus, which means the twin Janus, because of its two sets of doors. Some scholars think it began as a ceremonial gate on an old route into the Forum.

Where exactly did it stand? Ancient sources place it close to the Senate house, the Curia, near the point where a street called the Argiletum entered the Forum. Look for the tall brick building with the plain front. That is the Curia, rebuilt in late antiquity. The shrine stood somewhere in this zone. But archaeologists have never found remains they can identify with certainty. You are standing near the right place, but no one can point to the exact paving stone.

Roman tradition linked the shrine to Numa, the second king of Rome. Legend has it that Numa, a peaceful and religious ruler, set up the shrine and its custom of the doors. These early kings belong more to story than to documented history, so treat this as Rome's own memory of itself. What the tale tells us is clear, though. The Romans believed that peace was rare, precious, and worth marking in public.

Here is the second surprise. The doors were almost never shut. Roman writers say that across the long centuries of the Republic, the shrine was closed only a handful of times. One closing came after the First Punic War, the great struggle against Carthage, a rival city in North Africa. For most of Roman history, the doors simply stood open, because somewhere, on some frontier, Roman soldiers were always fighting.

Then came Augustus, the first emperor. He understood the power of a symbol better than almost anyone. In his own account of his reign, carved in stone and copied across the empire, Augustus boasted that before his birth the doors of Janus had been closed only twice in the city's history. Under his rule, he said, the Senate ordered them closed three times. It was a message in wood and bronze. The age of civil war was over, and the emperor had brought peace.

Later emperors followed his example. Nero closed the doors and celebrated the moment on his coins. Some of those coins show the shrine itself, with its doors shut and a garland across the front. These small pieces of metal are among the best evidence we have for how the building looked. Picture a neat rectangular structure, with walls, a lattice of decoration, and a firm closed door facing the street.

The shrine lasted a very long time. A Byzantine historian named Procopius, writing in the sixth century, described it as still standing in front of the Senate house. He said it was made of bronze, small enough to hold only the statue of the god, and that its two faces looked east and west. He also tells a story that during a war of his own time, someone secretly tried to force the old doors open. Even then, after Christianity had replaced the old gods, the gesture still carried meaning.

If you want to see real stones connected to Janus, walk south toward the river. In the old vegetable market, the Forum Holitorium, the Romans built another temple to the same god after a naval victory against Carthage. Remains of several Republican temples survive there, built into the walls of the church of San Nicola in Carcere. One of them is often identified with Janus, although scholars still debate which is which. Nearby stands the four-sided Arch of Janus, a later and quite different monument.

Now come back to where you are. Look at the ground, the scattered marble, the grass between the stones. The Forum was once covered with buildings packed side by side, and this shrine was one of the smallest. Yet it carried one of the biggest ideas in the city. It turned war and peace into something you could see. A closed door meant the soldiers were home. An open door meant they were not.

As you walk on, glance at any doorway you pass, here or later in the city. The Romans believed a threshold was a place of change, guarded by a god who looked both ways. You have come from somewhere and you are going somewhere else. For a few minutes, you have stood in that in-between space — exactly where Janus always stood.
