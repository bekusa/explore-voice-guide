The guide

The story

Step out of the traffic on Via del Quirinale and climb the few curved steps. Push open the door. The street noise drops away almost at once. In front of you the room opens sideways, not forward, and the air smells faintly of wax and old stone. Look down at your feet, then slowly up. The walls glow in deep rose and red marble, and high above, gold catches whatever light comes through the windows. You are standing inside an oval.

This is Sant'Andrea al Quirinale, a small church dedicated to Saint Andrew, the fisherman who became one of Christ's first apostles. It was designed by Gian Lorenzo Bernini, the sculptor and architect whose work shapes so much of Baroque Rome. Baroque here means the dramatic, theatrical style of the seventeenth century, made to move people as much as to impress them. Bernini built it in the second half of that century. Building began in the late 1650s.

The patron was Cardinal Camillo Pamphilj, a nephew of Pope Innocent the Tenth. The church served the Jesuits, the religious order founded by Saint Ignatius of Loyola. More precisely, it served their novitiate, the house where young men trained before taking their vows. So this was never meant to be a grand cathedral. It was a chapel for students, set on a hill beside the papal palace. That modest purpose explains its intimate size.

Now here is the first surprise. Most churches lead your eye along a long central aisle toward the altar. Bernini turned the oval on its side. You enter on the short axis, so the altar is surprisingly close, straight ahead. The long axis runs left and right, and the side chapels curve away from you. The effect is that the whole room seems to wrap around you at once. Nothing is far away. Everything feels within reach.

The site forced part of this choice. The plot was shallow, squeezed between the street and the Jesuit buildings behind. Bernini could have built a cramped rectangle. Instead he used the limit as an idea. Many visitors never notice that the church is wider than it is deep. Stand still for a moment, turn your head from one side to the other, and you can feel it. The space feels larger because it bends.

Look straight ahead at the high altar. Four columns of rich, dark-veined marble frame a recess. Inside hangs a painting of the martyrdom of Saint Andrew, shown on his cross. It was painted by Guillaume Courtois, a French-born artist working in Rome. Gilded angels seem to carry the painting down from above on a cloud of gold rays. It looks less like a picture on a wall and more like a scene arriving from heaven.

Notice where the light comes from. Bernini hid a window above the altar, so daylight falls onto the scene from a source you cannot easily see. He was a sculptor first, and he used light the way a stage designer would. On a sunny day the gilded rays almost seem to shine on their own. On a grey day, the room turns softer and deeper. The building changes with the weather outside.

Now follow the story upward. Above the altar's pediment, breaking out of the architecture, a white marble figure of Saint Andrew rises on a cloud. He looks up, arms open, toward the dome. This sculpture was carved by Antonio Raggi, one of Bernini's trusted assistants. Here is the second surprise. The church tells one continuous story in three levels. The martyrdom is painted below, the saint's soul rises in stone, and heaven waits in gold above.

Tilt your head back to the dome. Gilded coffers, the sunken panels of the ceiling, shrink as they climb toward the lantern at the top. Around the base, small white figures of children and young men sit along the window frames. Some hold garlands. Look for the fishermen and the nets and shells among them, a quiet reference to Andrew's first trade on the Sea of Galilee. At the very top, in the lantern, a dove represents the Holy Spirit.

The side chapels hold more paintings and coloured marble, and one deserves a slow visit. A chapel on the left is dedicated to Saint Stanislaus Kostka, a young Polish nobleman who walked to Rome to join the Jesuits. He entered this very novitiate and died here as a teenager in the sixteenth century. He later became a saint and a patron of young people. His remains rest in this church. In the rooms of the old novitiate, a separate area, a remarkable statue shows him on his deathbed.

According to an account left by Bernini's son, the artist thought of this church as one of the works that satisfied him most. Locals like to repeat a gentler version of the story, that the old sculptor would sometimes come here simply to sit and enjoy it. Treat that second part as a well-loved tale rather than firm record. Either way, it is easy to see why the building mattered to him. Every element serves one single idea.

When you leave, look back at the façade. A tall frame of flat pilasters holds a single large opening. Below it, a semicircular porch of columns pushes out toward the street, and curved walls reach out on either side like open arms. Above the door is the Pamphilj coat of arms, with a dove holding an olive branch. A short walk along this same street brings you to San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane, a tiny church by Bernini's great rival, Francesco Borromini. Seeing both in one walk is one of the best lessons in Baroque Rome.

Before you go, pause on the steps. Across the road stands the long wall of the Quirinal Palace, once home to popes and now to the President of Italy. Behind you, the quiet oval keeps its rose marble and gold away from the traffic. As you walk on, notice how plain the street feels after that interior... and how a small, shallow plot became one of the most complete rooms in the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a sunny day to see the hidden altar light.
Stand just inside the door and turn slowly side to side.
Pair the visit with Borromini's San Carlo down the street.
Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees, for this active church.
Ask whether the Saint Stanislaus rooms are open during your visit.
