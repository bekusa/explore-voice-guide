The guide

The story

Run your hand near the stone, and you will feel it first. The travertine is pale, porous, and rough, full of tiny holes like a dried sponge. Traffic hums along Via di San Vito. A scooter rattles past on the cobbles. And here, squeezed between ordinary apartment buildings and a small church, one tall Roman arch stands in the open air. It carries no fence or grand piazza. It simply stands among the doorways of a working neighbourhood.

This is Porta Esquilina. Most Romans today know it by another name, the Arch of Gallienus. The two names describe two lives of the same spot. The first name is much older. It belongs to a city gate on the Esquiline Hill, one of the famous seven hills of Rome. The second name belongs to an emperor who reigned during a crisis in the third century. To understand this arch, you need both stories.

Start with the gate. Long before this arch existed, a defensive wall ran across this hill. Romans called it the Servian Wall, after Servius Tullius, one of the early kings of Rome. That link to a king is tradition. Historians generally date the stone circuit we know to the fourth century before Christ, after the Gauls sacked Rome. Either way, this was a real opening in a real wall. People, carts, and animals passed through here into the city.

Now look at where the gate led. Outside it, the ground became a place for the dead. The Esquiline was a burial area for the poor, and Roman writers describe it as a grim, unpleasant place. Then, in the age of Augustus, the wealthy patron Maecenas built gardens over part of this land. The poet Horace, his friend, wrote about the change. Bones gave way to flower beds and shady walks. So this gate once stood on the border between a city and its cemetery.

The arch you see is not the original Republican gate. Under Augustus, the opening was rebuilt in a more monumental form. Scholars believe it originally had three passages. A wide central arch carried the road, and two smaller arches stood at the sides. Today only the central opening survives. Picture two more arches on either side of you, and the monument suddenly becomes much wider and more formal.

Here is the first surprise. The emperor whose name the arch carries did not build it. In the third century, an official named Marcus Aurelius Victor rededicated the existing gate to Emperor Gallienus and his wife, Salonina. He did this by adding a new inscription. In other words, the monument was recycled. Romans did this often. A new dedication was cheaper than new construction, and it still sent a clear message of loyalty.

Look up at the attic, the flat band of stone above the arch. The inscription is carved there in Latin capitals. It praises Gallienus as a most merciful ruler, whose unconquered courage was surpassed only by his devotion. It is warm language. But the reality was harsh. Gallienus ruled in a period of invasions, rebellions, and breakaway provinces. His father, Emperor Valerian, was captured by the Persians, a shocking humiliation for Rome.

So read the inscription with some care. Its words are confident, but the empire around them was under heavy strain. Within a few years, another emperor, Aurelian, began a much larger wall around Rome. That new circuit, the Aurelian Wall, enclosed far more of the city. Once it existed, this old gate stood inside the defences. It lost its military purpose and became a monument on a street.

Now notice the architecture itself. Flat pilasters frame the opening on each side. These are shallow rectangular columns set against the wall. Their tops are Corinthian, a style decorated with carved leaves. The stone is plain compared with the great triumphal arches near the Forum. There are no battle scenes and no marching soldiers. The power of this arch comes from its proportion and its height, not from decoration.

Here is the second surprise. The side arches did not fall down by accident. They were taken apart in the fifteenth century, during a period when Rome often treated ancient ruins as a source of building material. Old stone was cut, reused, or burned for lime. What survives here survived partly because it was useful as a passage. A road still runs through it, just as roads always have.

There is also a story from the Middle Ages. According to one tradition, a chain with the keys of a gate from Viterbo, a rival town north of Rome, once hung from this arch. It was a trophy, a public sign of victory over a neighbour. Treat this as a local tale passed down over the centuries. Still, it shows how people kept giving this stone new meanings long after the emperors were gone.

The church beside you belongs to the same story. It is dedicated to Saints Vitus and Modestus, and the arch almost leans against it. The street takes its name from Saint Vitus. Around you, the Esquiline today is one of the most varied districts in Rome. Nearby Piazza Vittorio has markets, grocery shops, and families from many countries. Ancient Rome and present-day Rome share the same pavement here.

Before you go, stand back on the far side of the street. Look at the arch against the buildings around it. Notice how the newer walls press close, almost touching the old stone. Listen to the traffic passing under an opening that carts and travellers used two thousand years ago. As you walk away toward the hill, the road beneath your feet still follows the line of that ancient gate.
