The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water falls into a stone basin somewhere behind you. Voices drift across a small sloping square, mixed with the clink of coffee cups and the scrape of a chair on cobbles. Above it all rises a pale church front, the colour of old travertine. Sunlight moves slowly across its pilasters. This is Santa Maria ai Monti, the parish church at the heart of Rome's Monti district, and the square in front of it is one of the city's favourite living rooms.

Before the church, a word about the neighbourhood. Monti means mountains, or hills. The name comes from the slopes of the Esquiline, Viminal and Quirinal hills, which meet in this low ground. In ancient times this area was part of the Suburra. That was a crowded, noisy, working district of tenements, taverns and small shops. The Roman Forum lies only a short walk downhill from here. Monti has always felt close to the old city's centre, but a little apart from its grandeur.

Now the story of the church itself. It begins not with an architect, but with an image. In the late sixteenth century, this site held the remains of an older religious house. According to the church's own tradition, workers found a painted image of the Virgin Mary with the Christ Child among the ruins. Some accounts say the space was being used to store hay. Word spread quickly through the neighbourhood. People began to say the image had worked miracles, and crowds came to pray before it.

The response was a new church, built to house that painting. Construction began around 1580, during the reign of Pope Gregory the Thirteenth. The design is attributed to Giacomo della Porta. He was one of the busiest architects in Rome at that time. He worked on the dome of Saint Peter's Basilica after Michelangelo, and he completed many church façades across the city. So this modest neighbourhood church has a link to some of the largest building projects of its age.

Look up at the front of the church. If it feels familiar, there is a good reason. Many people notice that it resembles the façade of the Gesù, the mother church of the Jesuit order in Rome. Della Porta was involved there too. You can see the same two-storey arrangement. A wider lower level is joined to a narrower upper level by large curving scrolls. This pattern became a model for countless churches across Europe and Latin America over the following centuries.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The church does not stand on a grand open piazza laid out for display. It sits on a sloping square, with streets entering from several directions. Della Porta and his builders had to fit a sixteenth-century church into an older, irregular street plan. Notice how the buildings lean in around it. Their ochre and rust-red walls, green shutters and small balconies frame the white façade. The church feels less like a monument and more like a neighbour.

Now step inside, if the doors are open. The interior follows a simple, clear plan. A single central nave leads your eye towards the high altar, with side chapels opening on either side. Above the crossing sits a dome, which pulls light down into the space. The decoration is rich with gilding, painted ceilings and coloured marble. Yet the scale stays human. It is a church built for a parish, for people who come in daily, not for processions of cardinals.

At the high altar, look for the image that started everything. This is the painting of the Madonna and Child that gave the church its name, Our Lady of the Hills. For more than four centuries, people from this district have come here to light candles before it. Many local families mark baptisms, weddings and funerals in this building. If you visit on a quiet weekday, you may see an older resident stop for a short prayer, then walk out again into the street.

And now, a second surprise. This church is the resting place of a saint who lived as a homeless beggar. His name was Benedict Joseph Labre. He was a French pilgrim of the eighteenth century who walked thousands of kilometres between holy sites across Europe. He spent his last years in Rome, sleeping rough near the Colosseum and praying for hours in churches. He died in this neighbourhood in 1783, and the people of Monti brought him here.

Labre owned almost nothing. He wore ragged clothes and ate whatever people gave him. Yet after his death, local devotion to him grew very quickly. The Catholic Church declared him a saint in the late nineteenth century. Today he is often remembered as a patron of the homeless and of pilgrims. Look for his tomb inside the church. It is a quiet reminder that this building has always belonged to ordinary people, including the poorest of them.

Back outside, turn to the fountain in the middle of the square. It is also linked to Giacomo della Porta and dates from the same late sixteenth century period. Its stone basin sits on a set of low steps. For centuries, people came here to collect water for their homes. Now the steps serve a different purpose. On warm evenings, they fill with students, locals and travellers, sitting with a drink, talking until late. It is one of the most relaxed scenes in central Rome.

The streets around the square are worth a slow walk. Monti today mixes old workshops, small bakeries, vintage clothing stores and family-run restaurants. Laundry hangs from upper windows. Ivy climbs across doorways. You may hear Italian spoken loudly between balconies, or the rattle of a scooter on the cobbles. Despite the visitors, many Romans still live here. That gives the area a lived-in feeling. The church remains its centre, ringing bells for the hours and for Sunday Mass.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the fountain and look back at the façade. Notice how the light changes on the stone as the day moves on. In the morning it looks cool and grey. Later, it turns warm and golden. Listen again to the water falling into the basin. People have heard that same sound here for more than four hundred years. As you walk away, notice the small shrines and old doorways around you. The neighbourhood still carries its long story quietly.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the square on a warm evening to see locals.
Dress modestly with covered shoulders before entering.
Keep quiet inside, as this is an active parish church.
Combine your visit with a walk downhill to the Forum.
Explore the side streets of Monti on foot afterwards.
