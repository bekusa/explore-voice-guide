The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the paving stones of Piazza Colonna. Traffic hums along Via del Corso, scooters weave past, and somewhere a police radio crackles. Above you rises a long, calm facade the colour of warm sand, its windows lined up in tidy rows. This is Palazzo Chigi, the Chigi Palace. Behind those shutters, the Prime Minister of Italy goes to work.

That is the first surprise. Many visitors walk past, admire the column in the square, and never realise they are standing outside the seat of the Italian government. Since 1961, the palace has housed the Presidency of the Council of Ministers. When Italian news reports what happened at Palazzo Chigi, they mean what happened inside these walls. The name has become shorthand for power itself.

Yet the building is much older than modern Italy. Construction began in the late sixteenth century for the Aldobrandini, a powerful Roman family. Rome at that time was the city of the popes. Noble families competed to build grand homes along the main streets. Via del Corso was one of the best addresses, a long straight road where processions and races passed under the windows.

The palace took many decades to finish. Several architects worked on it, one after another, as owners changed and ambitions grew. That slow process is common in Rome. A single building can carry the ideas of different generations. Look at the facade and you will see restraint rather than drama. It belongs to the sober end of Roman palace design, before the full energy of the Baroque took over.

In the middle of the seventeenth century, the building passed to the family that gave it its name. The Chigi were bankers from Siena. Their fortunes rose sharply when one of them, Fabio Chigi, became Pope Alexander the Seventh in 1655. A pope in the family meant money, prestige, and a need for a fitting Roman home. Buying this palace on the Corso was a clear statement. The Chigi had arrived.

Now look for their badge. The Chigi coat of arms shows a small stack of rounded hills, often topped by a star. You will find this symbol across Rome, on churches, fountains, and gates connected to Alexander the Seventh. Once you notice those little mounds, you start to see them everywhere. They are a kind of signature, left by a family that wanted the city to remember them.

The Chigi lived here for generations, and the palace also held a remarkable library. Over time it gathered manuscripts and rare books of great value. In the twentieth century, after the Italian state bought the building, that collection was given to the Vatican Library. So part of the old life of this house now sits across the river, in the care of the popes once again.

The state purchase came in the early twentieth century. The palace then entered a new career as a government building. For a time it served ministries connected to foreign affairs and Italy's colonies. During the years between the two world wars, it was linked to the foreign policy of Mussolini's regime. Those were difficult decades, and the building carries that memory too, alongside its papal past.

After the war, Italy became a republic. In 1961 the palace took on the role it holds today. Ministers meet here. Foreign leaders arrive here for official visits. Press conferences happen in rooms decorated centuries ago for a banking family. There is something very Roman in that layering. A Baroque salon becomes a modern office, and nobody thinks it strange.

Turn around now and look at the column in the centre of the square. This is the Column of Marcus Aurelius, the Roman emperor of the second century. A spiral band of carving winds up the shaft, showing his military campaigns. The square takes its name from this column, colonna in Italian. The palace was built to face it, so ancient Rome and government Rome stand side by side.

Nearby, a low fountain adds the sound of water to the scene. It dates from the late sixteenth century, roughly the same era when the palace was begun. Behind the palace, almost touching it, stands Palazzo Montecitorio, home of the Chamber of Deputies. In a few steps, then, you pass the heart of both the government and the parliament. Rome keeps its politics close together, in very old buildings.

A practical note. The palace is a working government office, so it is not open like a museum. Security staff and police are part of the scene, and you may see official cars come and go. On occasional special days, the doors open to the public by reservation, and visitors can see the courtyard and the main halls. If you are curious, it is worth checking ahead before your trip.

Until then, the outside tells its own story. Notice how the facade meets the street at eye level, with heavy stone around the main doorway. Look up at the cornice, the deep stone ledge along the roofline, which throws a strong band of shadow in the afternoon. Try to imagine the Corso four centuries ago, with carriages, horses, and crowds watching from these windows during carnival races.

As you leave, take one last look from the edge of the square. The emperor's column, the banker's palace, the parliament behind it, the shoppers on the Corso. All of them share the same small patch of ground. Listen again to the traffic and the water. This is not a frozen monument. It is a building still at work, and you have just walked past the door.
