The guide

The story

The air changes first. A few steps down from the daylight of the Roman countryside, the warmth falls away and a cool, steady dampness settles on your skin. The walls close in, soft brown and grainy. If you brush them with a fingertip, they feel almost like packed sand. This is tufa, a volcanic stone that is easy to carve when fresh and hardens when it meets the air. Everything around you was cut by hand, one gallery at a time.

You are standing in the Catacombs of Domitilla, one of the largest underground cemeteries in Rome. The tunnels run for many kilometres, stacked on several levels beneath the fields and lanes south of the city walls. Above you, traffic moves along modern roads. Below, the silence is so complete that you can hear the guide's footsteps several turns ahead. Few visitors expect the scale. This is not one room or one crypt... it is a buried city of the dead.

The name comes from Flavia Domitilla, a woman of the Flavian family, the dynasty of emperors that built the Colosseum. Ancient Christian writers describe her as a relative of the emperors who was sent into exile for her faith. Historians still debate the details of her story. What seems clear is that land here belonged to the Flavian family, and that a private family tomb on this property later grew into a Christian cemetery. So a burial ground connected to imperial power slowly became a place of the faithful.

Why dig underground at all? Roman law did not allow burials inside the city, so cemeteries spread along the roads outside the walls. Early Christians did not cremate their dead. They wanted to bury the body whole, in hope of resurrection. Land was expensive, and communities grew. The answer was to go down. Diggers cut long corridors into the tufa and carved shelves into the walls, then dug deeper when the upper levels filled.

Look at the walls of any gallery. You will see rows of rectangular niches, one above the other, like bunks in a narrow cabin. These are called loculi. Each one held a body wrapped in cloth. The opening was sealed with a slab of marble or with tiles held in place by mortar. Most of those slabs are gone now, taken over the centuries. Some niches are very small. They held children... and there are many of them.

Here and there, the corridor opens into a small room. These rooms are called cubicula, and wealthier families bought them as private chambers. Inside, you may see an arcosolium — a tomb set into the wall under a carved arch. These spaces were painted, and some paintings survive. The colours are mostly soft reds, ochres, and greens, applied to plaster that clings to the stone. In the lamplight they appear suddenly, as if a window has opened in the rock.

The images are a quiet surprise. Many are not grand scenes but simple signs. A shepherd carries a sheep across his shoulders. This is the Good Shepherd, an image of Christ that early believers borrowed from older pagan art. You may also see figures standing with arms raised in prayer, doves, and scenes from the Old Testament. In some tombs, pagan and Christian themes sit side by side. The line between the old world and the new was not as sharp as we often imagine.

One painting in this catacomb shows a woman named Veneranda being led into paradise by Saint Petronilla, a figure honoured by early Roman Christians. Another set of images shows the fossores, the men who dug these tunnels. They are shown with their tools — picks and lamps. It is rare for ancient art to honour labourers like this. These were the workers who shaped every metre you walk through, and here they are remembered in paint.

Now comes the second surprise. Part of this site is not a tunnel at all. In the late fourth century, a basilica was built here, sunk partly into the ground so that it reached the level of the martyrs' tombs. It is dedicated to Saints Nereus and Achilleus. According to an inscription linked to Pope Damasus, they were Roman soldiers who left the army after becoming Christians and were put to death for it. Their graves drew pilgrims, and the church grew around them.

Look closely at the remains of the basilica's columns. One small column carries a carved scene of a martyrdom, with a soldier about to strike and a name inscribed above. Scholars see it as one of the early images of a martyr's death in Christian art. It is easy to walk past. Many visitors do. Pause for a moment and you can see the hands of an ancient sculptor telling a story to pilgrims who could not read.

Over time, the danger passed and the habits changed. Burials moved back above ground, and the catacombs fell out of use. Relics were taken into churches inside the city. Earth and collapse sealed many entrances, and the galleries were forgotten for centuries. In the nineteenth century, the Roman archaeologist Giovanni Battista de Rossi led serious study of the Christian catacombs. His work helped bring this basilica and these galleries back into the light.

Today the catacombs belong to the Holy See, and a religious order cares for the site. Visits are guided, and for good reason. The tunnels twist and branch in every direction, and only a small part is open to the public. Without a guide, it would be very easy to get lost. Listen to the stillness between the guide's sentences. Notice how the sound stays close to you, absorbed by the soft stone instead of echoing back.

Think for a moment about the people who walked here first. Families came down these same narrow passages carrying oil lamps. They brought flowers, food, and wine to share at the graves on anniversaries. Some scratched names or prayers into the plaster. They were not hiding in fear, as old stories often claim. They were visiting their dead, the way people still do in cemeteries everywhere. The darkness was simply where their loved ones rested.

When you climb back up the steps, let your eyes adjust slowly. The warm air, the cypress trees, the noise of the road — they will feel sharper after the quiet below. Somewhere under your feet, kilometres of galleries continue in the dark, mostly unvisited. As you walk away, look at the ground you are crossing. Rome is not only the city you see. It is also the city beneath, carved by hand and still keeping its silence.

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for uneven, damp stone floors.
Stay close to your guide in the branching galleries.
Ask your guide before taking any photos underground.
