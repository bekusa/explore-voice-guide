The guide

The story

Listen first. Engines idle, a scooter whines past, and somewhere under the noise there is a thin, steady sound of running water. You are standing at one of the tightest crossroads in Rome. Four streets meet here at the top of the Quirinal Hill, and each corner has been cut at an angle to hold a fountain. The pavement is narrow, the stone is warm, and the traffic does not slow down for anyone.

This is Quattro Fontane, which simply means the Four Fountains. Most visitors reach it by accident. They are walking between two famous churches, or heading for the Trevi Fountain, and suddenly the street opens into this small square of chopped corners. It is not a piazza in the usual sense. There are no benches and no café tables. It is a junction, and it was designed as a junction. That is the first surprise, and it is the key to everything else here.

The man behind it was Pope Sixtus the Fifth, who ruled the Church for only five years in the late sixteenth century. He was in a hurry. Sixtus wanted to tie the great pilgrimage churches of Rome together with long, straight roads. Pilgrims could then walk from one holy site to the next without getting lost in the old medieval tangle. This crossroads sits on two of those new lines. In a sense, you are standing at a knot in his plan for the whole city.

Now try something most people miss. Turn slowly and look down each of the four streets. Along one axis, the street runs toward the Esquiline Hill and the basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore, where an Egyptian-style obelisk stands. In the opposite direction, the road climbs toward the Spanish Steps and another obelisk in front of the church of Trinità dei Monti. Along the cross street, you can see toward a third obelisk outside the Quirinal Palace. At the far end of the long straight road, a monumental city gate closes the view.

That gate is Porta Pia, and its design is credited to Michelangelo near the end of his life. So from one cramped street corner, you can line up views of distant landmarks across the city. This was intentional. Sixtus and his planners used obelisks and gates like markers on a map. They gave walkers a target to aim for. Today, cars and buses fill the line of sight, but the idea still works. Stand still for a moment, and the city suddenly feels organised.

The fountains themselves needed water, and water on a hill in Rome was a serious problem. The ancient aqueducts had long since broken down. Sixtus restored an old supply line and brought fresh water back to these eastern hills. The new aqueduct took his own birth name, Felice, and is known as the Acqua Felice. These four fountains were among the first places where that water appeared in public. For local people, clean running water here was a real change in daily life.

Look at each corner in turn. Two of the fountains show reclining river gods, bearded and heavy, resting on one elbow. One is the Tiber, the river of Rome, and you may spot a she-wolf near him, the animal from Rome's founding legend. The second river god is usually called the Arno, the river of Florence, though some scholars argue it is the Aniene, a river near Rome. Nobody has settled the question for certain, so you are free to choose.

The other two corners hold female figures. They are commonly identified as the goddesses Diana and Juno. They are often read as symbols of fidelity and strength. The carving is soft and a little weathered, because these figures have spent more than four hundred years beside a busy road. A recent restoration cleaned away much of the black grime from exhaust fumes. Even so, the stone still shows its age in rounded edges and faded details.

Now turn to the corner with the curving white façade. That is the small church of San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane, which Romans affectionately call San Carlino. It was designed by Francesco Borromini, one of the great architects of the Baroque era, the dramatic style that shaped seventeenth-century Rome. The site was tiny and awkward. Borromini answered with a front that seems to bend in and out, like a wave in stone. Notice how the fountain is built into the base of the church itself.

Here is the second surprise. Inside San Carlino, the oval dome is so small that, according to a popular comparison, the whole church could fit inside one of the great piers of Saint Peter's Basilica. Whether or not the measurement is exact, the feeling is true. The interior is pale, almost white, and full of geometry. Hexagons, crosses and octagons shrink toward the lantern at the top, so the dome looks deeper than it really is. If the door is open, step inside for a few quiet minutes.

Just a short walk along the street toward the Quirinal Palace, you will find another small church, Sant'Andrea al Quirinale. It was designed by Gian Lorenzo Bernini, Borromini's great rival. Their two churches stand within easy walking distance, each a different answer to the same question. How do you make a small space feel like heaven? Many visitors like to see both on the same walk and compare them. It is one of the best free lessons in architecture in Rome.

A practical word about where you are standing. The sidewalks here are very narrow, and the corners are tight. Cars, buses and scooters come fast around every turn. The best way to see all four fountains is to cross carefully at the lights and look from the opposite corner, rather than leaning into the road. Early morning is calmer, and the soft light falls kindly on the stone. Later in the day, the traffic becomes heavier and the noise rises.

Before you leave, stand still once more and listen. Under the engines, the water is still running, as it has since a pope decided to bring it up this hill. The river gods still lean on their elbows. The long straight streets still point toward obelisks you can just make out in the distance. As you walk away in any direction, look back once. You will see how this busy little crossroads quietly holds the shape of Rome together.
