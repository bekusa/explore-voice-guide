The guide

The story

Stand for a moment near the great triple arch of Septimius Severus and look down, not up. At your feet the ground drops into a patchwork of grey and brown stone. Here and there, between fences and fallen marble, you can see a rougher surface. It is soft volcanic rock called tufa, pitted and cut by hand. That plain stone is one of the oldest sacred places in Rome. It is the Vulcanal, the open-air sanctuary of Vulcan, god of fire.

Most visitors walk straight past it. There is no temple here, no columns, no dome. The Romans did not need one. The Vulcanal was an area, a raised platform of rock where people met the god under the open sky. Ancient writers called it the area Volcani, the precinct of Vulcan. Its simplicity is the point. It belongs to an age before marble, when the Forum was still a damp valley between hills.

Think about where you are standing. Behind you, the Capitoline Hill rises steeply. Ahead, the Forum stretches toward the Colosseum. Long before emperors, this low ground was marshy and prone to flooding. Huts stood on the surrounding hills, and the valley floor was a shared space between communities. A rocky outcrop above the wet ground was a natural place for meetings, oaths, and offerings. The Vulcanal sat on just such a rise.

Here is the first surprise. Romans usually placed shrines to Vulcan outside the city. Fire was dangerous, and a fire god belonged at a safe distance from homes. Yet this shrine sat at the very heart of public life. Roman writers explained the puzzle with tradition. They said the Vulcanal was older than the city as it later grew. In their telling, the shrine was here first, and Rome expanded around it.

Legend has it that Romulus himself founded this sanctuary. According to the old stories, he met here with Titus Tatius, king of the Sabines, after their two peoples made peace. Ancient authors also say Romulus set up a bronze chariot here in honour of Vulcan. These are tales the Romans told about their own beginnings. Historians treat them as legend, not record. Still, they show how deeply the Romans connected this rock with the birth of their city.

The Vulcanal also stood beside the Comitium, the assembly place of early Rome. Citizens gathered there to vote and to hear speeches. Very close by lies the Lapis Niger, the black stone, which marks another ancient shrine. Under it, excavators found one of the oldest Latin inscriptions known. Together, these places form the oldest civic core of the Forum. The Vulcanal stood a little higher than the rest, looking over the crowd.

Now a second surprise, and it involves a tree. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder described a lotus tree growing at the Vulcanal. He said people believed it was as old as the city itself. Its roots, he wrote, spread far under the Forum. Whether any tree truly lived that long, we cannot say. But imagine a gnarled, ancient trunk on this rock, shading the god's precinct in the middle of a growing capital.

The Vulcanal was also a place where Romans watched for signs. Roman historians sometimes report strange omens here, such as showers of what people called blood or stones falling from the sky. Romans took such events seriously. Priests would decide how to calm the gods. A shrine of fire, close to the voting ground, was exactly where people expected heaven to send messages to the city.

Each year, the Romans held a festival for Vulcan called the Volcanalia, in late August. It fell in the hottest, driest weeks of the year, when fire posed the greatest risk to crops and crowded houses. Ancient sources say worshippers threw small fish into fire as an offering. The idea seems to have been a trade. Give the god living things from the water, and he would spare the grain and the homes.

Over the centuries, the Forum changed around this rock. Brick and marble buildings rose on every side. In the early third century, the arch of Septimius Severus went up right beside the old precinct. Speakers' platforms were rebuilt. Ground levels rose. The open sanctuary shrank into a corner, then disappeared under later layers. For a long time, it was known mainly from books, not from the ground.

Archaeology brought it back to light. Excavations in the Forum around the start of the twentieth century uncovered the black stone and the early levels nearby. Archaeologists identified the cut tufa surface near the arch as remains linked to the Vulcanal. You can still see traces of channels and cuttings in the rock. Scholars continue to debate exactly what each feature was for. That uncertainty is part of the honest story of this place.

From where you stand, notice the mix of materials. Pale travertine and white marble belong to imperial Rome. Darker, crumbly tufa belongs to a much earlier city. Bricks show later repairs. Fences and modern walkways show the work of the people who protect the site today. In one small view, you can read several stages of Rome stacked together, from rough rock to polished stone.

Before you move on, take one more look at that low grey rock beside the arch. It is easy to miss, and that is fitting. The Romans came here not for grandeur, but for something older. As you walk toward the Via Sacra, listen to the footsteps and voices around you. People have gathered on this patch of ground for nearly three thousand years. Today, you are one of them.
