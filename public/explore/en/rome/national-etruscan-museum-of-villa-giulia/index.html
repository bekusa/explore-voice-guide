The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Rome fades behind you, and the gravel crunches softly underfoot. Ahead, a curved portico opens like a pair of arms. Its ceiling is painted with vines, birds and small playful figures. The air in the courtyard feels cooler than the street. You are standing in a Renaissance pleasure villa, and inside its rooms waits the largest collection of Etruscan art in the world.

First, the house itself. Villa Giulia was built in the 1550s for Pope Julius the Third. He wanted a retreat outside the city walls, a place for summer evenings, music and conversation. It was never meant as a main residence. Think of it as a papal garden house, built to impress guests rather than to shelter a household. Several of the best artists of the age worked on it.

The architect Giacomo Barozzi da Vignola designed much of the main building. Bartolomeo Ammannati, a sculptor and architect from Florence, shaped the courtyards. Giorgio Vasari, better known today for his biographies of artists, was also involved. Michelangelo is said to have been consulted along the way. So the walls around you carry the work of several hands. That explains why the villa feels like a sequence of surprises rather than one single design.

Walk through the courtyard toward the back, and look down. The ground drops away into a sunken garden called the nymphaeum. A nymphaeum is a cool, shaded space dedicated to water and to nymphs, the spirits of springs in ancient myth. Statues stand in niches. Stairs curve down on both sides. On a hot day, this lower level was the coolest place on the estate. Guests of the Pope came here to escape the Roman summer.

Now, the people this museum celebrates. The Etruscans lived in central Italy, in a region covering much of modern Tuscany, northern Lazio and part of Umbria. Their civilization flourished before Rome became powerful. In fact, early Rome borrowed a great deal from them, including religious rituals and symbols of authority. Over the centuries, Rome absorbed Etruscan cities one by one. The Etruscans did not vanish overnight. They slowly became Roman.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. We can read the Etruscan alphabet, because it comes from the Greek one. We can pronounce the words. But we still understand only part of the language. It is unrelated to Latin, Greek, or most other European tongues. Scholars know many names, numbers and religious terms. Long texts remain difficult. So the Etruscans speak to us mostly through objects, through the things they made, wore, and buried with their dead.

The most famous of those objects is the Sarcophagus of the Spouses. It was found in a tomb at Cerveteri, an ancient Etruscan city north of Rome. It dates to the late sixth century before the common era. It is made of terracotta, which is fired clay, not stone. A man and a woman recline together, as if at a banquet. His arm rests around her shoulders. They are smiling.

Stay with this couple for a minute. Look at their almond-shaped eyes and their long braided hair. Notice their hands. They once held small objects, perhaps a cup or a perfume flask, now lost. The scene matters because it shows a woman at the banquet beside her husband, as an equal guest. In classical Greece, respectable wives did not usually recline at dinner parties. Etruscan women, it seems, had a more public social life.

In another room stands the Apollo of Veii. This life-size terracotta god once stood on the roof of a temple at Veii, a powerful Etruscan city just north of Rome. He strides forward with a slight smile. Painted traces still cling to the clay. Seeing a full figure made of fired earth, rather than marble, surprises many people. Building something this large in clay required great skill in firing and assembly.

Now a second surprise. Look for three thin sheets of gold, known as the Pyrgi Tablets. They come from a sanctuary at Pyrgi, the old harbour of Cerveteri. Two carry text in Etruscan. One carries text in Phoenician, the language of traders from the eastern Mediterranean. Because the texts cover a similar subject, they helped scholars understand Etruscan words. A small piece of gold became a rare key to a lost language.

The museum also holds remarkable bronze and gold work. Look for the Ficoroni Cista, a cylindrical bronze box from Palestrina, a hill town east of Rome. Its surface is engraved with a scene from the story of the Argonauts, Greek heroes who sailed in search of the Golden Fleece. Elsewhere, the jewellery rooms show tiny granules of gold, fused onto earrings and brooches. The detail is so fine that you may want to lean close.

Before you leave, step out into the garden. You will find a reconstruction of an Etruscan-style temple, built in the late nineteenth century to show how these sanctuaries may have looked. Notice the deep porch, the heavy roof, and the brightly painted clay decoration. Etruscan temples used wood and terracotta far more than stone. That is why so few survive, and why rooftop figures like the Apollo of Veii are precious.

One thought to carry with you. A Pope built this villa for pleasure, music and shade. Centuries later, it became a home for the possessions of people who lived here long before Rome ruled anything. Two very different worlds now share one courtyard. As you walk back through the curved portico, look up once more at the painted vines. Then listen again for the gravel under your feet, and the quiet city waiting beyond the gate.
