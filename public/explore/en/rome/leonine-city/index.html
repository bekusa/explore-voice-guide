The guide

The story

Stand for a moment with your back to the river and listen. Traffic hums along the embankment. Footsteps echo off old brick. Somewhere a church bell sounds over the rooftops of the Borgo. Above you, a long wall of weathered brick and stone runs toward the great round fortress of Castel Sant'Angelo. That wall is one of the oldest pieces of planning on this side of the Tiber. It is the spine of what Romans once called the Leonine City.

To understand why it exists, picture this area in the early ninth century. Saint Peter's Basilica already stood here. But it was not the building you see today. It was an older church from the age of Constantine, and it sat outside the ancient Aurelian walls that protected the city of Rome. Pilgrims came from all over Europe to pray at the tomb of the apostle. Yet the most sacred site in Western Christianity had no real defences at all.

In 846, that weakness turned into disaster. Arab raiders from the Mediterranean, whom medieval chroniclers called Saracens, sailed up the Tiber. They reached the basilica and plundered it. Treasures and sacred objects were carried away. Across Christian Europe, the news caused deep shock. If the shrine of Saint Peter could be robbed, then nowhere felt safe. The papacy knew that something had to change, and quickly.

The man who acted was Pope Leo the Fourth, elected the following year. He ordered a ring of walls to enclose the Vatican hill, the basilica, and the settlement around it. The work took several years. When it was finished, around 852, the pope walked the full circuit in a solemn procession. He blessed the walls and their gates. The newly protected district took his name — the Civitas Leonina, the City of Leo.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Leo did not build these walls with only Roman labour. Workers came from estates and towns across the papal lands, and different groups took responsibility for different stretches. In a sense, the wall is a shared project of the whole region. It also had a military partner at sea. In 849, a fleet of allied Italian cities defeated another Saracen force near Ostia, the old port at the mouth of the Tiber.

That sea battle, and other legends of Leo's reign, lived on in art. Inside the Vatican Museums, the rooms painted by Raphael and his workshop in the 1510s include a fresco called the Fire in the Borgo. According to a popular tale, a fire swept through this neighbourhood, and Leo stopped it with a blessing from a balcony. It is a legend, not a recorded fact. But the painters gave the scene a classical grandeur, and it shows how strongly the Renaissance remembered this pope.

Now look at the name of the district around you — the Borgo. Many historians link the word to the Germanic term for a fortified town, the same root behind English place names ending in burgh or bury. Long before Leo's walls, communities of foreign pilgrims settled here. Among them were Saxons from England, who kept a hostel and church close to the river. The nearby church of Santo Spirito in Sassia still carries their memory in its name. Sassia comes from Saxon.

So this was never just a fortress. It was a crossroads of languages. Pilgrims from England, the Frankish lands, and beyond slept, ate, prayed, and were buried here. Hostels, small churches, and workshops crowded the lanes. Think of it as an early international quarter, built around a single tomb. When you walk these streets today, you follow paths that tired travellers walked over a thousand years ago, after weeks on the road.

The most dramatic surviving piece of the walls is the raised corridor above you, called the Passetto di Borgo. It runs along the top of the old defences, linking the Vatican palaces with Castel Sant'Angelo. In the late thirteenth century, Pope Nicholas the Third reworked it into a protected escape route. The idea was simple. If danger came, a pope could hurry along the wall, out of sight, and shelter inside the fortress.

That escape route was used in one of the darkest moments in Roman history. In 1527, mutinous imperial troops stormed and sacked the city. Pope Clement the Seventh fled along the Passetto to Castel Sant'Angelo while fighting raged below. The Swiss Guard stood their ground near Saint Peter's to cover his retreat, and most of them were killed. Today the guard swears in new recruits on the anniversary of that day, the sixth of May.

For centuries, the Leonine City kept a separate identity. It had its own walls, and in practice its own administration under the popes. That changed in the late sixteenth century, when Pope Sixtus the Fifth formally joined the Borgo to Rome as one of its official districts, or rioni. The old boundary faded on paper. But the walls remained, and so did the feeling that this neighbourhood belonged as much to the Vatican as to the city.

The twentieth century brought the biggest change of all. In the 1930s, under the Fascist government and after an agreement with the Vatican, a block of old houses in the middle of the Borgo was demolished. In its place came the wide, straight Via della Conciliazione, leading toward Saint Peter's Square. Some people praise the grand view it opened. Others still mourn the narrow medieval lanes it erased, and the sudden surprise of seeing the basilica appear.

Before you move on, look again at the brick wall and the arches of the corridor overhead. Notice the patched surfaces, the different colours of stone, and the marks of many repairs. Each layer belongs to a different century and a different worry. As you walk away toward the dome or the river, remember the first raid that started all this. These quiet streets were born from fear, and they grew into a home for pilgrims from everywhere.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the Passetto's length on Borgo Pio's side streets.
Visit Raphael's Fire in the Borgo inside the Vatican Museums.
Check whether guided Passetto openings run during your visit.
Come early morning for quieter lanes before Vatican crowds arrive.
Combine the walk with a visit to Castel Sant'Angelo.
