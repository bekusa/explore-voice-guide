The guide

The story

Stand on the riverbank for a moment and look up. Above the plane trees, a dome catches the light. It does not curve like the domes of Saint Peter's or the Pantheon. It is square. Its pale metal surface carries a soft grey sheen, and on bright days it turns almost silver. Behind you, the Tiber runs green and slow. Traffic hums along the embankment. In front of you rises the Great Synagogue of Rome.

Romans often call this building the Tempio Maggiore, which means the Major Temple. It stands on the edge of the old Jewish quarter, a short walk from Tiber Island. Look for the square dome first. It is one of the easiest ways to pick out this neighbourhood from any high point in the city. Many visitors expect a quiet, hidden house of prayer. Instead, they find a building made to be seen.

That choice was deliberate. To understand it, you need to know what stood here before. In 1555, Pope Paul the Fourth issued a papal decree that confined the Jews of Rome to a small walled district beside the river. This was the Roman Ghetto. Its gates were locked at night. It sat on low ground, so the Tiber flooded the narrow streets again and again. For roughly three centuries, the community lived crowded into a few city blocks.

Here is the first surprise. The Jewish community of Rome is older than the ghetto by a very long way. Jews have lived in this city since the second century before Christ. That is earlier than the first Christian churches here. It makes this one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities in Europe. Roman Jews have their own prayer traditions, which are neither Sephardic, from Spain, nor Ashkenazi, from Central and Eastern Europe. They are simply Roman.

The great change came in 1870. Italian troops entered Rome, and the city passed from papal rule to the new Kingdom of Italy. The ghetto was abolished, and Jewish Romans became full citizens. In the following decades, the city cleared much of the old district. The walls and many crowded houses came down. The riverbank was rebuilt with the high stone embankments that you see today. On part of the cleared land, the community decided to build a new synagogue.

The architects were Vincenzo Costa and Osvaldo Armanni. Construction ran in the first years of the twentieth century, and the synagogue was inaugurated in 1904. The builders wanted a style that did not copy a church. So they looked east and far back in time. You can see echoes of ancient Assyria and Egypt in the heavy columns, the palm-like capitals, and the strong, layered shapes. Architects call this mix eclectic, meaning it borrows freely from several traditions at once.

Now look again at the dome. Its square shape sets it apart from almost every other dome on the Roman skyline. The cladding is aluminium, a modern material at the time. Think of the message. For three hundred years, the community lived behind walls in the lowest part of the city. Now its house of prayer rose high above the river, in plain sight of the Vatican and the old city. Freedom became something you could see from across Rome.

If you go inside, the space opens up in height and colour. Painted decoration covers the ceiling and the inner dome. Light falls through tall windows. The interior is rich in pattern, with plant forms and geometric designs rather than human figures. That reflects Jewish tradition, which avoids images of people in places of worship. Many visitors say the inside feels warmer and more colourful than the stone exterior suggests. Take a moment to look up before you look around.

The twentieth century brought this community its darkest day. On 16 October 1943, during the German occupation, troops surrounded the old ghetto streets at dawn. They seized more than a thousand Jewish Romans, including children and elderly people. Most were sent to Auschwitz. Only a very small number came home after the war. As you walk the nearby streets, look down. Small brass stones set into the pavement carry the names of people who were taken from these homes.

The synagogue has also seen violence in more recent memory. In October 1982, terrorists attacked worshippers as they left the building after a holiday service. A two-year-old boy, Stefano Gaj Taché, was killed, and many people were wounded. His name is remembered here and across the city. This history explains something you will notice right away. Security around the synagogue is serious, with guards, barriers, and bag checks. It is part of daily life in this place.

Now the second surprise. In April 1986, Pope John Paul the Second came here to pray with the Chief Rabbi of Rome. It was a historic step. It is widely seen as the first time in modern history that a pope visited a synagogue. In his speech he called the Jewish people the elder brothers of Christians. The visit happened only a short distance from the old ghetto that a pope had created. Later popes have also come to this building.

Today the synagogue is still a working house of prayer. It is also the home of the Jewish Museum of Rome, which occupies rooms in the same complex. The museum holds ceremonial silver, embroidered textiles, and objects from the long life of the community. Visits to the prayer hall usually happen through the museum, often with a guide. This is the easiest way to see the interior and hear the stories of the families who worshipped here.

When you leave, walk a few steps into the old quarter. The ancient ruins of the Portico of Octavia stand nearby, with old Roman columns rising between later houses. Small restaurants serve dishes from the Roman Jewish kitchen. The best known is fried artichoke, flattened and crisp like a dark bronze flower. Shops, bakeries, and schools line the streets. This is not a museum district. It is a neighbourhood where people still live, pray, and meet friends.

Before you go, turn once more toward the river. Look at the square dome above the trees, and the wide embankment where the ghetto walls once stood. The water still runs past, as it did through all those centuries of floods and locked gates. Listen to the traffic, the voices from the cafés, the bells from churches across the water. All of them now share the same sky. Take that view with you as you walk on.
