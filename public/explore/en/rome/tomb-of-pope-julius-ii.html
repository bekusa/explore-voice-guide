The guide

The story

Step out of the Roman traffic and into the church of San Pietro in Vincoli. The air is cooler here, and the noise of scooters fades behind heavy doors. Your footsteps sound loud on the stone floor. Walk toward the right side of the nave, and you will see a wall of pale marble rising in two tiers. At its centre, a seated figure looks out over your head. That figure is Moses, and this is the Tomb of Pope Julius II.

The monument looks complete, even grand. But what stands in front of you is a small fraction of the original idea. Many art historians see it as the story of an ambition that shrank. To understand it, we need to go back to the early sixteenth century, to a forceful pope and a young sculptor from Florence.

Julius II was a della Rovere, a pope known for his temper, his military campaigns, and his appetite for building. In 1505 he called Michelangelo to Rome and asked him to design his tomb. The first plan was huge. It was a freestanding structure meant for Saint Peter's Basilica, with around forty life-size statues. It would have been one of the largest sculptural projects of the Renaissance.

Michelangelo threw himself into the work. He spent months in the marble quarries of Carrara, choosing blocks himself. Then the pope's attention moved elsewhere. Julius became focused on rebuilding Saint Peter's itself, and money for the tomb dried up. Michelangelo felt insulted. In 1506 he left Rome in anger and went back to Florence. The pope and the artist were later reconciled, but the tomb was already in trouble.

Here is a twist many visitors miss. Instead of the tomb, Julius gave Michelangelo another job — painting the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel. Michelangelo considered himself a sculptor, not a painter. Yet he spent roughly four years on that ceiling. So one of the most famous paintings in the world exists partly because this tomb was put aside. Julius died in 1513, and the project passed to his heirs.

What followed was decades of argument. The della Rovere family wanted the tomb finished. Later popes wanted Michelangelo for their own projects. Contracts were rewritten again and again, and each time the design became smaller. Michelangelo suffered under this pressure for most of his career. The long struggle later became known as the tragedy of the tomb. The final version was only completed in the 1540s — some forty years after it began.

Now look closely at Moses. Michelangelo carved him in the years around Julius's death, and later adjusted the figure. Moses sits with the Tablets of the Law under his right arm. His long beard twists and flows through his fingers. His left leg is pulled back, as if he is about to stand. His head turns sharply to one side. The body is calm, but the face is tense and alert.

You will notice two small horns on his head. This is not a mistake by the sculptor. In the Latin Bible used in Michelangelo's time, a Hebrew word describing rays of light from Moses's face was translated as horns. Medieval and Renaissance artists often showed Moses this way. So the horns are a sign of holiness — light from his meeting with God, turned into stone.

There is also a famous story about this statue. Legend has it that when Michelangelo finished Moses, he struck the knee with his hammer and asked why it did not speak. Some people even point to a small mark on the knee. It is a good tale, and locals enjoy telling it. But it is folklore, not documented history. What is certain is that the statue looks ready to speak.

On either side of Moses stand two quieter women. They are Rachel and Leah, figures from the Old Testament, carved by Michelangelo late in the project. They represent two ways of living a Christian life. Leah stands for the active life, and Rachel, with her face lifted in prayer, stands for the contemplative life. Compare their gentle surfaces with the energy of Moses. You can see how his style changed over the decades.

The upper tier was carved mostly by assistants working from his designs. Up there, Pope Julius himself lies on his side, propped on one arm, below a Madonna and Child. Here is the second surprise. Julius is not buried here. His remains lie in Saint Peter's Basilica. So this tomb, after forty years of work, stands without the man it was made for.

Other pieces of the original plan travelled far. Two statues of bound figures, often called the Slaves, are now in the Louvre in Paris. Several unfinished Prisoners, still half trapped in their marble blocks, stand in the Accademia Gallery in Florence. If you have seen them, you have seen parts of this same project. The tomb is spread across Europe, like pieces of one large puzzle.

The church around you has its own reason to exist. Its name means Saint Peter in Chains. Near the main altar, a reliquary holds chains that tradition associates with the imprisonment of Saint Peter. Many pilgrims come for those, not for Michelangelo. So in one building, you meet two kinds of visitor — those here for faith, and those here for art.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in front of Moses. Notice the way the light from the side windows moves across the marble. Notice the veins in his arm, the folds of cloth at his knee. Think of the forty statues that never came to be. Then walk out the door, back into the sound of Rome, carrying this unfinished story with you.

Practical tips

Stand slightly left of centre to catch Moses's turned gaze.
Visit early morning before tour groups fill the nave.
