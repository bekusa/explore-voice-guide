The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under your shoes, gravel crunches softly. Above you, the umbrella pines move in the wind, and the light comes down through them in broken patches. Around you are walls of thin, flat Roman brick, warm orange in the sun, standing without roofs and open to the sky. You are on the Palatine Hill, one of the seven hills of ancient Rome, and perhaps the most important of them all.

Start with the story the Romans told about themselves. Legend has it that twin brothers, Romulus and Remus, were abandoned as babies and nursed by a she-wolf in a cave at the foot of this hill. Locals still call that cave the Lupercal. According to the same tale, Romulus founded Rome right here, on the Palatine. Ancient writers gave a traditional date of 753 BCE. It is a founding myth, not a record — but archaeology adds a surprise.

Here is that surprise. Archaeologists working on the southwestern side of the hill found holes cut into the rock. These holes once held the wooden posts of small huts with thatched roofs. They date to the Iron Age, centuries before Rome had marble or emperors. So the legend is not proven, but the hill really was home to one of the earliest communities in this place. The Romans themselves kept a simple hut here and called it the Hut of Romulus.

Why settle here? Look at the ground around you. The Palatine rises steeply above two low valleys. In early times those valleys were marshy and wet. A high, dry hill near the river Tiber was safe from floods and easy to defend. From the edges of the hill you can still see how the land falls away. That simple advantage of height shaped everything that came later.

By the time of the Roman Republic, the Palatine had become the best address in the city. Wealthy and powerful families built their houses on its slopes. The orator Cicero owned a house here. So did Mark Antony. Living on the Palatine meant living close to the oldest sacred places of Rome, and above the noise of the Forum below. It was the ancient version of a hilltop neighbourhood where everyone important knew everyone else.

Then came Augustus, the first emperor. The Roman writer Suetonius tells us he was born on the Palatine, and he chose to live here as ruler. His house was modest by later standards. Nearby you can visit rooms linked to his wife, Livia, and to Augustus himself. Their walls still carry painted decoration — deep reds, yellows, garden scenes and false architecture. Some of these spaces need a separate reservation, so check before you plan around them.

After Augustus, the emperors kept building, and each one built bigger. Here is a fact many visitors miss. Because the emperors lived on the Palatine, the hill's Latin name, Palatium, came to mean the home of a ruler. That word travelled across languages and centuries. Today, when you say the English word palace, you are speaking the name of this hill. Every palace in the world carries its name.

The greatest palace complex was built under the emperor Domitian, in the late first century. Ancient sources connect it to an architect named Rabirius. One part, often called the Domus Flavia, held the public halls where the emperor received guests and held banquets. Another part, the Domus Augustana, held the private living quarters. As you walk, notice how the brick walls rise in layers. Imagine them covered in coloured marble, with high ceilings and fountains.

Look for a long, sunken open space with curved ends. It is often called the Stadium of Domitian. Its shape looks like a racetrack, but many scholars think it was a private garden for the emperor, a place to walk in the shade. Stand at the edge and look down. The scale feels strange for a garden. It shows how much space one ruler could claim for himself in the middle of a crowded city.

Now walk to the southern edge, if you can. Below you lies a long, flat, grassy valley. That is the Circus Maximus, where Romans watched chariot races. The imperial palace was built to overlook it, so the emperor could be seen by the crowd. Then turn and look north. On that side, the Roman Forum spreads out below, with its columns and arches. The Palatine sits between the two — politics on one side, entertainment on the other.

The story does not end with the emperors. After Rome declined, the palaces fell into ruin. Stone was taken for other buildings. Soil and plants covered the walls. In the sixteenth century, the powerful Farnese family laid out gardens over part of the hill. The Farnese Gardens had terraces, fountains and rare plants. Some of their terraces and pavilions remain, and they offer some of the finest views over the Forum.

As you move around, pay attention to the walls themselves. You will see different building methods side by side. Some walls are made of flat, thin bricks laid in neat lines. Others have small square stones set diagonally, in a net-like pattern. Romans used both, often with concrete inside. You may also see holes in the walls. Many once held beams, scaffolding or marble panels. The ruins show how the buildings were made, not only how they looked.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot and stand still for a minute. Feel the breeze that always seems stronger up here. Listen to the birds in the pines, and the distant hum of traffic below. The people who lived here — shepherds in huts, senators in fine houses, emperors in marble halls — all stood on this same hill and looked at this same sky. As you walk down, look back once. The brick walls are still catching the light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and uneven ancient stone.
Bring water and a hat; shade is patchy on the hilltop.
Visit early morning to enjoy the hill before tour groups.
Reserve ahead for Houses of Augustus and Livia separately.
Combine your visit with the Roman Forum below the hill.
