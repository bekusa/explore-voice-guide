The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in the small square and listen. Paper rustles at the print stalls nearby, and wheels clatter softly over the dark basalt cobbles that Romans call sampietrini. Above you rises a long, ochre-toned façade, calm and almost severe, with rows of tall windows set in pale stone frames. This is Palazzo Borghese. From the street it can look like one more noble house in a city full of them. But this building has a strange shape, and a strange nickname, and both are worth your time.

Romans have long called this palace il cembalo, which means the harpsichord. The name comes from its floor plan. The building is not a neat rectangle. It narrows at one end and widens at the other, like the body of an old keyboard instrument. The narrow end points toward the Tiber River, and Romans call that end the keyboard. You cannot see the full shape from where you stand. But once you know it, you start to notice how the walls bend away from you at odd angles.

The palace began life in the late sixteenth century, during a building boom in this part of Rome. The early design is usually linked to the architect Martino Longhi the Elder, although scholars debate exactly who drew what. The plot was awkward and squeezed between existing streets. That helps explain the unusual outline. The builders did not choose a harpsichord shape for beauty. They shaped the building to fit the land they had, and the result happened to look like music.

The great change came in the early seventeenth century. Cardinal Camillo Borghese acquired the palace shortly before he was elected pope in 1605, taking the name Paul the Fifth. A papal election transformed a family's fortunes in this era. Popes often gave money, land and titles to their relatives. The Borghese family, originally from Siena, rose quickly into the highest level of Roman society. They needed a residence that matched their new rank, and this palace became that house.

The architect Flaminio Ponzio carried out much of the expansion for the Borghese. His work gave the palace its grand scale and its famous courtyard. If you ever get a look through the gateway, you will see two levels of open arcades, called loggias, carried on paired granite columns. Ancient statues stand around the space. The effect is a quiet, shaded world inside the walls, very different from the noise of the street outside. It is one of the finest courtyards in Rome.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Beyond the main courtyard, toward the river end, lies a garden with decorative fountains and grottoes. Later in the seventeenth century, the architect Carlo Rainaldi worked on this part of the palace. The fountain area is often called the Bath of Venus, a name that suggests ancient pleasures and classical myth. It was designed as a small stage of water, stone and greenery, meant to surprise guests as they walked through the house.

For a long time, this palace held one of the greatest private art collections in Europe. Cardinal Scipione Borghese, the pope's nephew, was a passionate and sometimes ruthless collector. He gathered paintings and sculptures by the leading artists of his day. Many works from the family collection hung in these rooms for generations. In the late nineteenth century, the paintings moved to the family villa on the edge of the old city. Today that villa is the Galleria Borghese, one of Rome's most loved museums.

So think of this building as the first home of that collection. Before visitors queued at the villa, the art of the Borghese family lived here, behind these windows, close to the river and the busy port that once stood nearby. The Tiber was a working river then, with boats bringing goods into the city. The palace sat at the meeting point of trade, power and the Church. Its owners could watch the life of the river from their keyboard end.

The palace also has a famous resident from the age of Napoleon. In the early nineteenth century, Pauline Bonaparte, the sister of Napoleon, married Prince Camillo Borghese. Her life in Rome drew constant gossip. She is best remembered through the marble sculpture by Antonio Canova that shows her reclining as the goddess Venus. That statue now belongs to the Galleria Borghese. Locals still enjoy stories about her vanity and her parties, though many of those tales are closer to legend than to record.

The palace is not a museum, and that is part of its character. It remains a private building, and for many years it has been home to an exclusive gentlemen's club. Visitors usually cannot walk inside. This can feel frustrating. But it also means the palace keeps something of its original purpose. It is still a place where powerful and well-connected people meet behind closed doors, just as the Borghese and their guests once did centuries ago.

Now look around the square itself. Piazza Borghese is often home to a small market of old prints, maps and second-hand books. The stalls sit under simple awnings, and the sellers know their stock well. You might find an engraved view of Rome from an earlier century, or a faded map of the Papal States. Browsing here is a gentle way to connect with the palace. Many of those old prints show the same streets and façades you are standing among now.

From here, the rest of the neighbourhood opens easily on foot. The Tiber is only a short walk away. So is the Mausoleum of Augustus, the great round tomb of Rome's first emperor. Narrow streets lead toward Via del Corso and the shops near the Spanish Steps. This area feels lived-in, with cafés, small workshops and offices tucked into old buildings. It is a good place to slow down and see everyday Rome between the larger monuments.

Before you move on, look once more at the long façade. Picture the narrow end stretching toward the river, the hidden courtyard of granite columns, the fountains of the garden beyond. Think of the paintings that once hung inside, and the family that rose from Siena to the papacy. The harpsichord is silent from the street. But as you walk away across the cobbles, listen for the soft sounds of the square... the turning pages, the footsteps, the quiet music of Rome going about its day.
