The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is soft, grassy, and slightly worn into dusty paths by joggers and dog walkers. The long, shallow valley stretches away from you, its sides sloping gently upward. Traffic hums along the roads on either side. On a warm afternoon, the air smells of dry grass and pine. It feels like an ordinary city park... until you notice its shape. This is the Circus Maximus, and that long oval shape is the outline of a racetrack.

You are standing in the valley between two of Rome's seven hills. To one side rises the Palatine, covered with the brick ruins of imperial palaces. On the other side is the Aventine, quieter and leafier, with churches and gardens along its crest. Long before any stands were built here, this low ground was a natural arena. The slopes of the hills gave spectators a place to sit and watch. Romans simply used the landscape they already had.

The word circus did not mean clowns or acrobats to the Romans. It meant a long track for chariot racing — what the Greeks called a hippodrome. And this was the greatest of them all. Maximus simply means 'the largest.' The track ran for roughly six hundred metres. Estimates of its crowd capacity vary widely, but many historians suggest well over one hundred thousand people. That would make it one of the largest venues for public entertainment ever built.

The story of this place begins with legend. Roman tradition says that Romulus, the city's mythical founder, held games in this valley. During those games, according to the old tale, his men seized the women of the neighbouring Sabine people. Later tradition credits one of Rome's early kings, Tarquinius Priscus, with first marking out the track. These are stories that Romans told about themselves. They are not documented history, but they show how ancient this place felt, even to the Romans.

What we know with more confidence is that the circus grew over many centuries. Early stands were made of wood. Over time, they were rebuilt in stone and expanded. Julius Caesar enlarged the circus in the first century before Christ. Later emperors repaired and decorated it again and again, often after fires. Wooden seating and crowded shops nearby made fire a constant danger. In fact, the Great Fire of Rome in the year 64 is reported to have started in shops near this very circus.

Now picture race day. Twelve starting gates stood at one end of the track. Chariots burst out of them when a cloth was dropped as a signal. The drivers raced counter-clockwise around a long central barrier, usually seven laps. The turns at each end were the most dangerous moments. Chariots collided, wheels broke, and drivers could be dragged behind their horses. Crashes were common, and the crowd expected them.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. To help spectators count the laps, the central barrier held two sets of markers. One set was shaped like large eggs. The other was shaped like dolphins. After each lap, one marker was removed or turned. The dolphins were linked to Neptune, the god of the sea, who was also the patron of horses. So even the lap counter carried a religious meaning.

The drivers raced for teams called factions, and each faction had a colour. There were the Reds, the Whites, the Greens, and the Blues. Fans were deeply loyal to their colours, much like football supporters today. The best charioteers became celebrities. One driver, Gaius Appuleius Diocles, is known from an inscription that lists his victories and his huge prize money. Some of these racers were former slaves who became richer than senators.

The central barrier was also decorated with statues, shrines, and monuments. The emperor Augustus brought an Egyptian obelisk to Rome and placed it here. Centuries later, a second obelisk was added. Neither stands here today. Both were found buried in the ground centuries later and moved to other parts of the city. The obelisk of Augustus now stands in the Piazza del Popolo. The second one stands near the Lateran, beside the cathedral of Saint John. So a small piece of this racetrack still stands in other squares of Rome.

Look up toward the Palatine Hill. The emperors lived on that hill, in palaces directly above the track. They could watch the races from their own residence. There was also a special box near the track for the emperor and for the images of the gods. For ordinary Romans, the races were a chance to see their ruler in person. The crowd could cheer him, or sometimes shout complaints. The circus was entertainment, but it was also politics.

The races continued for centuries, even after Rome stopped being the centre of the empire. The last recorded races here took place in the sixth century, under an Ostrogothic king, a ruler of one of the Germanic peoples who controlled Italy after Roman rule collapsed. After that, the circus slowly fell apart. Its stone was taken for other buildings. The valley filled with soil and debris. For centuries, it was used for gardens, small farms, and later for industrial buildings.

Walk toward the curved end of the park, near the Aventine side. Here you can see the excavated remains — brick walls, arches, and the base of the old seating. Above them stands a small medieval tower. It was built in the Middle Ages by one of the powerful Roman families who fortified this area. That tower shows how many different periods share this one valley. Ancient Rome, medieval Rome, and modern Rome all stand together in a small space.

Today, the circus is used for very different events. Large outdoor concerts are sometimes held here. Romans gather on this grass to celebrate big sporting victories, just as their ancestors once cheered for their chariot teams. On ordinary days, people run laps around the old track. Without knowing it, they follow the same oval route as the racing chariots, two thousand years later.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look along the length of the valley. Try to imagine the noise of a full crowd, the dust, the thunder of hooves at the far turn. Then listen to the real sounds around you — a distant scooter, birds in the pines, footsteps on the dry path. The stands are gone, but the shape of the track remains in the land. As you walk away, notice how the grass still follows its long, curved line.

Key facts

Four racing factions: Reds, Whites, Greens, Blues.
Augustus's obelisk from here now stands in Piazza del Popolo.

Look closer

What to look for

The long oval dip that traces the old track.
Brick arches of the seating at the curved end.
The small medieval tower above the excavations.
Palace ruins on the Palatine slope above the track.
Worn running paths following the original racing line.
Before you go

Practical tips

Take Metro line B to the Circo Massimo stop.
Walk to the Aventine end to see excavated ruins.
Come late afternoon when the Palatine ruins catch warm light.
Bring water and a hat — the valley has little shade.
Climb the Aventine slope for a view along the track.
