The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic rolls around you in a wide, restless loop. Scooters whine past, buses sigh at the kerb, and pine needles crunch faintly underfoot. Look around for an ancient gate, and you will not find one. That absence is the first surprise of this place. You are standing where one of the most storied gateways of ancient Rome once opened onto the road to the south.

Porta Capena was a gate in the Servian Wall, the old defensive ring around the early city. Roman tradition credited the wall to Servius Tullius, one of the legendary kings, in the sixth century BCE. Archaeologists, however, date most of the surviving stone circuit to the fourth century BCE. That was the period after a Gallic army sacked Rome and the city decided it needed far stronger defences. So the name honours a king, but the masonry belongs to a frightened republic.

The gate sat in a low valley, a natural pass between hills. To one side rises the Caelian Hill. Behind you, toward the north, the slopes of the Palatine climb above the long hollow of the Circus Maximus. This dip in the ground made sense for a gateway. Travellers, carts and armies could enter the city without a hard climb. Today the same low ground funnels traffic, which is why this spot feels like a crossroads even now.

Here is the detail that gives the gate its weight in history. Porta Capena marked the starting point of the Via Appia, the Appian Way. The road was begun in 312 BCE under the censor Appius Claudius Caecus, and it carried his family name. It ran south toward Capua and, later, all the way to the port of Brundisium on the heel of Italy. Roman roads were the arteries of the empire, and this one was called the queen of roads.

Think about what passed through this opening over the centuries. Generals marched out toward wars in the south. Merchants came in with wine, oil and grain. Funeral processions moved slowly outward, because Roman law forbade burials inside the city, so tombs lined the Appian Way beyond the walls. For many Romans, leaving through Porta Capena meant beginning a journey. Returning through it meant coming home.

Now for the second surprise. Roman poets describe this gate as wet. The satirist Juvenal calls it dripping, and the poet Martial makes a similar joke. The reason was practical rather than poetic. An aqueduct channel passed over or beside the gate, and water leaked from it onto the people walking underneath. Imagine a great city gate where you had to step quickly to avoid a cold drip on the back of your neck.

The area around the gate also carried older stories. Just outside lay a sacred grove and spring linked to the Camenae, water goddesses of Roman religion. Legend has it that King Numa, the second king of Rome, met here with the nymph Egeria. According to the tale, she advised him on laws and rituals. Some scholars connect the name Capena with the Camenae, while others link it to a town called Capena. The true origin remains debated.

A darker legend belongs here too. The historian Livy tells the story of the Horatii, three brothers who fought for Rome in a duel against three champions from a rival city. One brother survived and returned in triumph through this gate. Livy writes that he met his sister, who mourned one of the fallen enemies, and killed her in anger. Locals in antiquity said her tomb stood near Porta Capena. It is a story told as early history, but it belongs to legend.

As Rome grew, the old Servian Wall lost its purpose. The city spread far beyond it. In the third century, the emperor Aurelian built a new and much larger wall. The Appian Way now left the city through a gate further south, in that later circuit. Porta Capena became an inner landmark rather than a border. Over time its stones were taken, buried, or built over, as happened to so much of ancient Rome.

Look around now at the modern square. The large, pale building on its edge was built in the twentieth century for the Fascist government's colonial ministry. Today it houses the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations. For decades, an ancient obelisk from Axum in Ethiopia stood in front of it, taken during Mussolini's invasion. Italy returned the obelisk in the early 2000s, and it now stands again in Ethiopia. The empty space carries that story too.

So this is a place where history is mostly invisible. That can feel strange at first. But Rome is full of sites like this, where names on street signs are the only remaining trace of something enormous. The square still carries the gate's name. The roads still follow lines the Romans laid out. To the south, the great ruins of the Baths of Caracalla rise above the trees, a reminder of how much grew along this route.

If you want to feel the gate's purpose, follow the direction of the ancient road. Walk south past the baths and the old route continues, eventually becoming the Appian Way you can still walk on, lined with tombs and umbrella pines. Or turn north and stand above the Circus Maximus, where chariot races once filled the valley with noise. Both directions tell you why a gate stood exactly here.

Before you move on, pause for a moment. Notice how the ground dips, how the hills close in on either side, how the traffic still squeezes through this natural pass. The Romans chose this spot for the same reasons that buses and taxis crowd it today. The gate is gone, the drip of the aqueduct is long silent... but people are still coming and going here, beginning journeys and coming home, just as they did more than two thousand years ago.
