The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen to the Tiber. The water moves slowly here, olive green and heavy with silt, sliding past the high stone embankments. Plane trees lean over the riverside roads, and the traffic hums above you. Somewhere along this stretch of river, long before any of that noise, Romans walked across a bridge made entirely of wood. It was the first bridge the city ever knew, and its name was the Pons Sublicius.

The name itself tells you what it was made of. In old Latin, a sublica was a wooden pile, a stake driven into a riverbed. So this was the bridge of piles. Picture timber posts hammered into the mud, beams laid across them, and planks for a deck. It was nothing like the marble and brick that Rome later became famous for. It was simple, practical, and for centuries it was the only way across the river at this point.

Roman tradition credits the bridge to Ancus Marcius, the fourth of the city's legendary kings. That places it, in the Romans' own telling, in the late seventh century before Christ. Historians treat the early kings with caution, because much of their story was written down centuries later. What is certain is that the Romans themselves regarded this as their oldest bridge. To them, it belonged to the very beginnings of the city.

Now here is the first surprise. According to ancient writers, the bridge had to be built without any metal at all. No iron nails, no bronze clamps — only wood, fitted and pegged together. This was not an engineering choice. It was a religious rule. When the bridge was damaged and repaired, the same rule applied. The Romans kept rebuilding it in timber long after they had mastered stone, simply because the bridge was sacred.

That sacred role leads to a second surprise. The care of the bridge was linked to the priests called pontifices. The word pontifex appears to contain the Latin for bridge, pons, and many scholars have connected it with bridge-making. The link is still debated. But the idea is striking. The title later carried by the chief priest of Rome, Pontifex Maximus, may echo this humble wooden crossing. Centuries later, the popes took the same title.

The most famous story about the bridge is a legend, and the Romans loved it. Late in the sixth century before Christ, an Etruscan army led by the king Lars Porsena marched on the young republic. The Etruscans were a powerful people who lived to the north of Rome. According to the tale, the enemy rushed toward this bridge, the open door into the city. One soldier, Horatius Cocles, stood at the far end and held them back.

The story says that Horatius ordered his comrades to cut the bridge down behind him while he fought. As the timbers crashed into the river, he turned and leapt into the Tiber in full armour. In most versions, he swam safely back to the Roman bank. Whether any of it happened, nobody can say. But every Roman schoolchild knew it, and the poet Thomas Macaulay later turned it into a well-known English ballad.

The bridge also carried a strange yearly ritual. Each May, ancient sources say, the Vestal Virgins threw figures made of rushes or straw from the bridge into the river. These figures were called the Argei. Even the Romans were unsure what the ceremony meant. Some thought it replaced an older human sacrifice. Others saw it as a way of cleansing the city. Today, scholars still argue about it.

The river was not always kind to its wooden bridge. The Tiber flooded often, and ancient writers record the bridge being damaged and swept away more than once. Each time it was rebuilt, still in timber, still following the old rules. It survived into the time of the emperors, standing near later stone bridges as a kind of living relic. No visible remains survive today, and its exact position is still uncertain.

Most scholars place the ancient bridge near the old cattle market, the Forum Boarium, a little upstream from where you may be standing. That market sat beside the river, below the Palatine and Capitoline hills. It was one of the busiest trading areas of early Rome. A crossing here made sense. Traders, animals, and armies could all move between the city and the lands on the western bank.

The bridge that carries the name today is a different structure altogether. The modern Ponte Sublicio was built in the early twentieth century, linking the Testaccio district with the area around Porta Portese. It is made of masonry, solid and arched, and cars and buses cross it all day. It honours the ancient bridge in name, not in material. Think of it as a memorial you can walk across.

Look around at the neighbourhoods on either side. On one bank lies Testaccio, a working district known for its old slaughterhouse and its food. Close by, the Aventine Hill rises with its gardens and churches. On the other bank, near Porta Portese, a large flea market fills the streets on Sunday mornings. The tall river walls below you were built in the late nineteenth century, after a severe flood in 1870.

Those walls changed how Romans live with the Tiber. Before them, the river came right up to houses, mills, and landing places. Now it runs deep below street level, and most people pass it without looking down. Yet the water you see is the same river that carried away the wooden piles, the straw figures, and, in legend, a soldier in armour.

So take one last look at the water before you walk on. Notice the green current, the gulls on the embankment, the reflection of the arches. Nothing of the first bridge remains for you to touch. But the crossing itself is still here — the need to get from one bank to the other. Rome began with that need, and with a few wooden posts driven into this riverbed.
