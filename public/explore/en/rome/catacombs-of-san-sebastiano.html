The guide

The story

Close your eyes for a moment and feel the air change. Up on the Appian Way, the sun warms the old paving stones and the umbrella pines throw long, soft shadows. Then you go down the stairs, and the warmth falls away. The air turns cool and still. It smells of damp earth and old stone. Somewhere ahead, a guide's voice echoes softly along a narrow passage cut into the rock. This is where the word catacomb begins.

That is not an exaggeration. In ancient times, this stretch of the road had a nickname. People called it ad catacumbas, a phrase usually traced to Greek words meaning near the hollows. The ground here dipped where workers had dug out stone, leaving a depression in the landscape. The name stuck to this one cemetery first. Only much later did people use the word for every underground burial place. So every catacomb in the world carries the name of this spot.

The road above matters too. The Appian Way was one of Rome's great roads, running south from the city towards the ports of the heel of Italy. Roman law did not allow burials inside the city walls. So families placed their dead along the roads just outside. Travellers walking into Rome passed tombs, monuments and memorials on both sides. A walk along this road was a walk past the names of the dead. The catacombs are the underground part of that same tradition.

Now look at the walls around you as you walk. The passages are narrow, often just wide enough for one person. On both sides, you see rows of horizontal niches cut into the soft volcanic rock. These are called loculi. Each one held a body wrapped in cloth. The opening was then sealed with a slab of marble or a layer of tiles and mortar. Some niches are tiny. Those were for children, and they are the hardest to look at.

Here is something most visitors do not expect. San Sebastiano is one of the very few Roman catacombs that was never completely lost. Many other underground cemeteries were abandoned in the early Middle Ages and forgotten for centuries. People rediscovered them much later, sometimes by accident. But pilgrims kept coming here. The reason is a story about two apostles. And that story is written, quite literally, on the walls below you.

According to an old tradition, the remains of Saint Peter and Saint Paul were kept here for a time in the third century, during a period of persecution. Historians still debate exactly what happened. But the evidence of devotion is real. Archaeologists found a space called the triclia, a kind of covered gathering area where early Christians shared funeral meals. Its plaster is covered with scratched graffiti. Hundreds of visitors wrote short prayers asking Peter and Paul to remember them.

Think about that for a moment. These are not carved inscriptions made by professionals. They are quick notes, scratched by ordinary people with a nail or a sharp stone. Some are in Latin, some in Greek. They are the ancient version of writing your name at a place that means something to you. Standing near them, you are standing where those visitors stood, perhaps seventeen centuries ago, asking for the same kind of comfort people ask for today.

Another surprise waits underground. Below the church, there is a small open area with three Roman mausoleums. They were built before this place became a Christian cemetery, when the hollow was still open to the sky. Their brick facades are remarkably well preserved. Inside, some decoration is pagan, and some later burials are Christian. The two worlds sit side by side. It is a reminder that early Christians did not appear from nowhere. They were Roman families, buried among Roman neighbours.

As you move through the galleries, watch for small painted or carved symbols. Early Christians often used simple signs. A fish was common, because the Greek word for fish formed an acronym for a short statement of faith. You may also see an anchor, a dove, or the shepherd carrying a sheep. These symbols were quiet and understated. To an outsider, they looked like ordinary decoration. To a believer, they carried a whole message in one small shape.

Now to the saint who gives this place its name. Sebastian was a Roman soldier and a Christian martyr. Tradition says he was shot with arrows under the emperor Diocletian, survived, and was later beaten to death. He was buried here, and his tomb became a focus for pilgrims. Over time, the church above took his name. In paintings across Europe, you will often see him tied to a post, pierced by arrows. That image began with the story told here.

When you come back up into the light, spend time in the basilica. A church first stood here in the fourth century and was known as the Basilica of the Apostles. The building you see today was largely rebuilt in the seventeenth century. Inside, look for the marble statue of Saint Sebastian lying in death, arrows in his body. There is also a chapel of relics. It holds a stone that, according to legend, carries the footprints of Christ.

That stone connects to a famous tale. Legend has it that Peter, fleeing Rome, met Christ on the Appian Way and asked him, where are you going? In Latin, that is quo vadis. Christ answered that he was going to Rome to be crucified again. Peter turned back to face his own death. A small church nearby marks the traditional spot. Whatever you believe, the story shaped how people saw this road for many centuries.

Before you leave, pause on the steps outside. Behind you, the cool tunnels hold thousands of quiet names and the scratched prayers of strangers. In front of you, the old road runs on between the pines and the low ruined tombs. Listen to the gravel under your shoes, the birds in the trees, maybe a bicycle passing. People have walked this road for more than two thousand years. Today, for a little while, you are one of them.
