The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Rome is still out there, beyond the walls, but here it fades into a soft, distant hum. Gravel crunches underfoot. Birds move through the pines and the clipped hedges. The air smells of cut grass and warm stone. You are standing inside the Vatican Gardens, on the green slope behind Saint Peter's Basilica, and in front of you is a quiet, modest building. This is the Mater Ecclesiae Monastery.

At first glance, it does not look like much. There are no frescoed façades here, no marble colonnades, and no crowds pressing forward with cameras. The building is simple and practical, with plain walls and ordinary windows. It sits close to the old defensive walls that once protected this hill. That plainness is the point. This was built as a place for prayer, not for display, and almost everything about it is designed to stay out of view.

The name comes from Latin. Mater Ecclesiae means Mother of the Church. It is a title for the Virgin Mary that Pope Paul VI formally proclaimed in 1964, during the Second Vatican Council. That council was the great meeting of the world's bishops in the 1960s that reshaped much of modern Catholic life. So the name itself carries a link to that period of change. It is a quiet name for a quiet house.

The story of the building as a monastery begins with Pope John Paul II. In the early 1990s, he wanted a community of contemplative nuns to live inside the Vatican itself. Contemplative nuns are sisters who live a life of enclosure and prayer, mostly separate from the outside world. Their task here was specific and personal. They were to pray for the Pope and for the work of the Church. The monastery opened for this purpose in 1994.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The house was never meant to belong to one religious order. Instead, different communities of nuns took turns living here, each for a period of several years. Poor Clares came first, followers of the tradition of Saint Clare of Assisi. Later came Carmelites, then Benedictines, and then sisters of the Visitation order. Each group brought its own rhythm of prayer, chant, and work to this small corner of the hill.

The nuns did not only pray. Their days also included manual work, as is common in monastic life. The grounds around the house include garden space, and the sisters were known to tend plants and grow produce here. Imagine the scene. Bells mark the hours of prayer. Between them, sisters work quietly among the beds and trees. Outside the walls, millions of people arrive each year for the basilica and the museums. In here, almost none of that reached them.

Then, in 2013, this building entered the history books in an unexpected way. In February of that year, Pope Benedict XVI announced that he would resign. No pope had stepped down in roughly six centuries. It was a decision that surprised the whole world. A practical question followed immediately. Where does a former pope live? The answer, after a period of renovation, was this house in the Vatican Gardens.

The last community of nuns had already left before the work began. Benedict first spent a few weeks at the papal summer residence at Castel Gandolfo, in the hills south of Rome. Then, in May 2013, he moved here. He took the title Pope Emeritus. He promised to live hidden from the world, devoted to prayer and study. For nearly ten years, this modest monastery was his home.

Think about what that meant. For almost a decade, two men who had both been elected pope lived inside the Vatican at the same time. Pope Francis lived at the guesthouse near the basilica. Benedict lived here, a short distance up the hill. Visitors and journalists sometimes reported seeing the two men meet. The house that John Paul II built for anonymous prayer became one of the most talked-about addresses in the Catholic world.

Benedict's life here was described as simple and regular. He was a scholar and a musician, known for his love of the piano and of books. Reports over the years described him walking slowly in the gardens around the house, often praying. As he grew older and weaker, those walks became shorter. He died in this monastery on the last day of 2022, at a great age. His funeral was held in Saint Peter's Square.

Now look at the building again, with that history in mind. It is easy to expect a grand residence for a figure of such importance. Instead, you see something closer to a large, well-kept family house. That contrast tells you something about the man who chose to retire here. It also tells you about the original idea of the place. Whoever lives here, the house was built to support a hidden life, not a public one.

A practical note. The monastery is not open to the public, and you cannot go inside. You can usually see it only from the paths of the Vatican Gardens. Visits to the gardens are normally made with an organised group and need to be booked in advance. Please keep your voice low as you pass. This is still a working part of the Vatican, and the gardens remain a place of quiet for the people who live and work here.

Before you move on, take one more look. Notice the pines above the roofline and the old walls close behind the house. Listen again to the gravel and the birds, and to the faint city beyond. Many rooms in Rome were built to impress. This one was built to be forgotten, and for years it held the quiet life of a pope who stepped aside. As you walk away, let the silence of this slope stay with you a little longer.
