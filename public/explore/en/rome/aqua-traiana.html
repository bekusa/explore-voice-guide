The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere near you, water is moving. On the Janiculum hill above Trastevere, it pours, it splashes, it runs through stone channels. The air near the great fountains feels cooler, and it carries a faint mineral smell. That sound is the reason we are here. It carries a story that begins with a Roman emperor and a lake far to the north.

This is the world of the Aqua Traiana, the aqueduct of the Emperor Trajan. Trajan ruled Rome at the start of the second century, when the empire reached its widest extent. Rome already had many aqueducts, but most of them fed the eastern hills and the old city centre. The western bank of the Tiber, the district we now call Trastevere, needed water of its own. Trajan answered that need.

The aqueduct was inaugurated in the year 109. Roman coins from Trajan's reign celebrate it. They show a river god resting inside an arch, with the words Aqua Traiana around the edge. For a Roman, a new aqueduct was no small thing. It was a public gift, and it was also a political statement. An emperor who brought clean water to the people showed that he cared for them.

The water came from springs in the hills around Lake Bracciano, north-west of the city. Do not picture tall arches marching across the countryside the whole way. Much of the channel ran underground, cut into rock or built in masonry tunnels. Roman engineers followed the shape of the land. They kept a gentle, steady slope so that gravity alone moved the water, mile after mile, without a single pump.

Here is the first surprise. This aqueduct did more than fill fountains and baths. When the channel arrived at the top of the Janiculum, the water dropped down the slope towards the river. Romans used that fall to turn water mills. These mills ground grain into flour for the city's bread. In a sense, the Aqua Traiana fed Rome twice, with water to drink and with flour to eat.

That role made the aqueduct a target. In the sixth century, Gothic armies besieged Rome and cut the city's aqueducts. The historian Procopius, who wrote about these wars, describes what happened next. The mills on the Janiculum stopped. The Byzantine general Belisarius, who was defending the city, found a clever answer. He placed mills on boats in the Tiber and let the river's current turn the wheels.

After the siege, the long decline of the ancient city began. Rome shrank. Many aqueducts fell into ruin, and channels filled with roots and silt. The water of the Aqua Traiana did not vanish completely. Over the centuries, parts of the system were repaired and used again. But the great flow of Trajan's day was gone for a very long time.

The revival came in the early seventeenth century. Pope Paul V, from the Borghese family, decided to restore the old aqueduct. His engineers reused much of Trajan's route and brought the water back to the Janiculum. The restored system took a new name from the pope: the Acqua Paola. So when you hear the name Acqua Paola in Rome today, remember that its bones are Roman, and nearly fifteen centuries older.

The most visible result is the great fountain on the Janiculum, the Fontana dell'Acqua Paola. Romans simply call it il Fontanone, the big fountain. Its white stone front looks like a triumphal arch or a church facade. Water pours from its openings into a wide basin below. Look at the inscriptions across the top. They celebrate the pope's work, and they speak proudly of an aqueduct brought back to life.

Here is the second surprise. For centuries, people argued about where the true source of Trajan's aqueduct lay. In the early 2000s, explorers working near Lake Bracciano studied an underground chamber beneath an old abandoned church. Some researchers believe it was a decorated spring house at the head of the aqueduct, a kind of shrine to the water. The debate continues, but the idea is a lovely one: the source itself may have been a sacred space.

Archaeology has also found traces in the city. Excavations on the Janiculum have uncovered remains of the mills and the channels that once powered them. Some of these remains lie beneath modern buildings, out of public view. This is common in Rome. The ancient city is not only above ground. It lies in layers under houses, gardens and roads, and new pieces still appear when workers dig.

Think about the scale of all this. A city of perhaps a million people at its peak depended on channels like this one. Every fountain, every bath, every kitchen relied on engineers who measured slopes by eye and by simple instruments. The Aqua Traiana was not the longest aqueduct, nor the most famous. Yet it served a district that grew into one of the most loved neighbourhoods in Rome.

Now, look around you. If you stand near the Fontanone, turn towards the city. The view stretches over domes, bell towers and terracotta roofs, down to the river and beyond. Below you lie the narrow lanes of Trastevere, the district Trajan wanted to serve. Small street fountains there still offer cool water to anyone who stops. Some of that water follows a path laid out by Roman hands.

Before you walk on, listen once more to the water. It sounds simple, almost ordinary. But it connects you to an emperor's gift, a general's clever boats, and a pope's ambitious repair. As you leave, notice the small fountains you pass, and the people who stop to drink. In Rome, water is history you can touch... and hold in your hand.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to the Janiculum for the Fontanone and city views.
Come in late afternoon when light warms the fountain stone.
Wear good shoes; the Janiculum paths are steep in places.
Refill your water bottle at Trastevere's small street fountains.
Combine this visit with a slow walk through Trastevere.
