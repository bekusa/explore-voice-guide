The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in Piazza Venezia. Traffic circles the square in a constant rush. Scooters weave past buses, horns sound, and the white marble of the Vittoriano monument shines hard in the light. Yet a few metres below your feet lies something very quiet. It is a space built for listening. Nearly two thousand years ago, people sat here in rows, in silence, and waited for someone to speak. That place is known today as Hadrian's Athenaeum.

The name comes from Athena, the Greek goddess of wisdom. In the ancient world, an athenaeum was a place devoted to learning and the spoken word. Roman writers record that the Emperor Hadrian founded one in Rome. He ruled in the second century, and he was famous for his love of Greek culture. For a long time, historians knew this school mainly from texts. Nobody could point to the ground and say where it stood.

That changed by accident. Rome has been building a new underground railway line, and the work is slow for one simple reason. Almost every time the machines dig, they find the past. During excavations beneath Piazza Venezia in the late 2000s, archaeologists uncovered the remains of large Roman halls. The news spread quickly. Here, under one of the busiest squares in the city, was a strong candidate for Hadrian's lost Athenaeum.

What did they find? The clearest feature is seating. The halls contain rows of steps that rise one above another, like the seats in a small theatre. These were not for chariot races or gladiators. Their scale is more intimate. A speaker at the front could be heard without shouting. Many archaeologists read these rooms as auditoria, which means halls designed for an audience to listen to speeches, poetry and debate.

Imagine the scene. A poet stands and reads new verses aloud. A teacher of rhetoric, the art of persuasive speaking, shows students how to build an argument. Educated Romans valued these skills highly. A good speaker could win a court case, gain a political office, or simply earn admiration at dinner. In a city without printed books or recordings, the human voice carried ideas from one mind to the next.

This tells us something about Hadrian himself. He travelled across his empire more than most emperors did. He spent long periods in Greece and admired Athens deeply. Some Romans even mocked him with a nickname that meant, roughly, little Greek. His Athenaeum was a way to bring a piece of Greek intellectual life into the heart of Rome. It was a statement that the capital should be a city of learning, not only of power.

Now look at what surrounds you, because it gives the Athenaeum its context. Just nearby rises Trajan's Column, carved with a spiral of battle scenes. Trajan was Hadrian's predecessor and adoptive father. Beyond it stretch the remains of the imperial forums, the great public squares that emperors built to display their glory. Hadrian's school sat at the edge of this grand district. Power and learning stood side by side.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Roman ground level was far lower than the street you walk on today. Over the centuries, Rome rose on its own debris. Floods left mud. Buildings collapsed and new ones went up on top. Rubbish, earth and rubble slowly raised the city. That is why the Athenaeum was buried and forgotten. It did not vanish. The city simply grew over it, layer by layer.

The halls did not stay unchanged after the Roman era. Archaeologists found evidence that later generations reused this space in different ways, long after anyone came here to hear poetry. This is very typical of Rome. Buildings here rarely have one life. A temple becomes a church. A tomb becomes a fortress. A lecture hall becomes something practical and ordinary. Each use leaves a trace, and excavators read these traces like pages in a long book.

Think, too, about the materials. Roman builders of this period worked mainly in brick and concrete. They then covered many surfaces with finer stone, such as marble, to make rooms look rich and polished. When you see Roman ruins today, you often see only the bare brick skeleton. The colour and shine have gone. Try to picture these halls as they once were, smooth, bright and full of people.

Access to the remains below the square is not always simple. The site sits within an active construction area for the metro, and arrangements for visits have changed over time. If you want to see the halls themselves, check the current situation before you come. Even if you cannot go underground today, standing in the square above still means something. You are standing on the roof of a two-thousand-year-old classroom.

This place also reminds us how much of Rome is still unknown. Archaeologists had read about the Athenaeum for centuries without finding it. Then a railway project opened the ground and brought it into the light. It is very likely that other famous buildings from ancient texts still lie hidden under streets, homes and shops. Every new excavation in this city carries the same quiet question. What else is down there?

So, before you walk on, listen to the square once more. Hear the engines, the voices of other travellers, a guide calling a group together. It is noisy and modern and full of movement. But somewhere beneath that noise, there are stone steps where people once sat in silence to hear a single voice. As you cross toward the forums or the column, remember that Rome rewards those who look down as well as up.
