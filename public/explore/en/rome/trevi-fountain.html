The guide

The story

You hear it before you see it — the rush of falling water echoing off the buildings of a small square, growing louder as you turn the last corner. Then the whole back wall of a palace opens into carved rock, sea gods, and plunging water. This is the Trevi Fountain, the grandest of Rome's fountains and one of the most crowded corners of the city.

The fountain is built against the back of a palace, filling one whole end of the little square. At its centre a powerful figure of the ocean rides a shell chariot pulled by sea horses, one wild and one calm, led by tritons, all carved in pale stone among artificial rock and pools of blue-green water.

It marks the end of an ancient water supply. The fountain stands where a Roman aqueduct, restored over the centuries, finally delivers its water into the city. Marking the end point of an aqueduct with a grand public display is a tradition that reaches all the way back to ancient Rome.

The fountain you see was completed in 1762, the work of the architect Nicola Salvi, who did not live to see it finished, and was completed by other hands. It is a high point of the Baroque, a style that loved movement, drama, and the play of water and light, and it delivers all three at once.

The famous custom is simple. Stand with your back to the water and throw a coin over your shoulder with your right hand, and tradition says you will one day return to Rome. So many visitors do this that a small fortune in coins is gathered up regularly and given to charity.

The water itself has a long reputation for purity, and for centuries Romans drew their drinking water from this supply. Small basins at the side were once used by ordinary people to fill their jugs, a working purpose hidden beneath the grand display at the centre.

The detail rewards a slow look. The two sea horses stand for the moods of the sea, calm and stormy; the figures above represent abundance and health; and the rock, plants, and shells are carved so naturally that the whole wall seems to be turning from architecture into living nature.

It is, frankly, almost always crowded. The square is small and the fountain famous, so expect a press of people, raised phones, and sellers, especially in the middle of the day. That crowd is part of the experience now, but it does shape when you might want to come.

For a calmer visit, come very early in the morning or late at night. After dark the fountain is lit, the water glows, and the crowds thin, and many people find the night view the most beautiful of all. Early morning offers the same calm with fresh daylight on the stone.

The fountain sits in a dense knot of narrow streets, a short walk from the Pantheon and other sights of the old centre. There is little to do at the fountain itself beyond look, throw a coin, and move on, so it pairs naturally with a longer wander through the surrounding lanes.

A few practical notes. Climbing into or touching the water is not allowed and is policed, and the steps around the basin fill quickly, so finding a clear viewing spot takes patience at busy times. Keep a hand on your belongings in the crush of people.

Throw your coin, then step back from the crowd to the edge of the square and simply listen for a moment. The same water has fallen here, fed by the same ancient source, for centuries, drawing people to make the same small wish. When you slip back into the side streets, the roar of the fountain fades behind you almost at once.
