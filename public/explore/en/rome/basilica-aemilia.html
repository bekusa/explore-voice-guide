The guide

The story

Look down at your feet. The ground here is flat, pale, and broken into slabs of old marble. Some pieces are white, some are streaked with colour. In a few places, if you look closely, you may see small green stains pressed into the stone. Those stains are one of the strangest marks in the whole Roman Forum. We will come back to them. First, let us get our bearings among these low walls and scattered column pieces.

You are standing in the Basilica Aemilia, on the north side of the Roman Forum. The Senate House, the tall brick building called the Curia, stands close by. Today the site looks like a field of foundations. Imagine instead a long, roofed hall, with rows of columns inside and a line of shops along the front. For many centuries this was one of the largest covered public spaces in the heart of ancient Rome.

The word basilica may make you think of a church. Here it means something older. In Republican Rome, a basilica was a civic hall. It was a place to do business, to settle legal disputes, to meet people, and to stay out of the rain or the summer sun. Bankers, lawyers, merchants, and idle citizens all came through these doors. Only centuries later did Christians borrow this building type for their churches.

The first hall on this spot went up in 179 BCE. Two censors built it, Marcus Aemilius Lepidus and Marcus Fulvius Nobilior. Censors were senior magistrates who managed public works, among other duties. For a time the building carried both of their family names. But the Aemilii, one of Rome's oldest noble families, kept returning to restore and improve it. Over the generations, the building became theirs in name and in reputation.

That family connection matters. In Rome, a public building was also a kind of advertisement. When an Aemilius paid to repair this hall, every visitor saw the family's generosity. In the first century before Christ, a major rebuilding was funded in part with money from Julius Caesar, who was gathering political allies at the time. So this hall was more than a marketplace. It was also a stage for ambition, built in marble.

After a fire in the Augustan age, the hall was rebuilt again, on an even grander scale. Along its front, facing the open Forum, ran a portico dedicated to Gaius and Lucius Caesar. They were the grandsons and chosen heirs of the emperor Augustus. Both young men died early, and the dynasty's hopes moved elsewhere. Their names, however, stayed fixed on this facade for generations of Romans walking past.

The Roman writer Pliny the Elder ranked this basilica among the most beautiful buildings in the world. That is high praise from a man who lived in the capital of an empire. Inside, coloured marble columns held up the roof. Above them ran a carved frieze. Fragments of that frieze survive, and they show scenes from Rome's earliest legends, including the story of the Sabine women. Romans could look up and see their own founding stories.

Now, back to those green stains in the floor. In 410, the Visigoths under Alaric captured and sacked Rome. It was the first time in about eight hundred years that a foreign army had taken the city. The basilica burned. Archaeologists found bronze coins melted into the marble paving by the heat of the fire. The green marks are the remains of that money, probably left behind by people who ran.

Think about what that means. Somewhere in this hall, a banker or a money changer was working on an ordinary day. Coins sat on a table or in a strongbox. Then the city fell, the roof came down in flames, and the metal fused into the floor. Few places in Rome show a single day of disaster so directly. You are not reading about the sack of Rome here. You are looking at its traces.

After antiquity, the basilica did not simply sit and wait. Through the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, people treated the ruins as a quarry. Marble was stripped and carried away for new buildings. Some was burned in kilns to make lime for mortar. That is why so little stands to any height today. Much of this hall now lives inside other structures across Rome, hidden in walls and floors that visitors never connect with this place.

Modern excavation began in the late nineteenth century and continued through the twentieth. Archaeologists slowly cleared the earth that had buried the Forum. They re-erected a few column pieces and laid out fragments so visitors could read the plan. As you walk the edge of the site, try to trace the long rectangle of the hall. Notice the row of shop spaces along the Forum side. Those small rooms once held some of the city's busiest trade.

Stand still for a moment and listen. Today you will hear footsteps on gravel, the voices of other visitors, perhaps birds in the trees on the Palatine Hill beyond. Two thousand years ago, this spot was loud in a different way. People argued over contracts, coins clinked on counters, and merchants called out to customers. The sound has changed completely. The ground, the light, and the line of the Sacred Way beside you are much the same.

The Sacred Way, or Via Sacra, is the main road through the Forum. It ran directly past the front of this basilica. Generals in triumphal processions travelled along it toward the Capitoline Hill. Imagine the crowds pressed against these columns to watch soldiers, captives, and carts of treasure pass by. The basilica was not at the edge of Roman public life. It stood on the route of its greatest ceremonies.

Before you move on, look once more at the floor and the scattered marble. Find a green stain if you can, or simply a coloured slab worn smooth. This hall served Rome for more than five centuries, through republic, empire, fire, and rebuilding. Then it gave its stone to the city that came after. As you walk toward the Curia or down the Sacred Way, notice how much of this ground still carries small marks of the people who once filled it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Enter through the main Roman Forum and Palatine ticket area.
Visit early morning to see the floor before crowds gather.
Bring a hat; the Forum has very little shade.
Combine with the nearby Curia, the Senate House.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven marble and gravel paths.
