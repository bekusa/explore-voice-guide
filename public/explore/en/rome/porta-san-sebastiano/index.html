The guide

The story

Listen first. Tyres hum over old stone, a scooter slows, and its engine echoes under the arch. Look up at the two towers on either side of the road. Their pale marble bases catch the light, and the brick above them turns warm and orange as the sun moves. You are standing at one of the doorways of ancient Rome. For centuries, travellers coming up from the south passed through exactly this gap in the walls.

This is Porta San Sebastiano, the largest gate in Rome's ancient defensive walls. It is also one of the best preserved. The walls themselves are named after the emperor Aurelian, who ordered them in the 270s. Rome had not needed serious walls for a very long time. But in the third century, the empire was under pressure from invaders, and the capital felt exposed. Aurelian's answer was a ring of brick and concrete running for many kilometres around the city.

In Roman times, this gate had a different name. It was the Porta Appia, because it opened onto the Via Appia, the Appian Way. That road was one of the first great Roman highways. It ran south from Rome toward Capua and later all the way to Brindisi, on the heel of Italy. Soldiers, merchants, pilgrims and funeral processions all used it. If you walk out through the arch, the road beyond still carries the old name.

So why is it called San Sebastiano today? The answer lies a little way down that road. Further along the Appian Way stands the Basilica of Saint Sebastian, built over catacombs where early Christians buried their dead. Sebastian was a Roman martyr, often shown in paintings pierced by arrows. Over the medieval centuries, so many pilgrims passed through here on their way to his shrine that the gate slowly took his name.

Now look closely at the gate itself. What you see is not one building from one moment. It is layers. Aurelian's original gate was simpler, with twin arched openings. Around the year 400, under the emperor Honorius, the walls were strengthened and raised. The gate was rebuilt with a single arch, taller towers and the marble facing you can see on the lower parts. Later repairs added still more changes. The stone shows centuries of patching, one generation on top of another.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Step back and compare the materials. The lower sections of the towers are faced with large blocks of white marble. Some of those blocks were very likely taken from older Roman buildings and reused. This was common in late antiquity. Builders in a hurry did not cut fresh stone when ruined monuments lay close at hand. So parts of this defensive gate may once have belonged to tombs or temples along the road.

Now walk to the inner side of the arch, the side facing the city. On the stone of the doorway, look for a small carved figure. It shows the archangel Michael, sword raised, standing over a dragon. Beside it runs a Latin inscription in medieval lettering. It records a battle fought at this gate in the fourteenth century, when local Roman forces held off an attacking army. It is easy to walk past. Once you find it, though, it is hard to forget.

The gate also saw grand arrivals, not only battles. In 1536, the emperor Charles the Fifth entered Rome through this arch. He was returning from a military campaign in North Africa, and the city prepared a triumphal welcome in the style of the ancient emperors. Decorations were put up along his route. Picture banners, crowds and horses on these same stones. The road you are standing on was turned into a stage for imperial power, more than a thousand years after Rome's empire had fallen.

Inside the towers there is something you might not expect. The gate holds the Museum of the Walls, a small museum about Aurelian's fortifications. You can climb through the inner rooms, which feel cool and quiet after the street. Narrow windows frame slices of sky and road. In the twentieth century, the fascist official Italo Balbo used these rooms, and some decorative floors from that period still survive. It is a strange mix: ancient brick, medieval stone, and modern political history in one building.

The best part of the museum, for many visitors, is the walk along the top of the walls. From the gate, a stretch of the old sentry walkway is open to the public. You move along a narrow passage where Roman guards once stood watch. Through the gaps, you see umbrella pines, rooftops and the line of the Appian Way leading out into the countryside. From up here, you understand how the wall works as a single system, tower after tower.

Come back down and walk a few steps inside the gate. Just behind it stands another arch, often called the Arch of Drusus. Despite the name, most scholars think it was not built to honour Drusus at all. It was part of an aqueduct branch that carried water toward the great Baths of Caracalla nearby. Later, it was absorbed into the defences around the gate. It is a good reminder that names in Rome are not always reliable history.

Now turn and face south, out through the arch. Beyond this point, the city changes quickly. The traffic thins. Walls of old villas line the road, and the paving turns to dark, rounded stones. This is the start of the Appian Way park, a protected landscape of tombs, ruins and fields. Many Romans come here on weekends to walk or cycle. Locals sometimes call this road the queen of roads, a nickname that goes back to the ancient poets.

Before you move on, take one more look at the arch. Notice how the road narrows as it passes through, and how every car has to slow down. That was the point. A gate controlled who came in and who went out. Merchants paid duties here. Guards watched faces here. For more than seventeen centuries, this opening has marked the edge between the city and the open road.

So stand still for a moment. Feel the uneven ground under your feet, and the cooler air in the shadow of the towers. Listen to the traffic passing through the same gap that soldiers, pilgrims and an emperor once used. When you walk away, whether into the city or out along the Appian Way, look back once. The towers will still be there, quietly doing the job they were built for.
