The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of the city has thinned out here, and in its place there is wind moving through umbrella pines and the soft crunch of gravel underfoot. Ahead of you, warm pinkish brick and pale travertine stone rise from a green slope. This is not the Rome of crowded piazzas. It is a quieter edge of the city, and the building in front of you is one of the most surprising places in it.

This is the Great Mosque of Rome, home to the Islamic Cultural Centre of Italy. It sits in the north of the city, close to the Parioli district, at the foot of the wooded hill called Monte Antenne. The river Tiber runs not far away, and sports fields and parkland fill the surrounding area. Many visitors to Rome never come this far north. Those who do find something that feels both very Roman and very new.

The story begins in the 1970s. The city of Rome granted the land for the mosque in that decade, at a time when Rome's Muslim community was growing. Diplomats, students, workers and families from across the Islamic world were living in the Italian capital. They needed a shared place of prayer and gathering. Funding came largely from Saudi Arabia and from a number of other Muslim-majority countries, whose embassies played a role in the project.

The design was led by the Italian architect Paolo Portoghesi, together with Vittorio Gigliotti and the Iraqi architect Sami Mousawi. Portoghesi was a leading voice in Italian architecture, known for his deep knowledge of the Baroque — the dramatic, curving style of seventeenth-century Rome. That background matters. As you look closely, you will see a conversation between two traditions: Islamic architecture and the architecture of Rome itself.

Construction took many years. The foundation stone was laid in the 1980s, and the mosque was inaugurated in 1995. That long timeline says something about the scale and ambition of the work. This was not a small neighbourhood prayer room. It was planned as a major religious and cultural centre, and it is often described as one of the largest mosques in Western Europe.

Now look at the materials. The walls combine travertine, the cream-coloured limestone that Romans have used since antiquity, with terracotta-toned brick. These are the same materials you see in the Colosseum and in countless Roman churches and palaces. Here is the first surprise many visitors miss. The architects chose Rome's own building language on purpose, so the mosque would feel rooted in the city rather than imported into it.

The minaret rises from the complex as a slender vertical line against the sky. Locals often repeat a story about its height. According to this popular account, the minaret was kept deliberately lower than the dome of Saint Peter's Basilica, as a gesture of respect toward the heart of Catholic Rome. Whether or not every detail of that story is exact, it tells you how carefully people watched this building as it rose.

If you are able to step inside the prayer hall, prepare for the second surprise. The columns do not end in simple capitals. Instead, slim ribs branch out from the top of each column and curve upward, crossing and interlacing as they meet. Many people say the effect looks like a grove of palm trees, or like open hands raised toward the ceiling. The whole hall feels less like a box and more like a sheltering canopy.

Above, the central dome is built up in concentric rings of light and shadow, stepping inward toward its centre. Smaller domes gather around it. Light falls through openings in a soft, even way rather than in harsh beams. The floor is covered with carpets, and the space is open and uncluttered, because prayer in Islam happens in rows of people standing, bowing and kneeling together. The architecture is shaped around that movement.

The mosque is more than a prayer hall. The Islamic Cultural Centre includes spaces for study, a library, and rooms for meetings and community events. On Fridays, the main day of congregational prayer in Islam, the complex can fill with people from many countries — North Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, West Africa and Italy itself. You may hear Arabic, Italian, Urdu and French within a few steps of each other.

Visiting is possible, but it works differently from visiting a museum. Guided visits are offered at certain times, and arrangements can change, so it is wise to check with the centre before you come. Dress modestly, with arms and legs covered. Women are generally asked to cover their hair, and everyone removes their shoes before entering the prayer area. Prayer times take priority over tourism, and a quiet, respectful manner is always welcome.

Take a moment to think about where you are standing. Rome has been a centre of the Christian world for many centuries. For a long time, the idea of a major mosque in this city seemed unlikely to many people. Today it stands on a green hillside, built from the same stone as the city's oldest monuments. For many Muslims in Italy, it is a symbol that they belong here, and for many Romans, it is part of the modern city's story.

Before you leave, walk slowly around the outside. Watch how the brick changes colour as clouds pass, from dusty rose to deeper orange. Notice the pines on the hill behind, and the way the white stone edges catch the light. Listen again to the wind and the gravel. When you return to the busier streets of Rome, you may see its travertine and brick a little differently — as a language that more than one faith now shares.
