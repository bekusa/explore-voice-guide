The guide

The story

Stand at the southern tip of the Palatine Hill and listen. Traffic hums along the wide road below. Pine needles crunch underfoot, and the long green hollow of the Circus Maximus opens out beside you. The air smells of warm dust and exhaust. Nothing here asks for attention. And yet, for more than a thousand years, this corner of Rome held one of the strangest buildings the city ever made: a giant decorated wall called the Septizodium.

Let us be honest from the start. If you look for the Septizodium today, you will not find it. There is no ruin to touch, no column to lean against. The building was taken apart in the sixteenth century. So this guide is about an absence. It is about imagining a tall screen of marble and columns rising right here, at the foot of the imperial palace, where today you see only slope, trees, and sky.

The builder was the emperor Septimius Severus, who ruled at the end of the second century and the start of the third. Severus was born in Leptis Magna, a city on the coast of North Africa, in what is now Libya. He was the first Roman emperor from Africa. His family transformed the Palatine with new palace wings, and the Septizodium was completed around the year 203, as part of that great building programme on the hill.

So what was it? Scholars still debate the exact purpose. The best evidence suggests a monumental facade, a decorative front with no real building behind it. Think of a stage backdrop made of stone. Columns stood in tiers, one row above another, probably three levels high. Between them were niches, and many historians believe water flowed through the structure, like a grand public fountain. Romans loved these display fountains, which they called nymphaea, after the water spirits of myth.

The name itself carries a puzzle. Septizodium seems to point to the number seven. One popular explanation links it to the seven planets known in the ancient world — the Sun, the Moon, and five wandering stars. These also gave their names to the days of the week. Some scholars think statues of these planetary gods stood in the niches. It is an attractive idea, but it remains a theory. No one has proven what figures filled those spaces.

Here is the first surprise. Why place a grand facade at this exact corner? Look at where you are standing. This is where the Appian Way, the great road from the south, came into the city. A Roman biography of Severus claims he built the Septizodium so that travellers arriving from Africa would see his work first. That source is known for colourful inventions, so treat the story with care. But the location fits. The facade faced the road, not the palace.

Picture a traveller in the third century. After days on the dusty road, they pass through the city gate nearby. Ahead, the Palatine rises, crowded with palace walls. And at its foot, a shining wall of coloured marble and columns catches the light. For visitors from the provinces, it would have been a clear message. This is the emperor's city. This is his home. And he wants you to notice it.

The second surprise is how long the Septizodium survived. Rome fell from power, the palace emptied, and many great buildings collapsed or were stripped for stone. But part of this facade kept standing through the entire Middle Ages. Medieval Romans built fortifications into ancient ruins, and the surviving section was used in this way. Powerful families turned old monuments into strongholds. A decorative wall built for show became, for a while, part of a defensive position.

By the Renaissance, only a fragment remained, perhaps three bays of columns stacked in tiers. Artists came to draw it. Their sketches matter enormously today, because they are the main record of how the Septizodium looked. Architects studied its proportions and its layered columns. Long after its original meaning was forgotten, the ruin was still teaching people how Romans combined columns, height, and rhythm on a single facade.

Then came the end. In the late sixteenth century, Pope Sixtus the Fifth ordered the remains taken down. Sixtus was a builder in a hurry. In just a few years, he cut new streets across Rome, raised obelisks, and finished major projects. Ancient ruins were a convenient quarry. The marble and stone of the Septizodium were carried away and reused in new buildings around the city. Fragments of this facade may still sit inside Roman churches, unrecognised by the people who pass them.

It is a strange thought. A monument built to greet arrivals from Africa did not fully vanish. It was scattered. Rome has always recycled itself in this way. Columns move from temples to churches. Blocks move from arenas to palaces. The city you are walking through is built partly from older versions of itself. The Septizodium is simply one of the clearest examples, because we know its name, its builder, and roughly when it was taken apart.

As you stand here, use the landscape to rebuild it in your mind. The road below follows the line of ancient traffic heading toward the city centre. The Circus Maximus, the old chariot-racing ground, stretches away to one side. Above you, the brick walls of the Severan palace still cling to the hillside. Those walls are real survivors from the same family and the same era. The Septizodium once stood in front of them like a bright curtain.

The brick arches above are worth a long look. They are the great platforms Severus built to extend the palace out over the slope. In the afternoon, the sun turns the brick a deep orange, and the arches throw long shadows. It is easy to miss them while watching traffic. But they are the closest you can come to the world of the Septizodium. The same emperor wanted both the palace and the facade to be seen from here.

Before you walk on, pause and look up at the empty space at the foot of the hill. Imagine three rows of columns, water running in the niches, and the sound of carts on the road. Then let the picture fade, and hear the modern traffic again. Rome is full of famous ruins. This corner holds a monument that is gone. You have to imagine the Septizodium to see it.
