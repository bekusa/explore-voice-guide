The guide

The story

Stand still on this corner of the Quirinal Hill and listen. Traffic hums along the wide avenue behind you. Water splashes from a large fountain just down the street. In front of you rises a pale travertine facade, cool and grey-gold, catching the light on its columns. The stone looks almost soft in the afternoon sun. This is Santa Susanna, and its front is one of the most important pieces of architecture in Rome.

That may seem like a big claim for a church that many visitors walk straight past. But look closely. The facade was designed by Carlo Maderno and completed in 1603. Architecture historians often describe it as a turning point. It is one of the first church fronts in Rome where you can see the energy of the Baroque style. Baroque is the dramatic, theatrical style that came to define the city in the seventeenth century.

So what makes this front different? Follow the columns from the outer edges toward the centre. Notice how they step forward, layer by layer. Flat pilasters at the sides become half-columns, and half-columns become fuller columns near the door. The whole facade seems to push gently toward you. It creates rhythm and depth on a flat surface. Earlier church fronts in Rome were calmer and more evenly balanced. Here, the eye is pulled firmly to the centre.

Here is something many visitors miss. A few years after finishing this church, Maderno was given an even larger job. He designed the facade of Saint Peter's Basilica in the Vatican. In a sense, the ideas you see on this quiet street corner were a rehearsal for the most famous church front in the Catholic world. Standing here, you are looking at the young architect's working notes, carved in stone.

The church itself is much older than its facade. Christians have worshipped on this spot since late antiquity. Santa Susanna is one of Rome's ancient titular churches. A titular church is one of the early community churches of the city, often founded in a private house. Each one is still assigned to a cardinal today. Excavations beneath the church have found the walls of Roman buildings, a reminder that the floor you would stand on rests on far older foundations.

Now for the woman behind the name. According to tradition, Susanna was a young Roman Christian who lived in the late third century, during the reign of the emperor Diocletian. Legend has it that she was asked to marry a member of the emperor's family. She refused, saying she had dedicated her life to God. The story says she was put to death for that refusal, in her own home. Her house, the tradition continues, later became this church.

It is worth pausing on that word: tradition. Historians cannot confirm the details of Susanna's life, and her story belongs to a large family of early martyr legends. What is clearer is that a place of worship stood here very early, and that the building was rebuilt many times. A major rebuilding took place in the late eighth century under Pope Leo the Third. Further restorations followed in the fifteenth century, long before Maderno arrived.

The great renewal around 1600 was paid for by Cardinal Girolamo Rusticucci, who held the title of this church. He wanted a building that matched the new confidence of Rome after the religious upheavals of the sixteenth century. The Catholic Church was investing heavily in art and architecture to inspire the faithful. This facade, with its strong vertical lines and central focus, was part of that effort. It was designed to stop you in the street.

If you are able to go inside, the interior holds a second surprise. The walls are covered with large painted scenes by Baldassare Croce. They tell the story of a different Susanna — the Susanna of the Old Testament. In that story, a virtuous woman is falsely accused by two elders and later saved by the young prophet Daniel. The painter framed these scenes to look like enormous tapestries hanging on the walls. From a distance, the illusion of fabric is surprisingly convincing.

For much of the twentieth century, this church had a special role. From the 1920s, it served as the national church for American Catholics living in or visiting Rome. It was cared for by an American religious order, the Paulist Fathers. Americans came here for Mass in English, for weddings, and for a sense of home far from home. That chapter ended in the 2010s, when the American community moved to another church nearby.

In recent years, the building has faced long periods of closure for structural repair and restoration. Access can be limited, so it is wise to check locally before planning a visit inside. The church is also connected to a community of Cistercian nuns. The Cistercians are a monastic order known for prayer, silence, and simple living. Their presence keeps this corner of the Quirinal a place of quiet devotion, even when the doors are closed to visitors.

Even from outside, this spot rewards a slow look around. Across the small square stands Santa Maria della Vittoria, also with a front by Maderno's circle of influence and an interior famous for Bernini's sculpture of Saint Teresa. A short walk away is the Fountain of Moses, with water pouring into a wide basin. Together they form a small open-air gallery of Rome around the year 1600, all within a few minutes on foot.

Before you move on, look up at the facade one more time. Let your eye travel from the edges to the centre, the way Maderno intended. Notice how the light falls differently on each layer of stone, creating soft bands of shadow. Listen again to the water from the fountain and the traffic on the avenue. Four centuries ago, this front was something new. Today it stands quietly, still guiding the eye toward its door.
