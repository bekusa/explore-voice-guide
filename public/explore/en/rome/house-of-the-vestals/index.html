The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches underfoot, swallows dart low over old brick, and somewhere nearby a tour leader's voice drifts across the ruins. In front of you lies a long, quiet courtyard. Pools of still water run down its centre, and pale marble women stand along its edges. This is the House of the Vestals, in the heart of the Roman Forum, at the foot of the Palatine Hill.

For centuries, this was one of the most unusual homes in the ancient world. It belonged to a small group of priestesses, usually six at any one time. They were the Vestal Virgins, and their duty was simple to describe and hard to carry out. They kept a sacred fire burning. Romans believed that if the flame went out, the safety of the whole city was at risk.

Turn toward the small round ruin just beside the house. That is the Temple of Vesta, goddess of the hearth. Inside it, the eternal flame burned day and night. The round shape is thought to echo the earliest huts of the Roman people, simple homes with a fire at the centre. Part of the temple was rebuilt in the twentieth century, so some of what you see is a careful modern reconstruction.

Now think about who lived here. A Vestal was chosen as a young girl, roughly between six and ten years old. She came from a respectable family, and she had to be free of physical defects. Once chosen, she left her father's authority completely. She entered the service of the goddess and moved into this house. Her childhood, in many ways, ended on the day she arrived.

Service lasted thirty years. Ancient writers describe it in three stages of about ten years each. In the first decade, a Vestal learned the rituals. In the second, she performed them. In the third, she taught the younger girls. After thirty years she was free to leave and even to marry. Here is the surprise... many chose to stay. After a lifetime inside these walls, the outside world felt foreign.

The rules were strict, and the punishments were terrible. A Vestal had to remain a virgin for her whole term of service. If she was accused of breaking that vow, she was not executed by the sword. Instead, she was led to an underground chamber near one of the city gates and sealed inside, alive, with a little food and a lamp. Romans believed this avoided spilling sacred blood. It was still a death sentence.

Yet these women also held privileges that most Roman women never had. A Vestal could own property and make her own will. She had reserved seats at public games. When she walked through the city, an official attendant went ahead of her. Important documents, including the wills of powerful men, were stored in the care of the Vestals. Legend says that if a Vestal happened to meet a condemned prisoner on his way to execution, he could be spared.

The house you see today is not the original one. The earliest home was smaller and stood here long before the imperial age. After the great fire of Rome in the year 64, under the emperor Nero, the area was rebuilt. Later fires and later emperors brought more changes. Most of the brick walls around you belong to the imperial period, when the house grew into a large building of several floors, arranged around this central courtyard.

Stand at one end of the courtyard and look down its length. Imagine it closed in by tall walls and covered walkways with columns. The rooms around it held kitchens, storerooms, and private quarters. Upper floors once rose above the ground level, though most of them are gone now. Some traces of heating systems and staircases survive. It was comfortable by Roman standards, but it was also inward-looking. Life here faced the courtyard, not the street.

Now walk slowly past the statues. They show senior Vestals, the women who led the order. Many stand on pedestals with carved Latin inscriptions praising their virtue and their service. Look closely at the inscriptions, and you may spot one where a name has been deliberately chiselled away. Nobody knows for certain why. One popular theory suggests that this Vestal later turned to Christianity, and her name was erased as a result. It is a tempting story, but it remains a theory.

The end of the order came in the late fourth century. As Christianity became the official religion of the empire, the old public cults lost their support. Under the emperor Theodosius, the sacred fire was put out and the Vestals were dissolved. After more than a thousand years of tradition, the house fell silent. Over the following centuries, earth and rubble slowly buried it, and the Forum itself became a field where cattle grazed.

Archaeologists uncovered this courtyard again in the late nineteenth century. Many of the statues were found here, some broken, some face down in the soil. Today, the pools are often planted with flowers, and in late spring you may find roses blooming along their edges. It is a gentle detail. It softens the stern history of the place and gives the courtyard a feeling of a garden, which it may well have been in ancient times.

Before you move on, pause here for a final moment. Look at the faces of the marble Vestals, worn smooth by weather and time. Then look past them, toward the round temple and the slope of the Palatine above. For centuries, women stood where you are standing, listening for the crackle of a flame they could not let die. As you walk away, notice how quiet this corner feels, even with the whole Forum moving around it.
