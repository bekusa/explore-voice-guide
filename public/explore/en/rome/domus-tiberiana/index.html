The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here on the Palatine Hill, the noise of Rome drops away. You hear gravel under shoes, the dry rattle of wind in the pine needles, and the high screech of swifts cutting across the sky. Below the edge of the hill, the Roman Forum spreads out like a broken stone map. You are standing on top of an imperial palace. Most of it is under your feet.

This is the Domus Tiberiana. The name means, simply, the House of Tiberius. Tiberius was the second Roman emperor, the stepson and successor of Augustus, and he ruled in the first century. Ancient writers tell us he was born here on the Palatine. That is fitting, because this hill was the most exclusive address in Rome. Senators, generals and powerful families had built their houses here long before any emperor arrived.

Here is the first surprise. The name may promise more than the building delivers. Tiberius is linked to this palace, but archaeologists believe much of what survives came later. A large part of the structure is thought to date to the reign of Nero, in the middle of the first century. Later emperors kept adding to it. Domitian enlarged it. Hadrian, in the second century, extended it further. So the House of Tiberius is really the work of many rulers, layered one on top of another.

Why build here? Look at the position. This northwest corner of the Palatine stands directly above the Forum, the political and religious heart of the city. From these terraces, an emperor could look down on the temples, the law courts and the speakers' platforms where Roman public life took place. Power was not only exercised in Rome. It was also displayed. A palace on this edge made the ruler visible, and it let him watch.

Ancient writers also tell a famous story about Caligula, the emperor who followed Tiberius. According to the historian Suetonius, Caligula pushed his palace down toward the Forum. He is said to have used the Temple of Castor and Pollux, below in the valley, as a kind of grand entrance hall. Historians treat parts of this account with caution. Suetonius enjoyed scandal. But the story shows how Romans remembered this palace — as a building that kept reaching outward.

Now, if you can, find a view of the hill's northern face from the Forum side, or from the paths that lead down. You will see tall brick arches, one stacked above another. These are substructures — huge supporting walls that held up the palace terraces above. Much of this brickwork is linked to Hadrian's expansion. The Romans built with concrete faced in thin red brick, and that combination let them raise these walls to the height of several stories.

Those arches also hide something clever. Domitian, at the end of the first century, built a monumental covered ramp that climbed from the Forum up toward the palace. It zigzagged up the slope in long sloping sections. Visitors could walk from the public square below to the private world of the emperor above, under cover, without the steep climb in the open sun. Parts of this ramp still survive inside the hillside.

The palace did not simply disappear when the empire faded. Over the centuries, its rooms were stripped of marble and decoration. Stone was reused in new buildings across the city. Earth and rubble slowly filled the lower levels. By the time of the Renaissance, much of the ancient structure lay buried. What remained above ground became the foundation for something completely different.

That something is the garden around you. In the sixteenth century, Cardinal Alessandro Farnese, from one of the most powerful families in Rome, created the Farnese Gardens on top of the ancient palace. They were among the first private botanical gardens in Europe. Terraces, staircases, fountains and rare plants replaced the halls of emperors. Here is the second surprise: for centuries, people strolling through these flowerbeds had little idea of the scale of the palace sleeping beneath them.

Look for the two small pavilions with steep roofs near the top of the garden. These are the Farnese aviaries, built to hold birds. Imagine the sound of them once: songbirds behind fine netting, water running in the fountains, the cardinal's guests walking slowly in the shade. Today the birds you hear are wild ones — swifts, starlings, gulls from the Tiber. But the garden still keeps its terraces, its gravel walks and its sense of calm order.

For most of the late twentieth century, the Domus Tiberiana itself was closed to the public. The structure needed long and careful work to make it stable and safe. It reopened in 2023, after roughly half a century of restoration. Visitors can now walk through parts of the ancient rooms again. Inside, the brick walls show patches of repair from many periods, and there are displays of objects found during the excavations.

As you move through, pay attention to the change in temperature. Step from the open garden into the vaulted rooms and the air turns cooler and still. The light comes in from the side, falling across rough brick and concrete. Notice how thick the walls are. These rooms were built to carry enormous weight above them. Standing here, you are inside the engineering that held up an emperor's world.

Before you leave, go back to the edge of the terrace. Look down at the Forum again — the columns, the arches, the paths full of small moving figures. Then look at your own feet, at the gravel and the grass. Somewhere under them, emperors ate, worked, schemed and slept. When you walk away, listen once more for the swifts, and take a last look at those red arches holding up the hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the Forum below on one route.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and uneven stone.
Bring water and a hat; terraces have little shade.
Check in advance whether the palace interiors need booking.
Visit late afternoon for softer light over the Forum.
