The guide

The story

Take a slow breath as you go down. The air changes within a few steps. It turns cool and damp, and it carries the mineral smell of old stone that has stayed in shade for centuries. The noise of the Forum fades above you: the voices, the footsteps, the scooters on the road. What is left is the sound of your own shoes on the stairs, and sometimes the quiet drip of water. You are now entering the oldest prison in Rome.

The ancient Romans called this place the Carcer, the simple Latin word for prison. The deepest chamber had its own name, the Tullianum. The name Mamertine came much later, in the Middle Ages, and nobody is fully sure where it came from. The building sits at the foot of the Capitoline Hill, where the hill meets the Roman Forum. That position mattered. The prison stood a few steps from the Senate house and the speakers' platform, close to the heart of Roman political life.

Here is the first surprise. Ancient Rome did not really use prison as a punishment. Romans did not sentence people to years behind bars. Instead, a place like this held prisoners for a short time, usually while they waited for trial or for execution. For many of the people brought here, this was the last stop. The state did not intend them to leave. That fact changes the meaning of the rooms around you. They were not built for long stays. They were built for endings.

The complex has two levels. The upper room has an irregular shape, with walls of large, heavy stone blocks. Below it lies the Tullianum, a lower and darker chamber. In the original design, there was no staircase to the lower room. The only way in was a round opening in the floor of the upper room. Guards lowered prisoners through that hole into the dark. Look up when you reach the bottom, and you can still see where that opening sits above you.

Why the name Tullianum? One old explanation links it to Servius Tullius, one of the legendary early kings of Rome. A more practical explanation links it to an old Latin word for a spring of water. That second idea fits what you find here. Water rises naturally in this ground, and some scholars think the lower chamber began as a cistern or a sacred spring before it became a cell. Archaeological work in recent years has studied these early phases and shown how old the site truly is.

The Roman historian Sallust wrote about this lower chamber in the first century BCE. He described it as a place sunk deep in the ground, enclosed by walls, with a vault of stone above. He said it was dark, filthy, and that its smell was terrible. It is a rare thing, to stand in a room and hear how a Roman writer described the very same space more than two thousand years ago. His account came from a dramatic political crisis of his own time.

That crisis was the conspiracy of Catiline in 63 BCE. Catiline was a Roman aristocrat accused of plotting to overthrow the Republic. The consul Cicero exposed the plot, and several of the conspirators were arrested. After a heated debate in the Senate, they were brought down into the Tullianum and strangled. Cicero then stepped out in front of the crowd and announced their fate with a single word in Latin, meaning simply, they have lived. The Romans often spoke of death with this kind of indirect language.

Other famous prisoners ended their lives here. Jugurtha, a king of Numidia in North Africa, fought a long war against Rome. After his defeat, he was paraded through the city and then left to die in this prison. Later, the Gaulish leader Vercingetorix, who had united tribes against Julius Caesar, was held for years after his surrender. He was displayed in Caesar's triumphal procession and then executed here. Defeated enemies of Rome were often shown to the people first, and killed afterwards.

Now for the second surprise: this pagan state prison became a Christian holy site. According to a strong Christian tradition, Saint Peter and Saint Paul were held here before their martyrdom. Legend has it that Peter converted two of his jailers, and that a spring rose miraculously from the floor so he could baptise them. Historians cannot confirm this story, and it is best understood as faith and legend rather than documented history. But it shaped the building completely, and pilgrims have come here for centuries because of it.

You will see signs of that devotion around you. In the lower chamber, look for an altar with an upside-down cross. It refers to the tradition that Peter asked to be crucified head downwards, because he did not feel worthy to die in the same way as Christ. You may also find a stone where, according to one popular tale, Peter's head struck the wall as he was pushed down the stairs. These are pious traditions, but they show how strongly people wanted to connect with this place.

When you step back outside, look up. Above the prison stands a church dedicated to Saint Joseph of the Carpenters, built by the carpenters' guild of Rome in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. So you have a church built directly on top of a prison, which itself may sit on top of an ancient spring. This layering is typical of Rome. The city rarely removed its past. It built over it. Each layer reused the old one and gave it a new purpose.

On the outer face of the old prison, look for the ancient Latin inscription carved into the stone. It names two Roman consuls and records work on the building during the early Imperial age. It is easy to walk past without noticing. Nearby, the path towards the Capitoline once held the Gemonian Stairs, where Romans sometimes left the bodies of executed prisoners on public view. Today that history is mostly invisible, but the ground under your feet is the same ancient slope.

Take a moment now, before you move on. The Forum lies just below you, with its broken columns and warm, honey-coloured stone in the sunlight. Behind you are two small, cold rooms where some of the most powerful enemies of Rome spent their final hours. As you walk away, notice how quickly the noise and the warmth of the city return. It is only a few steps from the open sky of the Forum to the darkness of the Tullianum, and only a few steps back.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for the steep, damp stairs.
Bring a light layer because the lower chamber stays cool.
Visit before exploring the Forum to understand its politics.
Go early in the day to avoid crowds in small rooms.
