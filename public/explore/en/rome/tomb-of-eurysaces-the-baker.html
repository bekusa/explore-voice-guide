The guide

The story

Listen first. Trams grind and rattle over the junction. Cars swing around the great arches of the old gate. Below all of this noise, in a sunken pocket of ground, sits a block of pale, pitted stone. It looks strange at first glance, almost industrial. Rows of round holes stare out from its sides like open mouths. This is the Tomb of Eurysaces the Baker, and it was built more than two thousand years ago. It is one of the most unusual monuments in Rome.

You are standing at Porta Maggiore, where two ancient roads once left the city: the Via Praenestina and the Via Labicana. Romans did not bury their dead inside the city. So the roads just outside the gates filled with tombs. Families competed for the best spots, where travellers would pass and read the names. A tomb here was a kind of billboard. It said: remember me, and remember what I achieved. Eurysaces chose his spot very well.

Who was he? The inscription gives his full name: Marcus Vergilius Eurysaces. Eurysaces is a Greek name. Many historians think this suggests he was a freedman, a former slave who won his freedom and then built a fortune. That is a likely reading, not a certainty. What is certain is the date. The style and materials point to the late first century before Christ, the last decades of the Roman Republic. Julius Caesar's world, or just after it.

Now look at the inscription itself. It runs around the top of the monument. In Latin it says, roughly: this is the monument of Marcus Vergilius Eurysaces, baker, contractor, public servant. The word for contractor tells us he did not simply sell loaves over a counter. He took state contracts. He probably supplied bread on a large scale, perhaps for public distributions. The last word in the line is ambiguous. It can mean an official attendant... or simply "it is obvious". Scholars still debate which meaning he intended.

Then there are those round holes. Look at how they sit in rows, some arranged upright, some lying on their sides. Most scholars connect them to the bakery. One popular theory says they show grain measures, the vessels used to count out flour. Another says they show the kneading machines used in large Roman bakeries. Either way, the message is the same. Eurysaces did not hide the source of his wealth. He built his tomb in the shape of his trade.

This is the first surprise. Roman elites often looked down on manual work and commerce. A noble family would decorate a tomb with ancestors, battles, or gods. Eurysaces decorated his with the bread business. It was a bold choice. Today, it feels very modern. It is as if a businessman built his grave in the shape of his factory, and was proud of it. The material helps too. This is travertine, a creamy local stone quarried not far from Rome.

Lift your eyes to the band of carving near the top. This frieze is the real treasure of the tomb. It shows the whole process of making bread, step by step. Workers bring in grain. Donkeys turn the millstones. Men sift the flour, knead the dough, and shape the loaves. Others slide the bread into an oven. Finally, the bread is weighed and checked by officials. It is a comic strip of daily work, carved in stone.

These scenes are a rare gift to historians. Ancient writers rarely described ordinary labour in detail. They wrote about emperors and wars. Here, instead, we see the tools, the tunics, and the teamwork of a Roman bakery. Notice the small figures in plain clothes, bent over their tasks. Some are probably enslaved workers. The scale of the operation is clear. This was not a corner shop. It was something close to a food factory.

The second surprise is how this tomb survived at all. In the late third century, Rome built the Aurelian Walls to defend the city. Later, in the early fifth century, the walls were strengthened under the emperor Honorius. Builders wrapped the old tomb inside a new defensive tower at the gate. For centuries, the baker's monument was hidden inside the fortification. Nobody walking past would have known it was there.

It came back into the light in the nineteenth century. Pope Gregory the Sixteenth ordered the later towers at the gate to be demolished. As the masonry came down, workers found the tomb inside, still standing. Not all of it survived. One side had been lost, probably during the earlier building work. That is why the monument looks incomplete from certain angles. What you see is what the tower protected.

Nearby, excavators also found a relief portrait of a man and a woman. It is usually identified as Eurysaces and his wife, Atistia. An inscription connected to her mentions a breadbasket as her resting place, fitting for a baker's wife. These finds are now kept in Rome's museums, not here on site. So imagine them for a moment: a husband and wife side by side, dressed formally, looking out at the road.

Now turn and look at the gate behind the tomb. Porta Maggiore is much grander than the baker's monument. Its massive arches were built under the emperor Claudius to carry two aqueducts over the roads. The water ran in channels high above, along the top of the structure. The rough, rusticated stone gives it a heavy, powerful look. Later, the aqueduct became part of the city walls. The tomb and the gate now share this crowded corner.

You cannot go inside the tomb. It is viewed from the street level above, so walk slowly around the railings. Each side shows something different. The light changes the carving a lot. In low sun, the shadows deepen and the bakers in the frieze stand out more clearly. At midday, the stone looks flat and pale. The traffic is heavy here, so take care when you cross the roads to get a better view.

Before you leave, look once more at those round openings. A baker made them his signature, two thousand years ago. Around you, the city still moves and eats and works. Somewhere nearby, an oven is probably warm right now, and someone is buying bread. Eurysaces wanted to be remembered for that simple, daily thing. As the next tram passes, you might say he got his wish.

Key facts

Built in the late first century before Christ.

Hidden inside a gate tower for many centuries.
Rediscovered when Pope Gregory the Sixteenth removed the towers.

What to look for

Rough rusticated arches of the Claudian aqueduct behind.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full railing to see each surviving side.
Come in early morning or late afternoon for sharper carving.
Cross the busy junction carefully at marked crossings.
Combine your visit with a close look at Porta Maggiore.
Bring a zoom lens to study the high frieze.
