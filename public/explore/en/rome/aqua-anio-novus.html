The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Trams grind and squeal around you, car horns rise and fall, and above all that noise stands something completely silent. It is a wall of pale, rough-cut stone, stacked high on arches. Run your eye along the top. Up there, a river once flowed through the air, carried into Rome from the hills to the east. This is the Aqua Anio Novus, the New Anio aqueduct. For centuries it was the highest water channel to enter the ancient city.

The name tells you where the water came from. The Anio is the river known today as the Aniene. It rises in the mountains east of Rome and joins the Tiber near the city. Rome already had an older aqueduct drawing from that river, the Anio Vetus, or Old Anio. So when a second one was built from the same source, the Romans simply called it the New Anio. It is a practical name, and the Romans were a practical people when it came to water.

The project began under the emperor Caligula, around the year 38. Caligula did not live to see it finished. He was murdered in 41, and the work passed to his successor, Claudius. Claudius completed it in 52, together with a second great aqueduct, the Aqua Claudia, which carries his own name. The two were planned and built as partners. And that partnership explains much of what you see in front of you.

Here is something many visitors miss. For the final stretch into Rome, the two aqueducts share the same arches. The Aqua Claudia runs in a channel along the top of the arcade. The Anio Novus runs in a second channel stacked directly above it. So when you look up at these arches, you are looking at two separate rivers of water, one riding on the back of the other. It was an efficient solution, and it saved the builders from raising a second line of arches across the plain.

The journey was long. The Anio Novus ran for roughly 87 kilometres, which made it the longest of all of Rome's aqueducts. Most of that distance was not on arches at all. It ran underground, in channels cut into rock or built in tunnels, following the gentle slope of the land. The water moved only by gravity. Roman engineers had to keep a steady, very slight downward gradient across valleys and hills, mile after mile, with simple surveying tools.

The arches only appear where the ground drops away, as it does on the low plain southeast of the city. Here the channel needed to stay high, so the Romans lifted it on these tall piers. Look at the stone. Much of it is a volcanic stone called tufa, along with travertine, a hard, pale limestone quarried near Tivoli. You can see the large, squared blocks. Some still show rough faces, left unfinished, which gives the arcade a rugged, almost wild texture up close.

Now a surprise about the water itself. The Anio Novus was not famous for its purity. Because it drew from a river, its water was often cloudy, especially after heavy rain in the hills. Mud and silt washed into the channel. This was a known problem in Rome. Around the end of the first century, a senior official named Sextus Julius Frontinus wrote a detailed report on the city's water supply. He described the New Anio as often muddy, and he noted that its water could spoil the cleaner supplies when they mixed.

Frontinus is a remarkable figure. He was the curator of the waters, the man in charge of Rome's entire aqueduct system. His book is one of the best sources we have on how the network worked. He wrote about leaks, illegal taps, and dishonest workers who diverted water for private customers. In other words, ancient Rome had water theft, and the man in charge was not afraid to say so in writing.

The muddy water problem did get a solution. Under the emperor Trajan, in the early second century, the intake was moved further up the valley. The new source was a set of lakes near Subiaco, in the mountains. These lakes had been created earlier by dams built for a villa of the emperor Nero. The still lake water let the sediment settle out before it entered the channel. After this change, the New Anio ran clearer, and it became one of the most valued supplies in the city.

Aqueducts needed constant care. Roots cracked the stone, mineral deposits clogged the channels, and arches slowly settled. Later emperors repaired this line more than once. If you are standing at Porta Maggiore, the great double gateway where the aqueducts cross two ancient roads, look at the long inscriptions on the top level. They record the work of Claudius, and later repairs under the emperors Vespasian and Titus. The letters are large and deeply cut, made to be read from the road below.

Porta Maggiore itself was not originally a city gate. It began as a monumental support to carry the aqueducts over the roads. Later, in the third century, the emperor Aurelian built new defensive walls around Rome. His engineers folded the aqueduct arches into those walls, and the crossing became a gate. Just beside it, look for the strange tomb covered in round openings. It belonged to a baker named Eurysaces, and those circles are often read as the shape of his bread-making equipment.

If you are further out, in the Park of the Aqueducts, the experience is very different. There the arches march across open grass, with sheep sometimes grazing and joggers passing underneath. The scale is easier to feel there. You can walk beside the piers and sense how high the top channel really sits. Late afternoon light turns the stone a warm gold. In both places, take a moment to look for the line where the upper channel sits on the lower one.

So, before you move on, look up one more time. That top channel is dry now, but for centuries it carried water from mountain lakes across a whole countryside, without a single pump. Listen again to the trams, the traffic, the city that grew up around these stones. As you walk away, notice how often modern Rome still bends around its ancient water lines. The aqueduct no longer carries a river. It still shapes the streets you walk on.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Porta Maggiore to read the imperial inscriptions.
Walk the Park of the Aqueducts for open views.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on stone.
Watch for trams when crossing at Porta Maggiore.
Bring water and sun protection for the open park.
