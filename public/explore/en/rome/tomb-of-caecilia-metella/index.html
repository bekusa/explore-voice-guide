The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the Appian Way, the noise of Rome falls away. You hear gravel under your shoes, the dry call of birds in the umbrella pines, and the clack of a bicycle on old paving stones. In front of you rises a great round drum of pale stone, warm in the sun and streaked grey by weather. It looks almost too solid to be a grave. Yet that is exactly what it is: a tomb built for one Roman woman, more than two thousand years ago.

This is the Tomb of Caecilia Metella. It stands at about the third mile of the Via Appia Antica, the ancient road that ran south from Rome toward the ports of southern Italy. Romans did not bury their dead inside the city walls. Instead, wealthy families lined the roads outside with monuments, each one competing for the attention of travellers. A traveller on this road would walk past a long parade of tombs. This one was built to win that contest, and it still does.

The tomb dates to the late first century before Christ. That was a turbulent moment, when the Roman Republic was ending and the age of the emperors was beginning. The design is simple and confident. A square base carries a huge cylinder, nearly thirty metres across. The drum is faced in travertine, a creamy limestone quarried near Tivoli, east of Rome. Behind that skin is a core of Roman concrete, the material that let Romans build on a scale nobody before them had managed.

Now look high up on the side that faces the road. You will see a marble panel with a carved Latin inscription. It reads, in short form, that the tomb belongs to Caecilia Metella, daughter of Quintus Creticus, wife of Crassus. That is almost all we know about her. Her father was a general who earned the title Creticus for conquering the island of Crete. Her husband came from the Crassus family — the same family as Marcus Licinius Crassus, once the richest man in Rome.

Here is the first surprise. Despite the size of this monument, Caecilia Metella herself remains a mystery. We do not know when she was born, how she lived, or how she died. Historians still debate exactly which Crassus she married. Her name survives because her family was powerful, and the tomb was really a statement about that family. So you are looking at one of the largest private tombs on the whole Appian Way, built for a woman whose life is almost completely lost.

Just below the inscription, follow the band of carving that runs around the drum. It shows garlands of fruit and flowers hanging between the skulls of oxen. These skulls were a common decoration linked to sacrifice. Over the centuries, local people noticed them, and the whole area took a nickname from them: Capo di Bove, which means the head of the ox. You may still see that name on maps and signs nearby. A small stone detail became the name of a neighbourhood.

Look up again, to the very top of the drum. Those notched battlements, shaped like the tail of a swallow, are not Roman at all. They are medieval. Here is the second surprise: for hundreds of years, this tomb was part of a castle. In the Middle Ages, the Appian Way was a dangerous road, and whoever controlled it could charge tolls and watch for enemies. A massive stone cylinder beside the road was the perfect ready-made tower.

In the early fourteenth century, the powerful Caetani family took control of this stretch of road. The Caetani were the family of Pope Boniface the Eighth. They built walls and a fortified settlement around the old tomb, and they added the battlements you see on top. Across the road, look for the roofless ruin of a small Gothic church dedicated to Saint Nicholas. It belonged to that same medieval complex. A Roman grave had become the heart of a little fortified village.

That later life explains why the tomb survived so well. Many monuments along this road were stripped for building stone, burned for lime, or simply fell apart. This one was useful, so people kept it standing. Even so, you can see the scars. Parts of the travertine facing are missing, and the rough concrete core shows through in places. The wall is a patchwork of Roman stone and medieval brick, built by different people with different ideas.

If the tomb is open when you visit, step inside. The burial chamber is a tall, narrow space, with walls of brick that rise toward a cone of darkness above you. The air is cool, and voices echo softly. Look down near the base, too. The tomb was built on top of an ancient lava flow that came from the volcanic Alban Hills, southeast of Rome. In places you can see this dark, rough volcanic rock. Here, a Roman monument sits directly on top of a much older piece of Italian geology.

The buildings around the tomb now hold a small museum. It displays fragments of sculpture, inscriptions, and carved stone collected from the Appian Way. Walk slowly past them. Each piece once belonged to another tomb, another family, another name meant to be remembered by travellers. Together they help you picture how crowded this road once was with monuments. The whole site is now cared for as part of the archaeological park of the Appian Way.

The tomb also had a second life in literature. In the early nineteenth century, the English poet Lord Byron wrote about it in his long poem Childe Harold's Pilgrimage. He wondered about the woman inside and about the love or pride that built such a tower for her. Many travellers on the Grand Tour, the classic educational journey through Europe, stopped here for the same reason. They looked at this enormous stone drum and asked the same question you might ask now: who was she?

Before you leave, walk a little way along the road itself. Feel the large, uneven paving stones under your feet, worn smooth by centuries of wheels and boots. Then turn back and look at the tomb from a distance. From here you can see all its layers together: the Roman drum, the ox-skull frieze, and the medieval battlements against the sky. One name is cut into marble, and one family wanted the world to remember it. More than two thousand years later, you are standing here, still reading it.
