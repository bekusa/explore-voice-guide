The guide

The story

Listen first. A handcart rattles over the road behind you. A trader calls out a price in Tamil, then in Sinhala. A horn sounds, then another. Then you look up from the crowded street, and the noise seems to fall away for a moment. In front of you rises a building striped in red and white, like a sweet wrapped for a festival. This is the Jami Ul-Alfar Mosque, which most people in Colombo simply call the Red Mosque.

You are standing in Pettah, the old market quarter of Colombo. The name comes from a Tamil word for the area outside a fort. For centuries this ground sat just beyond the walls that protected the colonial port. Merchants, sailors and labourers lived and traded here, outside the gates. The streets are still organised by trade, with whole blocks of fabric, hardware, spices or electronics. The mosque stands on Second Cross Street, in the middle of all this daily work.

The mosque was completed in the early twentieth century, around 1909, and it was built for the local Muslim community of Pettah. Many of these families had roots in South India and long histories in Indian Ocean trade. They needed a large place to pray close to their shops and warehouses. They also wanted a building that showed their presence in the city. Look at the result, and it is clear they achieved both goals.

Now for the first surprise. Most accounts say the mosque was not designed by a trained architect. The name usually given is Habibu Lebbe Saibu Lebbe, a local builder. According to the popular story, he worked partly from pictures of Indo-Islamic buildings that traders brought back from India. Whether every detail of that story is exact, nobody can fully say. But it explains a lot. The building looks like an imagined palace, assembled with enthusiasm rather than strict rules.

The style is often called Indo-Saracenic. That term describes a blend of Islamic architecture from India with ideas from European Gothic and other traditions. The British used this style for railway stations, courts and palaces across India in the late nineteenth century. Here you can see its features clearly. There are pointed arches, small domes on slim towers, and rows of decorative turrets. Yet the red and white stripes give the whole building a character that is entirely its own.

Let your eyes move slowly across the façade. The stripes run in bands, horizontal on the walls and curving around the arches. Some arches are pointed. Others have scalloped edges, like the rim of a leaf. Above them, little domed pavilions sit on the corners of the roofline. Each pavilion is crowned with a small finial, a pointed ornament that rises toward the sky. Nothing here is plain. Almost every surface carries a pattern, a frame or a band of colour.

Here is the second surprise, and it comes from the sea. Colombo's harbour lies only a short walk from where you stand. Before the city filled with tall towers, the Red Mosque was easy to see from the water. Locals say that sailors approaching the port used its red silhouette as a landmark. It is a lovely idea, a place of prayer acting as a guide for ships. Today the towers around it are taller, but from street level the mosque still holds your eye.

The building has not stayed frozen. Later in the twentieth century, the mosque was enlarged so that more worshippers could fit inside. The congregation grew as Pettah grew. On Fridays, at midday prayers, the streets around the entrance fill with men arriving from their shops and offices. They leave sandals by the door, wash at the ablution area, and move inside together. If you are here at that moment, you will see the building doing the job it was built for.

Visitors are generally welcome outside prayer times, but this is an active place of worship. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Women are usually asked to cover their hair, and you will remove your shoes before going inside. Speak quietly, and ask before you photograph anyone at prayer. Often a caretaker or volunteer at the entrance will guide you. A little respect here goes a long way, and people are often happy to answer questions.

If you step inside, notice how the mood changes. The street outside is loud and hot. Inside, the light is softer and the air feels calmer. Fans turn slowly overhead. The floor is covered with carpets arranged in rows, all facing the same direction. That direction is toward Mecca, marked by a niche in the wall called the mihrab. The interior is simpler than the façade, and that contrast is part of the experience.

Step back out into Second Cross Street and look at the neighbours. Shopfronts press close on every side. Signs in three scripts hang above doorways. Porters push heavy loads on wooden carts. Nearby, a short walk through these lanes, you can find Hindu temples, Christian churches and Buddhist shrines. The mosque is one part of a district where many communities have worked side by side for generations. Its bright walls are a reminder of that long shared history of trade.

If you want photographs, the street is narrow, so a wide view is difficult from the pavement. Many visitors stand at the far corner of the street to fit the towers into the frame. Morning light tends to be gentler, and the red appears warmer before the sun is overhead. Watch for traffic, though. Pettah moves fast, and cars and carts will not wait for your shot. Look for gaps between the buses, and be patient.

Before you leave, take one last look at those stripes. Count the bands on a single column if you like. Notice where the paint is fresh and where it has faded in the sun and rain. Then let the sounds of the market come back around you, the carts, the voices, the horns. The mosque has stood in the middle of this noise for more than a century. As you walk on, look back once. The red will still be there, rising above the street.
