The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. It comes straight off the Indian Ocean, warm and salty, and it pushes against your shirt. Under your feet, the grass is short and a little worn. Ahead, a low sea wall holds back the waves. Behind you, Colombo goes on with its traffic and its towers. Here, for a few hundred metres, the city opens up. This is Galle Face Green, and it has been the city's open-air living room for a very long time.

Look around you. The green is a long, narrow strip of lawn that runs along the coast. On one side is the sea. On the other side is Galle Road, one of the oldest roads in the city. The ground is flat and open. There are no tall trees in the middle, and there are few buildings close by. In a crowded city, that flat emptiness is very rare. People come here simply to have space.

That emptiness began as a military decision. Colombo's old fort stood just to the north of here. Under Dutch rule, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, historians generally say this ground was kept clear on purpose. An open field gave the fort's cannons a clear line of fire. No enemy could hide here. So the lawn where families now spread their mats started life as a deliberate gap in the city... a place built for watching, not for living.

The British changed its purpose. In the mid-nineteenth century, the colonial governor Sir Henry Ward oversaw the laying out of the promenade, and the work is usually dated to around 1859. An old inscription connected with the green is often quoted. In it, Ward recommends the walk to his successors for the enjoyment of the ladies and children of Colombo. It is a small detail. But it shows that, even then, this was meant to be a place for ordinary leisure.

For much of the colonial period, the green was a sports ground. Records describe horse racing here, and later cricket, golf, rugby and polo. Imagine riders in the heat, and spectators under parasols. Today the sports are more informal. You may see boys playing cricket with a taped ball, or a quick game of football on the grass. The tradition of play continues — it simply has fewer rules now.

Now turn towards the southern end. The large white building there is the Galle Face Hotel. It opened in the nineteenth century, and it is one of the oldest hotels in this part of Asia. Its long veranda faces the sea. For generations, travellers have sat there with a cold drink and watched the sunset over the water. Even if you are not staying there, the building gives the green a strong frame at its southern edge.

At the northern end, the view is very different. There you will see the colonnaded building of the Old Parliament, which now serves as the Presidential Secretariat. Beyond it rise the glass towers of the business district and the hotels. Further out to sea, land has been reclaimed for the new Port City development. So in one look, you can see the colonial past, the government, and the city that Colombo wants to become.

Here is something many visitors do not know. In 2022, this green became the centre of a large protest movement. During a severe economic crisis, thousands of Sri Lankans gathered here for weeks. They set up tents, kitchens and libraries on the lawn. People called the camp GotaGoGama. The movement is known in Sinhala as the Aragalaya, which means the struggle. The protests helped bring about the resignation of the president. The open ground that once served the fort's guns became a place for citizens to speak.

As the afternoon cools, the green changes. Late in the day, families arrive in large numbers. Children run across the grass. Couples sit on the sea wall. Vendors push their carts along the promenade. The sound changes too. The traffic is still there, but now you hear laughter, the call of sellers, and the steady crash of waves against the rocks below the wall. This is the hour when the place feels most like itself.

Look up, and you will probably see kites. Flying kites is a much-loved pastime here, because the sea wind is strong and steady. Some kites are simple paper diamonds. Others are long and bright, shaped like birds or dragons. Sellers often carry bundles of them along the path. If you have never flown a kite on a sea wind, this is a good place to try. The wind does most of the work for you.

Then there is the food. The smell of hot oil and spices will find you before you find the stalls. The best-known snack here is isso vadai — a fried lentil fritter topped with whole prawns. Vendors also sell spicy fried snacks, roasted corn, and fresh pineapple or mango sprinkled with chilli and salt. Many people buy something small, walk to the wall, and eat while they look at the sea. It is a simple ritual, and an old one.

The sunset here deserves your attention. Colombo faces west, so the sun goes down directly over the ocean. On a clear evening, the sky turns orange and then pink. The water reflects the colour back. The ships waiting offshore become dark shapes on the horizon. Many people stop talking at this moment. Even the vendors seem to slow down for a few minutes.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the sea wall. Feel the spray, if the waves are high. Watch the kites against the darkening sky. Notice the mix of people around you — office workers, students, grandparents, visitors from far away. This green was cleared for war, shaped for colonial leisure, and claimed by citizens in protest. Tonight, it is simply a place to breathe. As you walk back towards the city, listen for the sea behind you... it will stay with you for a while.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive about an hour before sunset for the best light.
Bring a hat and water for midday heat and sun.
Try isso vadai from a busy stall near the promenade.
Keep phones away from the sea wall on rough days.
Visit on a weekday evening for a calmer atmosphere.
