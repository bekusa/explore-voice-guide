The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, water rushes over stone, a steady roar that rises out of the canyon. The air is cool and thin, because you are high in the Andes of southern Colombia. Mist often drifts along the cliffs. Then the church comes into view: grey and white stone, pointed towers, and a bridge that spans a deep gorge. It looks less like a building and more like something carved out of the mountain itself.

This is the Sanctuary of Las Lajas, a short drive from the town of Ipiales and only a few kilometres from the border with Ecuador. The name comes from the Spanish word for flat stone slabs, the layered rock that forms these canyon walls. The church stands over the Guáitara River, a river that has cut this gorge over a very long time. The church does not sit beside the canyon. It sits inside it, built straight across the void.

The story of why anyone would build here begins with a legend. Locals say that in the middle of the eighteenth century, an Indigenous woman named María Mueses was travelling through this canyon with her young daughter, Rosa. A storm caught them, and they took shelter among the rocks. According to the tale, Rosa had never spoken. Yet in that cave she pointed at the stone and called out that a woman was calling her.

When the mother looked, the legend says, she saw an image of the Virgin Mary on the rock face. Later versions of the story add more miracles, including Rosa falling ill and being healed after her mother brought her back to pray at the stone. These accounts are folklore, passed down through generations, not documented history. But they explain something real. For centuries, people have walked down into this gorge to pray at the image on the rock.

And here is the first detail many visitors miss. The image is not a painting hung inside the church. It is on the canyon wall itself. When you step inside and look toward the altar, you are looking at living rock. The church was designed so that the cliff face forms the back wall of the sanctuary. The image shows the Virgin holding the infant Jesus, flanked by two figures, and its colours appear to sit inside the surface of the stone.

Before this building, simpler chapels of straw, wood, and later stone stood on the site. The church you see now was built in the first half of the twentieth century. Work began in 1916 and continued for decades, finishing in 1949. The money came largely from donations by pilgrims and local communities. In 1954, the Catholic Church gave it the rank of a minor basilica, a title for churches of special importance.

The style is Gothic Revival. That means the builders borrowed the look of medieval European cathedrals: pointed arches, slender towers, and tall, narrow windows. Notice how the stone alternates between grey and pale tones, giving the walls a striped, layered effect. It echoes the layered rock of the canyon around it. Here is the second surprise. This medieval look is less than a hundred years old.

Now look down at the bridge. It joins the two sides of the gorge and carries visitors straight into the church square. The basilica is often described as rising about a hundred metres above the river below. Standing on the bridge, lean carefully toward the railing and feel the drop. The sound of the water grows louder. On a misty morning, the bottom of the canyon can disappear completely.

As you walk down the path toward the church, look at the canyon walls on either side. They are covered with small stone and metal plaques. Each one is a message of thanks to the Virgin, left by a pilgrim who believes a prayer was answered. Some mention healings. Others mention safe journeys or recovered loved ones. Read a few. They are the most personal part of this place, written in plain words by ordinary people.

This sanctuary belongs to both sides of the border. Pilgrims come from across Colombia, but many arrive from Ecuador too. On religious holidays the path fills with families, candles, and the murmur of prayer. Vendors sell candles, small religious images, and snacks along the way. Even on a quiet weekday, you will often hear a mass echoing off the canyon walls, mixed with the sound of the river.

Take a moment to think about the engineering. Building a large stone church across a gorge, at high altitude, with the tools of the early twentieth century, was slow and dangerous work. Materials had to come down steep slopes. Foundations had to grip the rock on both sides. The result looks effortless from a distance. Up close, you can see the weight of the stone and the care of the masonry.

For the classic view, many visitors walk to a lookout across the canyon. From there, the whole composition comes together: the cliff, the bridge, the towers, and the white water far below. Light changes quickly here. Morning mist softens everything, while clear afternoons sharpen the stone against the green hillsides. In the evening, the church is often lit, and the glow rises out of the dark gorge.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Feel the cool air on your face and the damp stone under your hand. Listen to the river, the same river that was here long before any chapel. Whatever you believe about the image on the rock, this is a place shaped by faith, effort, and the patience of many hands. As you climb back up the path, glance once more at those small plaques. Each one is a single story, left behind in stone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear layers; the high Andean air feels cold and damp.
Arrive early morning to see mist filling the canyon.
Walk to the opposite lookout for the full view.
Allow extra breath and time for the steep return climb.
Visit after dark to see the church lit up.
