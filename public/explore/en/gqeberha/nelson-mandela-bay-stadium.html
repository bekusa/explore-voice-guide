The guide

The story

From a distance, it looks like a great white flower set down beside the water. You see it long before you reach it. Its pale roof curves in soft, overlapping petals against the sky. Beside it lies a calm lake, and the petals lean toward their own reflection. This is the Nelson Mandela Bay Stadium.

People here call it the Sunflower, and the name fits. The roof is made of white panels shaped like petals. They open outward around a wide bowl. Each panel is held up on a frame of steel and covered with a tight white membrane. From the ground, the structure seems light, almost soft, though it is built of metal and concrete. Up close, the white surface is not smooth at all. It is stretched fabric, pulled tight over curved arms of steel.

The stadium is young. It was built between 2006 and 2009. The reason was a single great event. South Africa had won the right to host the 2010 FIFA World Cup. It was the first time the tournament came to the African continent. Across the country, cities built and rebuilt stadiums to be ready in time.

This was one of three new stadiums placed along the coast for that World Cup. A German design firm, gmp architekten, drew the plans. The building cost around two billion rand. For a city this size, it was an enormous undertaking. The whole structure rose in only about three years.

The site was chosen with care. The stadium stands beside North End Lake, near the heart of the city. Water lies on one side. The streets and hills press in on the other. When the light is low, the white roof glows above the still surface of the lake, doubled in the water below. Birds rest on the flat water, and the reflection ripples and reforms.

Walk around the outside first. Follow the curve of the bowl. The shape is round and smooth, with no hard corners. The designers wanted every seat to feel close to the field. They wanted the crowd to wrap tightly around the players, so the sound and the feeling would stay trapped inside. Wide ramps and open concourses wrap the outside, so big crowds can move in and out with ease.

Step into the seating bowl if you can. It holds about forty-six thousand people. On a full match day, that is a wall of color and noise on every side. The stands rise in two main tiers. From the upper rows, you can see past the rim of the roof to the lake and the city beyond. On match days the noise rises and folds back down from the curved roof.

The stadium carries the name of the wider city region, Nelson Mandela Bay. That name honors Nelson Mandela. He spent twenty-seven years in prison, then became the first democratically elected president of South Africa. To play sport under his name is to keep that history present at every match. The wider bay, the airport, and the city district all carry his name as well.

In 2010, this field carried World Cup matches. Teams from around the world played here. Crowds filled every seat. The long plastic horns of that tournament, the vuvuzelas, made a steady roar that the whole city could hear. For a few short weeks, this corner of the coast belonged to the world. You can still feel the size of that moment in the empty bowl today.

The stadium did not fall silent when the tournament ended. It became a home ground for local sport. Rugby and football are both played here, and the field is wide enough for both. Concerts come here too, because the round shape holds music well. On show nights, light swings up across the white petals. The whole roof becomes a screen of moving color above the lake.

Notice the engineering when you look up. The roof has to stand against strong coastal wind. Salt air pushes in off the bay all year round. The steel frame is shaped to spread that load across the whole ring. What looks like a soft flower is in truth a careful answer to a hard problem. Engineers tested the shape against the wind before a single beam went up.

Stand back one more time and take in the whole shape. A flower made of steel, beside a quiet lake, named for Nelson Mandela. It was raised for one short tournament, but it was made to last far longer. Long after the final whistle of 2010, the white petals still lean toward the water, holding their shape against the wind.
