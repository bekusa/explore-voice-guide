The guide

The story

Stand at the rim for a moment and listen. The wind moves through the ponderosa pines behind you with a soft, steady hiss. The air is thin and cool, even in summer. Then you look down... and the ground falls away into a sea of orange, pink and cream stone spires, thousands of them, packed together like a silent crowd waiting for something to begin.

Welcome to Bryce Canyon National Park. The first surprise is in the name. This is not really a canyon at all. A canyon is cut by a river running through it. Bryce is something different. It is a chain of huge natural amphitheatres, bowl-shaped hollows carved into the eastern edge of a high plateau. The park sits in southern Utah, and its lands reach across both Garfield and Kane counties.

The plateau you are standing on is called the Paunsaugunt Plateau. The name comes from the Paiute language and is often translated as "home of the beaver." The rim here sits more than two thousand four hundred metres above sea level. That height matters. It explains the cold nights, the sudden summer storms, and the snow that can dust the spires even in late spring. If you feel a little short of breath on the trails, the altitude is the reason.

Those stone spires have their own name. They are called hoodoos. A hoodoo is a tall, thin column of rock, often with a harder cap on top that protects the softer stone below. Bryce has one of the largest concentrations of hoodoos anywhere on Earth. Look closely and you will see that no two are the same. Some are slim as chimneys. Others are thick and knobbly, with layers stacked like coins.

So how did they form? The rock here is mostly limestone, siltstone and mudstone, laid down in ancient lakes millions of years ago. Geologists call it the Claron Formation. The real sculptor is water, and especially ice. On many nights of the year, the temperature here drops below freezing, then climbs above it again by day. Melted snow seeps into cracks, freezes, and expands. Slowly, patiently, the ice pries the rock apart.

Rain finishes the work. The limestone here is slightly soluble, so rainwater softens and rounds the edges of each spire. This means the landscape in front of you is not finished. It is still changing. The rim itself is retreating, little by little, over the centuries. The hoodoos you see today will crumble one day, and new ones are already forming in the fins of rock behind them.

Now look at the colours. The warm reds, oranges and yellows come mainly from iron in the rock. The softer lavender and purple tones come from manganese. The pale cream bands have less of these minerals. As the sun moves across the sky, the same wall of stone can shift from pale peach to deep flame orange. Early morning and late afternoon light makes the colours glow most strongly.

People knew this place long before it had an English name. The Paiute people lived and travelled across this region. According to a Paiute story, the hoodoos were once the Legend People, beings who behaved badly. As punishment, the trickster Coyote turned them all to stone. Stand at the rim and look at the rows of figures, and it is easy to see why the story has lasted.

The park's English name comes from Ebenezer Bryce, a Mormon pioneer who settled near here with his family in the late nineteenth century. He helped build a road to gather timber and firewood. Locals say Bryce described the amphitheatre as a terrible place to lose a cow. Whether he said those exact words is uncertain, but the line has become part of the park's folklore. Anyone who has seen the maze below will understand the feeling.

By the early twentieth century, word of these formations was spreading. Railroad companies saw the tourist potential of southern Utah. Bryce became a national monument in the 1920s, and was made a national park later that same decade. Since then, viewpoints along the rim have taken names like Sunrise Point, Sunset Point, Inspiration Point and Bryce Point. Each one looks into the main amphitheatre from a slightly different angle.

If you have time and energy, walk down into the hoodoos. The view from the rim is grand, but the view from below is intimate. Trails like the Navajo Loop and the Queen's Garden Trail drop between the spires. Down there, the walls rise over you, the sounds soften, and the air feels cooler in the shade. Remember that every step down is a step you must climb back up, at high altitude.

The living world here is worth noticing too. Lean close to a ponderosa pine on a warm day and smell the bark. Many people say it smells of vanilla or butterscotch. On the higher, windier parts of the park grow bristlecone pines, a twisted species known for living to great ages. In open meadows, you may spot the Utah prairie dog, a small threatened animal that the park helps to protect.

And when the sun goes down, Bryce shows another side. The park lies far from large cities, so the night sky is exceptionally dark. On a clear, moonless night, the Milky Way stretches across the sky in a pale band. Thousands of stars appear that most city dwellers never see. The park is recognised for its dark skies, and rangers often run astronomy programmes in the warmer months.

Before you move on, take one last look at the amphitheatre in front of you. Watch how the light sits on the tips of the hoodoos. Notice the thin line of shadow running down each spire. Listen again for the wind in the pines. Everything you see is slowly being taken apart by ice and rain, grain by grain. You are seeing it in just one moment of its long life.

What to look for

Harder rock caps balanced on top of slim hoodoos.
Thin lavender bands running through the orange stone.
Vanilla-scented bark on warm ponderosa pine trunks.
Twisted bristlecone pines clinging to windy high ledges.
Prairie dogs standing upright in open grassy meadows.
Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive at Sunrise Point at dawn for the warmest colours.
Carry extra water and pace yourself at this altitude.
Pack a warm layer even on summer evenings.
Return after dark to see the Milky Way overhead.
Allow extra time for the climb back to the rim.
