The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the wind there is the low, steady sound of moving water. It is the Virgin River, and it is the reason everything around you exists. Feel the warmth coming off the rock, even in the shade. Look up, and the walls climb so high that the sky becomes a narrow blue ribbon. The stone glows cream at the top, then salmon, then deep rust near the riverbed. This is Zion, and it is a place made almost entirely by water and time.

Zion sits in the high desert of southwestern Utah. The park spreads across parts of several counties, and its eastern side reaches toward Kane County, where the land opens into slickrock plateaus. Most visitors know only the main canyon. But the park also holds sandstone mesas, hidden side canyons, and the separate Kolob Canyons section to the northwest. Wherever you stand, you are on the edge of a vast staircase of rock. That staircase steps down from the high plateaus of central Utah toward the Grand Canyon.

Start with the stone itself. Much of the great cliff face is Navajo Sandstone. It formed from a desert of wind-blown sand dunes, roughly two hundred million years ago. Look closely at the walls and you will see thin, slanting lines that cross one another at angles. These lines are the faces of ancient dunes, frozen in place. Wind laid them down grain by grain. Later, minerals cemented the sand into rock. So when you look at these cliffs, you are looking at a fossil desert, standing on its end.

Here is the first surprise. The river that carved this canyon looks small. On a calm day it seems almost gentle — shallow, clear, easy to wade in places. Yet it cut through thousands of feet of rock. The secret is sediment. The Virgin River carries enormous amounts of sand and grit, especially during floods. That grit works like sandpaper on the canyon floor. Over millions of years, the land slowly lifted, and the river kept cutting down. The result is the deep, sheer-sided gorge around you.

People lived here long before the park had a name. Ancestral Puebloan communities farmed along the river, and archaeologists have found traces of their storage structures and rock art in the region. The Southern Paiute have deep ties to this landscape, and they still regard it as part of their homeland. When you walk these trails, you walk through a place with a very long human story. Most of that story was never written down, but it is carried in the land and in living memory.

In the nineteenth century, Mormon pioneers settled in the valleys nearby. They farmed the river bottoms and grazed animals on the benches. One early settler is often credited with giving the canyon the name Zion, a Biblical word for a place of peace and refuge. Later settlers added more religious names to the rock formations. That is why you will hear names like the Court of the Patriarchs, with peaks named for Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The landscape carries the faith of the people who arrived here.

The road to protection began in 1909. That year, President William Howard Taft set aside the canyon as Mukuntuweap National Monument, using a name drawn from the Southern Paiute language. The name proved hard for many visitors to say. In 1918 it was renamed Zion National Monument. Then, in 1919, Congress made it a national park — the first in Utah. Within a few years, the railroad and new roads brought tourists. A quiet, remote canyon became one of the most visited parks in America.

Here is the second surprise, and it is on the eastern side of the park. The Zion–Mount Carmel Highway climbs out of the canyon in tight switchbacks and then enters a long tunnel bored through solid rock. It was completed in 1930. As you drive through, watch for brief flashes of daylight on one side. Those are windows cut into the cliff face, originally used to clear rock during construction. Beyond the tunnel, the landscape changes completely. You enter a world of pale, domed slickrock and strange cross-hatched patterns.

That eastern country is worth slowing down for. Checkerboard Mesa rises beside the road, its surface cut by a grid of lines. The horizontal lines come from those ancient dune layers. The vertical cracks come from repeated heating, cooling, and weathering. In this quieter corner, watch the ledges for desert bighorn sheep. Their tan coats blend into the stone, so you often see movement before you see the animal. Many visitors drive straight through. Those who stop often remember it as their favourite part of the park.

Back in the main canyon, water shapes life as much as it shapes stone. Where water seeps out of the sandstone, it creates what are called hanging gardens. Ferns, mosses, and wildflowers grow straight out of the cliff face. Weeping Rock is one of the easiest places to see this. Rain and snow soak into the porous rock above. The water slowly works its way down. When it meets a harder layer, it flows sideways and drips out of the wall, sometimes years after it first fell.

The same water can also be dangerous. The Narrows, upstream along the river, is a famous hike where the trail is the river itself. Hikers wade between walls that close in to a few metres apart. But summer storms miles away can send a flash flood rushing down without warning. Rangers post forecasts, and they close routes when risk is high. Angels Landing, a narrow fin of rock with steep drop-offs, now requires a permit. Both hikes reward care and planning.

In the busier seasons, the main canyon scenic drive is served by a free-running shuttle system, and private cars are restricted for much of the year. The change came about to reduce traffic and noise. The effect is striking. Step off at a quiet stop, and you may hear canyon wrens, with their falling, silvery song, echoing off the walls. You may notice the rustle of cottonwood leaves along the river. The canyon sounds closer to the way it did a century ago.

Before you move on, take one more look around. Notice how the light changes the colour of the cliffs as clouds pass. Find one of those slanting lines in the sandstone and follow it with your eyes. Listen again for the river. It is still working, still carrying sand, still deepening this canyon by tiny amounts. Zion is not finished. As you walk away, you are leaving a landscape that is still being made, one grain at a time.

Good to know

Key facts

First protected in 1909 as Mukuntuweap National Monument.
Became Utah's first national park in 1919.
Cliffs are mostly Navajo Sandstone from ancient desert dunes.
The Virgin River carved the main canyon over millions of years.
The Zion–Mount Carmel Tunnel was completed in 1930.
Look closer

What to look for

Slanting crossed lines in the cliffs from frozen sand dunes.
Ferns and moss growing straight out of Weeping Rock.
Brief daylight windows cut into the long tunnel wall.
The grid-like crack pattern on Checkerboard Mesa.
Cream, salmon, and rust bands changing colour down the walls.
Before you go

Practical tips

Use the canyon shuttle; private cars are restricted much of the year.
Apply in advance for the Angels Landing hiking permit.
Check the flash flood forecast before hiking The Narrows.
Drive the eastern highway slowly to spot bighorn sheep.
Carry plenty of water; the desert heat builds quickly.
