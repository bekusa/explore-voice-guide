The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and let your eyes adjust. In front of you, a ring of silver steel curves up from a green mound and loops back down again. The surface is not smooth. It is cut through with flowing Arabic letters, and the desert light slides across them as you move. Behind you, traffic hums along Sheikh Zayed Road. In front of you, there is only this strange, quiet shape.

This is the Museum of the Future. It opened in February 2022, on the twenty-second day of the second month of the year 2022. Many people in Dubai noted the repeating twos in that date. The museum is run by the Dubai Future Foundation, and its purpose is unusual. Most museums show you the past. This one asks a different question: what might the world look like decades from now?

Look at the overall form first. Architects call this shape a torus, which simply means a ring, like a doughnut stood on its side. The building rises roughly seven storeys above the mound. Inside, there are no columns holding up the main space. The steel shell and its internal frame carry the load together. That is why the ring can look so light, almost as if it is floating above the grass.

The building was designed by the Dubai-based studio Killa Design, led by the architect Shaun Killa. The engineering firm BuroHappold worked on the structure. A shape like this is very hard to build with ordinary drawings. The team relied heavily on computer modelling to calculate every curve, every panel, and every steel member. In a sense, the building is itself an exhibit about the future — it could not easily have been built a generation ago.

Now look at the open centre of the ring. That empty space is not an accident. It is often described as a symbol of the unknown — the future that has not yet been written. The solid steel around it stands for what humanity already knows and has built. The green hill beneath it stands for the earth. Take a moment to frame the sky through that gap. It changes the way you see the shape.

Here is the detail many visitors miss. The Arabic calligraphy on the facade is not decoration painted on top. The letters are the windows. Light passes through the cut shapes into the galleries, and at night the light passes outward, so the whole ring glows with words. The facade is made from more than a thousand stainless steel panels, and each one had to be shaped for its exact position on the curve.

The words themselves come from poetry by Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, the Ruler of Dubai. The calligraphy was created by the Emirati artist Mattar bin Lahej. One idea in the text, put simply, is that the future belongs to those who can imagine it, design it, and carry it out. So the building does not only house ideas about tomorrow. It writes one of those ideas across its own skin, in letters several metres tall.

Walk closer to the base of the mound. Notice the planting around you. The hill is covered in greenery, which is a deliberate choice in a city of sand and glass. The landscaping softens the steel and gives the ring a place to sit. On hot afternoons, you will feel the difference between the open pavement and the shaded edges near the building. Small changes in temperature tell you a lot about how this site was planned.

Inside, the museum works less like a gallery and more like a journey. The exhibitions change over time, so what you see today may differ from earlier visits. Past and current experiences have included an imagined orbital space station set in the year 2071, a vault that presents a living library of plant and animal life, and spaces focused on wellbeing and the human mind. There is also an area designed for younger visitors to explore and play.

That year, 2071, is worth a second thought. It marks one hundred years since the founding of the United Arab Emirates in 1971. The country has a national vision that looks ahead to that centenary. So when the museum invites you to imagine life in 2071, it connects personal curiosity with a national ambition. The future here is not abstract. It has a specific date attached to it.

Think about where you are standing. This stretch of Sheikh Zayed Road is lined with towers, and the Emirates Towers sit very close by. A walkway links the area to the metro, so many visitors arrive on foot above the traffic. Even surrounded by tall buildings, the museum does not try to compete in height. It competes in shape. Among all the straight lines of this road, it is the one building that refuses to have corners.

If you return after dark, the experience shifts completely. The lighting turns the calligraphy into lines of glowing text, and the steel becomes a dark frame around them. From across the road, the words seem to hang in the air. Photographers often prefer the hour just after sunset, when the sky still holds some colour and the letters begin to shine. Daylight shows the metal. Night shows the writing.

Before you go in, or as you leave, try one small exercise. Walk slowly around part of the ring and watch how the reflections move. You will see clouds, palm trees, the towers, and perhaps yourself, bent across the curving steel. The building never looks quite the same from two different points. That is part of its message. The future, too, depends on where you stand.

So take a last look at the ring, the open sky in its centre, and the letters carrying light through the steel. Listen to the traffic again, and to the voices of people arriving. Somewhere inside, a visitor is stepping into an imagined year that has not happened yet. And out here, on this green hill in Dubai, you are standing in the present... looking at a building that was made to point forward.
