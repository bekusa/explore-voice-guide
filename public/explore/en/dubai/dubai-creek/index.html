The story

A wide saltwater inlet curves into the heart of the old city, busy with small wooden boats ferrying people across, larger trading dhows loaded with goods, and the low buildings of the old quarters lining both banks. This is Dubai Creek, the waterway around which the city first grew and the heart of its history.

Long before the towers and the malls, this creek was the reason for Dubai. A natural harbour reaching inland, it gave shelter to boats and a route for trade, and the town grew up along its banks as a port of pearling, fishing, and commerce.

The creek divides the old city into two halves, the trading quarter on one side and the older town on the other, and crossing between them has always been part of daily life here. The water is still a working part of the city, not just a sight.

The classic way to cross is by abra, a small wooden boat that ferries passengers from bank to bank for a tiny fare, packed with commuters and shoppers. The short ride, low on the water with the breeze and the boats around you, is one of the cheapest and best experiences in the city.

Along the banks, wooden trading boats still load and unload goods bound for ports across the region, stacked high on the quays, a survival of the sea trade that built the city and a striking contrast to the gleaming modern port elsewhere.

The old quarters that line the creek, with their markets, wind-tower houses, and museums, are the historic core of the city, so the creek ties together the gold and spice souks, the old fort, and the restored historic district in one walkable stretch.

Longer boat trips and dinner cruises run along the creek and out toward the sea, offering a slower way to see the old city from the water, especially pleasant in the cooler evening when the banks are lit.

The creek is at its best early and late in the day, when the light is soft, the heat eases, and the boats are busy with people rather than just goods. Sunset over the water, with the dhows and abras crossing, is a fine sight.

It is a reminder of how young the modern city is. Within living memory this creek and its trade were the whole of Dubai, and the towers a short distance away did not exist, a contrast the creek makes vivid.

For all the development elsewhere, the creek keeps a working, slightly rough, genuinely old character that much of the city has lost, which is why it rewards time on foot and on the water.

A few practical notes. Crossing by abra is cheap and frequent; pay the small fare on board, hold on as the boat is low and busy, and combine the crossing with the souks and the old quarter on either bank. Evening is the most pleasant time.

Ride an abra slowly across the water, with the old quarters on both banks and the trading boats around you, and the original Dubai comes back into view, a port town on a creek. The water is where the city began. When you step off onto the far bank, the modern towers on the horizon tell the rest of the story.
