The guide

The story

In a restored wind-tower house in the old quarter, a quiet institution sets out to do something unusual for a tourist sight: to explain the local culture and faith openly, over a shared meal, to visitors from anywhere. This is the Sheikh Mohammed Centre for Cultural Understanding, one of the most worthwhile stops in the city.

Its motto, often translated as open doors, open minds, sums up its purpose: to break down misunderstandings between visitors and the local Emirati culture by encouraging honest questions. In a city of fleeting tourism, it offers something rare, real human exchange.

The centre runs cultural meals, where guests share traditional Emirati food while local hosts explain the dishes, the customs, and daily life, and answer any question, however basic or sensitive. The invitation to ask anything is the heart of the experience.

It also arranges guided visits to a nearby mosque for non-Muslims, one of the few ways visitors can step inside a working mosque in the city and learn about the faith directly, with time for questions afterward.

The centre is housed in the old historic district, in a traditional courtyard house with a wind tower, so the setting itself teaches something about how people lived and kept cool before air conditioning.

What sets it apart is the candour. Visitors are encouraged to ask about religion, dress, marriage, law, and anything else they are curious or unsure about, and the hosts answer frankly, which leaves many people with a far clearer picture than guidebooks give.

The shared meals are also simply a good way to taste authentic Emirati cooking, which is otherwise surprisingly hard to find amid the city's international restaurants. The food comes with explanation and generous hospitality.

For travellers who want more than a glittering, surface view of the city, the centre offers depth and context, helping make sense of the culture behind the malls and towers. It rewards curiosity and an open mind.

Sessions are scheduled and popular, so booking ahead is important, and modest dress is appropriate, especially for the mosque visits. The atmosphere is warm and unhurried rather than rushed.

The centre sits within the restored old quarter, among galleries, cafes, and wind-tower houses, so a visit pairs naturally with a wander through that atmospheric district and the nearby creek.

A few practical notes. Book a meal or mosque tour ahead online; dress modestly; and come with questions, as the whole point is to ask. Sessions take time, so plan for a relaxed couple of hours rather than a quick stop.

Sit on the floor over a shared dish, ask the question you were not sure you were allowed to, and listen to an honest answer, and the city opens up in a way no monument can match. The centre is built on conversation. When you step back into the lanes, you understand the place around you far better.
