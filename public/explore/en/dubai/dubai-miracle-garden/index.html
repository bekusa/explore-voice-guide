The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the hum of the city, there is the soft hiss of water dripping into soil. The air smells of wet earth and crushed petals, which is a strange smell to meet in the desert. Look down at your feet, and then look up. Walls of colour rise on every side: petunias, marigolds and geraniums packed so tightly they look like painted cloth. A few minutes' drive away, the sand is pale and dry. Here, everything is green, red, yellow and violet.

This is the Dubai Miracle Garden. It opened in 2013, on Valentine's Day, in the Al Barsha South area of the city. The name sounds like marketing, and partly it is. But the idea behind it is serious. The aim was to grow a flower garden on a very large scale in one of the hottest, driest places on Earth. The garden is often described as holding many millions of flowers. Whatever the exact count this season, it is far more than you can take in on one walk.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The garden does not stay open all year. Dubai summers bring heat that most of these plants cannot survive outdoors. So the garden works with the seasons. It usually opens in the cooler months, from late autumn, and closes again as the spring heat builds. Each new season, much of the planting is redone. The flowers you see today were not here a year ago, and many of them will not be here next year.

That cycle explains how the place feels. It is less like an old botanical garden and more like a stage set that changes every year. Gardeners plant into frames of metal and mesh, and the frames give the flowers their shapes. Look closely at any large figure and you will see small pots or plugs of soil. Each plant sits in its own pocket. From a distance, they merge into one smooth surface. Up close, you can count them one by one.

Water is the key to all of this. The garden relies on drip irrigation, which means thin pipes deliver small amounts of water straight to the roots. Very little is lost to the air. Reports about the garden describe the use of recycled water, which matters in a city where fresh water comes mostly from desalination plants on the coast. Desalination means removing salt from sea water. That is costly in energy, so every drop saved has real value here.

Now walk toward the largest structure, if you have not seen it yet. For several seasons, the centrepiece has been a full-size Emirates Airbus A380, the double-decker airliner, covered in flowers. It was built to the real dimensions of the aircraft. In 2016 it entered Guinness World Records as the largest flower structure in the world. Stand beside the wing and try to picture the frame underneath. Engineers had to support the weight of soil, water and plants at the height of a real wing.

The record was not the only one. In 2018 the garden unveiled a giant Mickey Mouse made of flowers and plants, produced in partnership with Disney. It was recognised as the world's tallest topiary structure. A topiary is a plant trained or shaped into a form, usually a figure or a geometric shape. Children tend to run straight to the cartoon figures. Adults often stop at them too, a little surprised that a garden can hold such a large and detailed figure.

Displays change from year to year, so what stands in front of you may differ from what a friend saw last winter. Visitors often find heart-shaped arches, tunnels of flowers and a floral castle. Some seasons bring giant teddy bears, peacocks or umbrellas hung overhead. Some displays return, and others appear for a single season and then go. This is part of the appeal. Locals who come back each winter often say they come to see what is new, not what is old.

Pause for a moment in one of the flower tunnels. The light inside is different. It filters through stems and leaves, so the ground under you is speckled with shade. The colour on the walls seems deeper here than in the open sun. Sounds soften too. Voices from the main paths drop away, and you hear footsteps on the pavement and the click of phone cameras. In the middle of the day, these shaded paths are also the coolest places to stand.

Notice the people around you. You will hear many languages in a single minute: Arabic, Hindi, Urdu, Tagalog, Russian, English and more. Dubai is a city where most residents were born elsewhere, and the garden reflects that. Families come with grandparents and strollers. Couples come for photographs at the heart arches. Groups of friends come at dusk. For many residents, a visit here is a winter ritual, like visiting a fair when the weather finally cools.

Here is a second surprise. Next door stands the Dubai Butterfly Garden, a separate attraction with its own entry. Inside its domes, thousands of butterflies fly freely in a controlled climate. The two places make a natural pair. One shows what careful watering can do for flowers in the open air. The other shows what a sealed, cooled space can do for living insects. If you have time and energy left, it is worth asking about it at the gate.

As evening comes, the garden changes again. The sun drops low and the flowers turn warmer in colour. Lights come on along the paths and inside some of the displays. The heat of the day eases, and the crowds often grow. Many visitors say the late afternoon is the best time to come, because you see the garden in daylight and then in lamplight. If you can, stay for both and compare how the shapes look.

Before you leave, stop at the edge of any flower bed and look at a single bloom. One petunia, one marigold. It was grown, carried here and set into a frame by a person's hands. Then it was watered, drop by drop, through the dry months of the year. Now look up at the whole wall of colour again. Thousands of single plants make up that wall. As you walk back toward the gate, listen once more for the hiss of water in the soil.

What to look for

Small individual soil pockets behind each flower on the frames.
The full-size floral A380 wing rising above the paths.
Speckled light on the ground inside the flower tunnels.
Thin black drip pipes running along the flower beds.
Heart-shaped flower arches where couples queue for photographs.
Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive in late afternoon to see daylight and evening lights.
Bring water and a hat for the sunny open paths.
Use the shaded flower tunnels to cool down midday.
Visit on weekdays to avoid the heaviest weekend crowds.
Check whether this season's opening dates have been announced.
