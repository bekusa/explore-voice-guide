The story

A single tower climbs higher than any other building on earth, a tapering spire of steel and glass that seems to go up without end, dwarfing the skyscrapers around it. This is the Burj Khalifa, the tallest building in the world, and the centrepiece of modern Dubai.

It opened in 2010, rising to a height that broke every previous record by a wide margin, well over eight hundred metres. Its slender, stepped form was inspired in part by a desert flower and by patterns in Islamic architecture, narrowing in stages as it climbs.

The design, by a team of engineers and architects, solved hard problems of wind and height, using a Y-shaped plan to brace the tower against the desert gusts. It remains, by a clear margin, the highest structure people can visit and live in anywhere.

The main draw for visitors is the climb to the observation decks high up the tower. High-speed lifts rise in moments to viewing floors where the whole city, the coast, and the desert spread out far below, and on a clear day the view runs for many miles.

There are several levels to choose from, the highest near the very top of the tower, with the prices rising with the floors. The view from any of them is vast; from the highest, the people and roads below shrink almost to nothing.

Sunset is the prized time, when the desert light turns gold and the city begins to glitter, so those slots sell out first and cost the most. Booking ahead online is essential, and a timed ticket saves long waits at the base.

The tower stands at the centre of a whole planned district of malls, hotels, and a man-made lake, built around it as a new downtown. At its foot lies a vast shopping mall, one of the largest in the world, so the visit easily fills half a day.

On the lake at its base plays a huge choreographed fountain, with water, light, and music, free to watch each evening. The tower and the fountain together are the set piece of modern Dubai, best seen as night falls.

The building is, frankly, a statement as much as a structure: a young, rich city announcing itself to the world by building higher than anyone had before. Whether that impresses or overwhelms you, its ambition is impossible to miss.

For all its height, the experience is highly managed, through the mall, the queues, and the lifts, so it feels more like a polished attraction than an adventure. Knowing that helps set expectations for a smooth, busy, crowded visit.

A few practical notes. Book a timed ticket online well ahead, especially for sunset; arrive early for your slot; and check the forecast, as haze or cloud can dim the view. The entrance is deep inside the adjoining mall, so allow time to find it.

Stand at the glass high above the city, with the towers reduced to models and the desert stretching beyond, and the scale of what has been built here in a few decades sinks in. The Burj Khalifa is a monument to ambition. When the lift drops you back to the ground, the ordinary city feels suddenly close again.
