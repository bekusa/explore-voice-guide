The guide

The story

The air up here is thin and cool, even in summer. Wind moves through scrub oak and rough grass. Somewhere below, a goat bell rings once, then falls silent. You are standing high in the mountains of the western Peloponnese, on a rocky shoulder above the deep valleys of Arcadia and Messenia. Look around, and you will see almost no sign of a town. Yet in front of you stands one of the best-preserved temples of the ancient Greek world.

This is Bassae, and the temple belongs to Apollo Epikourios. That second name matters. Epikourios means 'the helper' or 'the one who comes to aid'. The people who built it lived in Phigalia, a small hill city some distance to the southwest. The site sits at roughly 1,100 metres above sea level. Ancient worshippers had to climb hard to reach it, and so did you. That effort is part of the story of this place.

Why did the Phigalians thank Apollo as a helper? Our main ancient source is Pausanias, a Greek traveller who wrote a long guide to Greece in the second century of our era. He says the temple honoured Apollo for saving the city from a plague. He links it to the time of the Peloponnesian War, the long conflict between Athens and Sparta in the late fifth century before Christ. Modern scholars debate this explanation. Some suggest the 'help' may have been military. Either way, gratitude built this temple.

Pausanias adds another surprise. He names the architect as Iktinos — the same man credited with designing the Parthenon in Athens. Many historians accept this, and some remain cautious. But think about it for a moment. If Pausanias is right, a master of the most famous building in Greece came out here, to a remote mountain ridge, to design a temple for a small rural community. The temple dates to the second half of the fifth century before Christ, the high point of classical Greek architecture.

Now look at the stone. Most of the temple is built from local grey limestone, cut from the mountains around you. It is not the gleaming white marble of Athens. The grey stone matches the rocky slopes, so the building seems to grow out of the hillside. Marble was used only for a few finer parts, such as some sculpture and roof details. Notice how the weathered columns carry streaks of lichen and centuries of rain.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Most Greek temples face east, toward the rising sun. This one runs north to south. Scholars still discuss why. The narrow ridge may have forced the builders to follow the land. An older shrine may have stood on the same line. To keep a link with the east, the architect placed a doorway in the eastern side wall of the inner chamber. Morning light could fall through it onto the cult image.

The outside of the temple is Doric. That is the plainest of the Greek architectural orders, with sturdy fluted columns and simple, cushion-like tops. Count the columns if you can see them clearly: six across each short end and fifteen along each long side. That is a long, slender plan compared with many temples of its time. The proportions feel slightly old-fashioned, and that may be deliberate, a nod to tradition in a conservative mountain region.

Inside, the story changes completely. The inner chamber, called the cella, used the more elegant Ionic order, with columns topped by curling scroll shapes. These columns were not free-standing. They were joined to the walls by short spurs of stone, creating a series of small bays. And between two of them stood a single column with a capital in a new style, decorated with carved leaves. It is the earliest known Corinthian capital in Greek architecture.

Think about what that means. The Corinthian order, with its acanthus leaves, later spread across the Roman world. You can see it today on banks, parliaments and museums in cities everywhere. One of its first known appearances was here, on a lonely mountain in Arcadia. The original capital no longer survives. It was found broken in the early nineteenth century and later lost. We know its form mainly through drawings made at the time of discovery.

Those early nineteenth-century visitors also changed the temple forever. In 1811 and 1812, a group of European travellers, including the English architect Charles Robert Cockerell, explored the site. They uncovered a carved marble frieze that once ran around the inside of the cella. It showed Greeks fighting Amazons, the warrior women of myth, and Lapiths fighting Centaurs, half-human and half-horse. The panels were removed and sold. Since the 1810s, they have been displayed in the British Museum in London.

In 1986, Bassae became the first site in Greece added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. Its isolation helped save it. No later town grew on top of it, and few people took its stones for new buildings. But the mountain climate is harsh. Frost, wind, rain and earthquakes have stressed the blocks for over two thousand years. For decades, conservation teams have protected the temple under a large tent, while they study, repair and stabilise its structure piece by piece.

The tent surprises many first-time visitors. They expect an open temple against the sky and find a white canopy instead. It is worth seeing it differently. Under that cover, you can often see steel supports, numbered blocks and careful repair work. This is a working conservation site. The shade also lets your eyes adjust to the grey stone, and you can study the column surfaces and joints up close without harsh midday glare.

Before you leave, step away from the temple and look outward. The mountains fold into one another in long blue ridges. On a clear day, the view can stretch far across the Peloponnese. This is what ancient pilgrims saw after their climb. Listen again to the wind, and perhaps that goat bell. Then turn back for one last look at the grey columns — a small city's thank-you to its god, still standing on the mountain where it was built.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer; mountain air stays cool year-round.
Plan a car journey; public transport here is very limited.
