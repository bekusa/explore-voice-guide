The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the dry grass and the olive trees. Somewhere below, a goat bell knocks softly. Under your feet the ground is uneven, a mix of red earth, loose stones, and the worn edges of blocks that were cut by hand more than two thousand years ago. The air smells of thyme and warm dust. This is Xanthos, once the leading city of ancient Lycia, and today one of the quietest places in this corner of Türkiye.

You have come inland from the coast at Kaş, through the farmland of the river valley, past greenhouses and small villages. The modern village beside the ruins is called Kınık. The river below the hill is the one the ancient city took its name from. Today locals call it the Eşen. In antiquity it was the Xanthos, and its waters still run through the plain toward the sea, just as they did when this hill was crowded with houses, temples, and tombs.

So who were the Lycians? They were a people of this rugged southwestern coast, with their own language and their own script. Greek writers knew them well, and Homer places Lycian heroes in the Trojan War, fighting for Troy. The Lycians formed a league of cities that cooperated in politics and defence. Xanthos was among the most important of them. Ancient writers describe it as a proud city, and its history shows exactly how proud it could be.

Here is the story most visitors never hear. The Greek historian Herodotus wrote that in the sixth century before Christ, a Persian army under the general Harpagus marched on this valley. The men of Xanthos went out to fight a force far larger than their own. According to Herodotus, before the final battle they gathered their wives, children, slaves, and treasure on the acropolis and set it all on fire. Then they fought until they died. The city was later repopulated, he says, largely by families who had been away at the time.

Remarkably, history came close to repeating itself. In 42 BC, during the Roman civil wars that followed the murder of Julius Caesar, the Roman general Brutus besieged Xanthos to raise money and troops. The Greek writer Plutarch describes the citizens again choosing destruction over surrender, burning their city as the Romans broke in. Two sieges, separated by roughly five centuries, with the same terrible ending. Whatever the exact details, the stories show how Xanthos saw itself... as a place that would not kneel.

Now look around for the tall stone shapes rising above the ruins. These are pillar tombs, one of the most distinctive forms of Lycian burial. A solid stone shaft carries a burial chamber high in the air, lifted far above the ground. Nobody is entirely sure why the Lycians placed their dead so high. One common explanation is that height brought the dead closer to the sky and the gods. Another suggests it simply honoured powerful families in the most visible way possible.

The most famous of these stands near the theatre. It is known as the Harpy Tomb. At the top, the marble panels show winged female figures carrying small human forms, which early visitors took to be the Harpies of Greek myth. Many scholars now read them as sirens or spirits carrying souls of the dead. Here is the surprise: the reliefs you see on the tomb today are casts. The original panels were removed in the nineteenth century and are now in the British Museum in London.

That removal is part of the modern story of Xanthos. In the late 1830s and 1840s, the British traveller Charles Fellows explored this region and organised the shipment of many sculptures to England. The most famous piece taken was the Nereid Monument, a small temple-like tomb decorated with sea nymphs in flowing robes. It was rebuilt inside the British Museum, where it still stands. Here on the hill, only its foundations remain, so you are looking at the empty place where it once rose.

Walk toward the agora, the old public square, and find a tall stone pillar covered in tiny carved letters. This is the Inscribed Pillar, and it carries the longest known text in the Lycian language. Part of it is written in a second, related dialect that scholars call Lycian B, and part is in Greek verse. It records the deeds of a local ruler. For specialists, this single stone is one of the main keys to understanding a language that disappeared long ago.

Next to the pillar tombs sits the Roman theatre, cut into the slope of the hill. Its curved rows of seats face the open sky. Sit down on the stone for a moment. Notice how one of the old pillar tombs stands right at the edge of the seating, so close it almost seems to be part of the audience. The Roman builders worked around these older monuments rather than clearing them away, which tells you how much respect the tombs still commanded centuries later.

Xanthos did not end with the Romans. In the early Christian period a large basilica was built here, and parts of its floor still show mosaic work in geometric patterns. Walls from the Byzantine era appear across the site, sometimes built from older carved blocks reused as simple building stone. As you walk, look closely at the walls. You may see a fragment of decoration or an inscription set sideways into later masonry, a small trace of the city recycling its own past.

Xanthos is not alone in this valley. Just a short drive away lies the Letoon, the sacred sanctuary of the Lycian League, dedicated to the goddess Leto and her children Apollo and Artemis. Together the two sites were listed by UNESCO as a single World Heritage Site in 1988. If you have time, the pairing makes sense. Xanthos shows you the political and civic heart of Lycia, while the Letoon shows you where its people went to worship.

Before you leave, climb to one of the higher points of the site and look down at the river plain. Follow the line of the water across the fields toward the sea. Notice the stone tombs standing among the bushes, some whole, some broken, some carved into the cliff faces nearby. The wind is still moving through the grass. The goat bells are still somewhere below. And this hill, which burned twice rather than surrender, is quiet now, holding its stones in the afternoon light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strong midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose stones and uneven paths.
Bring water and a hat; shade is limited.
Combine your visit with the nearby Letoon sanctuary.
Climb the acropolis slope for views over the river plain.
