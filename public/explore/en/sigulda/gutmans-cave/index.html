The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water is running. It is a small, steady sound, the voice of a spring that slips out of the rock at your feet. The air here is cooler than the forest path behind you, and it smells of damp stone and wet leaves. Look up, and the cave opens above you like a wide, red mouth. The rock is warm in colour, somewhere between rust and terracotta, even on a grey day.

This is Gutman's Cave, in the Gauja valley near Sigulda in Latvia. It is often described as the largest cave in the Baltic states. That sounds grand, so it helps to be honest about scale. This is not a deep, winding cavern full of tunnels. It is a broad, open grotto, tall enough to walk into without bending. You can see the back wall from the entrance. Its fame comes from its story, not its size.

The rock around you is sandstone. It was laid down as sand during the Devonian period, an age hundreds of millions of years ago, long before dinosaurs. At that time this region lay under shallow water. Over time the sand pressed into stone. Then rivers and springs began to wear it away. The Gauja river cut this whole valley, and the spring you can hear slowly hollowed out the cave itself. The work is still going on.

Touch the wall gently, if you can reach it, and you will feel how soft this stone is. It crumbles a little under pressure. That softness explains almost everything about this place. It explains why water could carve such a large space. It also explains why, for centuries, people found it so easy to leave their mark here. And they did leave their mark... everywhere you look.

Look closely at the walls. They are covered in carved names, initials, dates and symbols. Some of these carvings are very old. The earliest are usually dated to the seventeenth century, and you may spot coats of arms cut by visitors from wealthier families. Layer upon layer, the inscriptions cross over each other. What began as graffiti is now treated as a historical record. Today carving is forbidden, and the old marks are protected.

Now, the name. Where does Gutman come from? Here we move from geology into folklore. According to a popular local tale, a healer once lived near this spring. People called him a good man, and the name Gutman is often linked to that idea. Legend has it that he used the cold spring water to cure the sick. No one can point to records that prove he existed. But the belief in the water stayed.

For generations, people have come here to drink from the spring or wash their faces in it. Locals say the water brings health, and some say it brings beauty or luck. You will often see visitors cupping their hands under the flow. Whether you believe in its powers or not, the ritual itself is real. It connects you to a long line of travellers who stood exactly where you stand, with wet hands and cold fingers.

The cave is best known, though, for a far sadder story. It is the tale of the Rose of Turaida. The story says that in the early seventeenth century, after a battle near Turaida Castle, a baby girl was found among the dead. She was named Maija and raised nearby. She grew up to be so beautiful that people called her the Rose of Turaida. She fell in love with a young gardener called Viktors.

According to the legend, a soldier wanted Maija for himself. He lured her to this cave by trickery. Maija, wanting to keep her honour, offered him a silk scarf. She told him it had magic power and would protect anyone who wore it from blades. She tied it around her own neck and asked him to strike. He did. The scarf had no magic. Maija died here, choosing death over dishonour.

It is a story of loyalty and love, and it has been retold in poems, plays and songs across Latvia. Some versions say it was based on old court documents. Even so, it is best to hear it as a legend shaped by many tellers. What is certain is how deeply it matters here. Just up the valley, near Turaida Castle, there is a grave marked for the Rose of Turaida. Couples still leave flowers there.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Not far from this cave, there is a smaller, neater opening in the cliff. It is known as Viktor's Cave. Local tradition says the gardener Viktors dug it out for Maija, so the two could meet there in private. Its shape looks more deliberate than the wide natural arch you are standing in. If you have time, it is a short walk, and it completes the story.

Step back now, and take in the wider scene. This cave sits inside Gauja National Park, a protected landscape of pine forest, river bends and red sandstone cliffs. The river is close, though trees may hide it. Across the valley lies the town of Sigulda, and in the other direction stands the brick tower of Turaida Castle. In autumn the slopes turn gold and orange, and the red rock glows against the leaves.

Before you leave, stand quietly for a moment at the mouth of the cave. Listen again to the spring. It was running before anyone carved a name here, and it will keep running after we are gone. Look one last time at the crowded walls, at all those strangers who wanted to be remembered. Then walk out into the forest light, and notice how the cool air of the cave slowly gives way to the smell of pine.

Practical tips
