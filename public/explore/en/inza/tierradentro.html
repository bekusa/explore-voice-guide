The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, water drips onto stone. Above you, the wind moves through tall grass on a green ridge, and a rooster calls from a farm across the valley. The air here is cool and damp, and it smells of wet earth and woodsmoke. You are standing in the mountains of Cauca, in southwestern Colombia. Beneath these quiet hills lies one of the strangest places in the Americas: a network of painted tombs, cut by hand into the rock, and reached by stairs that spiral down into the dark.

This is Tierradentro National Archaeological Park, near the town of Inzá. The name is Spanish, and it means something like "the land within" or "the inner land." Spanish colonists gave it that name because the region was so hard to reach. Deep river valleys and steep slopes of the Central Cordillera, one of the three Andean ranges that run through Colombia, closed it off from the outside world. Even today, the roads that wind up here take patience. That isolation is part of the reason these tombs survived.

The tombs are called hypogea. The word comes from Greek and simply means "underground chambers." Archaeologists generally date most of them to roughly the sixth to the tenth centuries of our era, long before the Spanish arrived. The people who made them had no metal picks of iron or steel. They carved down into soft volcanic rock, a stone called tuff, which forms when volcanic ash settles and hardens. It is soft enough to cut, yet firm enough to hold a ceiling. That balance made this whole landscape possible.

Here is the first surprise. These tombs were not simple pits. Many of them follow a careful plan. A steep staircase, often turning in a spiral, leads down several metres into the rock. At the bottom, the stairs open into a main chamber. Some chambers have side niches around the walls. Others have central columns, carved from the living rock and left standing to support the roof. The builders were not just digging holes. They were shaping rooms, with the same care someone might give to a house.

And then there is the paint. In many of the larger tombs, the walls and columns carry designs in red, black, and white. You will see diamonds, zigzags, lines, and rows of geometric shapes. In some tombs, you may also see faces or human figures, carved in low relief and then painted. The colours came from natural minerals and plant materials. After more than a thousand years underground, much of the paint has faded or flaked away. But in the right light, the patterns still hold their shape, and the effect is quietly overwhelming.

Why build such elaborate rooms for the dead? Archaeologists believe these were secondary burials. That means a body was first buried or left elsewhere, and later the bones were gathered and placed in ceramic urns inside the tomb. The painted chambers may have served whole families or lineages over long periods. Some researchers suggest the designs inside echo the patterns of houses of the living, so the tomb became a home for ancestors. That idea is an interpretation, not a certainty, but standing below ground, it is easy to feel.

The park is not one single site. It is a group of hilltop clusters, each with its own set of tombs, connected by walking trails across the ridges. The best-known groups include Alto de Segovia, Alto del Duende, Alto de San Andrés, and El Aguacate. Each sits on a high point with wide views over the valleys. That choice was likely deliberate. The dead were placed up on the ridges, close to the sky, looking out over the land their families farmed.

The second surprise is above ground. At a site called El Tablón, you will find stone statues, carved figures with large heads and stylised features. They look strongly related to the famous statues of San Agustín, another archaeological park further south in Colombia. The two places share some artistic ideas, and archaeologists still study how the cultures of this region connected with one another. Tierradentro and San Agustín were recognised together by UNESCO in 1995, though they are listed as separate World Heritage Sites.

The people who built the tombs are not the same as the people who live here today. Much about the builders remains unknown, including what they called themselves. The region is now home to the Nasa people, sometimes called the Páez, an Indigenous community with their own language, traditions, and long history of resisting outside control. As you walk, you may pass Nasa farms, small plots of coffee and maize, and families going about their day. This is a living territory, not an empty museum.

Near the park entrance, there are two small museums. One focuses on archaeology, with ceramic urns, tools, and objects found in the area. The other focuses on ethnography, which means the study of living cultures, and it introduces Nasa life, crafts, and beliefs. Visiting both gives you useful context before you walk the trails. The urns, in particular, help you picture what once rested inside the tombs you are about to enter.

A short walk away is the village of San Andrés de Pisimbalá. For many years, its small whitewashed church with a thatched roof was one of the most photographed buildings in the region. That church was lost to fire in the 2010s, and its story became part of local memory. The village itself remains a calm place, with simple guesthouses, small shops, and people who know these trails well. Many visitors stay here overnight, because the full circuit is too much for a rushed day.

Be ready for the walk. The trails climb and drop steeply between the hilltop groups, and the ground can turn to slick mud after rain. The tomb stairs are narrow and high, and you will often descend backwards or with one hand on the rock. Some tombs have lighting, while others are dim. A small torch helps you see the paint. Move slowly, and do not touch the walls. The oils from human hands damage pigment that has survived for centuries.

Now, wherever you stand, take a moment. Look at the ridge line around you, the patchwork of small fields, the clouds moving low over the hills. Somewhere close, under grass that looks like any other hillside, a painted room waits in the dark. As you walk on, notice how ordinary the surface seems. That is the quiet lesson of Tierradentro. The most remarkable things here were never meant to be seen from above. They were made for those below, and for those who would one day come looking.

Look closer

What to look for

Spiral staircases cut directly into the bedrock.
Red, black, and white diamond patterns on walls.
Carved central columns supporting tomb ceilings.
Low relief faces painted inside larger chambers.
Large-headed stone statues at El Tablón.
Before you go

Practical tips

Wear boots with good grip for steep, muddy trails.
Bring a small torch to see faded wall paint.
Visit both museums before walking the tomb circuit.
Stay overnight in San Andrés to see every site.
Carry rain gear, since mountain weather changes quickly.
