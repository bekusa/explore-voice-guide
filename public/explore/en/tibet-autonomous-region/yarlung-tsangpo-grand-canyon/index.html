The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, somewhere in the green haze, a river is moving with a low, steady roar. The air is damp and cool. It smells of wet moss, pine resin, and stone. Clouds slide along the slopes at eye level, then tear open for a moment. In that gap you may see a white summit far above the forest. Its snow sits close to a tropical valley floor. Welcome to the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon.

The river at the bottom has many names. In Tibet it is the Yarlung Tsangpo. It rises in the far west of the Tibetan Plateau, near the sacred region of Mount Kailash. Then it flows east for a very long way across high, dry country. Further downstream, in India, it becomes the Siang. After that it becomes the Brahmaputra. Finally it joins the Ganges system and reaches the Bay of Bengal. You are standing beside one of Asia's great rivers.

Here is the first surprise. For most of its journey across Tibet, the river runs wide and slow. It braids across gravel flats at very high altitude. Then, in this corner of southeastern Tibet, it meets a wall of mountains. It does not go around them. Instead, it turns sharply, loops around a giant peak, and cuts straight down through the Himalaya. Geographers call this the Great Bend. The canyon is the trench that the water has carved.

The peak inside that bend is Namcha Barwa. It rises to about 7,782 metres. On the opposite side of the river stands Gyala Peri, another summit above seven thousand metres. The two mountains face each other across the gorge. Between them, the river drops into a channel far below. Because the peaks are so high and the river is so low, the vertical distance is enormous. Chinese surveys in the 1990s described this canyon as the deepest on Earth, and many sources still repeat that claim.

Depth like this creates a second surprise. In a single view, you can see several climate zones stacked on top of each other. Near the river, the air is warm and humid. Monsoon moisture flows up the valley from the Indian lowlands. That moisture feeds broadleaf forest, bamboo, ferns, and orchids. Higher up, the forest changes to oak, then to fir and rhododendron. Above that come alpine meadows, bare rock, and finally ice. It is like travelling from the tropics to the Arctic without moving sideways.

For Tibetan Buddhists, this region has a special meaning. Much of the lower canyon lies in an area called Pemako. Tibetan tradition describes Pemako as a beyul, which means a hidden land. These are sacred valleys that, according to religious texts, were concealed for times of need. Pilgrims believe the guru Padmasambhava, a revered teacher who helped bring Buddhism to Tibet, blessed these lands. Some pilgrim routes here are walked as acts of devotion, through rain, leeches, and steep mud.

The canyon also has a famous mystery. For a long time, Western geographers did not know how the river got from Tibet to India. The Tsangpo sits very high. The Brahmaputra plain sits very low. So where did all that height disappear? Some people imagined an enormous waterfall hidden in the gorge. In the nineteenth century, British survey teams trained local explorers, known as pundits, to travel secretly into Tibet. They mapped what they could and reported back.

One of those explorers was a man named Kintup. According to the story usually told about him, he was asked to throw marked logs into the river. Surveyors downstream in India would then watch for them. The plan failed because his messages did not arrive in time, and years passed before he could report. His accounts were doubted for a long time. Later explorers confirmed that much of what he described was accurate. His story is still told with respect.

In 1924, the British plant collector Frank Kingdon-Ward travelled deep into the gorge. He was searching for plants, and also for the legendary falls. He found and described several waterfalls, but not the giant one people had imagined. He also collected many plants, including rhododendrons and the blue poppy that later became popular in European gardens. If you have seen a blue poppy in a garden, its ancestors may have come from these slopes.

The mystery was finally answered with a simple fact. The river does not lose its height in one great fall. Instead, it loses it gradually, through a long chain of rapids and smaller falls along the gorge. In the late 1990s, expeditions reached the remote inner section and documented a waterfall in the deepest part of the canyon. The legend of a single great cascade was not true. The truth was a river falling steadily, for kilometre after kilometre.

People live here too. The lower valleys are home to Monpa and Lhoba communities, as well as Tibetans. For generations, they travelled on foot trails, rope crossings, and suspension bridges. Medog County, in the heart of this region, was famous as one of the last counties in China to receive a road connection. Today, new roads and tunnels reach places that once took days to walk to. Farming villages, tea gardens, and small monasteries sit on the terraced slopes.

Change continues here. In recent years, China has begun a very large hydropower project on the lower river. Supporters see clean energy. Critics and neighbouring countries raise concerns about ecology, earthquakes, and the water flowing downstream into India and Bangladesh. This is a seismically active area, where the Indian and Eurasian plates press together. That pressure is one reason these mountains stand so high. The canyon is still being shaped, both by the earth and by people.

Now look again at the view in front of you. Notice how fast the clouds move across the slopes. Notice the difference in colour between the dark forest and the pale snow above it. Listen for the river, even when you cannot see it. Before you leave, take one slow breath of this damp mountain air. As you walk away, watch how the summit appears and disappears behind the clouds. The mountain is still there, even when the clouds cover it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange required Tibet travel permits well before your trip.
Allow several days for acclimatisation before driving this far.
Bring rain layers, because monsoon cloud often fills the valley.
Visit autumn mornings for the best chance of clear summit views.
Travel with a licensed local guide, as foreigners must here.
