The guide

The story

Listen to your footsteps on the old paving. The stone is uneven here, polished in some places and rough in others. Above you, yellow glazed tiles catch the light, and many of them are edged with a band of green. That green trim is the first clue that this palace is not simply a smaller copy of Beijing's Forbidden City. It belongs to a different story... the story of the people who came before the Forbidden City was theirs.

You are standing in the Mukden Palace, also known as the Shenyang Imperial Palace. Mukden is the Manchu name for this city. In the early seventeenth century, this was the seat of a rising power in the northeast. Its founders were not yet emperors of China. They were leaders of the Jurchen people, a group of tribes from the forests and river valleys of Manchuria. From this courtyard, they planned the conquest of an empire.

The first builder was Nurhaci, the Jurchen leader who united the tribes and organised them into military units called banners. He moved his capital to this city in the 1620s, and construction began soon after. Nurhaci died before the palace was finished. His son, Hong Taiji, completed and expanded it. In 1636, Hong Taiji declared a new dynasty here. He named it the Qing, and he renamed his people the Manchu.

Think about that for a moment. The dynasty that would rule China until the early twentieth century, the last imperial dynasty, was proclaimed not in Beijing but in these halls. Only in 1644 did the Qing court move south and take the Forbidden City. After that, this palace became a secondary residence. It was a place of memory, an ancestral home far from the capital.

Now walk toward the eastern part of the complex. Here you will find the oldest and most unusual layout. A single hall stands at the head of a long open court. It has eight sides, and its roof rises in layered eaves. This is the Dazheng Hall, often translated as the Hall of Great Administration. Nurhaci used it for the most important state ceremonies.

Look down the court toward the entrance. Two rows of smaller pavilions line the sides, facing each other like officers at attention. These are the Pavilions of the Ten Princes. Two of them belonged to the leaders of the left and right wings. The other eight belonged to the eight banners. The banner leaders sat here and governed beside their ruler. Here is the surprise: the whole arrangement looks like a military camp, with the commander's tent at the top and his chiefs in rows below.

That is the heart of this place. The early Manchu state was a shared enterprise of clan leaders and military chiefs. The architecture shows this clearly. The Forbidden City puts one emperor at the centre of a vast, closed world. Here, power is spread across a row of pavilions, each one a separate seat. You can see a nomadic and military tradition built in brick and timber.

Next, move to the central section. This part belongs mainly to Hong Taiji. The main hall here is the Chongzheng Hall, where he received officials and held court. The design is closer to Chinese palace tradition, with a throne hall at the front and residential buildings behind. But look closely at the details. Carved dragons and painted beams sit beside motifs and colours that came from Manchu and Tibetan Buddhist taste.

Now comes a second surprise. In most Chinese palaces, the throne hall is the highest building, and the living quarters sit lower. Here, it is the other way around. Behind the court hall, the residential palaces stand on a raised platform. The Phoenix Tower rises at its entrance. Historians often link this to Manchu tradition, in which homes were built on high ground. From the platform, the household looked down on the hall of state.

Climb up and visit the Qingning Palace, the Palace of Pure Tranquillity. This was Hong Taiji's own residence. Notice that the main door is not in the centre of the façade. It sits off to one side, in the style of a traditional northeastern Manchu house. Inside, there are kang, raised beds made of brick and warmed by fires underneath. In the long winters of this region, that heat was not a luxury. It was survival.

In the courtyard in front of this palace, look for a tall wooden pole standing on a stone base. This is a spirit pole. It was used in Manchu shamanic rituals, and offerings for crows or magpies were placed at the top. Manchu tradition honoured these birds, and one popular tale says a crow once saved the life of the clan's ancestor. The Qing court kept these rituals for generations, even after it adopted Confucian ceremonies in Beijing.

Now walk to the western section. This part came later, in the eighteenth century, under the Qianlong Emperor. He returned to the northeast on journeys to honour the ancestral tombs, and he expanded the palace. The best-known building here is the Wensu Pavilion. It was built to hold one of the few manuscript copies of a vast imperial library collection, the Siku Quanshu, or Complete Library of the Four Treasuries. Look at its dark roof tiles and cool green and blue paintwork, colours chosen to symbolise water and to protect books from fire.

In 2004, UNESCO added this palace to the World Heritage listing for the Imperial Palaces of the Ming and Qing Dynasties, together with the Forbidden City in Beijing. The two belong together. One shows the Qing at the height of their power. This one shows them at the beginning, still a frontier people deciding what kind of rulers they wanted to be.

Before you leave, stand still in one of the courtyards. Notice how compact the palace feels, how quickly one gate opens into the next. Listen to the city around these walls, the traffic and the voices of modern Shenyang. Then look up once more at the yellow tiles with their green edges. The dynasty that started here ruled China for more than two and a half centuries... and it began in these modest halls.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the eastern, central and western sections in historical order.
Arrive early to see the ten pavilions without crowds.
Wear comfortable shoes for the uneven old stone paving.
Climb the raised platform to compare it with the throne hall.
Visit in autumn for crisp light on the yellow tiles.
