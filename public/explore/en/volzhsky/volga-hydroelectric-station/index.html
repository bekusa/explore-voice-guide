The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the hum of traffic there is a deeper sound, low and steady, almost felt more than heard. It is the sound of water moving with enormous weight. Here the Volga, the longest river in Europe, meets a long wall of concrete and steel. The air near the dam often feels cooler and damp. On a windy day you may taste a little river spray. This is the Volga Hydroelectric Station, and it joins two cities across a great river.

On one bank stands Volgograd, the city once called Stalingrad. On the other bank stands Volzhsky, where you are now. The dam is not only a power plant. It is also a crossing. A road and a railway run along its crest, so people and freight pass between the two cities every day. For many locals, crossing the dam is simply part of the commute. For a visitor, it is a rare chance to see heavy industry this close.

The idea of taming the lower Volga is old. But the real work began in the early 1950s, in the years after the Second World War. Stalingrad had been almost destroyed in the great battle of that war. The Soviet state wanted to show recovery on a huge scale. A giant power station on the Volga was part of that plan. It would send electricity to the region and to Moscow, and it would help irrigate the dry steppe to the east.

The station was first named after Stalingrad. Later, after the city itself was renamed, the plant received a new official name. It was dedicated to the twenty-second congress of the Soviet Communist Party, a political meeting held in 1961. That long, formal name still appears in some documents. Most people simply call it the Volga station, or the Volzhskaya station in Russian. The change of names follows the political history of the whole country.

Now here is something many visitors do not realise. The city around you exists because of this dam. Volzhsky began as a settlement for the builders. Workers arrived from across the Soviet Union, and they needed homes, schools, shops and hospitals. Over the following decades the settlement grew into a full industrial city. When you walk its wide, planned streets, you are walking through the dam's own story. The power plant came first. The city grew around it.

The workforce had a darker side too. Historians note that forced labour from the Soviet prison camp system, known as the Gulag, was used in the early years of construction. Prisoners worked alongside free workers on many great projects of that era. This part of the story is rarely shown on official plaques. It is worth holding in mind as you look at the sheer amount of concrete in front of you. Much of it was poured by hand-driven effort in hard conditions.

The first turbines started turning in the late 1950s. By the early 1960s the station was working at full design power. For a time it was described as the largest hydroelectric station in Europe. Look along the length of the building and you can sense that ambition. The machine hall stretches far along the river. Inside it, a long row of turbines and generators turns the falling river into electricity, day and night, in every season.

Behind the dam, the river changes character. Upstream lies the Volgograd Reservoir, a wide, slow body of water that stretches far to the north. It is so broad that locals often call it a sea. Downstream, the Volga runs faster and narrower again. When the spillway gates open, usually during the spring flood, white water crashes and foams below the dam. That is the moment when the loud roar replaces the quiet hum.

The dam also changed life for the fish of the Volga. Sturgeon, the ancient fish that produce caviar, swim upriver every year to lay their eggs. The dam blocked their old route. To help them, engineers built a fish lift, a kind of elevator that carries fish over the barrier. Scientists still debate how well such systems work. Either way, it is a surprising detail: a giant power station with a lift built for fish.

Just downstream, another river begins its journey. The Akhtuba is a branch of the Volga that splits away near here and runs parallel to the main river for hundreds of kilometres. Between the two rivers lies a green floodplain of meadows, lakes and willow groves. This land depends on the spring water released from the dam. So the station does not only make power. It also shapes the rhythm of floods and farms in the valley below.

Stand still for a moment and look at the details. The concrete surfaces show decades of weather, with dark streaks where water has run for years. Heavy cranes rise above the spillway gates. Power lines leave the station and march away across the steppe on tall steel towers. Gulls often circle over the tailwater, looking for stunned fish. Fishermen sometimes gather on the banks below for the same reason. Nature and machinery share this place closely.

This is still working infrastructure, not a museum. Today the station belongs to a large Russian hydropower company and continues to feed the national grid. Security here is real. Some areas are closed, and photography of certain parts may be restricted, so watch for signs and listen to any guards. The best views often come from the riverbanks or from the approach roads on either side, where you can see the full length of the dam at once.

As you leave, look back once more. See the long grey line across the river, the cars moving slowly along its top, the water sliding through the gates below. Listen again for that low hum under everything. It has been there for more than sixty years. The people of Volzhsky hardly hear it anymore. For you, today, it is the sound of a river at work, and of a city that grew up beside it.

Before you go

Practical tips

View the full dam from the riverbanks on either side.
Visit in spring to see the spillway gates release floodwater.
Check signs before photographing; some areas are restricted.
Bring a windproof layer; the river crossing gets breezy.
Explore the Akhtuba floodplain downstream for green scenery.
