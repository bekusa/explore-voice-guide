The guide

The story

Wind moves across open wheat fields here, and it carries the dry smell of earth and warm stone. The land rolls away in long, pale folds toward low hills. The roar of Tunis is far behind you now, a long drive to the southwest. What you hear instead is birdsong, a distant engine, perhaps a dog barking from a farmstead. You are standing in the high grain country of northwest Tunisia, where one of the most famous names of the ancient world still lives on.

That name is Zama. For most people it means one thing: the battle in 202 BCE, when the Roman general Scipio defeated Hannibal of Carthage and ended the Second Punic War. Here is the first surprise, though. Zama Regia was a town long before it was a battlefield name. It was a living place, with houses, markets and walls. The battle took its name from the region, and its exact location is still debated by historians today.

The word Regia means royal. This was a town of the Numidian kings, the Berber rulers who controlled much of the interior of North Africa while Carthage held the coast. The Numidians were famous horsemen. Their cavalry fought on both sides of the Punic Wars, depending on which prince was allied with whom. A royal town in this fertile country made good sense. Grain fed armies, and whoever held these fields held real power.

So let us start with that battle, because it is the reason most visitors come. By 202 BCE, Hannibal had spent years in Italy after his famous crossing of the Alps with elephants. Rome finally carried the war to Africa, and Carthage called him home. Somewhere in this wider region, the two armies met. Hannibal brought war elephants. Scipio, according to the ancient historians, opened lanes in his lines so the charging animals passed through harmlessly.

The Numidian cavalry of King Masinissa, now fighting for Rome, helped decide the day. Carthage lost, and its days as a great Mediterranean power were effectively over. But remember the second surprise. The ancient writers who describe the battle do not agree on where it happened. Some scholars place it at some distance from the town itself. So as you look across these fields, you are looking at the landscape of the battle, not necessarily its exact ground.

The town of Zama has a second, quieter story in Roman history, and it is about loyalty. In the late second century BCE, Rome fought the Numidian king Jugurtha. The Roman historian Sallust wrote that a Roman army under Metellus besieged Zama and could not take it. The defenders held their walls. It was a rare moment when a Numidian town turned back a Roman army, and Sallust described the fighting with real admiration.

A few generations later, the story turned the other way. During Julius Caesar's civil war, the Numidian king Juba the First sided with Caesar's enemies. After their defeat at Thapsus in 46 BCE, Juba fled to his royal town. According to the ancient account, the people of Zama closed their gates against him. Their own king was locked out. Soon afterwards, Juba died together with his Roman ally Petreius. The royal town had chosen its own survival.

After that, Numidia was absorbed into Roman Africa, and Zama became a Roman town. This region turned into one of the great grain baskets of the empire. Ships carried African wheat to feed the city of Rome itself. The fields around you are part of that same story. Farmers have grown cereals on this land for more than two thousand years, and the rhythm of planting and harvest has hardly changed its shape.

For a long time, scholars argued about where Zama Regia actually stood. Several sites in this part of Tunisia were proposed. Today, many researchers identify the town with the ruins near the village of Jama, whose modern name still carries an echo of the ancient one. Excavation work here has continued in recent decades. That means this is still an active place of discovery, not a finished museum display.

As you walk, look closely at the stones underfoot and around you. Much of what survives is low: foundations, fallen blocks, fragments of walls and the outlines of buildings. Pale limestone has weathered to soft grey and honey tones. In places you may see cut blocks reused in later walls, a common habit in North Africa. Builders took good stone wherever they found it, so one wall can hold pieces from very different centuries.

Water was always the great question in this country. Keep an eye out for underground cisterns and channels, the quiet engineering that let a town survive long dry summers. Look too at the height of the ground. The town sits on raised land, with wide views in several directions. For a royal town in a land of horsemen, the ability to see riders coming from far away was not a luxury. It was security.

This is not a polished site with crowds and souvenir stalls. That is part of its value. You may share the ruins with a shepherd and his flock, or with nobody at all. Wildflowers push up between the stones in spring. In summer the light turns hard and white, and the stone throws back the heat. Bring water, wear solid shoes, and give yourself time to simply stand and look around.

Before you leave, turn slowly in a full circle. Notice the wheat, the olive trees, the line of hills, the small farms. Two kings, a Roman siege and one of history's decisive wars all belong to this view. Yet the land itself has stayed patient and practical, growing grain season after season. As you walk back toward the road, listen to the wind in the fields. It is the same sound the people of Zama heard.
