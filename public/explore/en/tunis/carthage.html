The guide

The story

The Mediterranean light is different here. It glances off pale limestone and catches in the low scrub, turning everything amber and silver at once. You are standing on the rim of what was once the greatest rival Rome ever faced — a city-state that commanded the sea, built an empire across three continents, and came within a breath of rewriting Western history.

Carthage was founded in the ninth century before Christ by Phoenician traders from the city of Tyre, far to the east on the Lebanese coast. They called it Qart-Hadasht, the New City, and built it as a harbour fortress on a peninsula jutting into the Gulf of Tunis. Within two centuries, it controlled shipping lanes from Gibraltar to the Levant, taxed every merchant who sailed the western Mediterranean, and planted colonies across North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, and the Iberian coast. By the third century before Christ, Carthage rivaled Rome in wealth and military reach.

That rivalry ignited the Punic Wars, three brutal conflicts that spanned more than a century. The second war brought Hannibal Barca and his famous crossing of the Alps with war elephants — an audacious march that nearly brought Rome to its knees. For fifteen years, Hannibal's armies roamed Italy, winning battle after battle, but never quite capturing Rome itself. The Romans regrouped, struck back, and in 202 BC defeated Hannibal at Zama, south of here in what is now Tunisia.

The third war ended in total annihilation. In 146 BC, after a three-year siege, Roman forces breached the walls, burned the city for seventeen days, and leveled it. Ancient sources claim the Romans sowed the ground with salt to render it barren, though modern historians doubt that detail — salt was too valuable to waste. What is certain is that Carthage was erased. The survivors were sold into slavery, the harbours filled with rubble, and the city disappeared from the map.

A century later, Julius Caesar proposed rebuilding Carthage as a Roman colony, and his successor Augustus made it happen. By the first century of our era, Roman Carthage had grown to become the second-largest city in the western half of the empire, with a population that reached half a million at its peak. The ruins you see around you now — the baths, the villas, the amphitheatre — are mostly from that Roman phase, not from the Punic city Hannibal knew. The original Carthaginian levels lie deeper, beneath your feet, harder to reach and harder to read.

The Antonine Baths, a short walk from here, are among the largest Roman bath complexes ever built. When they opened in the second century, their vaults soared fifteen metres high, and the frigidarium — the cold plunge pool — could hold hundreds of bathers at once. Today, only the basement levels survive, but even those convey the scale. The massive granite columns scattered across the site were shipped from Egypt, a logistical feat that reminds you just how far Roman supply lines stretched.

Up the hill, the amphitheatre once seated thirty-five thousand spectators. It hosted gladiatorial games, wild beast hunts, and public executions. Much of the stone was quarried away in later centuries to build medieval Tunis, so what remains is a footprint, a ghost of the original structure. Still, stand in the center of the arena and you can feel the curve of the tiers around you, the way the acoustics funnel sound up and out.

The Carthage Museum, housed in a former seminary on Byrsa Hill, holds some of the finest Punic artifacts recovered from the site. Look for the carved stone stelae from the Tophet, the sacred precinct where Carthaginians deposited urns containing cremated remains. For decades, scholars debated whether these were child sacrifices or simply infant burials — recent analysis suggests both practices occurred, but the full picture remains contested. The inscriptions on the stelae are in Punic script, a late descendant of Phoenician writing, and many are dedications to Tanit and Baal Hammon, the chief deities of Carthage.

The Punic Ports, just north of the museum, are the most evocative remnant of the original city. There were two harbours: a rectangular merchant port and a circular military port with a central island where the Carthaginian navy kept its fleet. At its height, the navy had over two hundred warships, sleek triremes that could outmaneuver anything Rome put to sea. The harbours silted up long ago, and the circular basin is now a shallow green pond ringed by eucalyptus trees, but the shape is still clear from above.

Carthage today is a suburb of Tunis, a scatter of villas and gardens draped across the hills where the ancient city stood. The archaeological zones are open to the sky, unfenced, threaded with footpaths and shaded by umbrella pines. Locals walk their dogs here, couples sit on the fallen columns at sunset, and the sea — the same sea Hannibal's ships sailed — glints blue in the distance.

The site is large and spread out. You will need a few hours to cover the main zones on foot, or you can take the TGM light rail that runs along the coast and stops at Carthage Hannibal, Carthage Byrsa, and Carthage Dermech. Each stop serves a different cluster of ruins. Wear comfortable shoes — the ground is uneven, the stone can be slippery, and there is little shade in summer.

As you leave, notice the quiet. For all the history compressed into this hillside, the place feels oddly peaceful, almost pastoral. The city that once terrified Rome, that built an empire on trade and sea power, that nearly changed the course of Western civilization — it is gone. What remains are fragments, outlines, the faint echo of grandeur. And the light, slanting through the pines, turning the ruins gold.
