The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the medina reaches you through sound. Shutters roll up, a vendor calls out, and slippers slap on worn stone. Then the air changes. Somewhere close, a seller of perfumes and oils has opened his jars, and a thick sweetness drifts through the covered lane. You are walking into the heart of old Tunis. At the end of these narrow alleys stands the building the whole medina grew around: the Al-Zaytuna Mosque, often called the Great Mosque of Tunis.

The name means "olive tree." Nobody can say for certain why. Legend has it that an olive tree once grew on this spot. Another popular tale links the site to an older Christian shrine. Both stories are told by locals, and neither can be proven. What is clear is that the name has lasted. For more than a thousand years, people in Tunis have spoken of this mosque simply as Zaytuna. That one word means a building, a school, and a whole way of learning.

The mosque is the oldest in the city. Tradition dates its first version to the early decades of Islamic rule in North Africa, around the turn of the eighth century. That early building was modest. The mosque you see today owes most of its form to the ninth century and the Aghlabids. They were a dynasty that governed this region on behalf of the caliphs in Baghdad. They rebuilt the prayer hall on a grand scale, and later rulers kept adding to it.

Here is the first surprise. Many of the columns inside the prayer hall are older than the mosque itself — much older. They came from the ruins of Roman and Byzantine sites, very likely including ancient Carthage, just a short drive from here. Builders in this period often reused fine stone like this. So when you glimpse those columns, you are looking at marble that stood in Roman streets centuries before Islam reached Tunisia. The capitals on top do not always match... and that mismatch is part of their story.

Now turn to the courtyard. It is wide and open, paved in pale stone, and it holds the light in a way the dark lanes outside do not. Arcades run along its edges. Their arches rest on slender columns, and the shade beneath them stays cool even on hot afternoons. This is the space most visitors will see. The prayer hall beyond is reserved for worshippers. But from the courtyard you can still sense its depth — rows of arches running back into the shadow.

Look up and find the minaret. It is square, tall, and covered in carved stone and patterned panels. It rises above every rooftop in the medina. Many visitors assume it is as old as the prayer hall. It is not. The present tower is a much later addition, from the late nineteenth century. Its builders chose an older North African style on purpose. They wanted it to feel ancient, and from the street below it truly does.

Zaytuna was never only a place of prayer. For centuries it was also a centre of learning — one of the oldest in the Islamic world. Students came here to study the Quran, Islamic law, grammar, and theology. They sat in circles around their teachers, often beside a column. A teacher's column could become known by his name. Over time, Zaytuna grew into a university in all but name, and much later it became one in the modern sense.

This tradition shaped some well-known minds. Ibn Khaldun, the fourteenth-century historian, was born in Tunis. He studied with scholars in this city, at a time when Tunisian learning centred on this mosque. Today many people consider him one of the early thinkers of history and society. Generations of Tunisian judges, scholars, and writers also passed through these lessons. When you stand here, you stand at the source of a long intellectual tradition.

The mosque also explains the shape of the medina around you. The old city grew outward from Zaytuna in rings. Closest to the mosque were the cleanest and most respected trades. Perfume sellers, booksellers, and makers of fine cloth set up nearby. Noisier and messier work, like tanning and metalwork, sat further out. You can still feel this pattern today. The Souk el-Attarine, the perfume market, still lies just beside the mosque walls.

Here is the second surprise. Many lanes in the medina feel narrow and enclosed. But the entrance from the main approach opens at the top of a short flight of steps. From there, you look back down a lane lined with shops and arches. Pause on those steps for a moment. Behind you is the scholarly calm of the courtyard. In front of you is commerce, conversation, and movement. That meeting of worlds has been the life of this place for centuries.

A few words of courtesy. This is an active mosque, not a museum. Five times a day, the call to prayer rises from the minaret and the courtyard fills with worshippers. Non-Muslim visitors are usually welcomed only at certain times, and only into limited areas. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Speak quietly, and follow any guidance from the people at the entrance. Their care for this building is the reason it still stands.

If you can, linger at the edge of the courtyard. Watch how the light shifts across the paving as the day moves on. Notice the smooth patches where feet have walked for generations. Listen to how the sound of the souk fades into a softer hum. Look at the arches and count how many you can see from one spot. Small details like these can stay with you longer than any single date.

When you are ready, step back into the lanes. Breathe in the perfume of the attarine one more time. Look over your shoulder and find the minaret above the rooftops. It will guide you as you wander. In many corners of the medina, you can still catch sight of it. And wherever you go next in old Tunis, remember that these streets began here — around an olive tree, perhaps, and a place where people gathered to pray and learn.
