The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry rustle of teak leaves underfoot, the sharp alarm call of a spotted deer, then a long stillness. The air smells of dust and warm bark. Somewhere in this scrubby forest, a lion may be resting in the shade, watching the track. You are in Gir, in the Saurashtra peninsula of Gujarat, in western India.

This forest holds something no other place on Earth can claim. It is the last wild home of the Asiatic lion. Lions once ranged from the Middle East across Persia and much of northern India. Over the centuries, hunting and the loss of open land pushed them back. By the start of the twentieth century, Gir was the final refuge. Every wild Asiatic lion alive today descends from the small group that survived here.

The story of that survival is a little surprising. Much of the early protection came from the Nawabs of Junagadh, the Muslim rulers of this princely state before Indian independence. At a time when lion numbers had fallen to a very small population, the Nawab's administration restricted hunting in these forests. Estimates from that period vary, and some early counts were rough. But historians agree that the population came perilously close to vanishing.

After independence, the state took up the task. Gir was declared a wildlife sanctuary in 1965, and a central core was later given national park status. The protected area today covers more than 1,400 square kilometres across Junagadh and neighbouring districts. The recovery has been remarkable. The most recent official count, in 2025, put the number of lions at close to nine hundred. So many lions now live here that some have spread far beyond the forest edge, onto farmland and even toward the coast.

Look at the landscape around you. This is not the dense green jungle many visitors expect. Gir is a dry deciduous forest. Teak trees dominate, along with acacia, jamun and flame of the forest. In the hot months, many trees drop their leaves, and the ground turns pale gold and brown. Low rocky hills rise and fall. Seasonal rivers, including the Hiran, cut through the land and leave pools that become precious as the dry season deepens.

If you see a lion, take a moment to study it. The Asiatic lion is a little smaller than its African cousin. The male's mane is usually shorter and sparser, so his ears often stay visible. Look along the belly, too. Many Asiatic lions have a long fold of skin running underneath — one of the features scientists use to tell the two apart. Here, male lions often move in small groups, while females and cubs keep to their own prides.

The lions are only part of the story. Gir also holds a large population of leopards, which are often harder to spot because they prefer cover and move at dusk. Spotted deer, called chital, graze in open patches. You may also see sambar, the large dark deer, and nilgai, the blue bull, which is actually India's largest antelope. Watch for the small four-horned antelope too. It is shy and rarely seen elsewhere.

Now for a detail many visitors miss. People live inside this forest. The Maldharis are traditional cattle herders, and their families have grazed buffalo here for generations. They live in small settlements called nesses, often simple homes with thorny fences around the cattle enclosures. Lions sometimes take their livestock. Yet the relationship is more complex than conflict alone. Many Maldharis speak of the lions with respect, and their long presence is part of why this shared landscape works.

There is a second human story here, and it surprises almost everyone. Near the forest live the Siddi people, a community of African descent. Their ancestors came to western India centuries ago, through trade across the Indian Ocean. Today they are Gujarati speakers, but they keep their own music and a lively dance tradition, called dhamal, with drums and shell ornaments. If you have the chance to see a performance nearby, it is worth your time.

Water shapes life in Gir. Kamleshwar Dam, inside the protected area, created a large reservoir. It is a good place to look for mugger crocodiles, a broad-snouted freshwater species, basking on the banks. Herons, storks and kingfishers work the shallows. Gir is also rich in birdlife beyond the water. Peacocks cross the tracks, and in quiet moments you may hear the crested serpent eagle calling high above the canopy.

Most visitors enter through Sasan Gir, the small village that serves as the main gateway. Safaris run in open vehicles along fixed routes, with a forest guide on board. The number of permits each day is limited, and it is wise to book well ahead. The forest closes to safaris during the monsoon months, when rains turn the tracks to mud and the animals breed. If you miss a permit, the nearby Devalia safari area offers a fenced zone where sightings are more likely.

On safari, patience matters more than luck. Guides read the forest constantly. They listen for alarm calls from deer and langur monkeys, and they look for fresh pugmarks, the paw prints, in the soft dust of the track. Keep your voice low and stay seated. The lions here are used to vehicles, but they are wild animals, and the rules exist to protect both of you.

Before you leave, pause and listen again. Notice the dry leaves, the shifting light through the teak, the distant bell of a herder's buffalo. Somewhere nearby, a lion may still be lying in the shade, unbothered by your passing. Every animal in this forest carries the legacy of a population that almost disappeared. As your vehicle rolls back toward the gate, look at the dust on the track. Sometimes the freshest pawprint is the closest you will come.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book safari permits well in advance through the official forest portal.
Choose an early morning safari for cooler air and active animals.
Plan around the seasonal monsoon closure of the main safari routes.
Try Devalia safari area if Gir permits are sold out.
Bring binoculars to spot leopards and birds deeper in the trees.
