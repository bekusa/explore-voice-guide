The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the smooth, pale paving of the Forum. The stones under your feet are polished by centuries of footsteps, and on a sunny day they throw the light back up at you. Café chairs scrape. Pigeons land and lift off again. In front of you, a small white temple rises on a high base. Its front columns stand tall and slender against the sky. This is the Temple of Augustus, and it has watched over this square for roughly two thousand years.

The first surprise is the size. Many visitors expect something vast, like the arena down the road. Instead, the temple is compact, almost intimate. It has a single inner room, called a cella, set behind a deep porch of columns. That modest scale was normal for a provincial temple. It was not built to overwhelm a crowd. It was built to make a clear statement in the heart of a Roman town — this place belongs to Rome, and to its emperor.

To understand that statement, picture Pula as the Romans knew it. The town became a Roman colony in the first century before Christ, and it carried the name Pietas Iulia. The Forum, where you are standing now, was its civic centre. Here people traded, argued, voted and worshipped. Temples, offices and shops framed the open square. Today the square is smaller and the buildings are newer, but the open space in front of you still follows the outline of that ancient plan.

The temple was dedicated to the goddess Roma and to Augustus, the first Roman emperor. Historians generally date its construction to the years around the turn of the millennium, between about 2 BC and 14 of the common era. That timing matters. Augustus was still alive. So this was not a memorial to a dead ruler. It was part of a careful political cult. Across the provinces, honouring the living emperor alongside the spirit of Rome was a way to show loyalty to the new imperial order.

Now look at the columns. They are Corinthian, the most decorative of the classical styles. You can recognise it by the capitals at the top, which are carved with curling acanthus leaves, a spiky Mediterranean plant. Step closer and follow the leaves with your eyes. Some edges are crisp. Others are softened or broken. Above the columns runs a frieze with a band of carved ornament, a quiet pattern of plant forms that is easy to miss if you only look at the front.

Here is a detail most visitors walk past. On the frieze facing the square, look for a line of small holes. Bronze letters were once fixed into the stone there, spelling out the dedication. The metal disappeared long ago, probably melted down for its value. Scholars have read the inscription from the pattern of fixing holes alone. It is a small act of detective work — reading words that are no longer there.

The temple survived the end of the Roman world partly because people kept finding new uses for it. Over the centuries it served as a church. At other times it stored grain. Practical reuse like this saved many ancient buildings in the Mediterranean. A temple with a roof and solid walls was too valuable to tear down. Later, in the nineteenth century, it became a place to display ancient stone carvings, an early step toward the small museum you may find inside today.

The second surprise is less comfortable. What you see is not entirely the ancient building. During the Second World War, an Allied bombing raid hit Pula, and the temple was badly damaged. After the war, restorers rebuilt it using the surviving fragments, which they put back in place, and new stone where pieces were missing. So the temple is both ancient and modern. It carries Roman carving and twentieth-century repair side by side.

Try to spot the difference. Walk slowly around the base and look at the surfaces. Some blocks have the warm, weathered tone of very old limestone, with pitting and fine cracks. Others look cleaner and more even. The joins are not always obvious, and that is the restorers' skill. But once you start looking, the building becomes a kind of puzzle. It is a useful reminder that many ancient monuments you visit have been pieced back together in a similar way.

Now turn a little to your right, toward the larger building beside the temple. This is the Communal Palace, the town hall, which has housed local government here since the Middle Ages. Look closely at its back wall, facing away from the square. Built into it are the remains of a second Roman temple, a twin to the one you have been studying. Tradition calls it the Temple of Diana, goddess of the hunt, though that name is not certain.

That pairing is worth a moment of thought. Two temples once stood side by side here, framing the northern edge of the Forum. One survived as a freestanding building. The other was absorbed into a working government office and almost vanished from view. The town hall itself shows many layers — medieval arches, later windows, and repairs from different centuries. Together, these two buildings show how the city never stopped using its ancient centre. It simply built on top of it.

If the temple is open when you visit, step inside. The interior is a single room, and it usually holds a small collection of Roman stone pieces and sculpture. It is quiet compared with the square outside. The thick walls hold the cool air, even on hot summer days. Pay attention to the change in sound as you cross the threshold. The chatter of the cafés drops away, and you are left in a space that people have entered for two thousand years.

Back outside, take a final look from the far side of the square. From a distance, you can see the temple as the Romans intended — raised on its podium, columns facing the open space, set slightly above everyday life. The modern town fills in around it with shutters, awnings and laundry. Children chase pigeons across the same ground where citizens once gathered to hear official announcements.

As you walk away, glance back once more. Notice how the late light turns the stone from white to honey. Notice the empty line of holes on the frieze, where the bronze words used to shine. The temple has outlived the emperor it honoured, the empire that built it, and the bombs that almost destroyed it. Today it simply stands in the square, while the town around it carries on.
