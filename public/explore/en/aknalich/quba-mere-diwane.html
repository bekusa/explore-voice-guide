The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the Ararat plain, the air often carries very little sound. You may hear a dog barking somewhere in the village, a car passing slowly, or wind moving through the orchards. Then you look up. A pale stone tower rises above the low houses of Aknalich, surrounded by a ring of smaller domes. It looks unlike anything else in this part of Armenia. Take a moment before you walk closer. This is Quba Mêrê Dîwanê, a temple of the Yazidi faith.

The Yazidis are a Kurdish-speaking religious community. For centuries their heartland lay in the mountains of northern Iraq, around the holy valley of Lalish. Over generations, persecution and war pushed many families north into the Caucasus. A large community settled in Armenia, mostly in farming villages on this plain. Aknalich is one of them. So the building in front of you is more than a monument. It is a home for a faith that has often had to survive without one.

Here is the first surprise. This temple is very new. It opened in 2019, and it is widely described as the largest Yazidi temple in the world. That is a remarkable claim for a small village in Armavir province, far from the faith's ancient centre. The project was funded by Mirza Sloyan, a Yazidi businessman who was born in this region and later lived in Moscow. Locals often speak of it as a gift back to the place his family came from.

Now look at the shape of the building. A central tower rises in the middle. Around it stand seven smaller domes. That number is not decoration. In Yazidi belief, God entrusted the world to seven holy beings, often described as angels. The chief among them is Melek Taus, the Peacock Angel. The name of the temple refers to these angels, and the architecture repeats their number in stone. You can count them as you walk around.

Melek Taus deserves a moment of explanation. For Yazidis, he is the leader of the angels and a loyal servant of God. Outsiders have misunderstood this figure for centuries, and those misunderstandings fed real violence against the community. Inside the faith, the peacock is a symbol of beauty and divine light. When you see bird or sun imagery here, it is worth remembering how much history sits behind it. A symbol that was mocked elsewhere is shown openly on this plain.

The sun matters too. Many Yazidis turn toward the sun when they pray, especially at sunrise. Sun motifs appear across Yazidi art, from embroidery to gravestones. As you look at the carved surfaces, watch for circles and radiating patterns. The ridged, fluted form of the domes also follows a long tradition. At Lalish in Iraq, the shrines carry tall conical spires with ribbed sides. This temple borrows that silhouette and builds it larger, in pale stone.

Before you go any further, a word on custom. This is a working place of worship, not a museum. Visitors are generally welcome, but behave as you would in any sacred space. Speak quietly. Ask before you photograph people. At many Yazidi shrines, worshippers take care not to step on the threshold of a doorway. They step over it instead, and some touch or kiss the doorframe. If you see this, follow their lead. It is a small gesture, and people notice it.

Now turn your attention to the smaller building nearby. Before the large temple was finished, the village already had a modest shrine on this ground. The two structures stand close together. The contrast is worth a moment. One is small and simple. The other is tall and ambitious. Together they show how quickly this community moved from keeping its faith quietly to building something that can be seen from across the plain.

Here is the second surprise. Look south on a clear day, and you may see Mount Ararat on the horizon. Its snow cap often appears to float above the haze. For Armenians, the mountain is a national symbol, even though it now stands across the border in Turkey. So from this one spot, you see the sacred architecture of one people framed by the sacred mountain of another. Very few views in the world hold two faiths together like this.

The village itself is worth noticing. Aknalich is a farming place. Around the temple you will see low houses, garden walls, fruit trees, and irrigation channels. In summer the plain becomes hot and dusty, and the shade of trees feels precious. In autumn the orchards and vineyards around Armavir are full of fruit. This is the everyday landscape of the community that worships here. The temple did not appear in a city. It grew out of fields.

The Yazidi faith is passed down mainly through families and oral tradition, more than through written scripture. Hymns, prayers, and stories travel from older generations to younger ones. A building like this gives that tradition a fixed point. Weddings, festivals, and gatherings of people from across the region can happen here. If you visit on a quiet weekday, you may have the courtyard almost to yourself. On a festival day, the scene can be completely different, with families arriving from many villages.

Why does this matter so much now? In 2014, Yazidis in northern Iraq suffered terrible violence, which many governments and international bodies have recognised as genocide. Communities abroad, including here in Armenia, felt that loss deeply. A temple built only a few years later carries that weight. It says that the faith continues. You do not need to know the theology to feel the meaning of a new building rising after such a time.

Take a final slow walk around the temple. Notice how the pale stone changes colour as the sun moves, warmer in the evening, flatter at midday. Count the seven domes one more time. Look out over the low roofs of Aknalich to the open plain, and, if the air is clear, to the white shape of Ararat. As you leave, you may hear the village again, the dogs, the wind, a voice calling across a garden. The temple stays behind you, quiet and upright, watching over the fields.
