The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water laps against stone, bicycle bells ring behind you, and trains roll in and out of the station across the canal. Then look up. A golden-yellow tower rises out of the water, with blocks of pink, green, and grey stacked around it like a child's building set. This is the Groninger Museum. It is one of the strangest buildings in the Netherlands, and that was the plan from the start.

Before we go inside, notice where you are standing. The museum sits on its own small island in a canal, directly opposite Groningen's central railway station. That position was a deliberate choice. A footbridge carries walkers and cyclists from the station, through the museum, and on towards the old city centre. Many people cross it every day without ever buying a ticket. For them the museum is simply part of the route home. Art becomes something you pass through, not only something you visit.

The building opened in 1994, and it caused a great deal of argument. The museum itself is much older. It has collected art and objects since the nineteenth century. But its old home had grown too small. The director at the time, Frans Haks, wanted something bold. He did not choose one architect. Instead, he invited several designers and asked each of them to shape a different part. The result is a group of pavilions, each in its own language.

The lead designer was Alessandro Mendini, an Italian architect and designer. Mendini was known for playful, colourful work that mixed high art with everyday objects. He tied the whole project together and designed the central golden tower. Here is a detail many visitors miss. That tower is not a viewing platform or a grand gallery. It holds the museum's storage. The treasures you cannot see are kept in the most visible part of the building.

Around Mendini's work, other designers added their own pavilions. The Italian designer Michele De Lucchi shaped a section in brick, a nod to the brick houses and warehouses of Groningen itself. The French designer Philippe Starck created a round pavilion for part of the applied arts collection. And the Austrian studio Coop Himmelb(l)au designed a section that looks almost broken. Its steel plates and slanted walls seem to collide. This style is often called deconstructivism, which means architecture that deliberately looks fractured or unstable.

As you walk between these spaces, pay attention to how your body feels. Floors change. Ceilings rise and drop. Light enters from unexpected angles. In one room you feel sheltered and calm. In the next, the walls lean toward you. This is part of the experience. The building does not want to disappear behind the art. It wants you to notice it, and to compare one designer's ideas with another's as you move.

Now to the collection itself. One of the museum's great strengths is a local art group called De Ploeg, which means "The Plough" in Dutch. It was founded in Groningen in 1918 by artists who wanted to bring fresh, modern ideas to a quiet northern province. Their paintings often show the flat Groningen countryside, with farms, dykes, and wide skies. The colours are strong and the shapes are simplified. This is expressionism, a style that shows feeling more than exact appearance.

If you have just arrived by train, these paintings may surprise you. You have probably seen this same landscape through the carriage window. Flat green fields. Long straight ditches. Low red-roofed farmhouses under a huge sky. The De Ploeg painters looked at that same scene a century ago and turned it into bright, almost electric colour. Seeing the paintings and then walking back outside can change the way you look at the horizon.

The museum also holds a respected collection of Chinese and Japanese porcelain. This may seem far from northern Holland, but it makes sense. For centuries, Dutch traders brought Asian porcelain to Europe, and wealthy families in towns like Groningen displayed it in their homes. Look closely at the blue-and-white pieces. Painted landscapes, flowers, and figures cover plates and vases, and many were made especially for the European market. They carry the story of global trade in a single piece of fired clay.

Alongside these older collections, the museum is known for contemporary art, fashion, and design. Exhibitions change often, so what you see today may be very different from what a friend saw last year. The museum has shown designers, photographers, and fashion makers next to painters and sculptors. That mix is deliberate. Here, a dress, a chair, or a poster can be treated with the same seriousness as an oil painting.

Here is a second detail worth looking for. Around 2010, parts of the interior were renovated, and several contemporary designers were invited to reshape public spaces. Some of the furniture and fittings you sit on or walk past are themselves design works. A bench or a lamp may be just as carefully made as an object in a display case. So before you rush past the café or the information area, slow down and look at what surrounds you.

Do not miss the stairways and transitions between floors. Mendini covered some surfaces in mosaic, small pieces of coloured tile set into patterns. These are easy to overlook when you are hurrying to the next gallery. Yet they are some of the most personal parts of the building. Up close, you can see the fine work of hands placing each fragment. From a distance, the patterns blur into colour, much like the De Ploeg paintings do.

The building's relationship with water is worth a quiet moment too. On a calm day, the canal reflects the yellow tower and the coloured blocks almost perfectly. On a windy day, the reflection breaks into moving streaks of gold and pink. Groningen is a student city, full of cyclists, and you will see a steady stream of them crossing the bridge. They are part of the picture, whether they notice the museum or not.

As you leave, stand on the bridge for a minute. Behind you is the station, with its trains heading out across the flat green land. In front of you is the old city, with its brick streets and church towers. And around you is this colourful, unlikely building, sitting in the water between the two. Listen again to the water against the stone, and the bicycle bells. Then choose which way to walk next.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive by train and walk straight across the footbridge.
Check the website for current temporary exhibitions before visiting.
View the building from the station side for the full facade.
Return at dusk to see the colours reflected in the canal.
Allow time to explore the design of the café and public spaces.
