The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the loudest sound is often the wind. It moves across dry grass with a soft, steady hiss. It lifts fine sand and drops it against your shoes. Somewhere far off, a bird calls once and then stops. The ground under your feet is not all soft dune. In many places it is hard, pale, and cracked, like a plate left too long in the sun. Welcome to Desert National Park, in the far west of Rajasthan.

This park sits in the Thar, the great desert that spreads across northwestern India and into Pakistan. It covers land in both Jaisalmer and Barmer districts. Its area is often given as roughly 3,162 square kilometres, which makes it one of the largest protected areas in India. The park was declared in the early 1980s. Its purpose was simple. It protects a living desert ecosystem, and not only a scenic one.

Here is the first surprise for most visitors. The famous sand dunes are only a small part of this park. Most of the land is something else. You will see rocky ridges, flat gravel plains, and low, compact earth. You will also see dry lake beds that hold a little water after rare rains. Fixed dunes, held in place by grass and shrubs, roll gently between them. The postcard image of endless golden sand tells only part of the story.

Look at the plants around you. They look sparse, almost stubborn. A tree you may notice is the khejri, with small leaves and thorny branches. Desert families have valued it for generations as shade, fodder, and fuel. Another common shrub is ker, a leafless, tangled bush with small fruits that people pickle. Clumps of tough grass, including sewan grass, cover the plains. This grass matters. It holds the soil and feeds the grazing animals of the desert.

Now, the bird that makes this park important. It is the Great Indian Bustard. It is a large, heavy bird with long legs, a pale neck, and a dark cap on its head. It walks more than it flies. It is the state bird of Rajasthan. Locally it is often called godawan. Today it is critically endangered. Only a small number remain in the wild, and this desert holds most of them.

Why is the bustard so rare? There are several reasons. Grasslands across India have shrunk and been ploughed. Hunting took a heavy toll in the past. In recent decades, a newer threat has grown. The bustard has poor forward vision and a heavy body. It often cannot turn in time to avoid overhead power lines. Collisions with these wires have killed many birds. Conservation groups and the courts have pushed for safer lines in bustard areas.

There is quiet hope, too. Conservation teams in the Jaisalmer area have run a breeding programme for the bustard. They collect eggs from the wild and raise chicks in protected centres. The long goal is to release birds back into safe habitat. Inside the park, some areas are fenced to keep out cattle and give grass a chance to grow. In these enclosures, bustards can feed and nest with less disturbance. If you see one, you are seeing something very few people ever see.

The bustard is not alone here. Watch for the chinkara, a small, graceful gazelle with a sandy coat. It can go a long time without drinking, and gets much of its water from plants. Blackbuck may appear in some areas. At dusk, a desert fox may trot across the track. Look low, too. The spiny-tailed lizard lives in burrows here. It is a stocky reptile with a thick, armoured tail, often seen basking in the morning sun.

Look up, as well. This desert is a strong place for birds of prey. Eagles, falcons, and vultures use the open sky to hunt and to search for food. In the cooler months, migrant raptors arrive from further north and join the residents. Many people come for the bustard, but leave talking about the eagles. A large bird resting on a lone tree, with nothing else on the horizon, is a strong desert image.

Here is the second surprise. This desert was once green. Not far from the park, at Akal, there is a wood fossil park. There, you can see the stone remains of ancient trees lying on the ground. They date back to the age of dinosaurs, many millions of years ago. At that time, this land was warm, wet, and forested. The trunks turned slowly to stone. Today they lie in a land that receives very little rain.

People live here, too. Villages sit inside and around the park. Herders move goats, sheep, and camels across the plains. Their homes are often low and made from local stone or mud, which stays cool in the heat. Water is precious, and old ponds and wells mark the places where life gathers. The park is not empty wilderness. It is a shared landscape, where conservation must work alongside people who have lived with this desert for centuries.

The light here changes the land through the day. At midday, the sun is hard and white, and colours fade. Shadows almost vanish. In the early morning, the light is soft and low. Every grass stem casts a long shadow. In the evening, the ground turns warm gold and then pink. This is also when animals are most active. The cool hours are the best hours, both for the eye and for wildlife.

Before you move on, take one more slow look around. Notice how far you can see. Notice how few lines break the horizon, perhaps one tree, one ridge, one distant hut. Feel the dry air on your skin. Listen again for the wind in the grass. Somewhere out there, a heavy bird may be walking quietly across the plain. As you leave, you carry a little of this silence with you, and a reason to protect it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange a forest department permit before entering the park.
Visit in early morning for active wildlife and soft light.
Carry plenty of water, sunscreen, and a wide-brimmed hat.
Bring binoculars to spot distant bustards and raptors.
Hire a local guide who knows bustard feeding areas.
