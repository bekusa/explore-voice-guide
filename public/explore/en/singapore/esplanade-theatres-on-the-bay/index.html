The guide

The story

Stand close to the outer skin of the building and look up. Hundreds of triangular metal shades overlap like scales, each one angled a little differently. On a hot afternoon the light coming through them softens and breaks into small shapes. Behind you the water of Marina Bay moves gently against the promenade. Somewhere nearby, a busker or a rehearsing musician sends a few notes across the stones. This is Esplanade – Theatres on the Bay, and it is easier to understand once you have stood beneath it.

Most people in Singapore do not call it by its full name. They call it the durian. The durian is a spiky tropical fruit with a famously strong smell, loved by many people across Southeast Asia and avoided by many others. When the two great domes appeared on the waterfront, the comparison came quickly. Some people also said they looked like the eyes of a fly. The nickname stayed, and the building now carries it with some humour.

The spikes are not decoration. They are aluminium sunshades, and there are thousands of them across the two shells. Each shade is shaped and angled to block the harsh equatorial sun while still letting daylight into the glass spaces inside. Singapore sits just north of the equator, so the sun is strong for most of the year. The architects needed a roof that could control heat and glare. The result is a building whose most famous feature is also a practical solution to the climate.

The complex opened in October 2002, after many years of planning. It was designed by a partnership between the local firm DP Architects and the British practice Michael Wilford and Partners. The early designs did not include the spiky cladding. That feature developed later, as the team looked for a way to shade the glass. Before it opened, Singapore had no single, purpose-built home on this scale for music, theatre, and dance. The Esplanade was meant to fill that gap.

Inside the two domes are the two largest venues. One holds the Concert Hall, which seats about 1,600 people. The other holds the Theatre, which seats around two thousand. The Theatre uses a horseshoe shape, with tiers of balconies curving around the stage. This layout follows the tradition of classic European opera houses. It keeps even the furthest seats relatively close to the performers, so faces and gestures stay visible.

Here is something most visitors never notice. The Concert Hall can change its own sound. Around the hall are reverberation chambers, hidden spaces behind large doors. When the doors open, sound travels into the chambers and returns, so the music lingers longer in the air. When they close, the sound becomes drier and clearer. Above the stage hangs an adjustable acoustic canopy that can be raised or lowered. Together these let the same room suit a full symphony orchestra, a solo pianist, or a spoken event.

The building also holds smaller spaces that are easy to miss. There is a Recital Studio for intimate concerts, and a Theatre Studio for experimental work. On an upper level you will find a library dedicated to the performing arts, part of Singapore's public library network. It holds music scores, recordings, and books about theatre and dance. For anyone who wants a quiet corner with a view of the bay, it is worth finding.

Now walk toward the water. Along the edge of the bay is the Outdoor Theatre, an open stage facing the promenade. Many performances here are open to anyone passing by. On a typical evening you might hear a jazz band, a school choir, or a group of traditional drummers. People sit on the steps and the stone ledges. Office workers stop on their way home. Joggers slow down for a song or two. This open stage is a large part of the building's daily life.

The Esplanade was built with Singapore's many communities in mind. The country's population includes people of Chinese, Malay, and Indian heritage, along with many others. The centre runs regular festivals that celebrate these traditions. Huayi focuses on Chinese culture. Pesta Raya celebrates Malay arts. Kalaa Utsavam presents Indian music and dance. Around these festivals, the foyers and outdoor spaces fill with performances, workshops, and families who come together for the evening.

If you take the stairs or lifts up to the roof terrace, the view opens across the bay. In one direction you can see the three towers of Marina Bay Sands, joined at the top by their long, ship-like platform. In another you can see the white Merlion statue, the half-lion, half-fish symbol of Singapore, spouting water into the bay. Behind you, the towers of the financial district rise above the older colonial buildings of the Civic District. Few places show so much of the city at once.

The land around the Esplanade has its own past. This stretch near the mouth of the Singapore River has been a gathering place for a long time. Before the arts centre was built, the area was known for open-air food stalls, where people came at night to eat satay, grilled meat on skewers. Next door is Esplanade Park, one of the older public green spaces in the city, with its trees, memorials, and shaded walkways. The new building grew up alongside these older layers.

As evening comes, the building changes. The glass behind the shades begins to glow from within, and the spikes become dark outlines against the light. Across the bay, the city starts its own lighting. Boats pass slowly on the water. The air cools a little, and the promenade fills with people walking, talking, and taking photographs. Many visitors come for a single show. Many more come simply to sit by the water and watch the light change.

Before you move on, take one more look at the shells above you. Notice how the shades are not identical. Some lie almost flat, others stand more upright, depending on which way they face the sun. Listen for the sound of the water, the voices on the steps, perhaps a few notes from the Outdoor Theatre. The durian, as locals call it, is not only a place for tickets and curtains. It is a place where the city comes to pause beside the bay.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the Outdoor Theatre schedule for open waterfront performances.
Head up to the roof terrace for wide views across the bay.
Come around dusk to watch the shells begin to glow.
Visit during Huayi, Pesta Raya, or Kalaa Utsavam festivals.
Find the performing arts library for a quiet, air-conditioned break.
