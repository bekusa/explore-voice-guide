Bukit Timah Nature Reserve

The climb begins almost at once.

⏱ 6 min

The story

The climb begins almost at once. Within a few steps of the entrance, the road tilts upward and the city sounds fade behind a wall of green. Cicadas drone in waves. The air grows close and humid under the canopy, and the light turns soft and dappled where it filters through the leaves. This is Bukit Timah Nature Reserve, a small patch of old rainforest holding the highest natural ground on the entire island.

The reserve was set aside in 1883, making it one of the oldest protected forests in the region. While most of the island's original lowland rainforest was cleared long ago for farms, roads, and housing, this hill was spared. What survives here is a fragment of primary forest, meaning woodland that was never fully cut down. That makes the reserve far older, as a living system, than almost anything around it.

At the center of the reserve rises Bukit Timah Hill itself, reaching about 163 meters above the sea. By the standards of mountains it is modest, but on this low and flat island it is the summit. The name comes from Malay, and the hill has long been a landmark. A paved road and several trails lead to the top, where a marker notes that you are standing at the island's highest natural point.

For its small size, the reserve holds a wide range of life. Naturalists have long pointed out that a single hillside here can contain more tree species than are found across large temperate countries. The dense layering of plants, from forest floor to high canopy, creates many small homes for animals and insects. Each level of the forest has its own residents, and the whole stack works as one connected system.

The most visible animals are the long-tailed macaques, which gather near the paths and the summit. Above them, in the canopy, live creatures most visitors never see, including the colugo, a gliding mammal sometimes called a flying lemur, though it is neither a lemur nor able to truly fly. Pangolins, squirrels, and a wide range of birds and insects fill out the community. Patience and a quiet step improve your chances of a sighting.

Several trails web across the reserve, ranging from the steep paved road to rougher forest paths with roots and steps. Some routes are short and direct, climbing straight for the summit. Others wind along the lower slopes for those who prefer a gentler walk. Mountain biking trails run around the edges of the reserve, kept separate from the main walking routes so the two activities do not collide.

Near one entrance lies Hindhede Quarry, a reminder that this hill was once cut for its stone. The reserve sits on a body of hard granite, prized for building, and quarrying went on here for many years. Today the old quarry has filled with water, forming a quiet green pool backed by a sheer rock face. A short walk and a viewing deck let you look out over it, a calm scene where heavy industry once worked.

The granite under your feet has a name of its own, Bukit Timah granite, and it underlies a good part of the island. Stone from this area helped build the growing town in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. As you climb, you may notice exposed rock along the path, pale and coarse-grained, the same material that was once blasted and hauled away from the quarries on the hill's flanks.

The reserve has needed care to survive so close to a dense city. For a period in the middle of the 2010s, the main road to the summit was closed so that slopes worn bare by heavy use could recover and be made safe. The reserve has also been recognized as an ASEAN Heritage Park, a regional listing for protected areas of special value. These efforts reflect how much pressure a small forest faces when millions live nearby.

This is a real climb, short but steep, so come prepared. Bring water, since the humidity will tire you faster than you expect, and wear shoes that grip on wet roots and stone. Start early to beat both the heat and the crowds. As at other reserves on the island, keep food hidden from the macaques, and do not feed them. Check at the visitor center for trail conditions, which can change after heavy rain.

Reaching the summit, you may be surprised how ordinary it looks, a clearing with a marker and a few resting walkers. The reward here is not a grand view but the forest itself, ancient and dense, carrying on its slow life on a hill the city chose to leave alone. Walking back down through the green shade, you carry the quiet knowledge that this small, old wood has outlasted almost everything built around it.

Key facts

Bukit Timah Nature Reserve was set aside in 1883.
Bukit Timah Hill rises about 163 meters, the island's highest.
It protects a fragment of primary rainforest.
Hindhede Quarry once cut granite from the hill.
The reserve is listed as an ASEAN Heritage Park.

What to look for

The summit marker at the island's highest natural point.
Pale, coarse-grained granite exposed along the climbing path.
The water-filled quarry backed by a sheer rock face.
Macaque troops gathering near the trails and clearing.

Practical tips

Start early to avoid both heat and crowds.
Carry water, since the humidity tires you quickly.
Wear shoes that grip on wet roots and stone.
Keep food hidden and never feed the macaques.
