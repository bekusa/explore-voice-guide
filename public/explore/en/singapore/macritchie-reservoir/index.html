The story

The water is still and brown-green in the early light, holding a mirror image of the trees that crowd its banks. A long-tailed macaque crosses the path ahead, glances at you, and moves on. The air smells of wet soil and leaves. Joggers pass in twos and threes. This is MacRitchie Reservoir, the oldest of the reservoirs built to hold the island's drinking water, and one of the easiest places in Singapore to step straight from the city into the forest.

The reservoir dates to the nineteenth century, when the growing town needed a reliable supply of fresh water. It takes its name from James MacRitchie, the municipal engineer who oversaw the work that expanded it. What began as a piece of engineering has, over the years, become something closer to a park and a refuge, ringed by walking trails and backed by the wider forest of the Central Catchment.

MacRitchie sits within the Central Catchment Nature Reserve, a large block of protected forest at the heart of the island. The trees here are not a garden planting but a working forest that shelters the water and the creatures that depend on it. Because the land is protected, the reserve holds species that have largely vanished from the built-up areas around it. The reservoir is the public doorway into that quieter world.

A network of trails and boardwalks loops around and away from the water. Some hug the shoreline, raised on wooden walkways over the shallows. Others climb gently into the forest, where the canopy closes overhead and the temperature drops a few degrees. The paths are marked and well kept, and you can choose a short stroll of under an hour or a longer loop that takes most of a morning.

The best known feature is the TreeTop Walk, a free-standing suspension bridge strung high between two of the area's hills. It stretches roughly 250 meters across a valley, and from the middle you stand level with the crowns of the tallest trees. The bridge sways a little underfoot. Below and around you, the forest falls away, and birds and squirrels move through branches you would normally only see from far below.

Wildlife is easy to find if you walk quietly. Troops of long-tailed macaques are the most common sight, and signs remind you not to feed them or carry food in the open. Monitor lizards, some of them large, slide through the shallows or rest on logs. Terrapins gather where the sun reaches the water. Overhead you may catch the call of a hornbill or the flash of a kingfisher dropping toward the reservoir.

A word about the monkeys is worth saying. They are wild, clever, and used to people, which is exactly why caution helps. Keep plastic bags out of sight, since the animals link them with food. Do not feed them, do not try to touch them, and give a wide berth to mothers with young. Treated with respect, they will usually ignore you and go on with their own business along the path.

Near the main entrance, the water itself is put to use. A small center rents kayaks and canoes, and on calm mornings you can paddle out onto the surface of the reservoir, looking back at the forest from the water. The activity is quiet by design, with no motors allowed, so the splash of your own paddle is often the loudest sound you hear.

One of the gentlest walks follows a boardwalk that crosses an arm of the reservoir on a zigzag bridge. The angled turns slow you down and give you reason to pause and look out over the water. Early and late in the day, the light lies flat across the surface, and the whole basin can fall completely still. It is a good place to stop, drink some water, and simply watch.

Come early if you can. The reserve is busiest and hottest in the middle of the day, and the wildlife is most active in the cool hours after dawn. Wear shoes with grip, since the boardwalks can be slick after rain, and rain arrives often. Carry your own water, keep it in a closed bag, and plan your route so you know how far the TreeTop Walk is before you set out, as it sits deep in the reserve.

MacRitchie holds two ideas at once. It is a piece of practical engineering, still part of how the island stores its water, and it is also a stretch of living forest a short ride from the towers of the city. Standing on the still boardwalk as the macaques settle and the light fades, you feel how thin the line can be between the built world and the wild one, and how worth keeping that quiet edge is.
