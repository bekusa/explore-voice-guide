The guide

The story

Place your hand on the rail and feel the cool, smooth steel under your palm. Above you, two thin metal spirals curve and twist. They wrap around the walkway like ribbons that never quite meet. The air off the bay feels warm and heavy, and somewhere below, water laps against the stone edges of the promenade. You are standing inside a sculpture that also happens to be a bridge.

This is the Helix Bridge. It is a pedestrian bridge that crosses the mouth of the Singapore River where it flows into Marina Bay. It joins Marina Centre on one side to the Bayfront district on the other, close to Marina Bay Sands. It opened in 2010, and from the start it was meant for people on foot. Traffic runs on a separate road bridge right beside it. Here, the only sounds are footsteps, voices, and the wind.

Look up again at those spirals. The design takes its idea from DNA, the molecule that carries the instructions for every living thing. DNA has a famous shape called a double helix, which means two strands twisting around each other. The architects turned that shape into steel. One spiral forms the main structure, and a second, finer spiral runs alongside it. Small rods connect the two, much like the rungs of a twisted ladder.

Here is a detail most visitors walk straight past. At night, small letters light up along the bridge in red and green. They read c, g, a, and t. These are the first letters of the four chemical bases in DNA — cytosine, guanine, adenine, and thymine. Every gene in your body is written with these four letters. So, as you cross after dark, you are walking along a glowing piece of the alphabet of life.

The bridge is the work of an international team. Cox Architecture, a firm from Australia, led the design together with the Singapore practice Architects 61. The engineering firm Arup worked out how to make the delicate curves strong enough to carry crowds. That was not simple. A helix looks light and playful, but every curve must carry weight and resist the wind. The result feels almost weightless, yet it holds thousands of people on busy nights.

Notice the long, gentle curve of the deck under your feet. The bridge does not run in a straight line. It bends in a soft arc across the water. This means the view keeps changing as you walk. One moment you face the hotel towers. A few steps later, the city skyline slides into view, and then the open water of the bay. Take it slowly. The curve is part of the experience.

Above parts of the walkway, you will see canopies made of glass and perforated steel mesh. They do a quiet but important job. Singapore sits just north of the equator. The sun here is strong for most of the year, and sudden tropical rain can arrive with little warning. These canopies give shade and shelter, while the small holes in the mesh let light and air pass through. On a hot afternoon, you will feel the difference.

Along the bridge, there are four viewing platforms that stick out over the water like small balconies. Each one gives you a wider view of the bay. Step into one and pause for a moment. From here, you can watch boats move slowly across the water and see joggers on the promenade below. On many evenings, these platforms fill with people waiting for the light show over Marina Bay to begin.

It helps to know how new this landscape is. Much of the land around you did not exist a few decades ago. Singapore built it through land reclamation, which means filling in parts of the sea to create new ground. The whole Marina Bay district grew on that reclaimed land. So the bridge connects pieces of a city that were shaped by human hands, not by nature. Even the shoreline here is a modern creation.

The bridge drew attention from the architecture world soon after it opened. It won recognition at the World Architecture Festival, an international event where designers from many countries compare their best buildings. For a footbridge, that was a notable honour. Bridges are often treated as simple pieces of infrastructure, built only to get people across. Here, the designers set out to show that a crossing could also be a place where people want to stop.

Now, think about what you can see from where you stand. On one side, the three towers of Marina Bay Sands rise above the water, with the long platform resting across their tops. Near the bridge's Bayfront end, you can see the white, petal-like shape of the ArtScience Museum, which many people say looks like a lotus flower. Across the bay, the towers of the financial district form a wall of glass and light.

If you come back after sunset, the bridge changes completely. Lights run along the steel spirals and glow softly against the dark sky. The water picks up the colours and moves them around in slow ripples. Couples lean on the rails. Families stop for photographs. Office workers walk home across the bay. The bridge becomes less of a structure and more of a gathering place, part of the evening life of the city.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment in the middle of the bridge. Feel the slight breeze coming off the water. Listen to the low hum of the city around you, and the quieter sound of footsteps on the deck. Look up one more time at the twisting steel above your head. As you walk away, glance back over your shoulder. The spirals keep turning in the light, the same shape that sits inside every living cell.
