The guide

The story

Behind palm-lined gardens and a white colonnaded front, a grand old hotel keeps the air of a more leisurely age, its long verandahs and high ceilings a survival of colonial Singapore. This is Raffles, the most famous hotel in the city and one of the most storied in the world.

It opened in the late nineteenth century, in 1887, and grew into the grand hotel of the region, named after the British founder of colonial Singapore. Through the decades it hosted writers, royalty, and travellers, and gathered a reputation for old-world luxury.

The building itself is a fine example of tropical colonial architecture: white walls, shuttered windows, deep verandahs, and gardens designed to catch the breeze in the days before air conditioning. Restored and polished, it gleams among the modern towers around it.

Raffles is famous as the birthplace of a cocktail, the Singapore Sling, said to have been mixed here in its long bar in the early twentieth century. Ordering one in that bar, where peanut shells are traditionally dropped on the floor, is a small ritual for many visitors.

The hotel has long been linked with famous writers who stayed or drank here, and stories, some true, some embroidered, cling to it. That literary and colonial glamour is a large part of what people come to feel.

For most of its life it was a place only for the wealthy, and it remains a luxury hotel, but parts of it are open to all. You can walk the gardens, visit the bar and some shops and restaurants, and take in the atmosphere without staying the night.

The arcades and courtyards hold smart shops and dining, and the whole complex is kept immaculately, so even a short visit feels like stepping into a carefully preserved past. Staff in traditional uniforms add to the sense of theatre.

It is worth being clear-eyed about what the hotel represents: a monument to the colonial era, glamorous and comfortable for some, built within a system that was far less so for others. Visiting it, you are touching that complicated history as well as its charm.

The hotel sits in the colonial heart of the city, near old civic buildings, museums, and churches, so it folds naturally into a walk through that quarter of grand white buildings and open green.

Dress and decorum matter here. Some areas have dress codes, and the hotel asks visitors to be respectful of guests, so it is not a place for beachwear or loud crowds, which is part of how it keeps its particular calm.

A few practical notes. You can visit the public areas, bar, and shops without being a guest; the famous cocktail is pricey but comes with the experience. Dress neatly, and come earlier in the day if you want the bar without a wait.

Sit on a verandah or in the long bar with a cool drink, with the fans turning overhead and the gardens beyond, and the slow rhythm of an older age settles around you. Raffles has kept that atmosphere for well over a century. When you step back out among the modern towers, the contrast is sharp.
