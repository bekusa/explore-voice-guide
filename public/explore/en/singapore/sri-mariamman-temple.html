The guide

The story

In the middle of Chinatown, a tower crowded with brightly painted gods and creatures rises above the shophouses, and through its gate lies a colourful courtyard thick with incense and ritual. This is the Sri Mariamman Temple, the oldest Hindu temple in Singapore, a vivid splash of southern India in the heart of the Chinese quarter.

The temple was founded in the early nineteenth century, around 1827, by immigrants from southern India, making it the oldest Hindu place of worship in the country. It has been rebuilt and enriched over the years, but its roots reach back almost to the founding of the colonial city.

Its most striking feature is the gate tower, a tall pyramid covered from base to top in dozens of brightly painted figures of gods, goddesses, and mythical beings, packed in tier upon tier. This style, from the temples of southern India, is unlike anything else in the quarter.

The temple is dedicated to a mother goddess associated with healing and protection from disease, much revered by the community that built it. Her shrine is the focus of the temple, and worshippers come to pray, make offerings, and seek her blessing.

Within the gate lies a courtyard and a series of shrines beneath a painted ceiling and rows of sculpted figures, all in vivid colour. The richness and brightness of the interior, alive with ritual, are a strong contrast to the calmer temples and the street outside.

Part of the temple's charm is its setting: a grand Hindu temple in the middle of Chinatown, a reminder that the quarter has always been mixed, and that Indian, Chinese, and other communities have long lived side by side in these streets.

Once a year the temple is the centre of a dramatic festival in which devotees walk across a bed of hot coals as an act of faith and thanksgiving. It draws large crowds and is one of the most striking religious events in the city.

This is an active place of worship, not a museum, with daily rituals, bells, and offerings. Visitors are welcome but asked to behave respectfully, to remove their shoes, and to dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees.

Look closely at the figures on the tower and the shrines, repainted in fresh, bright colours, each one a god, a guardian, or a scene from Hindu mythology. The sheer density of sculpture rewards slow looking, and a guide or sign helps make sense of who is who.

The temple sits among the food, shops, and other temples of Chinatown, so it slots easily into a wander through the quarter, a sudden burst of colour and ritual between the shophouses and hawker stalls.

A few practical notes. Entry is free, though there may be a small charge for cameras; remove your shoes at the entrance, dress modestly, and avoid the main prayer times if you only want to look. Step quietly and keep clear of worshippers.

Stand in the incense-filled courtyard, with the painted gods rising above you and the rituals going on around, and consider that this corner of southern Indian faith has stood in the heart of Chinatown for two centuries. The temple is a small lesson in how mixed the city has always been. When you step back into the street, the everyday quarter resumes.
