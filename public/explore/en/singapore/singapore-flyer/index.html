The guide

The story

Stand at the base for a moment and listen. Under the steady hum of traffic on the waterfront roads, there is a softer sound: the slow, almost silent turning of steel overhead. The air is warm and heavy, and it smells faintly of salt from the bay. Look up, and a huge white rim of cables and tubing fills the sky above you. It moves so slowly that, at first, you might think it is standing still.

This is the Singapore Flyer, a giant observation wheel on the eastern edge of Marina Bay. It began carrying passengers in 2008, and for several years it was the tallest wheel of its kind anywhere in the world. Its height is usually given as about 165 metres, roughly the height of a forty-storey building. In 2014, a wheel in Las Vegas took that record. But here, the Flyer still frames the southern tip of the island like a giant compass dial.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The ground under your feet did not exist a few generations ago. Much of the land around Marina Bay was reclaimed from the sea during the late twentieth century. Engineers filled shallow water with sand and earth and built new ground on top. So this wheel stands on land that Singapore made for itself. That small fact helps explain the whole skyline you are about to see from above.

Now look closely at the wheel itself. It is not a classic fairground wheel with hanging seats that swing. Instead, the rim carries twenty-eight large, enclosed capsules. They are mounted on the outside of the ring, so nothing blocks your view as you rise. Each capsule is about the size of a city bus and holds up to twenty-eight people. The glass is curved, and the cabins are air-conditioned — a real gift on a humid afternoon.

The design work brought together architects and engineers from several countries. The Japanese architect Kisho Kurokawa was involved in shaping the concept. He was known for buildings that tried to blend technology with natural forms. Look at the thin, spoked cables that tie the rim to the central hub. From a distance, they make the whole structure look light, almost like a bicycle wheel. Up close, you can see how much steel it takes to create that light effect.

And here is a second surprise, one that says a great deal about Singapore. When the Flyer first opened, it turned in one direction. Soon afterwards, the operators reversed it. The change followed advice from feng shui masters. Feng shui is the traditional Chinese practice of arranging buildings and spaces to bring good fortune. The concern was about which way luck would flow. Today the wheel turns so that it brings fortune toward the city, not away from it.

Once you board, the climb begins gently. One full rotation takes about thirty minutes, so there is no rush. The capsule does not stop for you to step in. It moves at walking pace, and you simply walk on. Then the ground falls away. First you see the car parks and the road. Then the trees. Then, slowly, the whole bay opens up in front of you like a map.

To the south and west, the view is dominated by Marina Bay Sands. Its three hotel towers support a long platform that sits across the top like a ship. Below it, the lotus-shaped ArtScience Museum points its white petals to the sky. Further along the water, look for the business district, where glass towers stand shoulder to shoulder. On a clear day, you can trace the river as it runs into the bay.

Turn toward the east and south, and you see a very different picture. The giant metal Supertrees of Gardens by the Bay rise out of the greenery. Beyond them, two large glass domes shine in the sun. Further out lies the Singapore Strait, one of the busiest shipping lanes on Earth. Count the ships waiting at anchor. On many days, there are dozens of them, lined up on the horizon like toy boats.

From the top, you may also notice a pattern of roads around the base of the wheel. Some of these streets become part of the Marina Bay Street Circuit during the Formula One night race. The pit buildings sit right next to the Flyer. During race season, the wheel becomes a grandstand in the sky. Even on ordinary days, you can follow the curves of the track if you know where to look.

The light changes everything here. In the morning, the haze over the strait is soft and silver. In the late afternoon, the sun drops behind the towers and turns the bay gold. After dark, the city lights come on block by block. Then the wheel itself glows with coloured lights, and its reflection stretches across the water. Many people say the hour around sunset gives the best of both worlds.

When your capsule comes back down, take a few minutes on the ground. At the base of the wheel there is a terminal building with shops, cafés and gardens. It is a calm place to cool off after the ride. Walk out to the waterfront promenade nearby. From there, you can look back at the Flyer and see the full circle at once. That view is hard to get when you are inside it.

So pause here for a moment. Look up again at the slow white ring above you. Somewhere near the top, a group of strangers is pressed against the glass, pointing at the ships. Feel the warm air, and listen to the low hum of the city. As you walk away along the bay, glance back now and then. You may find the wheel has moved a little — turning quietly, the way it was told to.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride around sunset to see both daylight and city lights.
Allow about thirty minutes for one full rotation.
Walk to the waterfront promenade for a full-circle photo.
Expect road closures and crowds during Formula One race season.
Bring sunglasses for the bright glare inside glass capsules.
