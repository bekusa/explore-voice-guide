The story

The air here is warm and thick with the smell of damp leaves and cut grass. Somewhere overhead, a family of orangutans moves through the branches, unhurried. There are no bars in front of you, no heavy cages. Just a wide moat, a low wall, and a stretch of green that seems to belong as much to the animals as to the people walking through it. This is the Singapore Zoo, and from your first steps inside, it asks you to look at captivity in a different way.

The zoo opened in 1973 on a piece of land in Mandai, in the northern part of the island. It sits on the edge of the Upper Seletar Reservoir, surrounded by water and secondary rainforest. The setting was chosen on purpose. Instead of building enclosures and then planting a few trees around them, the designers worked with the forest that was already here. The result feels less like a zoo and more like a walk through a managed piece of jungle.

The idea behind the design is often called the open concept. Rather than fences and glass, the animals are held back by moats, dry ditches, and natural barriers like rock and water. A drop you cannot see from the path keeps a leopard on its side and you on yours. Tall trees and dense planting hide much of the structure. The aim is simple. The visitor should feel close to the animal, and the animal should feel it is somewhere close to home.

The orangutans are the animals most people come to see. Singapore Zoo became known for letting some of them move through a free-ranging area above the walkways, climbing on tall frames and ropes strung between real trees. You may find yourself looking up to see a large reddish shape resting in the fork of a branch, one long arm hanging down. The morning feeding sessions draw quiet crowds who stand and watch, heads tilted back.

For many years, one orangutan in particular came to stand for the whole zoo. Her name was Ah Meng, a female Sumatran orangutan who took part in breakfast sessions with visitors and appeared in countless photographs. She became a kind of unofficial face for tourism on the island. Ah Meng died in 2008, at an old age for her species, and was mourned widely. Her descendants and others continue the line she made famous.

Beyond the orangutans, the zoo is laid out in zones that group animals by region or habitat. There is a section shaped around the Great Rift Valley of Ethiopia, with rocky cliffs for baboons. There is a stretch of African plains where giraffes and zebras share open ground. A walk-through space called the Fragile Forest places you inside a dome where fruit bats, lemurs, and butterflies move freely around you, with no glass between.

The zoo presents itself as a place for conservation and learning, not only display. Many of the species here are threatened in the wild, and signs along the paths explain the pressures they face, from forest loss to the illegal animal trade. Breeding programs work to keep healthy populations of certain species. Whatever you make of zoos in general, the message here leans toward protection and care rather than show.

Walking the loop takes a few hours if you let it. The paths rise and fall gently, shaded for much of the way. You hear the calls of gibbons carrying across the water, the splash of an otter, the low rumble of an elephant. Children press against the railings. The heat sits on your shoulders, and the cold drinks at the rest stops feel earned. Trams run along the main route for those who would rather ride than walk.

The zoo is one part of a larger wildlife area in Mandai. Next door sit the Night Safari and the river-themed park, and together they form a single cluster managed as one group. Many visitors plan a full day or even two to move between them. The forest and the reservoir tie the whole area together, so that crossing from one park to the next feels like staying inside the same green world.

Mornings are the best time to come. The animals are more active before the day heats up, and the feeding sessions are scheduled early. By midday, many creatures find shade and rest, and so will you. Rain comes often and hard, then clears. The covered shelters along the route are there for a reason, and a sudden downpour can be a fine time to stand and watch the animals carry on as if nothing has changed.

As you leave, the smell of the forest stays with you for a while. The Singapore Zoo does not hide that these are animals living under human care, far from the forests their ancestors knew. Instead it tries to make that care visible, and to ask what we owe the creatures we keep. You walk back out into the heat thinking, perhaps, a little longer than you expected to about the quiet shape resting in the branches above the path.

Key facts

Singapore Zoo opened in 1973 in northern Mandai.
Built on the edge of the Upper Seletar Reservoir.
Open-concept design uses moats instead of cages and bars.
Ah Meng, a Sumatran orangutan, died in 2008.
Managed today by the Mandai Wildlife Group.

What to look for

Orangutans resting in the tall trees above the walkways.
The dry moats that replace fences along the paths.
Fruit bats and lemurs inside the Fragile Forest dome.
Giraffes and zebras sharing the open African plains exhibit.

Practical tips

Arrive early when animals are active and feedings are scheduled.
Ride the tram if you would rather not walk the loop.
Carry water and a light rain cover for sudden downpours.
Set aside a full day to include the neighboring parks.
