The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the foot of the steps. Behind you, the open green of the Padang stretches toward the bay. Ahead, a long row of tall stone columns rises in the heat. The air is heavy and warm. And then you step inside, into cool, filtered light falling through a roof of glass and gold-coloured metal. That shift, from tropical glare to quiet shade, is the first thing this building gives you.

What you are entering is not one building but two. National Gallery Singapore lives inside the former City Hall and the former Supreme Court. These are two colonial-era civic buildings that stand side by side. For most of the twentieth century, they were where the island was governed and where justice was handed down. Today they are joined into a single museum. It opened to the public in 2015, the year Singapore marked fifty years of independence.

Let's start with the older of the pair. City Hall was completed in 1929. It was first known as the Municipal Building. Look at its front: a long, even line of classical columns facing the Padang. It is a style the British used across their empire, borrowed from ancient Greece and Rome. The style was meant to project order, permanence, and authority. In the tropics, it also had a practical side. Deep verandas and high ceilings helped keep the rooms in shade.

Now look to the right, toward the green dome. That is the former Supreme Court, completed at the end of the 1930s. It is widely described as one of the last grand classical buildings the British raised in Singapore. Above its entrance, the triangular pediment holds a sculpted group of figures. The work is by an Italian sculptor. At its centre stands a figure representing Justice. It is easy to walk straight past it. Take a moment, and look up.

These walls carry real weight in the story of the nation. In September 1945, at the end of the Second World War, the Japanese surrender in Southeast Asia was formally signed inside City Hall. Years later, the building became a stage for the island's path toward self-government and independence. Leaders were sworn in here. Crowds gathered on the Padang outside. So when you walk these corridors, you are walking through rooms where the modern country took shape.

Turning two heavy colonial buildings into one gallery was a puzzle. A French practice, studioMilou, led the design, working with a Singapore firm. Their answer was to keep the old facades and much of the historic interior intact. Then they connected the two buildings with new spaces between and above them. Look up at the canopy overhead. It rests on slender columns that branch like trees. The pattern softens the sunlight, a little like light through leaves in a forest.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of the old courtrooms have been kept, with their wooden benches, panelling, and raised seats for the judges. You can sit where lawyers once argued cases. Beneath the Supreme Court, there are also former holding cells. Accused people waited there before they were led up into court. Standing in those narrow spaces is a very different feeling from the bright galleries above. It is a quiet reminder of what this building used to do.

Now to the art itself. The Gallery holds one of the largest public collections of modern art from Singapore and Southeast Asia. The focus is on the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. That is the key to this place. It is not a museum of European masters. Instead, it tells the story of how artists in this region saw themselves. They painted through colonial rule, war, independence, and rapid change. Many of these works are rarely shown together anywhere else in the world.

The collection is mainly shown in two long-term galleries. One is dedicated to the art of Singapore. The other looks across the wider region, from Indonesia and the Philippines to Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia, Myanmar, and beyond. Walk through them in order, and you can follow ideas moving between countries. You will see artists learning from Europe, then turning away from it. You will see them looking back to local traditions, village life, and daily labour.

Here is a second surprise. Look for the work of Raden Saleh, a Javanese painter of the nineteenth century. He trained in Europe and became famous there, at a time when that was almost unheard of for an artist from the colonies. One of his large, dramatic canvases is often shown here. It depicts wild animals trapped by a raging forest fire. Stand close, and look at the faces of the animals. Then step back, and take in the whole scene at once.

When your eyes need a rest, head upstairs. The rooftop opens onto a garden terrace with wide views across the Padang. On a clear day, you can see the towers of the financial district and the shape of Marina Bay Sands across the water. In the late afternoon, the light turns soft and golden over the stone. It is a good place to pause, to let the heat touch your skin again, and to sit down.

A visit can easily fill several hours, so it helps to choose. If time is short, pick one gallery and give it your full attention. Families will find dedicated spaces for children, with activities designed to make art feel hands-on. And keep an eye on the temporary exhibitions. They change through the year, and they often bring work from far beyond the region.

Before you leave, walk back to the front steps and turn around once more. Look at the long row of columns, the green dome, and the glass and gold canopy rising between them. Two buildings made to govern a colony now hold the art of the people who lived there. Then face the Padang again. Listen to the traffic, the birds, and the voices on the grass. The city you hear outside is the same story you just saw on the walls.
