The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water is dripping somewhere close by, steady and patient, off fern fronds and moss and the dark leaves overhead. The air smells green, and slightly cold. Then the cloud shifts, and above the treetops you see something that should not belong here at all... a river of white ice, pouring down from the mountains toward the forest at your feet. Welcome to Westland Tai Poutini National Park.

The name itself tells you where you are. Westland is the long, narrow district on the western side of New Zealand's South Island. Tai Poutini is the Māori name for this whole West Coast. The park runs from the high peaks of the Southern Alps down toward the Tasman Sea. On the far side of the main mountain ridge lies Aoraki Mount Cook National Park. Together they share some of the highest ground in the country.

What makes this place so strange is the distance. In most parts of the world, glaciers live high in the mountains, far above any forest. Here, the two biggest glaciers come down remarkably low. They reach into valleys where temperate rainforest grows, not far above sea level, and only a short drive from the surf. Few places on Earth bring ice and rainforest this close together. That is the first surprise of the park, and it is the reason most visitors come.

The reason is the weather. Moist winds blow in from the Tasman Sea and slam straight into the Southern Alps. The air rises, cools, and drops its water. On this side of the mountains, that means rain... a great deal of rain. High up, the same moisture falls as snow. Year after year it piles up in wide basins called névés, packs down into ice, and slides downhill under its own weight. The rain that soaks you on the coast is the same water that feeds the glaciers.

The two glaciers have both European and Māori names. The northern one is Franz Josef Glacier, named in the 1860s by the explorer Julius von Haast after the Austrian emperor. Its Māori name is Kā Roimata o Hine Hukatere, which means the tears of Hine Hukatere. The southern glacier, Fox Glacier, takes its name from William Fox, a New Zealand premier. In Māori it is Te Moeka o Tuawe, the resting place of Tuawe.

Those Māori names carry a story. According to the Māori legend, a young woman named Hine Hukatere loved the mountains and persuaded her lover, Tuawe, to climb with her. An avalanche swept him from the peaks, and he was killed. Hine Hukatere wept so long and so hard that her tears flowed down the mountain. The gods froze them in place as a memorial. Locals say that is the glacier you see today... frozen grief, still moving slowly toward the sea.

The forest around you deserves as much attention as the ice. This is lowland and montane rainforest, thick with tree ferns, mosses, and old conifers such as rimu and kahikatea. Kahikatea is New Zealand's tallest native tree, and it likes wet, swampy ground. Look at the trunks and branches. Many are covered in other plants... ferns, orchids, and mosses that live on the trees without rooting in the soil. Every surface holds something growing.

Now look at the valley floors as you approach the glaciers. Here is the second surprise. The bare rock walls and gravel plains you walk through were under ice within living memory. Both glaciers have advanced and retreated many times. Over the last century, the overall trend has been a long and dramatic retreat. Signposts along some walking tracks mark where the ice once stood. Seeing how far you have walked from those markers is a quiet and powerful lesson in a changing climate.

The ground beneath this park is restless too. The Alpine Fault, one of the great faults on the planet, runs along the western foot of the Southern Alps. Here, two tectonic plates grind past each other. Over immense spans of time, that movement has pushed the mountains upward, while rain and ice wear them down. The steep, raw slopes you see are the result of that constant contest between uplift and erosion. It is one reason the valley walls look so sharp and young.

Keep your ears open for wildlife. In the high country, you may hear a loud, rolling cry overhead. That is the kea, the world's only alpine parrot, olive-green with a flash of orange under its wings. Kea are famously curious and will investigate bags, boots, and car parts. In the forest, listen for the tūī, a bird with a white tuft at its throat and a song full of clicks and bell-like notes. Please do not feed any of the birds.

Most visitors explore on foot through the valley walks that lead toward each glacier's terminal face. The tracks cross gravel riverbeds and pass waterfalls that tumble straight off the cliffs. Ranger barriers mark how close you can safely go. Respect them. Glacier ice collapses without warning, and the valley walls drop rocks. Rivers here can rise very fast after rain. If you want to stand on the ice itself, guided trips by helicopter are the usual way up.

Not far from Fox Glacier, many people make time for Lake Matheson. On a still morning, the dark water mirrors the forest and the high snowy peaks above it. On clear days, those peaks include Aoraki Mount Cook, New Zealand's highest mountain. The lake's colour comes from natural tannins washed out of the forest floor, the same tea-brown tint you see in many West Coast streams. It makes the reflections deeper and sharper.

This park is also part of something larger. It belongs to Te Wāhipounamu, a vast UNESCO World Heritage Area in the south-west of New Zealand. Te Wāhipounamu means the place of greenstone, after the prized jade stone that Māori found in this region. Ice, fault line, rainforest, and coast are all protected here together, as one connected landscape rather than separate scenic spots.

Before you move on, pause where you are standing. Feel the damp air on your skin. Notice how quickly the light changes as clouds slide across the peaks. The ice above you is moving, slowly, even now. The ferns around you are drinking the same rain that built it. As you walk back toward the road, listen once more for the dripping water... it is the sound of this whole park being made.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack a waterproof jacket even when the morning looks clear.
Carry insect repellent for the persistent West Coast sandflies.
Stay behind ranger barriers near glacier faces and valley walls.
Visit Lake Matheson early for the calmest mirror reflections.
Book helicopter glacier trips ahead and allow for weather cancellations.
