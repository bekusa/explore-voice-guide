The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across Salisbury Plain without anything to stop it. It carries the cry of jackdaws, the low hum of distant traffic, and sometimes the bleat of sheep. Under your feet, the chalk grassland is short and springy. Ahead of you, grey stones stand in a ring against a very wide sky. They look smaller than you expected... and then, as you walk closer, much larger.

This is Stonehenge, about two miles west of the small town of Amesbury in Wiltshire. It is one of the most studied prehistoric monuments on Earth. Yet it still keeps many of its secrets. Archaeologists agree on a broad outline of its story. The details are still debated, and new discoveries arrive every few years. So think of what you hear today as the current best understanding. The monument has a habit of surprising the experts.

The story begins not with stone, but with earth. Around 3000 BCE, people dug a circular ditch here and piled the chalk into a bank. Picture that bank freshly cut, a ring of brilliant white across the green plain. Inside it, they dug a circle of pits. These are called the Aubrey Holes, after John Aubrey, a seventeenth-century antiquarian who first noted them. Some held cremated human remains. So from its very beginning, this was a place connected with the dead.

The great stones came later. Around 2500 BCE, builders raised the large sarsens you see in front of you. Sarsen is a hard sandstone that forms in blocks across this part of southern England. For a long time, nobody knew exactly where these blocks came from. Then a study published in 2020 matched most of them to West Woods, near Marlborough, about fifteen miles to the north. Some of these stones weigh more than twenty tonnes. Nobody knows for certain how they were moved.

Now look for the smaller stones, darker and set among the giants. These are the bluestones. Here is the first real surprise. Most of them came from the Preseli Hills in west Wales, roughly 150 miles away. People moved them across that distance more than four and a half thousand years ago, without wheels as far as we know, and without metal tools. Why carry stones so far when good stone lay much closer? That question is still open. Perhaps the stones themselves carried meaning from their homeland.

And there is a second surprise, discovered very recently. A large flat stone lies fallen near the centre, partly buried under other blocks. It is called the Altar Stone, though there is no evidence it was ever an altar. For decades, people assumed it also came from Wales. In 2024, researchers showed that it most likely came from northeast Scotland, around 450 miles away. That finding changed how archaeologists think about the reach of these Neolithic communities. They were connected across the whole island of Britain.

Study the way the stones fit together. The upright pairs with a stone laid across the top are called trilithons. The horizontal stones on top are called lintels. Each lintel sits on knobs carved into the uprights, like a peg in a socket. Neighbouring lintels lock into each other with a tongue and groove. These are carpentry joints, borrowed from woodworking. And the lintels of the outer ring are gently curved, so together they once formed a smooth circle in the air. This was careful, planned work.

Now turn your attention to the sky. The monument is aligned with the sun. On the longest day of the year, in June, the sun rises close to the Heel Stone, a large rough sarsen standing outside the circle near the road. On the shortest day, in December, the sun sets in the opposite direction, framed by the stones. Many archaeologists now believe the midwinter sunset may have mattered most. It marked the turning of the year, when the days begin to grow longer again.

That alignment continues beyond the circle. A pathway called the Avenue runs from the entrance toward the River Avon. It is marked by parallel banks and ditches, now very low in the grass. About two miles to the northeast lies Durrington Walls, a huge earthwork enclosure. Excavations there found traces of houses and the remains of large feasts, with many pig bones. Some researchers think the builders of Stonehenge lived and gathered there. So this circle was one part of a much larger ceremonial landscape.

You may have heard that the Druids built Stonehenge. They did not. The monument was already ancient long before the Druids of Iron Age Britain appeared. Legend tells a different story too. In the twelfth century, the writer Geoffrey of Monmouth claimed that the wizard Merlin brought the stones from Ireland by magic. It is pure folklore. But it is interesting that the legend describes stones coming from far away. In a strange way, the old story points toward a truth.

The stones also carry marks from later ages. In the 1950s, archaeologists noticed carvings of Bronze Age axe-heads and a dagger on some of the sarsens. Laser scanning in 2012 found many more. They are faint and very hard to see with the naked eye, especially in flat light. You can also find much later marks. Visitors in past centuries carved their names into the stone, and some even chipped off pieces as souvenirs. Today the stones are protected, and that damage has stopped.

The modern story has its own twist. In 1915, a local man named Cecil Chubb bought Stonehenge at auction. In 1918, he gave it to the nation. Since 1986, it has been part of a World Heritage Site together with Avebury, another great stone circle to the north. English Heritage cares for the monument today, and the National Trust looks after much of the surrounding land. Those wide fields around you are full of burial mounds. Look for the soft rounded bumps on the horizon.

As you walk the path around the circle, notice how the view keeps changing. From one side, the stones overlap into a dense grey wall. From another, gaps open up and the sky shows through the trilithons. Watch the colour of the stone as well. Lichens in yellow, grey and pale green grow across the surfaces. Jackdaws often perch on the lintels and nest in the gaps. They have made their own home in this ancient structure.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and face the circle. Feel the wind again, the same wind that crossed this plain when the first ditch was dug. Thousands of people worked here over many centuries, with antler picks, ropes, and enormous patience. We do not know their names or their language. But we can still see what they made. As you walk away, look back once more. Notice how small the stones become against the wide plain... and how hard it is to stop looking.
