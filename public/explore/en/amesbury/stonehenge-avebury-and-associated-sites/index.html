The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across Salisbury Plain with almost nothing to stop it. It presses the grass flat, catches the edges of your coat, and then it reaches the stones. Up close, the sarsens are rough and pitted, grey stained with pale green and gold lichen. They are not smooth monuments. They are weathered rock, shaped by hand, standing in open chalk country under a very large sky.

You are standing inside a landscape, not just beside a single monument. Stonehenge is one part of a World Heritage Site inscribed by UNESCO in 1986. That listing joins it with Avebury, about forty kilometres to the north, and with hundreds of burial mounds, earthworks and ceremonial sites scattered across this part of Wiltshire. Together they form one of the densest clusters of Neolithic and Bronze Age monuments anywhere in Europe.

The Neolithic, if the word is new to you, is the age of the first farmers in Britain — people who kept cattle, grew grain, and made tools of polished stone. Stonehenge began with them. Around 3000 BCE, people dug a circular ditch and bank here. Only later did the great stones arrive. Archaeologists now see the monument as something built, rebuilt and rearranged over many centuries. It was never one single project.

Look at the tall stones of the outer ring. These are the sarsens, a hard sandstone found on the chalk downs. Most were raised around 2500 BCE. In 2020, chemical tests matched many of them to West Woods, a site near Marlborough, roughly twenty-five kilometres away. Moving blocks this size over that distance, without metal tools or wheels as we know them, took enormous planning. Sledges, timber rails, ropes and many people were almost certainly involved.

Now find the smaller, darker stones inside the ring. These are the bluestones. They came much further — from the Preseli Hills in west Wales, more than two hundred kilometres away. And here is a surprise most visitors miss. In 2024, researchers traced the large Altar Stone, lying flat near the centre, to northeast Scotland. That is a journey of over seven hundred kilometres. Nobody yet knows exactly how it got here.

Notice how the horizontal stones sit on top of the uprights. They are lintels, and the builders fixed them with joints taken from woodworking. A knob on top of each upright fits into a hollow cut into the lintel above. Neighbouring lintels lock together end to end. The lintels of the outer circle are even curved slightly, so that together they follow the line of the ring. These were people who thought like carpenters, but worked in stone.

Turn toward the northeast, and look for a single stone standing apart, outside the circle near the road. This is the Heel Stone. On the morning of the midsummer solstice, seen from the centre, the sun rises close to it. In midwinter, the alignment runs the other way, toward the setting sun. Many archaeologists think midwinter may have mattered most. Large numbers of pig bones found at nearby Durrington Walls suggest great winter feasts.

That raises the question everyone asks — what was Stonehenge for? The honest answer is that nobody knows for certain. Human remains show that it was a place of burial in its early centuries. It was clearly tied to the movements of the sun. Some researchers see it as a monument to ancestors. Others see a place of healing, or of gathering. The theories about Druids and ancient astronauts? Those are later inventions, not evidence.

Now picture the other half of this World Heritage Site. Avebury is a very different experience. Its stone circle is the largest in Britain, so large that a village sits partly inside it. A huge ditch and bank surround the stones. You can walk among them, touch them, and then stop for a drink in a pub that stands within the ancient ring. Sheep graze between the stones as if nothing unusual is happening.

Avebury's stones were not shaped like those at Stonehenge. They were left largely in their natural forms, tall and narrow, or broad and squat. Locals sometimes describe them as male and female stones, though that is a modern reading. Over the centuries, many stones were toppled, buried or broken up for building. One story told about the site is grim. A medieval man was found crushed beneath a falling stone, still carrying scissors and coins.

In the 1930s, the archaeologist Alexander Keiller bought much of the land at Avebury. He dug up buried stones and raised them again. Where stones were lost, he marked their places with concrete posts. You can still see them today. Close by stand more great monuments. Silbury Hill is the largest prehistoric artificial mound in Europe, a green, flat-topped cone made of chalk. West Kennet Long Barrow, an older chambered tomb, lies on the ridge above.

All of these sites belong to the same broad culture and the same long span of time. Earthen avenues once linked monuments to rivers and to each other. At Stonehenge, a banked route called the Avenue runs from the stones down toward the River Avon. Walking these lines today, you follow paths that people walked more than four thousand years ago. The chalk under the turf is the same chalk they dug.

A practical word. At Stonehenge, the visitor centre sits a couple of kilometres from the stones, with a shuttle or a footpath between them. Walking the path lets you see the monument appear slowly over the rise, as it would once have appeared. Visitors usually view the stones from a path around them. Special access inside the circle exists at some times, but it must be arranged in advance.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Look at the gaps where stones have fallen, and the lichen on the ones still standing. Listen to the wind again, and perhaps a skylark somewhere above the grass. Count the round burial mounds on the skyline around you. Each one is a grave, a memory, a small part of the same story. As you walk away, keep turning back. The circle changes with every step.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the path from the visitor centre to see stones appear.
Bring a windproof layer; the open plain gets cold fast.
Book special inner-circle access well in advance.
Pair Stonehenge with Avebury for a full day out.
Come early or late for softer light and fewer crowds.
