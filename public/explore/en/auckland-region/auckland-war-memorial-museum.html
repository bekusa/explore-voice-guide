The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the steps and feel the slope of the grass behind you. The ground here rises gently, then falls away towards the water. You are standing on the rim of an old volcano. The hill is called Pukekawa, and the white building in front of you sits on its crown, looking north over the harbour and the islands beyond.

This is the Auckland War Memorial Museum. In the Māori language its name is Tāmaki Paenga Hira. Tāmaki is the old name for this isthmus, the narrow neck of land where Auckland grew. The museum is one of the oldest public institutions in New Zealand. Its story began in the middle of the nineteenth century, in a small cottage, with a handful of specimens. Today it is three museums in one: a place of Māori and Pacific treasures, a natural history collection, and a memorial to the dead of war.

Look up at the front of the building. The style is neoclassical, which means it borrows its shape from the temples of ancient Greece. Tall stone columns hold up a heavy pediment, the triangular gable above the entrance. The building opened in 1929. It was paid for partly by public money raised to remember the soldiers of the First World War. So from the very start, this was both a museum and a monument.

Now turn around. On the grass in front of the steps stands the Cenotaph. A cenotaph is an empty tomb, a memorial to people whose bodies lie somewhere else. For New Zealand soldiers, that somewhere else was often very far away — Gallipoli in Turkey, the fields of France, North Africa. Every year on Anzac Day, the twenty-fifth of April, thousands of people gather here before sunrise. They stand in the dark, in silence, and wait for the first light over the harbour.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past. High on the building, carved in stone, are words taken from an ancient Greek speech. They come from Pericles, a leader of Athens, honouring his own city's war dead nearly two and a half thousand years ago. Part of the passage says that the whole earth is the tomb of famous men. The builders chose these words with care. They linked a young nation's grief to one of the oldest funeral speeches in Western history.

Walk inside and the mood changes. Behind the old stone shell, a modern atrium opens up under a wide, curved ceiling of timber. This addition arrived in the early twenty-first century. The old building and the new structure meet without hiding each other. You can see the original walls, and above them the soft ribs of the new roof. Light falls down through the space, and voices carry upwards.

On the ground floor are the Māori and Pacific collections, and this is the heart of the museum for many people. One of the most important pieces is Hotunui, a carved meeting house built in the late nineteenth century. A meeting house, or wharenui, is more than a building. Māori often describe it as the body of an ancestor. The ridge pole is the spine, the rafters are the ribs, and the carved front boards reach out like arms to welcome you.

If you step close, notice the carvings themselves. Faces with wide eyes, tongues pushed out in defiance, spiral patterns cut deep into the wood. Small pieces of shell sit in the eyes and catch the light. Each figure represents an ancestor of the people who built the house. Visitors are asked to treat the space with respect. It is still a living treasure, a taonga, not simply an object behind glass.

Nearby lies Te Toki a Tapiri, a great war canoe. It is carved from a single enormous tōtara tree, a native New Zealand timber prized for its strength. It dates from the early nineteenth century and is the last surviving war canoe of its kind from that era. Picture it on the water, filled with paddlers moving in rhythm. It is long enough that you need to walk its full length to take it in.

Upstairs the museum turns to the natural world. New Zealand split from other land masses long ago, and its wildlife developed in isolation. Birds took the place of large mammals. The most famous of these was the moa, a giant flightless bird that once walked these forests. It disappeared within a few centuries of human arrival. Its tall skeleton gives you a sense of how strange and how fragile this island world once was.

The top floor holds the war memorial galleries. After the Second World War, the building was extended to honour a second generation of the fallen. Here you will find the Halls of Memory. The names of New Zealanders who died in service are carved into the walls, row after row. Families still come to find a grandfather or a great-uncle. Some leave small red poppies tucked beside a name.

The galleries also hold aircraft, including a Spitfire, the British fighter plane, and a Japanese Zero fighter. Seeing these two machines in the same building is a surprise for many visitors. Once they were enemies in the sky. Here they rest under the same roof, quiet and still. It is a strong reminder that the museum tells the story of war from many sides, not only one.

When you step back outside, take a moment on the steps. The Auckland Domain spreads around you — old trees, playing fields, winding paths through native bush. This is the oldest park in the city. On a clear day you can see the harbour and the low green shape of Rangitoto, a younger volcano rising out of the sea. This whole city sits on a field of volcanoes, and you are standing on one of them.

So pause here before you walk on. Feel the wind coming up the hill from the water. Listen to the birds in the trees of the Domain, and the faint sound of the city below. Behind you are canoes, carved ancestors, and thousands of names in stone. In front of you is the living city they helped to shape. Carry a little of that quiet with you as you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with the Māori and Pacific galleries on the ground floor.
Save the war memorial floor for the end of your visit.
Walk around the building to see the harbour views.
Allow at least two to three hours inside.
Treat the Hotunui meeting house as a sacred space.
