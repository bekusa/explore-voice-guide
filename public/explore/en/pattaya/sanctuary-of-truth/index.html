The guide

The story

Listen first, before you look up. Somewhere nearby, a chisel is tapping into wood. It is a small, steady sound, almost like a clock. Under it, you can hear the Gulf of Thailand moving against the shore. The air smells of salt and fresh sawdust at the same time. And then, rising in front of you, is a building made almost entirely of carved wood. Spires, gables, figures, and flames of timber climb toward the sky. This is the Sanctuary of Truth.

The first surprise is simple. The building is not finished. It has never been finished. Construction began in 1981, and work continues to this day. Scaffolding often clings to one side or another. Carvers sit in open workshops near the entrance, shaping new panels by hand. Many visitors arrive expecting a temple or a museum. What they find is closer to a workshop that happens to be a monument. You are walking into a project that is still being made.

The idea came from Lek Viriyaphant, a Thai businessman with a deep interest in Asian art and philosophy. He is also known for Ancient City, a large open-air park near Bangkok, and for the Erawan Museum with its giant three-headed elephant. Here in Pattaya, his vision was different. He wanted a structure that presented ideas about human life, knowledge, and the universe. These ideas draw on several Eastern traditions. The building is meant to be read, almost like a book carved in wood.

Now look at the scale. The central spire rises more than one hundred metres above the ground. That is taller than many apartment towers in the city behind you. Yet nothing here is concrete or steel on the surface. The structure is held together largely with traditional wooden joinery, using pegs and interlocking pieces. The builders describe this as an old Thai method. Seen from below, the whole mass seems light, almost like lace, even though it is enormous.

The wood itself matters. The carvers use tropical hardwoods, including teak, chosen for strength and grain. Walk close to a pillar and notice the colour. Older sections have weathered to a soft grey or silver. Newer panels are warm brown or almost honey gold. The difference is not decoration. It is time. The sea air, the salt, the heat, and the monsoon rains all wear the wood down. So the sanctuary is always partly new and partly old, side by side.

This is the second surprise. The building is not only being finished. It is also being repaired, constantly. Panels that crack or rot must be carved again and replaced. Some staff describe the work as a cycle with no real end. A carver may spend weeks or months on a single figure. By the time one area is complete, another needs care. In a way, the sanctuary survives because people never stop working on it.

Each side of the building has its own character. The design brings together influences from Thai, Khmer, Chinese, and Indian art. Khmer refers to the ancient kingdom centred in what is now Cambodia, famous for Angkor Wat. You may notice towers that echo Khmer temple shapes, and faces and gestures drawn from Hindu and Buddhist imagery. The four main directions carry different themes. Together they form a kind of map of Eastern thought, laid out in timber.

Look at the figures. There are gods and goddesses, celestial dancers, mythical birds, and guardians. Some ride animals. Some hold weapons, flowers, or symbols of learning. You will see family scenes too, parents and children, which link the cosmic themes to ordinary life. A guide may explain that the carvings speak about creation, about the elements, and about the bond between people and the world. Even without the explanation, the sheer density of faces is striking. Every surface has someone looking back at you.

Step inside, and the light changes. Daylight falls through gaps in the carving and lands in thin shafts on the floor. The interior feels cooler and quieter. Your eyes follow pillars upward into a forest of beams and figures. The roof is so high that details near the top become hard to read. This is deliberate in many sacred buildings in the region. Height pulls the eye, and the mind follows it. Take a moment here and simply look up.

The setting is part of the experience. The sanctuary stands on a point of land at the northern edge of Pattaya, close to the Naklua area. The sea is right beside it. From the waterline, the building seems to rise directly out of the coast. Behind it is the modern city, with hotels, traffic, and neon. In front of it is open water. Few places in Pattaya show the old and the new in such a sharp contrast.

Visiting comes with a few rules. Because this is treated as a place of respect, shoulders and knees should be covered. Wraps are usually available at the entrance if you need one. In some areas, you may be asked to wear a hard hat. That is not for show. Work is happening overhead, and pieces of wood and tools are in use. Guided walks are common, and they help you understand the symbols you are seeing.

Before you leave, visit the carving workshop if it is open. Watch how a carver holds the chisel at a low angle and taps with a wooden mallet. Notice the drawings and templates pinned up beside the work. A rough block slowly becomes a hand, a face, a lotus petal. This is the human heart of the place. The sanctuary is not only a vision from one founder. It is the patient work of many hands, over many years.

So, as you stand here, listen once more. The chisel is still tapping. The waves are still moving against the rocks. Somewhere above you, a panel carved this year sits beside one carved decades ago. When you walk back toward the city, turn around once. Look at the grey and gold wood against the sky... and remember that the building you are leaving will look a little different the next time someone sees it.
