The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see a single bird, you will hear them. There is a dry clatter, like wooden sticks knocking together, as storks greet each other high in the trees. There is a low honking from the water, and a soft rush of wings overhead. The air smells of damp earth, reeds, and warm mud. Under your feet, the raised path is packed and dusty, and on either side the shallow water holds the reflection of the sky.

You are standing in Keoladeo National Park, on the edge of the town of Bharatpur in eastern Rajasthan. Many people in India still call it by its older name, the Bharatpur Bird Sanctuary. Locals often use a shorter word, Ghana, which means dense or thick. That word fits. Much of the park is a tangle of thorny acacia woodland, grassland, and marsh, all packed into a surprisingly small area.

Here is the first surprise. This famous wetland is largely made by people. The land sits in a natural depression, a low bowl in the plain where rainwater collects. In the late nineteenth century, the rulers of Bharatpur improved it with embankments, channels, and sluice gates. They wanted to hold monsoon water in place and attract ducks. The marsh you see is the result of careful engineering, not only of nature.

And the reason for that engineering was hunting. For decades, the maharajas of Bharatpur held grand duck shoots here. They invited British viceroys, visiting royalty, and important guests. The shoots became famous across India for their enormous numbers of birds killed in a single day. Near the centre of the park, a stone tablet records some of these hunts and their totals. Many visitors walk past it. Read it slowly, and then look up at the living birds around you. The contrast is hard to forget.

The story changed in the second half of the twentieth century. Hunting ended, and the area became a protected sanctuary. It was declared a national park in the early 1980s. In 1985, UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site, recognising its importance for migratory birds. It is also listed under the Ramsar Convention, an international agreement that protects wetlands of global value. A royal hunting ground became one of Asia's most important bird reserves.

The name Keoladeo comes from a small temple inside the park, dedicated to the Hindu god Shiva. The temple is modest, a quiet shrine among the trees, and it is still a place of worship. You may see local people making offerings there. It is a useful reminder that this landscape has always had a human meaning, long before anyone counted its birds.

Now, the birds themselves. The park is home to more than three hundred and fifty recorded species. Some live here all year. Others arrive in winter from Central Asia, Siberia, and beyond, flying thousands of kilometres to reach these warm, shallow waters. In the cooler months you may see huge rafts of ducks and geese on the open water, along with pelicans, cormorants, and herons. The quieter you are, the more you will notice.

Look up into the acacia trees standing in the water. If it is late in the monsoon or early winter, they may be crowded with nests. Painted storks are the stars here. They have pale bodies, pink-tinged feathers near the tail, and heavy yellow bills. Their nests sit close together, sometimes several in one tree, and the noise of the chicks begging for food carries across the marsh. Openbill storks, egrets, and darters often nest nearby.

Keep your eyes on the grassland too. This is a good place to look for the sarus crane, the tallest flying bird in the world. Sarus cranes stand roughly as tall as an adult person, with grey bodies and a bright red head. They usually stay in pairs, and pairs are known to stay together for many years. If you hear a loud, trumpeting duet, look for two cranes calling together with their heads raised to the sky.

Here is a sadder story, and one that many visitors do not know. Keoladeo was once the main winter home in India for the western population of the Siberian crane, a pure white crane with a red face. For years, small groups arrived every winter. Then their numbers fell, and by the early 2000s they stopped coming altogether. Their decline was caused by dangers along their long migration route. Their absence is still felt here.

The park is not only about birds. Watch the edges of the path for nilgai, a large antelope with a grey-blue coat. Spotted deer move through the woodland, and golden jackals sometimes trot along the embankments in the early morning. In winter, Indian rock pythons may lie in the sun near their burrows, which is why one part of the park is known as python point. Monitor lizards and turtles also live along the water's edge.

The way people move through this place is part of its character. Motor vehicles are kept out of the core of the park. Instead, visitors walk, ride bicycles, or travel by cycle rickshaw. Many rickshaw drivers here are licensed by the park and have learned to identify birds by sight and sound. Some carry binoculars and bird books. A good driver will stop quietly, point into a tree, and show you an owl you would never have found alone.

Water is the heart of everything here, and it is never certain. The wetland depends on monsoon rain and on water released from nearby dams and canals. In dry years, parts of the marsh shrink, and the birds are fewer. Park managers work hard to keep enough water flowing in. So the scene in front of you is not fixed. It changes with every season, and from one year to the next.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the light on the water, and the shape of the trees standing in it. Listen again for the clatter of storks and the call of a distant crane. As you walk back along the embankment, remember that every bird here chose this place, sometimes after crossing mountains and deserts. You came a long way too. For a short time, you share this marsh with them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come at dawn when birds are most active and noisy.
Hire a park-licensed rickshaw driver who knows the birds.
Rent a bicycle to explore the quieter side paths.
Bring binoculars for nests high in the acacia trees.
Visit in winter for the largest numbers of migratory ducks.
