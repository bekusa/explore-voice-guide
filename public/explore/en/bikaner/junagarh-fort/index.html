The guide

The story

Feel the heat rising from the ground before you even reach the gate. Here in Bikaner, the desert air is dry and bright, and the light falls hard on red sandstone. In front of you, long walls run along the flat land, broken by round bastions and a line of small battlements. There is no hill under this fort. That one detail sets it apart from many of the great forts of Rajasthan, and it is a good place to begin.

Most Rajput strongholds, built by the warrior clans of this region, sit high on rocky ridges, where the land itself does half the work of defence. Junagarh had no such advantage. It stands on the open plain of the Thar Desert. So its builders made their own protection — thick walls, heavy gates, and a moat that once circled the outer defences. Distance helped too. Any army that wanted this fort first had to cross the desert to reach it.

The city around you is older than the fort. Bikaner takes its name from Rao Bika, a Rathore prince who founded a kingdom here in the late fifteenth century. But the fort you see today came about a century later. It was built under Raja Rai Singh, who ruled Bikaner in the late sixteenth century. Construction is generally dated to the late 1580s, and the main work was finished in the early 1590s.

Rai Singh was not only a local ruler. He was also a trusted general in the army of Akbar, the Mughal emperor who controlled much of northern India at the time. That relationship matters here. It brought Rai Singh wealth, prestige, and contact with the Mughal court. You can see the result inside the walls — a mix of Rajput fortress building and the finer, more decorative taste of Mughal palaces.

Here is something many visitors miss. The name Junagarh simply means "old fort". It was not always called that. The fort was originally known as Chintamani. The new name came in the early twentieth century, when the royal family moved to a newer residence, Lalgarh Palace, on the edge of the city. Once there was a new palace, the old one needed a new name. And so this fortress became, quite literally, the old fort.

As you walk toward the inner palaces, pay attention to the gates. Each entrance is called a pol, the local word for a gateway. Suraj Pol, the Sun Gate, is one of the main entrances. Watch for the sharp turns in the path between the gates. They were not designed for comfort. They stopped elephants and soldiers from building speed for a charge, and they made attackers slow down under the walls.

Near one of the inner gates, you may notice small handprints set into the stone. Stop for a moment here. These prints are linked to the practice of sati, in which royal widows died on the funeral pyres of their husbands. The handprints are a memorial left by those women. It is a quiet, difficult corner, and many visitors walk past it without noticing. It is worth pausing to look.

Inside, the mood changes. The hard red exterior gives way to courtyards, carved balconies, and rooms filled with colour. Over the centuries, later rulers added their own palaces to Rai Singh's original fort. That is why the complex feels less like one building and more like a small walled town. Each generation built its own rooms, in its own style, around the courtyards.

The most richly decorated rooms show a local art form linked to the Usta craftsmen of Bikaner. Their work uses gold leaf on raised designs, often painted in deep reds and greens. In rooms such as Anup Mahal, the walls and ceilings shine with this gold work. The light from small windows moves across the gilded surfaces. Take your time here. The detail is easy to miss at first.

Now comes one of the nicest surprises in the fort. Find the room known as Badal Mahal, the Cloud Palace. Its walls are painted with blue clouds, and in some scenes, falling rain. Think about where you are standing. Outside these walls is one of the driest landscapes in India, where rain is rare. Inside, a ruler could sit among painted storm clouds. It is a desert dream of the monsoon, painted onto plaster.

The twentieth century also left its mark. Maharaja Ganga Singh, who ruled Bikaner for several decades in the early twentieth century, became one of the best-known Indian princes of his time. He served with British forces in the First World War, and he backed a major canal project that brought water to this dry region. One unexpected object in the fort's collection comes from his era — a First World War biplane, displayed indoors among the old weapons and royal objects.

The fort is also proud of its record. It is often said that Junagarh was never taken by direct assault — a remarkable claim for a fort on flat ground. Its strength came from good design, careful planning, and the hard desert around it. Today, the only crowds at the gates are visitors. You will hear guides calling out in many languages, the shuffle of feet on stone, and pigeons moving along the high ledges.

Before you leave, step back into an open courtyard and look up. Notice the carved stone balconies, the screens of lattice stone, and the small domed pavilions on the roofline. Feel how the stone holds the day's heat. As you walk back out through the gates and into the noise of Bikaner, turn around once more. Look at those long red walls on the flat desert ground — no hill, no cliff, just stone and the work of many generations.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning to avoid the strongest desert heat.
Carry water because the courtyards hold heat through the day.
Wear comfortable shoes for steep, narrow palace staircases.
Check whether photography requires a separate ticket at entry.
Pair your visit with nearby Lalgarh Palace on the same day.
