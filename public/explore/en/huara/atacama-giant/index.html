The guide

The story

The ground here crunches underfoot. It is a dry, gravelly sound, like walking on broken pottery. The air smells of dust and warm stone, and the light is so clear that distant hills look close enough to touch. Ahead of you, a low, bare hill rises from the flat desert floor. At first it looks like any other slope in this empty land. Then your eyes adjust... and a figure appears on its side, looking back at you.

This is the Atacama Giant. In Spanish it is called the Gigante de Atacama. It sits on the western face of a hill known as Cerro Unita, in the Tarapacá region of northern Chile. The small town of Huara lies a short drive away. The figure is a geoglyph — a large image made on the surface of the earth. It is often described as the largest prehistoric human-like figure in the world, though experts debate exactly how to measure such claims.

Take a moment to trace its shape. You can see a tall, rectangular body and a square head. Thin lines rise from the top of the head, like rays or a crown. Look for the short legs and the arms. Some viewers see objects held near the body. The figure is stiff and geometric, almost like a robot drawn by a child. That simple style is common in the rock art of this part of the Andes.

So how was it made? The method is simpler than you might expect. The desert surface here is covered with darker stones, coloured by long exposure to sun and wind. The makers moved these dark stones aside. This exposed the lighter ground underneath. Where they cleared stones, lines appeared. Where they piled stones, edges formed. No paint, no carving — just the careful arrangement of the desert itself.

Who made the Giant? It was created by Indigenous peoples of the Andes, centuries before the Spanish arrived in South America. Researchers generally place it roughly a thousand years ago, but the exact date remains uncertain. Stone figures are hard to date precisely, because there is often no organic material to test. What is clear is that the people of this region left many geoglyphs across these hills. The Giant is the largest and best-known of them.

Here is the first surprise. Archaeologists believe the Giant may have worked as a kind of calendar. One widely discussed theory links the rays on its head to the position of the moon as it moves across the sky. By watching where the moon set in relation to the figure, people could perhaps track the seasons. In a desert this harsh, knowing when the rare rains might come could decide whether a community survived. This idea is a theory, not a proven fact — but it is a compelling one.

Think, too, about the people who travelled through here. For centuries, llama caravans crossed this desert. They carried goods between the high Andes and the Pacific coast. Many geoglyphs in the region sit beside these old routes. They may have served as markers, as guides, or as messages to travellers. Standing here, you are near paths that people walked long before modern roads existed.

Now look around you, beyond the hill. This is the Atacama Desert, one of the driest places on Earth. Some weather stations in the wider desert have recorded almost no rain for many years. That dryness is the reason the Giant still exists. With little rain and few plants, nothing washes the lines away or grows over them. The desert has acted like a giant archive, keeping marks that would vanish anywhere else.

Here is the second surprise. The greatest threat to the Giant has come from modern visitors, not from nature. In past decades, off-road vehicles drove across parts of the hill and left tracks in the fragile surface. A single tyre track can last for centuries here, for the same reason the figure itself has lasted. For that reason, visitors should not climb the hill or walk on the figure. Please stay at the base and view it from a distance.

That distance actually helps you see it. The Giant was designed to be viewed from below and far away. Up close, it would look like scattered stones. From the flat ground in front, the whole shape comes together. Step back slowly and watch how the figure sharpens. Try a few different angles. The lines become clearer when the sun is lower and casts long shadows across the slope.

The Giant is part of a wider story in this region. Not far from here, along the main highway, you can find the old saltpetre works of Humberstone and Santa Laura. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, these towns mined nitrate, a mineral used for fertiliser and explosives. Today they are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In one afternoon, you can move from ancient Andean art to the ghost towns of an industrial boom. Both stories were preserved by the same dry desert air.

The area around the hill is quiet and open. There are few services nearby, so the experience feels raw and direct. You may hear only the wind and the tick of gravel moving under your shoes. The sun is strong, even when the air feels cool. The land stretches out in shades of tan, grey, and pale rose. It is easy to understand why people once looked at these hills and chose them as a place to leave a sign.

Before you leave, look at the Giant one more time. Notice the rays above its head and the straight lines of its body. Someone placed each of those stones by hand, with a clear plan and a reason that mattered to them. As you walk back across the gravel, listen to the crunch under your feet. You are leaving your own small marks on this ground, too... and in a place like this, marks can last a very long time.
