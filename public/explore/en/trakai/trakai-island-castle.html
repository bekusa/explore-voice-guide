The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against wooden planks under your feet, and somewhere across the lake a boat engine hums and fades. Ahead of you, red brick rises straight out of the water. It is a warm, rusty red, set on a base of grey fieldstone. On a still morning, the whole castle sits twice on the lake: once in the air, and once upside down in the mirror of the water below.

This is Trakai Island Castle, on Lake Galvė, a short drive west of Vilnius. Trakai was once one of the most important places in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. That medieval state was, at its height, among the largest countries in Europe. It stretched from the Baltic Sea far to the south and east. Trakai was not a quiet lakeside retreat. It was a seat of power.

The castle you see was built in the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. Work began under Grand Duke Kęstutis, a fierce defender of Lithuania against the crusading Teutonic Knights. That crusading order of German knights had pushed east into the Baltic lands for generations. Kęstutis's son, Vytautas, completed the castle. In Lithuania he is remembered as Vytautas the Great, and his name will follow you all around this town.

Think about the choice of site. An island is a natural moat. Attackers had no dry ground to stand on, no easy place for siege ladders. Lake Galvė holds many islands, and its water is cold and deep. For the people inside, the lake offered fish, protection, and a long view of anyone approaching. The castle did not need a wide ditch dug by hand. The lake did that work.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look closely at the walls as you cross toward the gate. Brick sits above stone, but the colours are not uniform. Some bricks are darker and rougher. Others are smoother and more even. That difference is not just weathering. It shows two different lives of the same building: the medieval castle, and the reconstruction that brought it back from ruin in the twentieth century.

Because yes — for a long time, this was a ruin. In the seventeenth century, wars with Muscovy brought heavy damage to Trakai. The castle lost its military role and slowly fell apart. Roofs collapsed. Walls crumbled into the lake. For generations, travellers came to see broken towers and grass growing on the stones. The romantic ruin became a favourite subject for painters and poets.

Rebuilding began in the twentieth century and continued for decades. Much of the work happened during the Soviet period, when Lithuania was part of the Soviet Union. The reconstruction was ambitious, and it still sparks debate among historians. Some argue it rebuilt too much, with too much guesswork. Others say it saved a national symbol. Today the castle houses a history museum, so the debate continues inside the very rooms it created.

The castle has two main parts. First comes the outer bailey, a wide courtyard ringed by defensive walls and towers. Then, across a second moat, stands the ducal palace with its tall central tower, called a donjon. A donjon is the strongest tower of a castle, the last place to hold out in an attack. Walk slowly between the two parts. The space narrows, and you can feel how the design controlled every visitor.

Vytautas the Great died here at Trakai in 1430. By then, the town around the lake had become something unusual in medieval Europe. Vytautas brought people from Crimea to settle here, including the Karaim. The Karaim are a small Turkic-speaking community with their own branch of faith, rooted in the Hebrew scriptures. Some served as guards. Their descendants still live in Trakai, more than six centuries later.

When you return to town, look at the wooden houses along the main street. Many Karaim homes face the street gable-first. Locals like to say they have three windows: one for God, one for the family, and one for Vytautas. It is a story, not a record, but people tell it with real pride. Nearby you can also find kibinai, crescent-shaped pastries filled with meat, a dish linked to the Karaim and now loved across Lithuania.

Inside the palace, the museum gathers objects from the castle's long story. Expect coins, weapons, tiles, and household items, alongside rooms that suggest the life of a ducal court. Pay attention to the light. Narrow windows let in thin bands of daylight across the brick floors. Even on a sunny afternoon, the inner rooms feel cool and heavy. That coolness is the thickness of the walls, still doing its old job.

In summer, the courtyard often fills with concerts and festivals, and the lake fills with boats. Yachts lean in the wind. Small rowing boats and kayaks drift past the walls. Seeing the castle from the water changes it completely. From a boat, the towers rise high above you, and you understand why an approaching army would hesitate. In winter, the lake can freeze, and people walk on the ice beneath the silent red walls.

Take a moment before you leave the island. Turn and face the shore. The wooden bridge carries a steady line of people, the same direction the dukes' guests once came. Beyond them, the town sits low among the trees and water. The castle you are standing in is part medieval, part twentieth century, and fully loved by the country that rebuilt it.

As you walk back across the bridge, listen again. The water still laps at the posts. The bricks behind you hold the warmth of the day a little longer than the air does. And when you reach the far end, look back once more. If the lake is calm, the castle will be there twice — once on the island, and once in the water, waiting for you to notice.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning for calm water and fewer crowds.
Rent a boat to see the walls from the lake.
Try kibinai pastries at a Karaim café in town.
Walk the main street to see the Karaim wooden houses.
Wear warm layers; the inner rooms stay cool year-round.
