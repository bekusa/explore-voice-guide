The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the open plateau, the wind moves over dry grass and low stone. Sheep bells sound somewhere in the distance. A rounded mound rises from the fields beside the village of Hisarköy. At first glance, it looks like a natural hill. It is not. Beneath your feet lie the layers of a city that was once one of the most important places in the Byzantine world.

This is Amorium. Today it sits quietly in the farmland near Emirdağ, in Turkey's Afyonkarahisar province. But for several centuries, its name carried weight in Constantinople and in Baghdad. Armies marched toward it. Emperors came from it. Chroniclers wrote about it in Greek and in Arabic. It is hard to imagine all of that while a tractor passes along the road. Yet that contrast is part of what makes this place so moving.

The story begins long before the Byzantines. People lived on this spot in the age of the Phrygians, the ancient Anatolian people remembered in legend through King Midas. The settlement grew through the Hellenistic and Roman periods. The city minted its own coins under Roman rule. An old tradition also links the fable-teller Aesop to Amorium. Historians treat that claim with caution, so think of it as a story the city told about itself, not a proven fact.

The real rise came later, in the Byzantine era. Amorium stood on important roads across central Anatolia. That position made it a natural military base. In time, it became the headquarters of the Anatolikon theme. A theme was a Byzantine military province, a district where soldiers held land and defended the frontier. So this was not a sleepy market town. It was a fortress city, guarding the eastern approaches to the empire.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the ninth century, a man from Amorium became emperor. He is known as Michael the Second, and his family is often called the Amorian dynasty. His son Theophilos and his grandson Michael the Third also ruled from Constantinople. For a few generations, the fortunes of the whole empire were tied to a family from this plateau. That fame would soon bring danger.

In 838, the Abbasid caliph al-Mu'tasim led a huge army into Anatolia. The Abbasids ruled a vast Islamic empire from Iraq. According to the chronicles, the campaign followed a Byzantine raid that had struck towns on the Abbasid frontier. Al-Mu'tasim chose Amorium as his target, partly because of its link to the ruling family. After a siege, the city fell. Much of it was burned, and many people were killed or taken captive.

The fall of Amorium echoed far beyond these fields. Among the captives were a group of Byzantine officers. Years later they were executed in the Abbasid city of Samarra after refusing to give up their faith. The Orthodox Church remembers them as the Forty-Two Martyrs of Amorium. On the Arabic side, the victory was celebrated in a famous poem by Abu Tammam. One city, two very different memories — triumph for one empire, grief for the other.

Amorium did not vanish after the siege. People returned and rebuilt. The city recovered and remained a regional centre for some time. But its later history grew quieter, especially after the Seljuk Turks moved into Anatolia from the late eleventh century. Slowly, the great walls crumbled. Earth and grass covered the streets. The village of Hisarköy grew up beside the ruins. Its name hints at a castle, a reminder that people always knew something old stood here.

Modern excavations began in the late 1980s, led first by the British archaeologist Martin Harrison and later by Christopher Lightfoot. Since then, international and Turkish teams have worked here season after season. The site is valuable for one simple reason. Unlike many Byzantine cities, Amorium was never covered by a modern town. That leaves rare evidence of daily life in the so-called Dark Ages of Byzantium, a period with few surviving written sources.

As you walk, notice the two parts of the site. The mound is the Upper City, the old citadel. Spread around it is the Lower City, once enclosed by its own defensive walls. Look for lines of cut stone along the edges of the fields. In places, the excavators uncovered towers, gates, and stretches of wall. Some blocks are clearly reused from older Roman buildings. Builders here often recycled what was already lying around.

One of the most important finds is a large church in the Lower City. Its foundations, columns, and floor patterns help archaeologists trace how the building changed after the siege of 838. Teams also uncovered a bath building and houses. They found coins, pottery, tools, and burnt layers from destruction. Ash in the soil is not dramatic to look at. But to an archaeologist, a thin dark band can mark one terrible day. Many of the objects from the site are now kept in the Afyonkarahisar Archaeological Museum.

Now look outward. The plateau stretches wide and flat in nearly every direction. In summer the fields turn gold and the light becomes hard and bright. In spring, the land is green and the air feels cool. There is very little shade and very little noise. You might hear birds, the wind, perhaps a dog in the village. That openness explains a lot. An army could be seen coming from far away, and a city on a mound could watch the roads.

This is not a site of grand reconstructed temples. Much of its story lies underground, and the visible remains are modest. That is exactly why it rewards a slow walk. Stop beside a fallen block and run your eye along its tool marks. Look at the mound and picture houses stacked across its slopes. Imagine market voices, church bells, soldiers on the walls. The silence today is part of the history too.

Before you leave, turn back toward the mound once more. The low hill against the sky holds Phrygian farmers, Roman citizens, Byzantine soldiers, an imperial family, and the survivors of a famous siege. Then look at the village, the fields, and the road that brought you here. Life still continues around Amorium, just as it did for thousands of years. As you walk away, listen again to the wind across the grass.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a hat and water; the plateau offers almost no shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and loose stones.
Visit Afyonkarahisar Archaeological Museum to see finds from the site.
Come in spring or early autumn for gentler temperatures.
Stay on paths and respect active excavation areas.
