The guide

The story

Start at the gate. Run your hand along the brick of the entrance tower and feel how uneven it is. Some bricks are worn smooth and others are still sharp at the edges. Above you, the tower rises in a solid red wall cut by narrow openings. Behind you, the old town of Lutsk falls away towards the river. Listen for a moment. You may hear pigeons in the upper windows, footsteps on the cobbles, and a voice drifting up from the streets below.

This is Lubart's Castle, also known as the Upper Castle of Lutsk. It stands on a raised piece of ground that was once almost an island. The Styr River and its smaller branches curved around it, and marshes filled the low ground. A moat added one more barrier. For anyone building a fortress in the Middle Ages, this was ideal ground. Water did much of the defensive work before a single brick was laid.

People fortified this site long before the walls you see today. The earliest defences were made of timber and earth. Wood was plentiful in this part of Volhynia — the historic region of northwestern Ukraine — and it was quick to build with. But wood burns. Over time, rulers of Lutsk wanted something more permanent, and the answer was brick and stone.

The castle takes its name from Lubart, a prince from the Lithuanian ruling family. He was a son of Gediminas, the Grand Duke of Lithuania, and he ruled Volhynia in the fourteenth century. In that era, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania was one of the largest states in Europe. It reached far to the south and east of modern Lithuania. Lubart made Lutsk his centre, and the stone fortress grew during his rule and in the decades after it.

Look at the walls as you walk the courtyard. The lower parts often show stone, and the upper parts show red brick. This mix is common in castles of the region. Brick was practical — it could be made locally from clay, and it was easier to shape than stone. The result is a fortress that looks warm in colour, almost soft, but was built to stop armies. The walls are thick enough that you can sense their weight just by standing beside them.

Three towers mark the corners of the castle. The entrance tower is the one you came through, and it is the tallest and most recognisable. Another tower is linked by name to Lubart himself. The third is called the Bishop's Tower, because the bishops of Lutsk once had their residence on the castle grounds. Each tower has its own shape and its own building history, so it is worth walking the full circuit to compare them.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Take out a Ukrainian two hundred hryvnia banknote, if you have one. On the back, you will see this very entrance tower. The front shows the poet Lesya Ukrainka, who was born in Volhynia. So the castle travels in wallets across the whole country. Hold the note up against the real tower and compare the two. It is a small moment, but people enjoy it.

Now for a story of real European importance. In 1429, Lutsk hosted a large gathering of rulers, often called the Congress of Lutsk. It was organised by Vytautas, the Grand Duke of Lithuania. Guests included the King of Poland and Sigismund of Luxembourg, who held the crowns of Hungary and Germany. They discussed politics, alliances and the threats facing Europe at the time. For a few weeks, this small city on the Styr was a meeting point for some of the most powerful people on the continent.

The castle also saw conflict. In the fifteenth century, struggles over who would rule Lithuania and its lands brought armies to these walls. Lutsk was besieged, and the fortress showed its strength. Later centuries brought slower changes. As warfare changed and the region passed between different states, the castle lost its military role. Parts of it fell into ruin. Other parts were adapted for church use and for daily life.

Inside the courtyard, look down as well as up. Archaeologists have excavated the foundations of an old cathedral here, dedicated to Saint John the Theologian. Its outline lies within the walls, and it reminds you that this was not only a fortress. It was also a religious and administrative centre. The bishop, the prince and the garrison all shared this enclosed space. Imagine the noise of horses, bells and builders crowded together within these few hundred metres.

Today, the castle is part of a protected historical and cultural reserve. Several small museums use the towers and buildings. Depending on what is open during your visit, you may find collections of bells, old printed books, or historic weapons. Some exhibitions need a separate ticket, so check at the entrance. The towers are worth climbing if access is open. From the upper levels, the view takes in the roofs of the old town and the green line of the river valley.

On some days the courtyard fills with music, markets or medieval reenactment events. Lutsk takes pride in this place, and local families use it as a meeting point as much as tourists do. On quiet mornings, though, the courtyard can feel almost empty. Then the main sounds are wind in the trees and the creak of an old door. Both experiences are worth having, if your schedule allows it.

Before you leave, walk down into the streets around the castle. The old town of Lutsk keeps churches, former monasteries and houses from many periods, and the castle once controlled them all. Turn back once or twice as you go. Watch how the red brick changes colour as the light moves across it. The towers will stay in view for a long time — the same towers that greeted kings in 1429, and that still stand over the river today.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full courtyard circuit to compare all three towers.
Climb a tower if open for views over the old town.
Visit early morning for a quiet, empty courtyard.
Ask at the entrance which museums need separate tickets.
Continue into the old town streets after your visit.
