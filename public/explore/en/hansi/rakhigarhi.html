The guide

The story

Listen first. A tractor coughs somewhere beyond the fields. Buffalo shift in a muddy pond. Children call out between brick houses. Under your feet, the ground rises in low, uneven humps of dusty soil. It looks like any farming village on the plains of Haryana. But scattered across the surface, you may notice small fragments of red pottery, worn smooth by rain and ploughs. Those fragments are thousands of years old. You are standing on one of the largest cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation.

The Indus Valley Civilisation, also called the Harappan Civilisation, flourished across what is now Pakistan and northwest India. Its mature phase reached its height roughly 4,500 years ago, in the third millennium before the common era. Its people built planned towns with baked-brick drains, standard weights, and carved stone seals. Most people have heard of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, both now in Pakistan. Fewer know that Rakhigarhi, here in Hisar district, is often counted among the very largest Harappan settlements anywhere. Some archaeologists argue it may rank at the very top.

So why does it look like this? Why no grand ruins, no towering walls? The answer is time and soil. Over thousands of years, mud-brick buildings collapsed and melted back into earth. Layer settled on layer. The result is a series of mounds, rising gently above the flat fields around them. Archaeologists number these mounds and study them one by one. Today the modern villages of Rakhi Khas and Rakhi Shahpur sit partly on top of the ancient city. People live, farm, and build directly above it.

That overlap is the first surprise for most visitors. In many countries, an ancient city of this importance would sit inside a fenced park. Here, the past and the present share the same ground. Houses stand on mounds that hide streets and workshops below. Cattle graze where excavators have dug. This creates a real tension. Protecting the site means asking hard questions about the lives of the families who call it home. It is a living village first, and an archaeological site second.

Serious excavation here began in the late 1990s, led by the Archaeological Survey of India. Later, from the 2010s, a team from Deccan College in Pune carried out major new seasons of digging. Together, these projects uncovered parts of a large settlement with a long history. The earliest layers seem to come from before the mature Harappan period. This suggests people lived here for many centuries, as a village slowly grew into a city. Rakhigarhi helps show that this civilisation had deep local roots in this region.

The finds tell a story of skilled, busy people. Excavators found streets, drains, and houses built from mud-brick and fired brick. They found pottery painted in red and black, terracotta figurines, and toy carts. They found the famous Harappan seals, small square stones carved with animals and with a script that nobody has yet deciphered. There is also evidence of craft work, including bead-making. Fine beads of semi-precious stone, shell, and copper suggest trade networks that stretched far beyond these fields.

Picture the city in its prime. Smoke rises from kilns at the edge of town. Craftsmen grind and drill tiny beads by hand. Carts with solid wooden wheels roll along packed-earth streets. Farmers bring in barley and wheat. Traders check goods against standard stone weights, the same system used in cities hundreds of kilometres away. That shared system is one of the most remarkable things about the Harappans. Across a huge area, they followed common rules for bricks, weights, and town planning.

Water shaped everything here. Scholars believe Rakhigarhi stood near an ancient river channel, part of the Ghaggar-Hakra river system. Today that system is mostly dry or seasonal in this area. Thousands of years ago, it likely carried far more water. Many researchers link changes in rivers and climate to the slow decline of Harappan cities. The civilisation did not end in one dramatic collapse. Instead, its big cities seem to have faded gradually, as people moved toward smaller settlements in new places.

Now the second surprise. Rakhigarhi has given us some of the most important scientific evidence about who the Harappans actually were. Archaeologists excavated a cemetery here and found many burials. The dead were often laid out with pottery placed beside them, and some with ornaments. One grave held a man and a woman buried together, facing each other. Nobody knows their exact relationship. But the image of two people placed side by side, thousands of years ago, stays with many visitors.

From one of these skeletons, a woman's, scientists managed to recover ancient DNA. This is very difficult in hot climates, where genetic material breaks down quickly. The results were published in 2019 in a major scientific journal. They suggested that this woman's ancestry came largely from ancient South Asian and Iranian-related populations. The study sparked wide debate in India about early population history. For the first time, a person from a Harappan city could speak, in a sense, through her genes.

In recent years, the government of India has named Rakhigarhi as one of several archaeological sites to develop for visitors. Work on a museum and visitor facilities has been part of that effort. What you find on the day may depend on the current state of these projects. Some excavation trenches may be covered or fenced. Others may be closed to protect fragile layers. Local guides or site staff can often explain which mounds are open and what has been found there.

As you walk, look at the ground with care. Pottery fragments lie all over the surface of the mounds. They are part of the site, so please leave them where they are. Notice the height of the mounds compared to the surrounding fields. That height is made of human history, built up over generations. Watch the village too. Women carry water and fodder. Men talk outside small shops. Daily life here continues on top of one of the oldest urban landscapes on earth.

Before you leave, stand still on one of the mounds for a moment. Look across the flat green and brown fields toward the horizon. The same sun rose over this plain when potters fired their kilns and children pulled toy carts through these streets. Feel the dust under your shoes. Listen again to the tractor, the buffalo, the voices. Then walk back slowly, and watch the ground as you go. Every step crosses a city that is still waiting to be fully understood.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in cooler winter months to avoid harsh summer heat.
Ask local guides which mounds and trenches are currently open.
Wear closed shoes for uneven, dusty mound surfaces.
Carry water and sun protection; shade is limited.
Respect villagers' homes and ask before photographing people.
