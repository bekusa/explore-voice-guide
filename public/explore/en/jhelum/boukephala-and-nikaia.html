The guide

The story

Stand near the bank of the Jhelum River and listen for a moment. Water moves past in a wide, brown sheet. Traffic hums somewhere behind you on the road. The air carries dust, diesel, and the faint mineral smell of river silt. It looks like an ordinary working river town. Yet somewhere along this stretch of water, more than two thousand years ago, a Macedonian king founded two cities and named one of them after his horse.

The river you are looking at had another name in the ancient world. Greek writers called it the Hydaspes. In 326 BCE, Alexander the Great reached this river with his army, after years of marching east from Macedonia, through Persia and over the mountains of what is now Afghanistan. On the far bank, an Indian king waited for him. The Greek sources call him Porus, and he had brought war elephants.

What happened next is known as the Battle of the Hydaspes. It is one of Alexander's most studied battles. According to the ancient historian Arrian, the river was swollen with rain, and a direct crossing in front of the enemy looked impossible. So Alexander used deception. For many nights his troops made noise and moved along the bank, until the other side stopped reacting to the false alarms.

Then, on a stormy night, Alexander took part of his army upstream. They crossed at a point hidden by a wooded island, while thunder and rain covered the sound. By morning they were on the other side. The battle that followed was hard and bloody. The elephants terrified the horses and trampled soldiers on both sides. In the end, the Macedonians won — but the ancient writers describe it as a costly victory.

Here is the first surprise. Porus did not lose his kingdom. The ancient accounts say that Alexander, impressed by his courage, asked how he wished to be treated. Porus is said to have answered, "like a king." Whether those were the exact words or a later polish by storytellers, we cannot know. But the historical outcome is clear enough. Alexander let Porus keep his lands, and even added territory to them, as an ally.

After the battle, Alexander ordered two new cities to be founded. One was Nikaia, a Greek name built on the word for victory. Arrian places it on the site of the battle itself. The other was Boukephala, also written Bucephala, placed near the point where the army had begun its crossing. Two cities, one on each side of the story — the place of the risk, and the place of the reward.

The name Boukephala comes from Bucephalus, Alexander's famous horse. The name roughly means "ox head". Bucephalus had carried Alexander since his youth, and he died around the time of this campaign. The sources do not fully agree on how. Arrian says the horse died of age and exhaustion, while other writers mention wounds from the battle. Either way, a king who founded cities named after himself also gave one to his horse.

The second surprise is about where these cities actually were. Nobody knows for certain. Historians and archaeologists have proposed several locations along the Jhelum. Some place Boukephala near the modern city of Jhelum, others near Jalalpur, further downstream. Nikaia has been linked with sites such as Mong, on the opposite side of the river. None of these suggestions has been confirmed by clear excavated remains. The cities are recorded in texts, but they have not been firmly found on the ground.

Why is it so hard? The answer is in front of you. The Jhelum is a moving river. Over two thousand years, it has shifted its course, flooded its banks, and laid down fresh silt again and again. Arrian himself notes that when Alexander later returned to this river, he had to repair the new cities, because heavy rains and floods had damaged them. The river that made these places important was also the force that could erase them.

This river was also a turning point in a larger sense. After the victory here, Alexander pushed further east. But at the next great river, the Hyphasis, now the Beas, his exhausted soldiers refused to go on. He turned back. And it was here on the Hydaspes that his fleet was built for the long voyage downstream towards the sea. In a way, the farthest reach of his ambition and the beginning of his return both pass through this valley.

As you look around, notice what still makes this location useful. The river is wide, but it can be crossed. To the west rise the low, dry ridges of the Salt Range. The plains open to the east. This has always been a natural gateway between the uplands of the northwest and the plains of Punjab. Not far from here stands Rohtas Fort, built in the sixteenth century by the ruler Sher Shah Suri to guard the same corridor. Armies, traders, and travellers kept choosing this crossing long after Alexander.

Today the modern city of Jhelum lives its own life. You will see bridges carrying road and rail traffic, markets, mosques, and a large military presence, which is part of the city's identity. The ancient names do not appear on many signs. There is no gate or column to photograph and say, "this is Boukephala." Instead, the history here is layered into the landscape itself — the river, the ridges, and the crossing.

So take a last look at the water. Watch how fast it moves near the middle, and how slowly it turns near the edge. Imagine the same river on a dark night of rain, with soldiers wading through it and elephants waiting on the far bank. Somewhere beneath this silt, or perhaps long washed away, lie two cities named for a victory and a horse. As you walk back towards the town, the river will keep running behind you, as it has since long before anyone gave it a name.
