The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the cobbles in front of the west wall. The stone in front of you is not grey. Look closely and it carries a cool blue-green tint, like river water under clouds. This is soapstone, a soft local stone that masons can carve almost like wood. When rain falls in Trondheim, the whole facade darkens and deepens in colour. On a dry day it turns pale and chalky. The building changes its face with the weather.

This is Nidaros Cathedral, the largest medieval building in Scandinavia and often called the northernmost medieval cathedral in the world. But its story does not begin with a building. It begins with a body. In 1030, King Olav Haraldsson died in battle at Stiklestad, a field to the north of here. He had worked hard to bring Christianity to Norway, and he had made many enemies doing it. His death changed everything.

According to the old sagas, his followers secretly buried him on the sandy bank of the river Nid, close to where you stand now. When the grave was opened about a year later, people said his body had not decayed. His hair and nails had kept growing. Treat this as medieval tradition, not documented fact... but the people of that time believed it completely. Olav was declared a saint. Soon after, pilgrims began to arrive from all over northern Europe.

A small wooden church rose first over the grave. Later, in the late eleventh century, work started on a stone church at the same spot. Over the next two centuries, builders kept expanding it. The older, rounder style with heavy arches gave way to Gothic, the style of tall pointed arches and large windows that came from France and England. You can still see both styles in the building today, side by side, like two generations standing together.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The east end of the cathedral has an eight-sided shape called the octagon. It sits directly over the place where tradition says Saint Olav was buried. The whole church, in a sense, grew outward from that one grave. Inside, there is also a well. Locals say a spring burst from the ground where the saint first lay, and that its water could heal. Whatever you believe, the well is real.

For centuries, Nidaros was the spiritual heart of Norway. It became the seat of an archbishop whose territory once reached far across the sea, as far as Iceland, Greenland, and the islands north of Scotland. Kings were crowned here. Pilgrims walked for weeks along mountain paths and coastal roads to reach the saint's silver shrine. Trondheim, then called Nidaros, was full of hostels, chapels, and travellers in worn shoes.

Then came fire, again and again. Over the centuries, flames swept through the cathedral several times, destroying roofs and bringing down parts of the walls. In the 1530s, the Protestant Reformation reached Norway. The shrine of Saint Olav was taken away, and his remains were buried somewhere in the church. Nobody knows exactly where. That is the second surprise of this place — the saint it was built for is still here, probably, but his grave is lost.

By the nineteenth century, much of the great church was a ruin, with only parts still in use. Then Norway began to rediscover its own identity after centuries of union with Denmark. The cathedral became a national symbol. In 1869, a long restoration began. It did not finish until 2001 — more than 130 years of work. Generations of stonemasons spent their whole careers here. Some sons continued what their fathers had started.

Now turn your eyes back to the west front. It is crowded with statues of saints, apostles, kings, and biblical figures, set in rows of niches. It looks ancient, but most of these figures were carved during the twentieth century, by many different sculptors. If you look carefully, you will notice that the faces and styles do not all match. Some feel stiff and medieval. Others feel almost modern. The wall is a gallery of a whole century of Norwegian art.

Above the main doors sits a large round rose window. When you step inside, you will see it from the other side, glowing with coloured glass. The interior is cool and dim, and your eyes need a minute to adjust. Notice how the long nave pulls your gaze toward the east end, toward the screen and the octagon beyond it. The stone carries the marks of many different hands and centuries of repair.

The cathedral still matters to Norway today. In 1991, King Harald and Queen Sonja received a royal blessing here, keeping an old link between the monarchy and this church. Next door, in the Archbishop's Palace, you can see the Norwegian crown regalia. And the pilgrims have come back. Each summer, walkers arrive along the restored Saint Olav's Ways, some after weeks on foot, often around Olav's feast day, the twenty-ninth of July.

Before you leave, walk slowly around the outside of the building. The churchyard is quiet, with old graves and tall trees. On the walls, look up for small carved heads and creatures tucked into corners and along the roofline. Some are grotesque, some are almost funny. Medieval masons and their modern followers liked to leave a little humour in the stone, high up where few people would ever look.

Take one more look at that blue-green wall. Somewhere beneath the floor lies a king whose death drew people across mountains and seas for five hundred years. You are standing where those pilgrims stood when they finally arrived. As you walk away, toward the river and the old wooden bridge, listen for the bells. They have marked time over Trondheim for a very long time... and they will ring long after we have gone.
