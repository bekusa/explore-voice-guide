The guide

The story

Listen first. Outside these walls, the wind runs across volcanic gravel and old ice, and it rarely stops. Step through the door and the sound drops away. The air is still and dry, and there is a faint smell of wood and warm radiators. Your boots may squeak a little on the floor. After the white glare outside, your eyes need a moment to adjust to the softer light inside. This is the Chapel of the Snows, at McMurdo Station on Ross Island, close to the bottom of the world.

It is a small building, and that matters. Most people expect a church at the end of the Earth to look dramatic. Instead, this one looks almost ordinary. It has a simple frame, a modest steeple, and pews for a few dozen people. It stands among warehouses, dormitories, and fuel lines. That plain quality is the point. The chapel was built for a working community, and it fits into the daily life of the station.

McMurdo is the largest research station in Antarctica. It is run by the United States, and it sits on the southern tip of Ross Island. Its population rises sharply in the southern summer, when scientists, pilots, cooks, mechanics, and support staff arrive. Many fewer people stay through the dark winter months. For all of them, this chapel is one of the few rooms on station that is not built for work. People come here to think, to be quiet, or simply to sit somewhere that feels different.

The story of the chapel begins in the mid-1950s. At that time, the United States Navy was building a base here as part of Operation Deep Freeze. This was a large logistics effort that supported research during the International Geophysical Year, a worldwide scientific programme. Navy construction crews, known as the Seabees, put up the first chapel in 1956. According to station history, they built much of it in their spare time, with spare and salvaged materials.

That first building did not survive. In 1978, a fire destroyed the original chapel. Fire is one of the most serious dangers in Antarctica. The air is extremely dry, and wooden buildings burn fast. Water for firefighting must be made from snow or ice, or stored carefully. A loss like this hits a remote community hard. After the fire, services continued in temporary space, and the station began planning a replacement.

The building around you now was dedicated in 1989. It is not the first Chapel of the Snows, but it carries the same name and the same purpose. Here is something many visitors do not realise. The chapel is non-denominational. It is not tied to one church. Over the years, it has hosted Catholic Mass and Protestant services, and it has also been used by people of other faiths and by people of no faith at all. In a small, isolated community, one shared room has to serve everyone.

Now look towards the windows. The chapel is known for its stained glass, which carries Antarctic imagery rather than the usual scenes of a European church. On a bright summer day, the sun stays up around the clock, and light comes through the coloured glass at every hour. In winter, the opposite happens. The sun disappears for months, and the windows go dark. The same window can look completely different depending on the season you visit.

That cycle of light shapes life at McMurdo. In summer, there is no night. People sleep with blackout curtains and lose track of the time. In winter, there is no day, and the station runs under artificial light. A room like this chapel gives people a fixed point. Some residents say it is one of the few places on station where time feels normal. Whether or not they pray, many people come here to reset.

The chapel also plays a role in community life. Over the decades, it has been a place for memorial services. Antarctica is a dangerous continent, and the station has lost people to accidents in the air, on the ice, and in the field. Weddings have reportedly taken place here too, which surprises many visitors. People do meet and fall in love at the bottom of the world. The chapel has also been used for quieter gatherings, such as music and small concerts.

Step back outside and turn around slowly. On a clear day, you may see Mount Erebus to the north. It is the southernmost active volcano on Earth, and it is often topped with a thin plume of steam or gas. Across McMurdo Sound, the Transantarctic Mountains rise on the far horizon. The air here is so clear that distances are hard to judge. A mountain that looks an hour's walk away may be many kilometres off.

This ground also carries an older story. Not far from the station, at Hut Point, stands the Discovery Hut. Robert Falcon Scott's expedition built it in 1902, during the heroic age of Antarctic exploration. Above the station rises Observation Hill, topped by a large wooden cross. It remembers Scott and the men who died with him on their return from the South Pole in 1912. Faith, loss, and memory have been part of this place for more than a century.

So the chapel belongs to a long line. The early explorers held services in their huts and on the ice. The Navy crews of the 1950s built a church with their own hands. Today's residents keep using it, season after season. Each group came for science or adventure, and each group still wanted a quiet room. That human need did not change, even in the coldest and driest place on the planet.

Before you leave, take one more look at the building itself. Notice how small it is against the hills and the sky. Notice how the steeple rises only a little above the roofs around it. Listen again to the wind, and feel the cold on your face after the warm air inside. Few people ever stand where you are standing now. As you walk back into the station, carry a little of that quiet with you.
