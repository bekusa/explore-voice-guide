The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes. Somewhere in the canopy, a koel calls in rising, insistent notes. Under that sound is something slower, the tide moving through the mangroves of the creek. The stone around you is dark and pitted, and thick roots grip it. This is Fort Vasai. For about two centuries it was one of the most important Portuguese strongholds on the western coast of India.

A quick note on geography before we begin. You may have travelled here from Mira-Bhayandar, which sits just to the south. The fort itself stands in Vasai, across Vasai Creek. That creek matters to the whole story. The water was the reason this place was built. It was the reason the fort was fought over. And it explains why the walls face the way they do.

The Portuguese called this town Baçaim. English writers later turned the name into Bassein. In 1534, Sultan Bahadur Shah of Gujarat signed a treaty that handed this coastal territory to the Portuguese. He was under pressure from the Mughals and needed allies at sea. The Portuguese soon began to fortify the site. Over the following decades, a walled city grew up here.

It is easy to picture a single castle. The reality was much larger. Inside these walls stood a full colonial town. There were streets, residences for nobles, offices, and several churches and convents run by different Catholic religious orders. Portuguese sources describe Baçaim as a wealthy place. It traded in timber, stone, and the produce of the fertile land around it. For a time, it ranked among Portugal's most valued possessions in Asia, alongside Goa.

Look at the walls as you walk. They follow an irregular, many-sided outline. At the corners they push outward into bastions, which are projecting platforms built for cannon. This design let defenders fire along the face of the wall at anyone who tried to climb it. The stone is local basalt, dark volcanic rock that is common across this region. Where the plaster has fallen away, you can see how the blocks were fitted together.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The fort had two main gates with very different purposes. One opened toward the water and the boats. The other faced the land and the roads inland. The sea gate let ships supply the garrison even during a siege on land. As long as the Portuguese held the sea, they could hold on. That single fact shaped the fort's long survival, and in the end its fall.

Now the ruins of the churches. Their stone facades still rise among the trees, though their roofs are long gone. Some walls still carry carved details and the outlines of arched windows. Look for the bell towers and the deep niches where statues once stood. Sunlight now falls straight onto the old church floors. Plants grow from the cracks. The effect is quiet and a little strange, a European church plan open to a tropical sky.

Locals say the great missionary Saint Francis Xavier passed through Bassein in the sixteenth century. The story is told with pride by the area's Catholic community, whose roots go back to the Portuguese period. Many families in Vasai still keep that heritage today, in their names, their village churches, and their festivals. So the fort is not only a ruin. It is linked to a living community that still lives around it.

The turning point came in the eighteenth century. The Maratha Empire, the great Hindu power of western India, was expanding toward the coast. In 1739, Maratha forces led by Chimaji Appa laid siege to the fort. Chimaji Appa was the younger brother of the Peshwa Bajirao, the Maratha chief minister. The siege was long and costly for both sides. Mines were dug beneath the walls. In the end, the Portuguese garrison surrendered, and Portuguese rule here came to an end.

That victory is still remembered in Maharashtra as a great military achievement. As you walk, you may notice small shrines and signs of devotion inside the walls. These belong to the Maratha period and to later local worship. The fort carries both layers at once. Portuguese church facades stand only a short walk from Hindu temple stones. Neither layer has fully erased the other.

The British came next. In the late eighteenth century, during wars with the Marathas, British forces took control of the fort. The town's English name also entered the history books in another way. In 1802, the Treaty of Bassein was signed between the British and the Peshwa. Historians often see it as a key step toward British dominance in western India. The name of this quiet riverside town became tied to a turning point for the whole subcontinent.

Today the fort is a protected monument. Much of it is in ruins, and nature has moved in. Banyan and peepal trees push their roots between the stones. Monkeys and birds are common. Film crews have used these ruins as a backdrop many times, because the arches and overgrown walls look dramatic on camera. On a weekday morning, though, you may find long stretches where you are almost alone.

A few practical thoughts. The ground is uneven, and old stairs can be narrow and broken, so wear sturdy shoes. Carry water, because shade is patchy and the coastal humidity builds quickly. In the monsoon the whole site turns deep green, but paths can get slippery and muddy. Early morning and late afternoon bring the softest light on the basalt. They also bring the coolest air.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the walls that face the creek. Look out at the water and the mangroves. This was the view that mattered to every power that held this place. It mattered to the sultan who gave it away, to the Portuguese who built it, to the Marathas who took it, and to the British who followed. The stones are quiet now. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again for the koel, and the slow tide moving under it.
