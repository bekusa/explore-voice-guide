The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the grass outside, and listen. You may hear the Allan Water running below the town, rooks in the old trees, and the soft crunch of gravel as someone walks the path. In front of you rises a long building of grey and honey-coloured stone. It is weathered and quiet, and it is still very much alive. This is Dunblane Cathedral, and people have worshipped on this small rise for well over a thousand years.

The story begins with a name. Dunblane is usually explained as the hill fort of Blane. Saint Blane was an early Christian missionary linked with the Isle of Bute, off Scotland's west coast. Tradition places his work here in the early medieval period, long before any of the stone you see today. Very little about his life is certain. What lasts is the memory of a holy man and a sacred site. Later generations chose to build on that site again and again.

Now look at the tower. It does not quite line up with the rest of the building, and that is your first clue. The lower part of the tower is the oldest standing structure here. It is plain, solid Romanesque work. Romanesque is the round-arched style of building that spread across Europe before the Gothic period. Historians believe this tower once stood on its own, as a separate structure. The church you see now grew up around it, and the tower was later folded into the side of the nave.

Look higher up the same tower and the stonework changes. The upper stages were added much later, in the late medieval period. Builders raised the height and gave it a different finish. So in one tower you can read two very different eras. The lower stone is older and heavier. The upper stone is lighter and more confident. It is a small history lesson made of masonry, and most visitors walk straight past it.

The main body of the cathedral is mostly thirteenth century work. Much of the credit goes to Bishop Clement. He took charge of the diocese at a time when it was poor and neglected. Under his leadership the church was rebuilt in the Gothic style. Gothic means pointed arches, tall windows, and walls that open up to let in light. Walk along the nave and notice how the arches lift your eye upward. The rhythm of the arcades is calm and even.

Now walk round to the west end, the gable facing away from the altar. Here is one of the cathedral's quiet treasures. High in the wall is a pointed oval window, framed with carved leaves. The Victorian art critic John Ruskin admired this west front very much. Because of his praise, the oval window is often called the Ruskin Window. It is a modest detail on a modest wall. Yet many people consider it one of the finest pieces of design in the whole building.

The Reformation of 1560 changed everything here. Scotland broke with the Roman Catholic Church, and cathedrals lost their bishops, their wealth, and often their purpose. At Dunblane, the eastern part of the church, the choir, stayed in use for worship. The great nave was left without care. Over time its roof was lost and it stood open to the sky. For roughly three centuries, the space you may be standing in was a roofless shell of walls and weather.

Rescue came in the late nineteenth century. The architect Robert Rowand Anderson led a major restoration and gave the nave its roof once more. Look up at the timber ceiling. It is part of that Victorian revival. Later, in the early twentieth century, the architect Robert Lorimer carried out fine work in the choir, including carved woodwork. So the building you see is not frozen in one moment. It is medieval stone, repaired and loved by Victorian and modern hands.

In the choir, look for the old oak stalls with their dark, polished surfaces. These are among the oldest pieces of furniture in the cathedral. They are usually linked to Bishop James Chisholm, who led the diocese around the turn of the sixteenth century. Lean in close, if you are allowed, and study the carving. The wood carries the marks of tools from more than five hundred years ago. It also carries the polish of countless hands and sleeves.

Now a story that sits between history and legend. In the choir floor are three slabs of blue-grey stone. Tradition says they mark the graves of Margaret Drummond and her two sisters. Margaret was a close companion of King James the Fourth of Scotland. The three sisters died suddenly in 1502. Local tradition says they were poisoned, perhaps to clear the way for a royal marriage. Nobody has proved this. But the slabs remain, and the tale has survived for five centuries.

There is also a far more recent memory in this building, and it deserves a gentle voice. In 1996, sixteen children and their teacher were killed at the town's primary school. The whole of Scotland grieved with Dunblane. Inside the cathedral, a carved standing stone remembers them. Many visitors pause here in silence. You may wish to do the same. It is a reminder that this is a living parish church, and that it holds the town's sorrows as well as its celebrations.

When you step back outside, take a short walk around the cathedral precinct. The streets nearby are narrow and built of old stone. Close by is the Leighton Library, founded with the books of a seventeenth century bishop, Robert Leighton. It is often described as one of the oldest private library buildings in Scotland. Follow the slope down towards the river. From below, you can see how the cathedral sits on its rise, just as the old name suggests.

Before you go, turn once more to face the building. Notice how the light moves across the stone. In sunshine it looks warm and golden. Under cloud it turns soft and grey. Look at the tower again, with its older base and later top. Think of Saint Blane, of Bishop Clement, of the roofless centuries, and of the town that still gathers here. Then listen again... the river, the rooks, the gravel underfoot. The place is still here, and so are you.
