The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, a group of friends is laughing, the half-embarrassed kind of laugh that breaks out when nobody knows quite where to look. Under your feet, a path winds between clipped lawns and dark pines. The air smells of cut grass and warm stone. Then you turn a corner and meet your first sculpture. And you understand the laughter at once.

Welcome to Love Land, an outdoor sculpture park on the edge of Jeju City. Its subject is human intimacy, in every form, size and pose an artist can imagine. The park is for adults only, and it makes no apology for that. Yet most people here are not shocked for long. Within a few minutes, the mood usually changes from surprise to curiosity, and then to something close to delight.

To understand why a park like this exists on Jeju, you need to know a little about the island's past. For several decades in the late twentieth century, Jeju was the favourite honeymoon destination for South Korean couples. Overseas travel was difficult or restricted for many Koreans, so newlyweds came here instead. The island was warm, green and far from home. It felt almost foreign, yet it was still part of the country.

Many of those couples had met through arranged introductions. Some barely knew each other on their wedding day. Korean society at the time was quite conservative, and open talk about romance or the body was rare. The story locals like to tell is that Jeju became the place where shy young couples began to learn about each other. Love Land plays openly on that memory. It is, in a sense, a cheeky monument to the honeymoon island.

The park opened in 2004. It grew from the work of art students and graduates from Hongik University in Seoul, one of the country's best-known art schools. That origin matters. These are not cheap souvenir figures made in a factory. Many of the works show real skill in carving, casting and composition. Take a moment to look past the subject and notice the craft — the polish on the stone, the balance of the forms, the way some figures seem caught in motion.

As you walk, notice how varied the styles are. Some sculptures are realistic and almost classical, like figures from a European garden. Others are abstract, all curves and swelling shapes that only suggest the body. Some are cartoonish and silly. A few are clearly meant as jokes, and they work best when you read them that way. The mix gives the park its rhythm: a serious piece, then a playful one, then something that simply makes you shake your head.

Here is something many visitors miss. Humour is the real language of this place. Several works invite you to touch them, sit on them, or pose with them. That is deliberate. The artists wanted to take a subject that was usually hidden and make it something people could laugh about together in daylight. Watch the other visitors around you. You will see couples teasing each other, groups of friends daring one another, and older visitors who are clearly enjoying themselves more than anyone.

Look too at the setting itself. The paths, hedges and trees are carefully kept, like a city park or a botanical garden. This gentle, ordinary landscaping is part of the joke. A bench under a pine tree, a neat flower bed, a quiet lawn — and then, standing in the middle of it all, a sculpture you would never see in an ordinary park. The contrast between the calm setting and the bold art is what makes so many people stop and stare.

Jeju has its own long tradition of stone carving. Across the island you will see dol hareubang, the stone grandfather figures carved from dark volcanic rock. They stand at gates and roadsides, and people traditionally saw them as guardians. Jeju is also known for older beliefs linked to fertility and family. So in a strange way, Love Land is not completely out of place here. It takes an island used to carved stone figures and gives that habit a modern, mischievous twist.

Beyond the outdoor garden, there is usually indoor space as well, with smaller works, displays and a gift shop. The shop is worth a quick look, if only for the creativity of the souvenirs. Some are clever, some are simply absurd. Many visitors leave with a small, slightly embarrassing gift for a friend back home. It is one of the few museum shops in the world where the people at the counter often seem to be trying not to laugh.

A word on how people experience this place. For some visitors, it is pure comedy. For others, it is a surprisingly thoughtful look at how a society talks, or does not talk, about the body. South Korea has changed fast in recent decades, and attitudes have changed with it. Love Land sits somewhere between those two worlds. It belongs partly to an older, shyer Korea, and partly to a newer, more relaxed one that can laugh at itself.

If you are visiting with a partner, this is an easy place to break the ice. If you are with friends, expect a lot of photographs and a few running jokes for the rest of the trip. And if you are here alone, you may find it the most relaxed way to watch people anywhere on the island. Everyone here shares the same small secret: they all chose to come, and they all know exactly why.

Before you leave, stop for a moment on the path. Listen again to the laughter drifting between the trees. Feel the sun, or perhaps the island wind coming in from the sea. Look around at the lawns, the pines and the strange stone crowd standing among them. As you walk back toward the gate, notice how lightly people move here — a little pinker in the face, perhaps, and almost certainly smiling.
