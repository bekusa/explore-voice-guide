The guide

The story

The captain cuts the engine, and the boat glides on silence. Below the hull, through water so clear it hardly seems present, shapes slide past — a straight wall, a flight of steps, the square corner of a room. Above the surface, the same masonry continues up the island's shore, half in the sea and half out. It takes the mind a moment to accept what the eyes report. You are floating over the streets of a drowned town.

This is the sunken city of Kekova, strung along the northern shore of Kekova island, opposite the fishing villages of Üçağız and Kaleköy. On the island's rocky flank you can trace house foundations, stairways, quays, and doorways at every level — some high and dry, some at the waterline, some fully submerged. Purple-tinged staircases descend from the rock straight into the blue and keep going, as if the residents might climb out dripping at any moment.

The culprit was the restless ground itself. This coast belongs to an active earthquake zone, and ancient writers and modern geology agree that the land here has shifted repeatedly. Earthquakes in the second century are generally blamed for dropping this shoreline by several metres, sliding the lower town beneath the sea. What had been a working harbour settlement — warehouses, homes, landing stages serving ships on the Lycian coast — became a reef with architecture.

Lycia, by the way, is the name to hold onto here — the ancient seafaring region of this coastline, famous for its tombs and its fiercely independent cities. The Kekova roadstead, the sheltered channel between island and mainland, gave Lycian sailors calm anchorage in all weathers, exactly as it now shelters your tour boat. Good harbours never go out of fashion. Only their owners change.

Protection today is firm. Kekova and its waters were declared a specially protected area in 1990, and swimming, snorkelling, and diving are banned over the sunken ruins themselves to guard them from souvenir hunters and anchor damage. Boats keep to marked approaches. You view the drowned walls through clear water or glass-bottomed panels, and swim stops happen in permitted bays nearby. The rules keep the place readable for the next century of visitors.

Most tours pair the island with Kaleköy, the tiny village stacked on the mainland shore opposite — and Kaleköy deserves equal billing. It sits on ancient Simena, and no road reaches it even now. Everything arrives by boat or on foot. Stone houses, pomegranate trees, and pensions cling to the slope beneath a small medieval castle, whose walls rise from foundations laid in antiquity. The climb up is short, sweaty, and worth it.

Inside the castle waits one of the coast's most charming finds — a tiny theatre cut directly into the living rock, with seven rows of seats and room for perhaps three hundred people. It is usually called the smallest theatre in Lycia. Whatever was staged here, the audience already had the best view on the coast: the red roofs below, the channel, the island, and yachts idling where triremes once anchored.

Down at the water's edge, Kaleköy keeps its signature image. A Lycian sarcophagus stands in the shallows just off the shore, waves lapping at its base, its arched lid pointed at the sky. It has become the emblem of the whole region — the tomb in the sea. Around the village, more sarcophagi lean among olive trees and back gardens. At neighbouring Üçağız, ancient Teimiussa, a whole necropolis of them crowds the shoreline behind the harbour.

Üçağız itself is the usual starting point — a small village of jetties, fish restaurants, and lanes where chickens outnumber cars. From here you choose your craft. Big wooden gulets circle the island with lunch on board. Small motorboats run flexible hops. Sea kayaks, best of all for the quiet-minded, let you paddle the shoreline at eye level with the ruins, silent enough to hear fish break the surface. Morning kayaking, on glassy water, is the connoisseur's choice.

Give some attention to the water column itself. In the shallows, the seabed shows amphora shards and worked stones furred with sea grass. Cormorants dry their wings on rocks that were once rooflines. In summer the channel glitters with light bouncing between island and mainland. It is a working seascape too — fishing boats still set out from Üçağız at dawn, as boats have from this exact anchorage for thousands of years.

If time allows, stay for the evening. When the day boats leave, Kaleköy returns to the population of a large family dinner. Restaurant platforms glow over the dark water, grilled fish arrives with lemon, and the castle floats above in the last light. Sleeping in a village with no road, ancient stones underfoot, is the kind of travel memory that outlasts whole itineraries.

The word Kekova today names the island, the channel, and the whole bay region. Yachts cruising the Lycian coast treat it as a favourite overnight anchorage, dropping anchor in quiet inlets around Üçağız much as trading ships once did. In winter the villages shrink back to their own company, fishing and mending nets, while herons stalk the shallows above the drowned walls. The ruins do not mind the off season. They have been waiting in this water, patient as stone, for nearly two thousand years.

Before you head back, look once more over the side of the boat. A staircase runs down through the sunlight into deep blue and simply keeps going. Whoever cut those steps expected feet, errands, ordinary days. The sea took the town but kept the shapes, holding them in cold clear storage. Few places let you look so directly at how provisional a shoreline really is — and how gracefully it can drown.
