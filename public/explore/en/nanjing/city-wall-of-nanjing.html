The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the wall. The brick is cool, slightly rough, and the colour of weak tea. Run your fingers along the surface and you may feel shallow grooves pressed into the clay. Those grooves are letters. They were stamped into this brick more than six hundred years ago, and they are still here, waiting for someone to read them.

You are standing beside the City Wall of Nanjing. It is one of the longest and best-preserved city walls ever built in China. It was not built to look pretty. It was built to make a new dynasty feel safe. And for a few decades in the fourteenth century, this river city on the Yangtze was the capital of the most powerful state in East Asia.

The man behind the wall was Zhu Yuanzhang. He was born into poverty, worked as a monk, then became a rebel leader during the collapse of Mongol rule. In 1368 he founded the Ming dynasty and took the throne as the Hongwu Emperor. He chose Nanjing as his capital. Even before he declared the new dynasty, work on the defences had already begun. The full project took roughly three decades to complete.

Here is the first surprise. Most Chinese capitals were laid out as neat rectangles, following old ideals of order and symmetry. Nanjing's wall ignores that rule. It bends and twists. It follows the hills, the lakes, and the river. The builders used the land itself as part of the defence. So when you look at a map, the wall looks less like a box and more like an irregular loop drawn by hand.

There was not just one ring. The Ming capital had layers. At the centre stood the palace city. Around it was the imperial city. Then came this great brick wall, the capital city wall, which ran for more than thirty kilometres. Far beyond it was an outer wall, built mostly of packed earth, which has almost entirely disappeared. Today about two thirds of the brick wall still stands, which is a remarkable survival for a structure this old.

Now, back to those letters on the bricks. This is the detail most visitors walk past. Each brick was made under a strict system of responsibility. The stamps often record the province or prefecture that produced it, along with the names of officials, supervisors, and sometimes the individual brick maker. If a brick cracked or failed, the authorities knew exactly whom to blame. In a sense, every brick here is a signed document.

The bricks came from a wide region, mostly from kilns spread along the Yangtze River and its tributaries. They were shipped downstream by boat to the capital. Think about the scale of that effort... millions of bricks, each one fired, stamped, inspected, and carried by human hands. Many of the workers were conscripted labourers. The wall was a public work, but it was also a heavy burden on ordinary families across the empire.

What holds the bricks together is another quiet surprise. Builders in this period sometimes mixed lime with a soup made from sticky rice. Modern scientists have studied this kind of mortar and found that the rice gives it extra strength and flexibility. It helps explain why some Ming structures have survived earthquakes, storms, and wars. So the wall that protected the emperor was partly glued together with the same grain that fed his people.

There is also a famous story connected to the wall, though you should treat it as legend. Locals tell of Shen Wansan, a fabulously wealthy merchant. According to the popular tale, he offered to pay for a large part of the wall, and the emperor grew jealous of a subject who seemed richer than the throne. The details change from teller to teller. Historians doubt much of it. But the story shows how people remembered the power, and the suspicion, of this emperor.

If you visit the southern side of the city, look for Zhonghua Gate. It is one of the largest surviving gate complexes of its kind in China. Instead of a single doorway, it has a series of enclosed courtyards, one behind another. An enemy who broke through the first gate would find himself trapped in a stone box, with defenders above. Inside the thick walls are vaulted chambers, which could store supplies or shelter soldiers waiting for an attack.

Along the northern stretches, the mood changes. The wall runs beside Xuanwu Lake, and on clear days Purple Mountain rises in the distance. Here the wall feels less like a fortress and more like a long terrace above the water. Joggers pass. Older residents walk slowly with their hands behind their backs. In spring and summer the lake below fills with lotus leaves, and the air smells of damp earth and green water.

The wall also carries the marks of later centuries. Some sections were damaged in wars and political upheavals. Some were taken down as the modern city grew. In the late twentieth century, people in Nanjing worked to repair and protect what remained. When you walk the top, you will notice that some bricks look newer and more even. Those are repairs. The older bricks are darker, softer at the edges, and often pitted by weather.

As you walk, try to slow down. Listen to the traffic below, and notice how the thick brick seems to soften the noise. Look for a weathered stamp on a brick at knee height. Watch how the light falls across the parapet, turning the grey to gold late in the day. Six centuries ago, soldiers stood where you are standing, watching the same horizon. Today the wall guards nothing... and yet it still holds the city together, one signed brick at a time.

Successive enclosed courtyards behind the southern gate.
Crenellated parapet edges along the top walkway.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the top near Xuanwu Lake for lake and mountain views.
Visit Zhonghua Gate to see the layered courtyard defences.
Come in late afternoon when low sun warms the brick.
Wear comfortable shoes for long, uneven walkway sections.
Bring water in summer; the wall top has little shade.
