The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the trees on Purple Mountain, the city of Nanjing sounds far away. Your footsteps fall on old stone, worn smooth and slightly uneven. In spring the air may carry the sweet scent of plum blossom. In autumn, ginkgo leaves turn the path a deep, buttery yellow. You are walking toward the grave of a man who began life as a poor peasant and ended it as an emperor. This is the Xiao Mausoleum, known in Chinese as Ming Xiaoling.

The man buried here is Zhu Yuanzhang. History knows him as the Hongwu Emperor, founder of the Ming dynasty. The Ming ruled China for almost three centuries, from the late fourteenth century to the middle of the seventeenth. Zhu was born into deep poverty. As a young man he lost much of his family to famine and disease. He spent time as a novice monk and as a wandering beggar. Then he joined a rebel army, rose through its ranks, and drove the Mongol Yuan dynasty from power.

Zhu chose Nanjing as his capital. He also chose this mountain for his tomb. Work began in the early 1380s. His wife, Empress Ma, died in 1382 and was buried here first. Zhu joined her in 1398. The word Xiao in the tomb's name means filial devotion, the respect a child owes to parents. It also echoes the posthumous title of Empress Ma. So the name honours both of them together, side by side inside the mountain.

The mountain itself matters. Purple Mountain, also called Zhongshan, rises on the eastern edge of the city. Its name comes from the purple and gold glow that its slopes take on at certain hours of the day. Chinese tradition held that a good burial site should have mountains behind it and water before it, protecting and nourishing the spirit. Zhu's builders placed the tomb on the southern slope, facing the sun, with the high ridge guarding its back.

Now to the part of the site that surprises many visitors. The Sacred Way, the ceremonial road that leads toward the tomb, does not run straight. In most imperial tombs, the spirit road is a single, formal line. Here it bends and curves around the foot of a small hill. That hill is often called Plum Blossom Hill today, and it holds the grave of Sun Quan, a ruler from the third century who also made this region his base.

Legend has it that the builders wanted to remove Sun Quan's grave to make way for a straight road. According to the popular tale, Zhu Yuanzhang refused. He said that Sun Quan was a brave man in his own time, and that he could stay and guard the gate. Historians point out that the curving path also follows the natural landscape. Either way, the result is a road that feels less like a parade ground and more like a walk through a garden.

Along one stretch of the Sacred Way stand the stone animals. There are six kinds: lions, camels, elephants, horses, and two mythical creatures. One is the xiezhi, a single-horned beast believed to tell right from wrong. The other is the qilin, a gentle animal linked with wise rulers and good fortune. There are four of each kind. For every animal, one pair stands upright and one pair kneels or rests. Some say this was so that one pair could keep watch while the other rested, through day and night.

Look closely at the elephants. They are among the largest carvings on the road, and each one was cut from a single block of stone. Their surfaces are rough now, softened by more than six hundred years of rain. Children often climb and touch them, and generations of hands have polished certain spots smooth. Further on, the road turns. Tall carved columns appear, followed by stone generals in armour and civil officials holding tablets, standing in silent service to their emperor.

Beyond the Sacred Way, the main tomb complex begins. Much of what you see is the stone and brick bones of the original buildings. The great wooden halls that once stood here were badly damaged in the wars of the mid nineteenth century, especially during the Taiping uprising. What survives are foundations, terraces, gateways, and stone bases. In some places, newer halls stand on the old platforms. The empty spaces between them help you imagine the scale of what once rose here.

Near the entrance area you will find a square, roofless stone pavilion. Inside sits a giant tortoise carrying a tall stele, an engraved stone tablet. It was raised by Zhu's son, the Yongle Emperor, to praise his father's achievements. The pavilion once had a roof, and today the sky shows through above it. Elsewhere on the grounds stands another tablet linked to the Kangxi Emperor of the later Qing dynasty. He visited this tomb and honoured the Ming founder here, even though his own dynasty had replaced the Ming.

At the far end rises the Square City, a massive fortress-like brick structure, with the Ming Tower on top. A sloping tunnel passes through it and climbs toward the burial mound behind. The mound is a large, round hill covered with trees. Here is a detail most visitors miss. The tomb chamber has never been opened by archaeologists. Surveys suggest that it may still be intact, and the emperor and empress are believed to rest undisturbed beneath your feet.

This tomb shaped much of what came after. Its layout, with a long spirit road, stone guardians, and a round mound behind a fortified tower, became the model for later imperial tombs. The famous Ming Tombs near Beijing, and many Qing tombs too, follow patterns first set here in Nanjing. In 2003, the Xiao Mausoleum was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, as part of a group of imperial tombs from the Ming and Qing dynasties.

Before you walk on, pause wherever you are standing. Notice the colour of the bricks, a faded red against the green of the mountain. Notice the moss in the cracks of the stone and the sound of birds in the canopy. Six centuries ago, this was the most guarded ground in China. Today it is a place for slow walks and quiet thoughts. As you leave, glance back at the stone animals. They are still keeping watch, just as they were carved to do.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the Sacred Way slowly rather than taking a shuttle.
Come in autumn for golden ginkgo leaves along the path.
Visit Plum Blossom Hill in early spring for blossoms.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven stone paths and long distances.
Arrive early to enjoy the stone animals before crowds.
