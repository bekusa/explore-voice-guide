The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beneath your feet, a freight train is rolling through the lower deck. You feel it before you fully hear it, a low steady hum in the steel and concrete. Above you, buses and cars move along the road deck. Below all of that, the Yangtze moves too, wide and brown and patient, carrying barges toward the sea. This is a bridge with three layers of motion, and you are standing in the middle of them.

The Nanjing Yangtze River Bridge opened in 1968. The railway deck carried trains first, in the autumn, and the road deck followed near the end of the year. It is a double-deck steel truss bridge. Trains run on the lower level, and road traffic runs on the upper level. For a city that had always been split by this enormous river, the change was huge. Before the bridge, trains crossed here by ferry, one carriage load at a time.

To understand why people in Nanjing still speak about this bridge with pride, you need one piece of context. China had already crossed the Yangtze once, at Wuhan, in the 1950s. That earlier bridge was built with help from Soviet engineers. Work at Nanjing began around 1960, just as relations between China and the Soviet Union broke down. Soviet experts left, and they took support with them. Chinese engineers and workers carried on alone.

So this became known as the first major bridge across the Yangtze that China designed and built entirely by itself. That is the fact most visitors hear first, and it explains almost everything you see here. The bridge was never only about transport. It was a statement about self-reliance, made at a time when the country felt isolated. Many local people grew up seeing this structure on posters, stamps, notebooks and even household items.

The river here was not an easy place to build. The Yangtze at Nanjing is deep, fast, and wide, and the riverbed is complicated. Engineers had to sink deep foundations for the piers that hold up the steel spans. Those foundations had to resist strong currents and heavy floods. The bridge was also built high enough for large river ships to pass underneath. When you look down at the water, try to imagine sinking that work into the current with the tools of the 1960s.

Now look along the road deck itself. The lamp posts are not plain poles. Their tops are shaped like magnolia blossoms. The magnolia is a flower closely linked to this part of China, and it gives the bridge a softer touch than you might expect from a project of its era. At night, when the lamps glow in long lines, the deck turns into a ribbon of light stretching across the river.

Here is a detail many people walk past without noticing. Look at the side railings. Set into them are cast iron relief panels. Many show scenes of work and industry, farms and factories and people building a new country. Others show sunflowers, which were a common political symbol at the time. Each panel is a small picture, and together they read almost like a comic strip of the era's hopes. Stop at a few and look closely.

At each end, the bridge rises into tall bridgehead towers. These are its most recognisable silhouettes. Look up at the sculptures there. They show large figures of workers, farmers and soldiers, standing together and moving forward. The style is bold and heroic, with strong shapes meant to be read from far away. On top of the main towers you will see red banners rendered in solid form. They were designed to catch the light and the eye from across the river.

The bridge is not only a monument. It has been working hard every day for more than half a century. For decades, it was a key link on the railway between Beijing and Shanghai. Trains that once had to wait for ferries could suddenly run straight through. Later, newer bridges and tunnels were built across the Yangtze nearby, and some of the pressure moved elsewhere. Still, the lower deck remains a busy railway crossing.

That long working life eventually showed. After many years of heavy traffic, the road deck closed for a major renovation in the mid-2010s. It reopened in 2018. The engineers repaired and strengthened the structure, but they also tried to protect its original character. The magnolia lamps, the iron railings and the tower sculptures were kept and restored. That choice says something. The city decided this bridge was heritage, not just infrastructure.

If you want the big picture, head to Daqiao Park, on the southern bank. From there you can look up at the full length of the steel trusses running out over the water. Inside the bridgehead tower, an elevator and stairs can take you up to the decks. The view from the upper levels takes in the river, the riverbanks, and the city behind you. It helps you feel how much distance the bridge really covers.

While you are here, notice the rhythm of the river itself. Long cargo barges pass slowly underneath, often low in the water with sand, coal or containers. Their horns carry a long way across the surface. This river is one of the great working waterways of the world. The bridge was designed to let that traffic keep moving while trains and cars crossed overhead. Three kinds of travel, stacked on top of one another.

There is one more quiet layer to this place. For older residents of Nanjing, the bridge is personal. Some remember school trips here, or family photos taken in front of the towers. Some remember the day they first crossed the river without a boat. You may see older couples walking slowly along the deck, or someone pointing out a railing panel to a grandchild. In those small moments, the bridge becomes a family memory as well as a national symbol.

Before you go, pause for a moment and feel the deck again. Wait for the next train below. Listen for the hum rising through the steel, then fading away toward the far bank. Look at the magnolia lamps lined up into the distance, and the brown river sliding past beneath them. When you walk back toward the city, glance over your shoulder at the towers once more. They have watched this crossing for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at Daqiao Park on the southern riverbank.
Ride up the bridgehead tower for the deck view.
Return after dark to see the lamps lit.
Wear comfortable shoes; the deck is very long.
Wait for a passing train to feel the vibration.
