The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here the wind moves through the grass on the old earthworks, and somewhere below you can hear the town of Halden going about its day. Under your feet the ground rises and falls in long, deliberate shapes. These are not natural hills. They are walls, ramparts and ditches, shaped by hand, built to stop an army. Welcome to Fredriksten, the fortress that has watched over this border town for more than three and a half centuries.

Start by looking at where you are standing. Fredriksten sits on a high, rocky ridge directly above the town. Below, the river Tista runs out into the Iddefjord, a long inlet that marks the boundary between Norway and Sweden. The Swedish border is very close. That geography explains almost everything about this place. For centuries, Halden was a front-line town, and this hill was its shield.

The story begins in the middle of the seventeenth century. Norway was then united with Denmark under one crown, and Sweden was a powerful and ambitious neighbour. In the wars of the 1650s and 1660s, Swedish forces attacked Halden several times. The townspeople and soldiers held out behind simpler defences on this ridge. Those sieges convinced the king that a proper fortress was needed. Construction began, and the new stronghold was named after King Frederick the Third of Denmark and Norway. Fredriksten simply means Frederick's stone.

As you walk, notice how the fortress is not one single building. It is a layered system. There is a main fortress on the highest ground, and smaller outworks spread along the ridge. Each one could cover the others with gunfire. Soldiers called this kind of design a star fort, because the angled bastions stick out like points of a star. The angles let defenders fire along the face of the wall, so attackers had nowhere safe to hide.

Now for the first surprise. In 1716, the Swedish king Charles the Twelfth marched into Norway and took the town of Halden itself. The fortress above still held. And the people of Halden made a hard choice. Rather than let the Swedish army shelter in their homes, they set fire to their own town. The Swedes were driven out of the streets, and the defenders up here kept firing. Norwegians still remember this act of self-sacrifice as one of the proudest moments in the town's history.

Charles the Twelfth came back two years later, in 1718. This time he laid a full siege to Fredriksten. His soldiers dug trenches up the slope, creeping closer to the walls night after night. On the evening of 30 November 1718, the king looked over the edge of a trench to watch the work. A bullet struck him in the head, and he died instantly. The siege collapsed, and the Swedish army withdrew.

Here is the second surprise. Nobody knows for certain who fired that shot. The obvious answer is a defender on the walls of Fredriksten. But for three centuries, people have argued that the bullet may have come from his own side. Historians, doctors and enthusiasts have examined the king's remains and debated the angle of the wound. The question has never been settled. It remains one of the great unsolved mysteries of Scandinavian history.

The spot where the king fell lies on the slope outside the main walls, and a memorial marks the place. Standing near it, you can understand the danger of that night. The trench line faces uphill, straight into the guns. The distance to the walls is short. In the dark, with frost on the ground and cannon smoke drifting, any soldier peering over the edge was taking a terrible risk.

Fredriksten was besieged several times in its working life, but it was never taken by force. That record gave the fortress a special place in Norwegian identity. Over time, the town below grew back from its ashes. It was rebuilt and later renamed Fredrikshald, in honour of the fortress and the king. In the twentieth century, the town returned to its older name, Halden, which it still uses today.

Inside the walls, look at the buildings that once kept a garrison alive. A fortress was a small town of its own. Soldiers needed bread, beer, water, gunpowder and a place to sleep. Historic buildings here once served those everyday needs, including a bakery and a brewery. Some now hold museum exhibitions about the fortress, the sieges and military life. Take a moment in these quieter spaces. They show the ordinary routine behind the dramatic battles.

Now walk to the edge of the ramparts and look outward. On a clear day the view runs across the rooftops of Halden, down to the river and the long, narrow fjord. The water is calm here, often dark and still. Across it is Sweden. The hills on both sides look much the same — forest, granite and scattered houses. It is a peaceful scene now. Two neighbours that fought here for generations share a quiet, open border.

The materials around you carry their own history. The walls are built from heavy grey stone, much of it granite from this rocky landscape. Look closely and you will see different kinds of stonework. Some sections are rough and irregular. Others are neatly cut and fitted. These differences come from centuries of building, repair and strengthening. Grass now grows over the earth banks, which once absorbed the shock of cannonballs.

Today Fredriksten is a place for walking, picnics and summer events, as much as for history. Families spread blankets on the grass where soldiers once drilled. Children run along paths that were laid out for marching columns. In warm weather the slopes fill with people, and in winter the walls stand quiet under snow. The fortress has slowly changed from a weapon into a shared park for the town.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen again. The wind, the voices, the sound of the town below. Look at the stone under your hand, cool and worn smooth in places. As you walk back down toward Halden, turn around once and look up. The ramparts sit along the skyline, just as they did when an army camped on this slope... and the town still lives safely in their shadow.
