The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Rooks call from the old trees, gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere a gardener's barrow rattles over stone. The walls around you are Cotswold limestone, the colour of honey left in the sun. Run your hand along a weathered edge and it feels soft, almost warm. This is Sudeley Castle, just outside the small town of Winchcombe, a short drive northeast of Cheltenham. It is a working family home, a ruin, a garden and a royal grave, all at once.

The castle sits in a green bowl of the Cotswold hills, with the town's rooftops close by. Winchcombe was once an important Saxon centre, with a large abbey of its own. Today it is quieter. Walkers on the long-distance Cotswold Way pass close by, boots muddy, maps in hand. Many of the visitors you meet here will have arrived on foot. Others have come for one woman, who lived here for only a short time.

First, the bones of the place. A castle has stood on this ground since the medieval period. The version whose ruins you see was largely the work of Ralph Boteler, Lord Sudeley, in the fifteenth century. He grew rich and powerful during the long wars of that era. But power in medieval England was never safe. Boteler fell out of favour with the new Yorkist king, Edward the Fourth, and the castle passed to the Crown.

One of its royal owners was Richard the Third, before he became king. The roofless ruin often called the Banqueting Hall is linked with his time here. Look up at its tall window openings. Today they frame only sky and ivy, but once they lit a hall built to impress. Stand inside and notice how the wind moves differently there — open, but sheltered by stone on every side.

Now to the woman most visitors come to find. Katherine Parr was the sixth and last wife of King Henry the Eighth. She outlived him. Soon after his death, she married Thomas Seymour, the brother of Henry's third wife, Jane Seymour. Sudeley became their home. With them came a young ward — Lady Jane Grey, the girl who would later be queen of England for only nine days.

Katherine was a serious, educated woman. She published books of prayer and reflection under her own name, which was rare for a woman of her time. Here at Sudeley, she was pregnant for the first time, in her mid-thirties. In 1548 she gave birth to a daughter, Mary. Within days, Katherine died of fever after the birth. It was one of the most common dangers of the age, and it reached even a queen.

She was buried in the small chapel of Saint Mary in the castle grounds. Lady Jane Grey, still a child, was chief mourner at the funeral. Here is something many visitors do not realise. Katherine Parr is the only English queen buried on private land. Her tomb lies inside that chapel today. It is a carved Victorian memorial, made long after her death, because her original resting place was nearly lost.

That loss came with war. In the seventeenth century, England split in a civil war between King Charles the First and Parliament. Sudeley stood on the King's side. Charles himself is said to have stayed here, and his nephew, Prince Rupert, used the castle as a base. The fighting damaged the chapel badly. When Parliament won, the castle was deliberately made indefensible — a process called slighting, where walls and defences are broken so an enemy cannot use them again.

For roughly two centuries afterwards, Sudeley lay in ruins. Grass grew in the halls. In the late eighteenth century, people searching the ruined chapel found Katherine's coffin. According to accounts from that time, it was opened more than once by curious visitors, and the remains were treated with little respect. It was only later that the coffin was properly reburied and protected. Her quiet tomb today is partly an act of repair.

The rescue of the castle began in 1837. Two brothers, John and William Dent, bought the estate. They were glove makers from Worcester — a city famous for its leather gloves. Industry money, not ancient lineage, saved Sudeley. They restored part of the castle as a home, and they began to rebuild the chapel. Later, Emma Dent, who married into the family, carried the work forward with great energy through the Victorian era.

Emma Dent shaped much of what you see now. She collected objects connected to Katherine Parr and the castle's past. She cared deeply about the gardens and about the people of Winchcombe, supporting the town in practical ways. Her descendants still live here today. That is why parts of the castle are private. As you walk, you may notice doors that stay closed and windows with family curtains — a reminder that this is someone's home.

Now look at the gardens. The Queen's Garden is the one most people remember. In summer, roses fill it with scent and colour, set between dark, clipped yew hedges. The name honours the queens connected to Sudeley. Elizabeth the First, Katherine's stepdaughter, is recorded as visiting the castle during her reign. Other gardens around the grounds mix formal shapes with softer planting, and the old ruins become part of the design, with climbing plants softening the broken stone.

Before you leave, walk back to the chapel. Stand quietly in front of Katherine's tomb. Then step outside and look at the ruins, the lived-in wings, the roses and the hills beyond. Every part of this place has been broken and rebuilt at least once. Listen again for the rooks in the trees. They were probably calling here in Katherine's time too.
