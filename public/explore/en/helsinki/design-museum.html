The guide

The story

On a quiet street in Helsinki stands a building of dark brick, with pointed windows and a tower that recalls an old school. Inside, the rooms hold chairs, glassware, textiles, and lamps, set out with care. This is the Design Museum, where Finland tells the story of how everyday objects are shaped, and why their shape matters.

The museum was founded in 1873. That makes it one of the oldest design museums in the world. It began at a time when many countries were setting up such collections to teach craftspeople and raise the quality of goods. The aim was practical: to show makers, students, and the public what good design could be.

For its first century the museum moved between several homes. Then, in 1978, it settled into its present building on Korkeavuorenkatu street. The building was not made for a museum. It was finished in 1895 as a school, designed by the architect Gustaf Nyström in a neo-Gothic style, with pointed arches and brick walls.

The old school suits its new use well. High ceilings and large windows let in even, northern light, the kind that shows true color. Wooden floors sound softly under your steps. The rooms are calm and plain, so the objects on display hold all of your attention, with little to pull the eye away.

For many years the museum carried the name Taideteollisuusmuseo, which means the museum of applied arts. In 2002 it took a simpler name: Designmuseo, the Design Museum. The change matched the way people had come to speak. The word design had entered daily Finnish, and the museum followed the language of its visitors.

Finnish design has a strong and clear character. It favors simple shapes, natural materials, and objects that work well for ordinary life. A chair should be honest. A glass should feel right in the hand. The collection shows how this way of thinking grew and spread over more than a hundred years.

Among the displays you may meet famous names of Finnish design. Alvar Aalto bent wood into soft curves for his furniture. Kaj Franck stripped tableware down to pure, plain form. The textile house Marimekko brought bold print and bright color. Their work shaped how much of the world pictures Finland today.

The collection holds tens of thousands of objects, along with drawings and photographs. Only a part is shown at any one time. The permanent display gives the long story, from early craft to modern industry. Around it, changing exhibitions look at single designers, single themes, or fresh ideas brought in from abroad.

The museum also keeps a library and archive used by students and designers. Drawings, catalogues, and photographs are stored there for research. In this way the place is not only for visitors passing through; it is a working memory for the field, a record of what Finnish makers have done and dreamed.

Move slowly here. Many objects reward a second look. A plain wooden stool may hide a clever joint. A coffee cup may sit in the hand more lightly than you expect. Design lives in these small choices, the ones a maker thought about with care so that the user would not have to.

The museum does not only look back. It asks where design is going. Exhibitions explore how objects can use less material, last longer, and waste less when their life is done. In a world full of things, the question of what we should make, and why, feels more pressing with each passing year.

The area around the museum carries the same spirit. The streets nearby hold design shops, studios, and small galleries. This part of the city is sometimes called its design district. A visit here pairs well with a slow walk through the surrounding blocks, where the ideas inside the museum spill out into the shops.

As you leave, look again at the everyday things in your own hands: your bag, your phone, your shoes. Each was designed by someone who made choices about its shape and feel. The museum trains the eye to see those choices. Once you start to notice them, the made world around you never looks quite the same.
