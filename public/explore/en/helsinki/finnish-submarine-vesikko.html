The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across the low granite of Suomenlinna. Gulls call over the water, and somewhere a ferry horn sounds toward the city. In front of you, raised up on dry land, sits a long grey hull. It looks almost small against the old fortress walls. This is Vesikko, a Finnish submarine that once slid beneath the Baltic Sea. Today it rests in the open air, so you can see the whole shape that its crew never saw from outside.

Take in the outline first. The hull is narrow and tapered, like a fish built from steel plates and rivets. A slim conning tower rises from the middle. That is the small raised structure where the commander stood when the boat ran on the surface. Below it, the deck runs forward toward the bow. Vesikko is a coastal submarine, made for shallow and enclosed waters like the Gulf of Finland. It was never meant to cross oceans. Its length is roughly forty metres, about the size of a large tram.

Now for the first surprise. This boat was not built simply as a Finnish warship. It began as a quiet experiment. After the First World War, the Treaty of Versailles banned Germany from building submarines. German engineers found a way around the ban. They worked through a design office in the Netherlands, which drew up new submarine plans for foreign buyers. One of those plans became this boat, built at the Crichton-Vulcan shipyard in Turku, on Finland's southwest coast, in the early 1930s.

The boat was first known only by a shipyard code, CV 707. German specialists took part in its trials at sea. They tested how it dived, how it handled, and how its systems worked under pressure. What they learned went straight back to Germany. When Germany openly rebuilt its navy in the mid-1930s, its first small U-boats, called Type II, followed this design very closely. So the hull in front of you is, in a real sense, the prototype of a whole family of German submarines.

Finland bought the boat in 1936 and gave it a new name. Vesikko is the Finnish word for the European mink, a small, quick animal that hunts along riverbanks and swims well. It is a fitting name for a compact boat that worked close to the shore. Finland's other submarines carried animal and water-spirit names too. Together they formed a small fleet for a young country with a long coastline and a large neighbour across the sea.

That neighbour was the Soviet Union. In the winter of 1939, war broke out between the two countries, a conflict Finns call the Winter War. Vesikko patrolled the Finnish coast during those months, though the frozen sea limited what any submarine could do. Then, in 1941, fighting began again, in what Finland calls the Continuation War. Early in that second war, Vesikko torpedoed and sank a Soviet merchant ship named Vyborg. It was the boat's best-known action at sea.

Now imagine the crew. About twenty men lived and worked inside this narrow hull. There was no real privacy, and very little fresh air. When the boat ran submerged, it moved on electric batteries. On the surface, diesel engines drove it and charged those batteries again. The air inside carried the smell of diesel fuel, machine oil, sweat, and cooking. Men slept wherever space allowed, sometimes close to the torpedoes they would fire.

If the boat is open when you visit, you can climb inside. Many visitors find this the most memorable part. The passage is tight, and you move through low, round openings between compartments. Pipes, valves, gauges, and hand wheels cover almost every surface. In the control room, notice how close everything is. One person could reach several controls without taking a step. Space was the rarest thing on board, and every centimetre had a job.

Here is the second surprise. Vesikko is the only Finnish submarine that survives. After the Second World War, the peace terms signed in Paris in 1947 forbade Finland from having submarines at all. The other boats were sold and broken up for scrap. Vesikko was kept, partly because of its historical importance as a prototype. For years it waited in storage. Later it was restored and placed here on Suomenlinna as a museum, which it has been since the 1970s.

The setting itself adds meaning. Suomenlinna is a sea fortress spread across several connected islands at the entrance to Helsinki's harbour. Its building began in the eighteenth century, when Finland was part of the Swedish kingdom. Today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, a list of places protected for their value to all humanity. So this twentieth-century submarine sits among stone walls and cannons from much older wars. Two very different ideas of sea defence share the same shoreline.

As you walk around the hull, look at the details. Near the bow you can see the openings for the torpedo tubes, where the boat fired its main weapons. On the deck there is a gun mount. Look also at the steel surface itself. Weld lines, rivets, and patched plates show where the boat was built, repaired, and later restored. These marks are not decoration. They are the practical record of a working machine.

The hull is managed today by the Finnish Military Museum. Inside and around it, information panels tell more about the crew and the boat's service. Take a little time with them if you can. Many visitors rush through the interior because it feels cramped. But if you pause in one compartment and simply look around, you begin to understand the daily life of the men who served here, sealed in steel under the cold Baltic.

Before you leave, step back a few paces and look at the whole boat again. Notice how low it sits, how little of it would have shown above the waves. Behind it the fortress walls rise, and beyond them the open sea stretches toward the horizon. Listen once more to the wind and the gulls. Then, as you walk on along the island paths, you may notice the ferries crossing the harbour, moving easily on the surface where this small grey boat once travelled unseen below.
