Finnish National Gallery is an organization of three museums that make up the largest art museum institution of Finland. It is in Helsinki, Finland. The gallery unites Ateneum, Kiasma and Sinebrychoff museums. The free Lokali audio guide runs 7 minutes.

The guide

The story

Start with the sound of the city around you. Trams squeal as they turn near Railway Square. Gulls call from the harbour, not far away. Footsteps echo on the paving, and the cool northern light flattens the colour of the stone. In front of you, or very close by, is one of the buildings of the Finnish National Gallery. This is the house of Finland's largest public art collection, and one of the main places where the country tells its own story in paint.

Here is the first surprise. The Finnish National Gallery is not one building at all. It is an institution with three museums under one name. The Ateneum Art Museum holds the classic national collection. Kiasma, the Museum of Contemporary Art, shows art from recent decades. The Sinebrychoff Art Museum, a short walk south, holds older European paintings. Together they hold tens of thousands of works, and only a small share is on display at any one time.

Let us begin with the Ateneum, the grand building facing Railway Square. It was completed in the late nineteenth century, in the 1880s. At that time Finland was a Grand Duchy under the Russian Empire. The name comes from Athena, the Greek goddess of wisdom and the arts. The building first housed an art school and the collection of the Finnish Art Society. The idea was simple, and ambitious: a small nation would learn to make art, and see art, in the same place.

Look up at the facade. It is a palace of learning in the classical style, with columns, sculpted figures, and portrait busts of great artists from the ancient and Renaissance past. These faces were a statement. They said that Finnish art belonged to the long European tradition. Notice how pale and formal the front looks against the newer glass and steel of the streets around it. In winter, when snow sits on the ledges, the details become sharper, like a drawing in pencil.

Inside the Ateneum, you meet the painters who shaped how Finns see themselves. This was the so-called Golden Age of Finnish art, around the turn of the twentieth century. Akseli Gallen-Kallela painted scenes from the Kalevala, the Finnish national epic of songs and myths. Albert Edelfelt painted ordinary people with quiet dignity. Pekka Halonen painted snow-covered forests and lakes. For a country without its own state, these images became a kind of shared identity.

One small painting carries enormous weight here. It is The Wounded Angel by Hugo Simberg, painted in the early twentieth century. Two solemn boys carry a stretcher. On it sits an angel with a bandage over her eyes and a hurt wing. Behind them is a grey shoreline, almost empty. Simberg never fully explained its meaning. In a public vote in the 2000s, Finns chose it as their national painting. Many visitors find it strangely moving, even without knowing why.

Do not miss the women of this collection. Helene Schjerfbeck is one of the most admired Finnish painters, and her work grows more striking across her long life. Her early paintings are careful and realistic. Her later self-portraits become spare and almost ghostly, with faces reduced to a few lines and patches of colour. Standing in front of them, you can follow one artist looking at herself honestly over many decades. It is one of the quietest and most powerful experiences in the building.

Here is the second surprise. The Ateneum also holds works by famous foreign artists. Among them is a painting by Vincent van Gogh, a street scene from the French village of Auvers-sur-Oise. The museum bought it in the early twentieth century, when Van Gogh was far from the celebrity he is today. Finland was a small, distant place, yet its collectors were paying close attention to new art in Paris. That early confidence still feels a little bold.

Now walk a few minutes west, toward the statue of Marshal Mannerheim on horseback. Behind it rises Kiasma, with its curving silver form. The American architect Steven Holl designed it, and it opened in 1998. The name comes from the word chiasma, which means a crossing, like the place where nerves cross. The building has very few right angles. Light enters from above and moves slowly across white walls through the day.

Kiasma caused an argument when it was planned. It stands close to the Parliament House and the Mannerheim statue, in a very symbolic part of the city. Some people felt a modern museum did not belong there. Today it has become part of the everyday life of Helsinki. Students sit on the steps, people meet at the cafe, and the exhibitions change often. Video, installation, sound, and painting share the same rooms.

The third museum feels completely different. The Sinebrychoff Art Museum stands on Bulevardi, a tree-lined street in the south of the city centre. It was once the home of the Sinebrychoff family, who ran one of the oldest breweries in the Nordic region. In the early twentieth century, the family's art collection was donated to the Finnish state. Today you can see old Dutch, Flemish, Italian, and Swedish paintings, together with furnished rooms that show how a wealthy merchant family lived.

Together, these three museums trace a long line. At Sinebrychoff, you see the European past that Finnish collectors admired. At the Ateneum, you see a young nation learning to paint its own landscapes and legends. At Kiasma, you see artists asking new questions about the present. In 2014, the gallery became an independent foundation, and it continues to buy, conserve, and lend art. Much of its work happens behind the scenes, in storage rooms and conservation studios.

Before you move on, take a moment where you stand. Notice the stone under your feet, the noise of trams, and the people crossing the square without looking up. Many of them pass these buildings every day. Inside, a boy carries a wounded angel along a grey shore, and a forest stays covered in snow forever. When you walk away, look at the northern light on the facades once more. It is the same light that these painters tried so hard to catch.
