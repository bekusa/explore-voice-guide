The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes. Gulls call from the harbour at one end of the park, and trams rattle past at the other. Above you, rows of linden trees hold their branches over a long, narrow strip of green. In summer the leaves turn the light soft and pale. In winter the bare branches carry strings of small lights. This is Esplanadi, and locals simply call it Espa.

The shape of the place is easy to read. Esplanadi is a long boulevard park. It runs from the edge of the Market Square and the South Harbour in the east to the Swedish Theatre in the west. Two streets frame it on either side. Pohjoisesplanadi runs along the north, and Eteläesplanadi runs along the south. The names simply mean North Esplanade and South Esplanade. The park in the middle is the shared living room between them.

To understand why it exists, go back to the early nineteenth century. In 1812, Helsinki became the capital of the Grand Duchy of Finland, which was then ruled by the Russian Empire. The city needed a new centre that looked the part. Planners led by Johan Albrecht Ehrenström drew a grid of broad streets. The architect Carl Ludvig Engel shaped much of the monumental core nearby. Esplanadi was part of that vision: an open, green promenade for a city that was trying to look like a capital.

In those early decades, a walk along Esplanadi was a social event. The idea of a promenade was to see and to be seen. Officials, merchants and their families came here in their best clothes. Over time, the park became less formal, but that old habit never quite disappeared. Today people still walk slowly here. They stop to talk. They sit on the benches and watch the crowd move past, much as earlier generations did.

Near the middle of the park, look for the bronze statue on a tall stone base. This is Johan Ludvig Runeberg, the poet whom many Finns call their national poet. He wrote in Swedish, and his poem Our Land became the words of the Finnish national anthem. The statue was made by his own son, the sculptor Walter Runeberg, and it was unveiled in the 1880s. A son shaping his father in bronze — that detail surprises many visitors.

Walk around the base of the statue. Below the poet stands a female figure. She represents Finland, a symbol that appears often in art from that period. At the time, Finns were building a stronger sense of national identity while still under Russian rule. A statue of a poet who wrote about the land and its people carried real meaning. So this is not only a portrait. It is also a statement about who the Finns wanted to be.

Now look towards the eastern end of the park, where a light, elegant pavilion stands among the trees. This is Kappeli, one of the oldest restaurants in Helsinki. It has served guests here since the 1860s. Its large windows and iron details give it the feel of a glass garden house. Writers, artists and musicians have met here for generations. On a cold day, the warm light inside is a welcome sight.

Across the path from Kappeli is the Espa Stage, a small open-air stage. In summer it hosts free concerts and performances, from jazz and folk to choirs and children's shows. People bring blankets and spread out on the grass in front of it. In a city with long, dark winters, these summer evenings matter. When the sun barely sets in June, the music can feel like a whole city stretching after a long sleep.

Closer to the western end, look for another poet in bronze. Eino Leino was one of the great Finnish-language poets of the early twentieth century. His statue sits in the park, and it feels more relaxed than Runeberg's tall monument. Between the two figures, you can trace a shift in Finnish culture. One poet wrote in Swedish, the language of the old elite. The other wrote in Finnish, the language of most of the people.

At the far western end stands the Swedish Theatre, with its curved front facing the park. Swedish is still one of the two official languages of Finland, and you will see it on every street sign around you. Look up at the signs as you walk. Each name appears twice, once in Finnish and once in Swedish. This small detail shows a bilingual country, and many visitors walk past it without noticing.

Now turn your attention to the streets on either side. Pohjoisesplanadi, the northern street, holds many of the city's best-known Finnish design shops. Here you can find bold printed fabrics, glassware and ceramics that have shaped how the world sees Nordic design. Along this same street stands Hotel Kämp, a grand hotel from the late nineteenth century. The buildings show a mix of styles, from stone facades with heavy details to cleaner, later lines.

At the eastern end, Esplanadi opens onto the Market Square and the sea. Here you will find the Havis Amanda fountain by Ville Vallgren, unveiled in 1908. A bronze young woman rises from the water, surrounded by sea lions. Locals say she represents Helsinki itself, born from the sea. Every year on the eve of May Day, students climb up and place a white student cap on her head. The crowd cheers, and spring officially begins.

Esplanadi changes completely with the seasons. In summer the lawns fill with picnics and the cafe terraces spill onto the paths. In autumn the lindens turn yellow and leaves gather along the edges of the gravel. In winter the park is quieter, and the lights in the trees glow against the snow. Around the Christmas season, the streets nearby fill with decorations. The same park can feel like four different places in one year.

Before you move on, pause for a moment. Look along the length of the trees, from the theatre towards the harbour. Notice the sound of the gravel, the gulls, the distant trams. Notice who is around you — a student with a coffee, a grandmother on a bench, a child chasing pigeons. This park was planned as a stage for a new capital. Today it is simply the place where Helsinki comes to slow down, and for a few minutes you are part of it.
