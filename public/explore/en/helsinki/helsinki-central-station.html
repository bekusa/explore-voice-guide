The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wheeled suitcases rattle over stone. A train announcement rings out in Finnish, then Swedish, then English. Cold air drifts in each time the heavy doors swing open. Above you, the clock tower rises over Railway Square, and four stone giants stand at the entrance with glowing globes in their hands. This is Helsinki Central Station, where much of Finland arrives in the capital and leaves it again. Before you hurry inside, take a minute to look at the building itself. It is one of the most important works of architecture in the country.

The station was designed by Eliel Saarinen, a young Finnish architect who won the design competition in the early 1900s. Construction stretched across the 1910s, and the station opened to passengers in 1919. That timing matters. Finland was still a grand duchy of the Russian Empire when the work began. By the time the first passengers walked through these doors, Finland had declared independence. In a quiet way, this building connects two very different chapters of Finnish life.

Saarinen's first competition entry looked quite different from what you see today. It belonged to a style called National Romanticism, a movement that looked to Finnish forests, folklore and medieval castles for inspiration. Critics said the design looked too old-fashioned for a modern railway. Saarinen took the criticism seriously. He reworked his plans and produced something cleaner and more rational. The final building keeps the heavy, earthy feeling of Finnish granite, but its lines are simpler and more vertical. Many historians see it as a bridge between romantic tradition and early modern design.

Look closely at the walls. The facade is built from pale Finnish granite, cut into large, smooth blocks. Granite is everywhere in this country. The bedrock pushes up through parks and city streets, so building in this stone felt natural and national at the same time. Notice how the surfaces stay mostly plain. There is very little fussy decoration. Instead, the building relies on mass, proportion and a few strong sculptural moments. Those moments are what most visitors remember.

The four figures beside the main entrance are known in Finnish as the Lantern Carriers. They were made by the sculptor Emil Wikström, one of the leading Finnish sculptors of his generation. Each pair stands calm and still, holding a round lamp in front of its chest. Their faces are serious, almost severe. At night, when the lamps glow, the figures look like guardians welcoming travellers in from the dark. In a city where winter days are very short, that image carries real meaning.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. These solemn stone men have become a kind of public joke that the whole city shares. On special occasions, they are dressed up. During the coronavirus pandemic, the figures wore giant face masks. When Finnish teams compete in big sporting events, the statues have appeared in scarves or team jerseys. A digital version of the Lantern Carriers has even appeared in advertising for the national railway. Locals feel a real affection for them.

Now look up at the clock tower. It rises well above the surrounding rooftops and acts as a landmark for anyone walking through the city centre. The clock faces are easy to read from far away, which was the whole point. In an age before mobile phones, a railway clock set the rhythm of daily life. Above the tower, and across the roofs, you will see green copper. That colour comes from patina, a layer that forms naturally as copper ages in wind and rain.

Step inside, and the scale changes. The main hall is tall and bright, with arched forms and a calm, ordered feeling. Even at the busiest hours, the space handles crowds with surprising grace. This station is one of the most heavily used buildings in Finland. Commuter trains arrive from the suburbs every few minutes. Long-distance trains leave for cities across the country. Below your feet, the Helsinki metro runs through its own underground station, and a city rail line connects the centre with the airport.

The station has also lived through difficult times. Before it officially opened, parts of the new building were used as a military hospital during the First World War. During the Second World War, Helsinki was bombed, and the area around the station suffered damage. The building survived. For decades, trains also ran east from here toward Russia. Those international services no longer operate, which changes the feeling of the departure boards. The station now looks more toward the rest of Finland and the airport.

Part of the building has found a new life in recent years. Offices that once belonged to the railway company were turned into a hotel, so some visitors now sleep inside the station itself. There is also a special waiting room, created for important guests. It was used in the era of the Russian emperors, and later by Finnish presidents and state visitors. It is not a normal public space, but it shows how this station was planned as a ceremonial gateway, not just a transport hub.

Walk back outside and look across Railway Square. The square is wide and open, and it often fills with buses, cyclists and people crossing in every direction. On the north side stands the Finnish National Theatre, built in a similar granite style. On the opposite side you will see the Ateneum, the national art museum, with its classical columns. Together, these buildings frame one of the most important civic spaces in the city. Saarinen's tower ties the whole scene together.

It is worth thinking about where Saarinen went next. His work in Finland, including this station, made his name known abroad. In the 1920s he moved to the United States, where he taught and designed buildings for many years. His son, Eero Saarinen, became one of the best-known architects of twentieth-century America. So when you look at this granite tower, you are looking at an early chapter in a family story that later shaped architecture far from Finland.

Before you move on, find a quiet spot near the entrance and simply watch. Notice the commuters who pass the Lantern Carriers without even glancing up. Notice the tourists who stop to take the same photograph you may have taken. Listen to the doors, the footsteps and the announcements in three languages. More than a century after it opened, the station still does what it was built to do. It welcomes people, sends them on their way, and keeps time for the city.
