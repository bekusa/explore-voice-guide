The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums along the wide avenues of sector G-6, and somewhere nearby a rickshaw engine coughs and fades. Then the call to prayer rises from the loudspeakers above you, and the street noise seems to step aside. Look up at the walls. They are painted a deep, warm red, the colour of brick and baked earth. That colour gave this mosque its name. Lal Masjid simply means the Red Mosque.

Islamabad is a young city. It was planned in the late 1950s and 1960s as a new capital for Pakistan, laid out in a grid of numbered sectors with green belts between them. Lal Masjid belongs to that early generation of buildings. It went up in the mid-1960s, when much of this land was still open ground below the Margalla Hills. So when you stand here, you are standing in one of the oldest religious spaces in a city that is barely older than many of its residents.

The mosque sits close to Aabpara, one of the capital's earliest market areas. In the city's first decades, this was a practical place to worship. Government workers, shopkeepers and families from the surrounding sectors came here for daily and Friday prayers. For many years, Lal Masjid was one of the larger congregational mosques in Islamabad. Its imams were well known in the city, and its pulpit carried real influence in local religious life.

Now take in the building itself. The design is fairly simple compared with the grand Mughal mosques of Lahore or Delhi. There are minarets, a prayer hall, and an open courtyard where worshippers gather in good weather. The red paint covers much of the exterior, and it changes with the light. In the harsh noon sun it looks almost orange. Late in the afternoon it darkens to something closer to rust.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. A mosque in Pakistan is often more than a prayer hall. It can also be a school, a meeting point, and a centre of community life. Lal Masjid was linked to religious seminaries, known locally as madrassas, where students study the Quran and Islamic law. The best known of these is Jamia Hafsa, a seminary for women. That connection would later place this quiet neighbourhood at the centre of national news.

In the years after 2001, the mosque's leadership grew more openly confrontational with the Pakistani government. The mosque was led by two brothers, Maulana Abdul Aziz and Abdul Rashid Ghazi. Their father, Maulana Muhammad Abdullah, had been the mosque's first prayer leader, and he was killed in 1998. Under the brothers, students from the affiliated seminaries began campaigns in the city that the authorities saw as a direct challenge to the law.

Tensions built through early 2007. Students occupied a nearby public library and carried out actions against shops and businesses they considered immoral. The government and the mosque leaders negotiated, then broke off, then negotiated again. Pakistan watched closely. Many people were uneasy about armed confrontation in the heart of the capital. Others felt the state had waited far too long to act.

In July 2007, the standoff turned into a siege. Security forces surrounded the mosque and the seminary complex. Some students left, but others stayed inside. Maulana Abdul Aziz was arrested while trying to leave the area disguised in a burqa, the full-body covering worn by some women. After days of tension, troops entered the compound. Many people died in the fighting, among them Abdul Rashid Ghazi. The exact number of dead is still disputed.

The events of that summer still divide opinion in Pakistan. For some people, the operation was a necessary defence of the state. For others, it was a tragedy that cost many young lives and deepened anger in parts of the country. In the following months and years, Pakistan saw a wave of militant violence, and many observers link part of that wave to the siege. It is worth knowing this history, because it shapes how people here speak about the mosque.

After the siege, the damaged buildings were repaired, and the walls were painted red again. Prayers resumed. Today the mosque is an active place of worship once more. You may notice a visible security presence in the surrounding streets. Police and checkpoints are a normal part of life near sensitive sites in Islamabad, so please do not be alarmed. Simply follow any instructions, stay calm, and keep your documents with you.

If you go closer, remember that this is a working mosque, not a museum. Dress modestly, with arms and legs covered. Women should carry a scarf for their hair. Shoes come off before you step onto the prayer area. Access for non-Muslim visitors can vary with the day and the mood of the moment, so ask politely at the gate. Avoid the main prayer times, especially Friday midday, when the space fills with worshippers.

As you look around, notice the ordinary life of the street. Fruit sellers push their carts past. Children walk home from school. Men pause at the ablution taps, where worshippers wash their hands, faces and feet before prayer. The sound of running water mixes with the traffic. For the people of this neighbourhood, Lal Masjid is not mainly a headline. It is a place where they pray, meet neighbours and mark the hours of the day.

Before you walk on, look once more at those red walls. They have been painted, damaged, repaired and painted again. The colour stays the same, but the story behind it has many layers, and Pakistanis still argue about what it means. As you turn back toward Aabpara, listen for the next call to prayer. It will rise from these same loudspeakers, as it has for decades, over a city that is still writing its own history.
