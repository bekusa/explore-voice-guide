The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you will hear it. A low, dry rattle of grass in the wind. The click of insects in the heat. And somewhere beyond the trees, a sound like a distant engine that turns out to be a flock of birds rising from the water. Out here, the air smells of dust, warm earth and wild sage. The light is wide and flat, and the horizon seems very far away.

This is Zakouma National Park, in the southeast of Chad. The nearest town of any size is Am Timan, the capital of the Salamat region. Between the town and the park, the land opens into a broad floodplain of grass, acacia and scattered palms. Seasonal rivers cross it. For part of the year they run full, and for the rest of the year they shrink back into chains of pools. Almost everything about life here follows that rhythm of water arriving and water leaving.

Zakouma is the oldest national park in Chad. It was created in the 1960s, not long after the country gained independence from France. It covers roughly three thousand square kilometres of Sudanian savanna. That is the belt of grassland and open woodland that runs across Africa just south of the Sahara. Sahelian scrub lies to the north. Wetter forest lies to the south. Zakouma sits between them, and it collects animals and birds from both worlds.

The heart of the park is its water. When the rains end, the rivers stop flowing and the floodplain slowly dries. Shallow pans hold the last of the water, and the wildlife gathers around them. In the late dry season, a single pool can draw buffalo, antelope, warthogs, storks and cranes all at once. If you sit quietly near one of these pans in the early morning, the scene changes minute by minute as different animals come down to drink.

Now for the story that most visitors come to understand. Zakouma is famous above all for its elephants — and for how close it came to losing them. In the early 2000s, the park held several thousand elephants. Then heavily armed poachers arrived, many crossing from neighbouring regions. Within less than a decade, the herds collapsed. By around 2010, only a few hundred elephants remained. Researchers found that almost no calves were surviving, because the frightened herds had stopped breeding.

The cost was also human. Park rangers stood between the poachers and the elephants, and some of them paid with their lives. In 2012, a group of rangers was killed in an attack near the park. Their names are remembered here, and their deaths changed how the park is protected. If you meet rangers during your visit, you are meeting the people who inherited that responsibility. Many come from the villages around Zakouma.

In 2010, the government of Chad began a long partnership with African Parks, a conservation organisation that manages protected areas across the continent. Together they rebuilt the ranger force, improved communications and trained teams to patrol on foot, on horseback and by air. They also worked closely with nearby communities, through schools and local programmes. Slowly, the killing stopped. Here is the first surprise for many visitors: the elephant population did not just stabilise. It began to grow again, and young calves returned to the herds.

Today, Zakouma is often described as one of Africa's great conservation recoveries. Elephants move through the park in family groups, and sometimes in very large gatherings. Watch how the adults position themselves around the smallest calves. That behaviour is ordinary in a healthy herd. In Zakouma, it was absent for years. Seeing it now is a quiet measure of what has changed.

The second surprise is the giraffe. Zakouma protects a significant share of the world's remaining Kordofan giraffes. This is a subspecies of giraffe found only in a few parts of central Africa, and it is rare. Its coat pattern is paler and more irregular than many people expect. Look for them browsing at the tops of acacia trees, often standing very still, so they are easy to miss until one turns its head.

The park holds much more. Large herds of buffalo move across the floodplain, raising dust as they go. Lions hunt here, and you may hear them at night. Antelope are everywhere — roan antelope, hartebeest, kob and others. In 2018, black rhinos were brought back to Zakouma from South Africa. The species had disappeared from Chad decades earlier. Their return was a sign of confidence that the park could now keep them safe.

For bird lovers, Zakouma is extraordinary. Hundreds of species have been recorded. In the dry season, the remaining wetlands fill with storks, pelicans, herons, ducks and crowned cranes. The most dramatic sight is the red-billed quelea. This small finch gathers in flocks so large that they move like smoke over the grass. When a flock lifts off all at once, you feel the sound in your chest. That was the engine-like roar you may have heard earlier.

The experience of being here is shaped by distance and season. During the rains, much of the floodplain turns to mud and water, and many tracks become impassable. Most visits happen in the drier months, when roads are open and animals gather near water. The days are hot. Dust settles on everything. In the evening, the light turns gold and the temperature finally drops. The sky after dark is wide and very clear, with few artificial lights for many kilometres.

Before you move on, take a moment where you are. Look at the ground — the cracked mud, the hoofprints, the tracks of birds along the water. Look at the line of trees, and at the open grass beyond them. Each animal you see today is alive partly because people decided this place was worth defending. As you drive away, keep watching the edges of the road. In Zakouma, the most memorable moment often arrives when you think the day is over.

What to look for

Pale, irregular coat patterns on Kordofan giraffes.
Huge quelea flocks moving like smoke over the grass.
Fresh hoofprints in the cracked mud around drying pans.
Crowned cranes with golden crests wading in shallow water.
Before you go

Practical tips

Plan your visit for the dry season when tracks are open.
Wait quietly near a waterhole in the early morning.
Bring binoculars for giraffes high in the acacia trees.
Carry plenty of water and sun protection for the heat.
Follow your ranger's guidance closely around elephant herds.
