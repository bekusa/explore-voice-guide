The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look around. Children's voices rise and fall. Small shoes squeak on hard floors. Somewhere, an excited shout bounces off the walls. A little higher up, light comes through a tall column of coloured glass. This is the sound of a museum built for people who are still learning to read the labels. Welcome to The Children's Museum of Indianapolis. By most measures, it is the largest children's museum in the world.

The story starts small. In 1925, a local woman named Mary Stewart Carey decided Indianapolis needed a museum just for children. She was inspired by a children's museum in Brooklyn, one of the first of its kind. The early collection was modest, and it was shown in a carriage house. Families donated shells, fossils, dolls, and curiosities from travels abroad. From those humble shelves, the collection grew, decade after decade, into what you see today.

The museum moved several times as it grew. Its home here, north of downtown along Meridian Street, opened in the 1970s and has expanded many times since. Today the collection holds well over a hundred thousand objects. Some are scientific specimens. Some are toys. Some are artefacts from ancient civilisations. The founding idea has not changed. Children learn best when they can see real things, up close, and ask questions about them.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Before you even came inside, the building was under attack. Look again at the outside walls when you leave. Life-size dinosaur sculptures appear to break into the building, and others appear to break out. Long necks rise above the street. It is a joke, but a clever one. It tells every child walking up the path exactly what kind of place this is... a place where the ordinary rules bend.

Those dinosaurs lead to one of the best-loved galleries, called Dinosphere. Inside, the lighting shifts and the sounds change, as if you have walked into a prehistoric landscape. Real fossils fill the space. The best-known resident is a Tyrannosaurus rex nicknamed Bucky. He is often described as a teenager, a young animal rather than a full-grown adult. That detail matters. It reminds children that even the most famous predators were once young, growing, and still learning to survive.

Near the heart of the building, look up. A tower of blown glass rises through the open space. It is called Fireworks of Glass, and it is the work of the American glass artist Dale Chihuly. Thousands of curling, twisting pieces in red, yellow, blue, and green stack upward like a frozen burst of colour. Here is the second surprise. Visitors can lie down beneath part of the display and look straight up through a glass ceiling covered with more of his work.

The museum also moves people around in an unusual way. A broad spiral ramp connects the floors. Children often run ahead on it, and parents follow behind. As you walk, you see different levels open up around you, and the glass tower turns slowly in your view. It is worth taking the ramp at least once, rather than the stairs or the lift. The building makes more sense when you circle through it.

Now listen for music from an older age. The museum holds a historic carousel from the early twentieth century. It once turned in Broad Ripple Park, a neighbourhood on the north side of Indianapolis. The carved animals were made by hand, with painted manes, glass eyes, and polished brass poles. Generations of local families rode it before it came here and was carefully restored. For many grandparents, this is the same ride they remember from their own childhood.

Other galleries carry visitors much further away. In one area, called Take Me There, families explore everyday life in other countries. The galleries have featured places such as Egypt, China, and Greece. Children can try on clothing, visit a model home or market, and handle objects that people actually use. The aim is simple. The world becomes less strange when you have stood, even briefly, in someone else's kitchen.

One gallery asks for a quieter voice. It is called The Power of Children. It tells the stories of three young people who faced great injustice. Anne Frank hid from the Nazis during the Second World War. Ruby Bridges was a small girl who helped integrate a school in the American South. Ryan White was a teenager from Indiana who faced fear and prejudice after he was diagnosed with AIDS. Each story shows that children can shape history, not only learn about it.

Science has its own space too, where children build, test, and get a little messy. Outside, the museum has added a large sports area where families can try different games in the fresh air. Indiana takes its sport seriously, especially basketball, so this fits the state well. Across the whole campus, the same pattern repeats. There are fewer glass cases than you might expect, and many more things to touch, push, climb, and try.

It is easy to think of a children's museum as a place only for children. Watch the adults around you, though. A father reads a fossil label out loud and then reads it again, more slowly, for himself. A grandmother pauses at the carousel and smiles at nothing in particular. The best exhibits here work on two levels. Children see adventure. Adults often see their own curiosity again, the kind that daily life sometimes pushes aside.

So before you move on, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Notice the light on the coloured glass, the hum of voices, and the small hands pressing against the displays. A century ago, this whole place began as a few donated objects in a carriage house. As you walk back outside, look up at the dinosaurs on the walls one more time. They are still trying to get in.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the spiral ramp at least once between floors.
Arrive early on weekends to avoid the biggest school crowds.
Check whether the carousel requires a separate ride token.
Step outside afterwards to photograph the dinosaur sculptures.
Plan quiet time in The Power of Children gallery.
