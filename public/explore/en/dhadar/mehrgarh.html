The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves across the Kachi plain and carries a fine, pale dust. Underfoot, the ground is hard and cracked, scattered with small fragments of red and buff pottery. To the west, the dark ridges of the Balochistan hills rise from the haze. Nothing here looks grand. There are no towers and no gates. There are only low, weathered mounds of earth. Yet you are standing at one of the oldest farming villages known in all of South Asia.

This is Mehrgarh, close to the town of Dhadar, at the foot of the Bolan Pass. For thousands of years, that pass has linked the high plateau of Balochistan with the river plains below. Herders, traders and travellers have moved through this gap since prehistoric times. The Bolan River still runs nearby. Its water and its silt made this corner of the plain a good place to settle... and people noticed that very early.

For a long time, scholars believed that farming in this region arrived late, and mostly from the west. Mehrgarh changed that view. From the 1970s, a French archaeological mission led by Jean-François Jarrige excavated here, working with Pakistani colleagues. Season after season, they dug down through layer after layer of settlement. The deepest layers turned out to be astonishingly old. The earliest village life here began around 7000 BCE, and perhaps a little earlier.

Think about what that means. When the first farmers lived on this spot, the cities of the Indus Valley did not yet exist. Writing did not exist anywhere in the world. The pyramids of Egypt lay thousands of years in the future. Yet here, on this dusty plain, people were already building permanent homes, storing grain and raising animals. Mehrgarh is one of the places where the long story of settled life in South Asia begins.

The first villagers built with mud brick. Their houses were small, rectangular and divided into rooms. Some buildings were divided into tiny compartments, and archaeologists think many of these were used for storing grain. The farmers grew barley, and they also grew forms of wheat. Charred seeds and the impressions of grain in mud bricks helped researchers to identify these early crops. So the soil you are standing on once held some of the region's first harvests.

Animals tell an equally important story. In the lowest layers, the bones belong mostly to wild game, such as gazelle and wild cattle. Higher up, the picture shifts. Sheep, goats and cattle become more common. Among them is the zebu, the humped cattle that you still see across South Asia today. Many researchers see Mehrgarh as one of the early places where people in this region domesticated zebu. The animals in fields across Pakistan and India carry a very long history.

Now comes one of the most surprising discoveries. In burials from the early village, scientists studied human teeth. Several molars showed small, neat holes, drilled while the people were still alive. The study, published in 2006, described this as evidence of very early dentistry, from roughly nine thousand to seven and a half thousand years ago. Researchers think the villagers may have used flint drill tips, the same tools they used to make beads. It is strange to imagine a dentist working here, on the edge of the hills... but the teeth are clear evidence.

The graves hold other clues. People were often buried with personal objects. These included beads of shell, stone and later copper. Some of the shells came from the coast of the Arabian Sea, far to the south. Some blue stones, such as turquoise and lapis lazuli, came from distant mountain sources. So even this early village was connected to wider networks. Goods travelled across long distances, from hand to hand, through passes like the Bolan behind you.

Here is another small detail with a big meaning. Inside a copper bead from one burial, researchers found mineralised plant fibres. They identified them as cotton. This is among the earliest known evidence for the use of cotton anywhere in the world. Today, cotton fields cover large parts of Pakistan, and cotton cloth is part of daily life across the region. One thread of that story leads back to a single bead in a grave here.

Over the following millennia, the village grew and changed. The settlement shifted across the plain, so that different mounds belong to different periods. Pottery became more refined. Some pots were made on the wheel and painted with bold geometric patterns. Craftspeople worked with copper. In later layers, small clay figurines appear, many showing women with elaborate hairstyles and ornaments. Their exact meaning is debated. They may have had a ritual purpose, but nobody can say for certain.

Around the middle of the third millennium BCE, Mehrgarh was abandoned. People did not simply disappear, however. Nearby sites, such as Nausharo, continued to be occupied. By then, the great cities of the Indus Valley civilisation were rising to the east. Many archaeologists see Mehrgarh as one of its deep roots. The farming, the crafts and the trade networks that began here helped to prepare the ground for those later cities, with their planned streets and brick drains.

As you walk, look closely at the surface of the mounds. Erosion keeps bringing small pieces of the past into view. You may see fragments of pottery, some with traces of painted lines. You may notice the faint edges of mud-brick walls in the cut faces of the earth. Please leave everything where it lies. Each fragment belongs to its layer, and its position helps archaeologists to read the site. The plain itself is the archive here.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look around. Notice the hills to the west, the opening of the pass, and the flat plain stretching away toward the east. The first villagers looked at almost the same horizon. They watched the same light change across these hills in the evening. As you walk back across the cracked earth, listen again to the wind... and to the quiet of a place where people first chose to stay.
