The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Out here it moves across open ground with very little to stop it — a dry, steady sound that brushes over gravel and low stone. The light is hard and pale. Underfoot, the hilltop is loose and pebbly, and the horizon is wide in every direction. This small rise in the northeastern Sinai desert does not look like much at first. Yet the few walls on this hill held some of the most debated words ever found from the ancient Near East.

The name itself is a clue to the landscape. Kuntillet Ajrud is usually understood to mean something like the small hill of Ajrud, and the word Ajrud is linked locally with nearby water sources. In Hebrew, the site is often called Horvat Teman, the ruin of Teman. Both names point to the same idea. This was a lonely rise near water, beside routes that crossed the desert. In this land, water and a safe place to rest decided where people stopped.

The ruins date to the Iron Age. Most scholars place the main occupation around the late ninth or early eighth century BCE, roughly two and a half thousand years before the present day, and more. That was the time of the kingdoms of Israel and Judah, of Phoenician traders on the coast, and of caravans moving between Arabia, Egypt and the Mediterranean. The site does not seem to have been used for very long. It was a short chapter, but the people who stayed here left an unusual record behind.

The archaeological work that made this place known came in the 1970s. An Israeli team from Tel Aviv University, led by the archaeologist Ze'ev Meshel, excavated the hilltop in 1975 and 1976. At that time, Sinai was under Israeli control. Later, after the peace agreement between Egypt and Israel, the peninsula returned to Egypt. The full scholarly report took many years to appear, and the discussion about what the team found still continues today.

So what stood here? The main structure was a rectangular building made of local stone, with plastered walls and an entrance area lined with benches. There were storage rooms and a courtyard. A second, smaller building stood nearby. It was not a city, and it was not a fortress in the usual sense. Many scholars describe it as a way station — a place where travellers could stop, find water, and perhaps leave an offering. Others see a religious function. The evidence allows more than one reading.

Now, the first surprise. The plaster on some walls once carried painted scenes and written texts in ink. Much of it was found in fragments, fallen and broken. Some of the wall inscriptions are written in a script close to Phoenician, the alphabet of the coastal trading cities in what is now Lebanon. That detail matters. It shows that people with links to the coast, or scribes trained in that tradition, passed through this remote desert stop.

The most famous finds, however, are two large storage jars. Archaeologists call this kind of jar a pithos, a big clay vessel for holding grain, oil or water. On their surfaces, someone drew figures and wrote short texts in ancient Hebrew. These were not carefully planned monuments. They look more like informal drawings and blessings, added by people who were here for a while. That casual quality is part of their value.

Here is the second surprise, and the reason scholars still argue about this hill. Some of the inscriptions give blessings in the name of Yahweh, the God of Israel. One text mentions Yahweh of Samaria, and another mentions Yahweh of Teman. Both phrases are followed by the words and his Asherah. Asherah was the name of a goddess known across the region, and also the name of a sacred wooden pole or symbol. Which meaning the writers intended is the heart of the debate.

Why does it matter? The Hebrew Bible describes a worship centred on one God. These short texts suggest that, in daily life, some people's religious practice may have looked more varied. Some researchers read the phrase as a goddess paired with Yahweh. Others read it as a cult object, or as a symbol of blessing. No single view has won. What is clear is that a few lines of ink on a jar in the desert changed how many people study ancient Israelite religion.

The drawings on the jars add more questions. Among them are standing figures that resemble Bes, a protective Egyptian god often shown with a broad face and short legs. There is a seated figure who seems to play a lyre, a small stringed instrument. There is a cow nursing a calf, a motif known widely in the art of the region. Whether these images connect to the written words beside them is uncertain. They may have been drawn at different times, by different hands.

It is worth knowing that the objects themselves are not here. The jars, the plaster fragments and other finds were removed during excavation for study and safekeeping. What remains on the hill today is the outline of stone walls, low and weathered, and the shape of the ground. If you are standing here, you are seeing the setting, not the treasures. But the setting explains a great deal. It shows how exposed this place was, and how much a building with water nearby meant to anyone crossing this country.

A practical note. North Sinai is a region with security restrictions, and access to remote sites like this one is often limited or closed. Visiting may require official permission, local guides, and current advice from the authorities. Conditions change, so check before you travel. Out here there is no shade worth the name, and no services. Water, sun protection and a reliable plan matter far more than any itinerary.

Take a moment now to look at the line of the land around the hill. Think of the caravans that may have come through — camels or donkeys loaded with goods, people tired from the heat, a scribe with ink and a brush. Someone sat near these walls and wrote a blessing. Perhaps it was a prayer for a safe journey. Perhaps it was a way to pass a quiet evening. We do not know their names.

As you turn to leave, listen again to the wind over the gravel. The hill is small, and the walls barely rise above the ground. Yet the words once kept here still travel through books, lectures and museum halls around the world. Look back once from the path. Notice how quickly the rise blends into the desert, and how easy it would be to pass it by — as so many travellers once did, before stopping for water.
