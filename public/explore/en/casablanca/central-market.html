The guide

The story

The smell reaches you first — a wave of spice and citrus, salt from the Atlantic, fresh mint bundled in thick green sheaves. Step through the arched entrance and the noise folds around you: vendors calling prices in Darija, the thud of cleavers on wooden blocks, the rustle of plastic bags handed over counters piled high with olives. This is Casablanca's Central Market, the Marché Central, a working hive at the heart of the modern city where the rhythm of daily life has played out for more than a century.

The market was built between 1917 and 1920, in the early years of the French Protectorate, when Casablanca was growing from a modest port into Morocco's commercial engine. The new European quarter was laid out along wide boulevards, and this was its main marketplace. The architect Pierre Bousquet gave it a grand gateway in the Neo-Moorish style, modelled on the gates of Morocco's imperial cities, Marrakesh, Fes, Rabat and Meknes. It was built to look orderly, but the goods inside have always been Moroccan to the core.

Walk the perimeter and you pass pyramids of oranges from Berkane, fat purple eggplants, tomatoes still warm from the sun. Vendors stack their produce with care — each lemon placed just so, each bunch of parsley trimmed and tied. The displays are small works of geometry, rebuilt every morning. This is not a tourist bazaar. The customers are Casablancan housewives, restaurant buyers, office workers on lunch breaks, all moving through with the practiced efficiency of people who know exactly which stall has the sweetest strawberries this week.

The fish section sits under the octagonal central cupola, where the catch comes in fresh each day. Sardines gleam silver. Prawns lie on crushed ice. Monkfish, sea bream, squid — the variety shifts with the season and the mood of the Atlantic. Oysters from Dakhla are shucked on the spot, one by one. Fishmongers gut and scale on request, their knives flashing. The air here is sharp and briny, a reminder that the ocean is close by.

Turn inland and you enter the spice quarter. Sacks of cumin, coriander seed, paprika, saffron threads in small glass vials. Dried roses for tea. Blocks of aker fassi, the poppy-red cosmetic paste women use for lips and cheeks. The vendors here are often older men, seated on stools, who measure out portions in brass scales and fold them into paper cones. They know the blends by heart — ras el hanout, the complex thirteen-spice mix, varies slightly from stall to stall, each merchant guarding a family ratio.

Above the stalls, daylight filters down through the roof. The market has weathered a great deal: a bomb attack by the nationalist resistance fighter Muhammad Zarqtuni in December 1953, independence in 1956, the economic shifts of the seventies and eighties, the rise of supermarkets in the suburbs. It remains stubbornly alive, a place where haggling still happens and cash changes hands in crumpled dirhams.

The butcher stalls occupy the interior rows. Whole lamb carcasses hang from hooks. Chickens, some still feathered, wait in wire cages. Beef liver, kidneys, tripe — nothing goes to waste. The butchers work in white coats streaked with blood, and they know their customers by name. A woman asks for a kilo of shoulder, specifies how she wants it trimmed, and the butcher obliges without writing anything down. Trust is the currency here, built transaction by transaction over years.

Scattered among the food vendors are small cafés, barely more than counters with a few stools. They serve mint tea in glasses, thick and sweet, and sometimes a bowl of harira, the tomato-lentil soup that is a Moroccan staple. Workers take their breaks here, leaning against the tile, watching the flow of shoppers. The tea is poured from a height, the stream of liquid aerating as it falls, a gesture so automatic it looks like choreography.

The market is busiest in the morning, when the light is still cool and the produce has just been unloaded. By mid-afternoon the pace slows. The floors get hosed down, the scraps swept into bins. The rhythm is tidal, rising and falling with the day. Come early and you see the first crates stacked and awnings unfurled, the first customers arriving before the city fully wakes.

Casablanca is not a city that trades heavily on its past. It looks forward, builds upward, replaces the old with the new. The Central Market is an exception — a place that has stayed roughly true to its original function, adapting just enough to survive but never abandoning its core identity. The stalls are smaller now, the aisles more crowded, but the essential transaction — farmer to vendor to household — remains unchanged.

Outside the market, Mohammed the Fifth Boulevard runs north toward the medina, lined with cafés and phone shops and men selling newspapers from folding tables. The city hums around you, motorcycles weaving through traffic, the distant call to prayer from a nearby mosque. But inside these walls, the tempo is different. Slower. More tactile. A place where you still pick your own peppers, smell your own mint, and carry it all home in a woven basket.

As you step back into the street, the noise of the market fades but the smells linger on your clothes — coriander, salt, the faint sweetness of overripe melon. Casablanca moves fast, but the Central Market holds a pocket of the city's older self, a place where the work of feeding a metropolis still happens hand to hand, voice to voice, one careful transaction at a time.
