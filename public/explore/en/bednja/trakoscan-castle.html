The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches underfoot. Somewhere below, water laps against a reed bank. The air carries pine resin and damp leaves. Then the trees open. A white castle stands on a wooded hill above a long, quiet lake. Its towers rise against the green slopes of northern Croatia. This is Trakošćan, and it looks almost too perfect, like an illustration from a book of fairy tales. That perfection hides a story about defence, a famous family, and a nineteenth-century dream of the Middle Ages.

You are in Hrvatsko Zagorje, the hilly region north of Zagreb. Zagorje means, roughly, the land behind the hills. The region has small vineyards, scattered villages, and old churches on ridgetops. For centuries this was border country, close to the meeting point of Croatian, Hungarian, and Austrian lands. Hills like this one were valuable. From a high point, a garrison could watch the valleys and the roads that ran through them. So the first fortress here was a practical building, not a romantic one.

The castle's exact beginnings are uncertain. Most historians place a fortified tower on this hill in the late Middle Ages, probably around the thirteenth century. It was a small, tough stronghold with thick walls and limited comfort. And the name? Locals tell a popular tale that links Trakošćan to the ancient Thracians, or to a Roman fort on the same spot. It is an attractive story. But it is folklore, and there is no firm evidence for it. The truth is simpler: the name's origin is still debated.

In the fifteenth century, the castle belonged to the Counts of Celje. They were one of the most powerful noble families in Central Europe at that time. Their lands stretched across large parts of present-day Slovenia and Croatia. When their line died out, the property passed through several hands. Then, in 1584, it came to the Drašković family. That family name will follow you through every room inside, because the Draškovićs stayed for more than three and a half centuries.

The Draškovićs were a Croatian noble family. Over the generations they produced bishops, military commanders, politicians, and patrons of culture. For a long time, though, Trakošćan was not their favourite home. They had other estates and more comfortable residences. The old fortress fell slowly out of use. Its walls weathered. Its interiors emptied. By the early nineteenth century, it was close to a ruin. Here is the first surprise for many visitors: the castle you see today owes much less to the Middle Ages than its appearance suggests.

In the middle of the nineteenth century, the family decided to rebuild it. They did not restore it as a working fortress. They rebuilt it in the Romantic style, which was popular across Europe at that time. Romanticism, in architecture, meant a fascination with an idealised, poetic past. So builders added neo-Gothic details — pointed windows, crenellated walls, and decorative towers. In other words, the castle became a nineteenth-century vision of what a medieval castle should look like. It is a dream of history, built on top of real history.

The second surprise is the lake. It looks completely natural, framed by forest and reeds. In fact, the family created it as part of the same Romantic project, by damming the valley below the hill. The lake was designed to complete the picture. Seen from the far shore, the white castle sits above its own reflection. That view was not an accident. It was planned, like a painting, by people who understood landscape as a kind of theatre.

The park around the castle belongs to the same idea. Paths wind through mixed woodland of beech, oak, and conifers. The planting softens the hill and frames the walls from below. As you walk up, notice how the castle appears and disappears between the trees. Each turn of the path gives a new view. This was the English landscape-garden approach, where nature looks wild but is carefully arranged. It still works today. The approach feels like a slow reveal, step by step.

The Drašković family left Trakošćan in 1944, during the final years of the Second World War. They went abroad, and after the war the castle became state property. Then something important happened. Instead of dividing up the contents, the new authorities kept much of the collection together. In 1953, the castle opened as a museum. That decision explains why the rooms inside still feel lived in. The furniture, portraits, and objects mostly belong to this house and to this family.

Inside, you move through a series of rooms arranged by period and purpose. There are halls with dark wood panelling, heavy furniture, and tiled stoves. There are rooms filled with weapons — swords, firearms, and pieces of armour from several centuries. And everywhere there are faces. Family portraits line the walls, generation after generation of Draškovićs in uniforms, robes, and formal dresses. Together they form a kind of family album, painted in oil, covering hundreds of years.

Look out for the works of Julijana Drašković, a member of the family by marriage. She was a painter, and her pictures hang in the castle. In a house full of portraits made by others, her paintings offer something different — a family member's own view, from inside the household. It is a small, personal detail. Many visitors walk past it without noticing. Yet it brings a human voice into rooms that otherwise speak mostly of rank and lineage.

As you move through the castle, pay attention to the windows. They frame the lake, the forest, and the soft hills of Zagorje beyond. Imagine this view in winter, with bare trees and mist over the water. Or in autumn, when the beech woods turn copper and gold. The thick walls keep the interior cool, even on a hot summer day. Floors creak under your feet. The rooms smell faintly of old wood and wax. These sensations belong to the place as much as any date.

When you leave, take a few minutes for the lakeside path. From the water's edge, turn and look back up at the hill. You will see the same composition the family planned in the nineteenth century — white walls, dark trees, and still water. Listen to the birds in the reeds and the wind in the forest. A medieval watchtower stood here once, guarding a border. Now the hill holds a castle built from memory and imagination... and the lake holds its reflection.

Good to know

Key facts

Drašković family acquired the castle in 1584.
Counts of Celje owned it in the fifteenth century.
Rebuilt in Romantic neo-Gothic style, mid-nineteenth century.
The lake below is artificial, created by damming.
Opened as a public museum in 1953.
Look closer

What to look for

Neo-Gothic pointed windows added during the nineteenth-century rebuild.
Rows of Drašković family portraits in formal dress.
Paintings by Julijana Drašković, a family member and artist.
Displays of swords, firearms, and pieces of old armour.
Window views framing the lake and Zagorje hills.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the lakeside path for the classic castle reflection.
Wear comfortable shoes for the uphill walk through woods.
Visit in autumn when the beech forest turns gold.
Allow extra time to see the collection unhurried.
Bring a light layer; the stone interiors stay cool.
