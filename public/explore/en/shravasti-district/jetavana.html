The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the trees, the air carries a low hum of chanting, rising and falling in a language you may not recognise. The ground underfoot is soft earth and old brick, worn smooth by bare feet. Here and there, a small square of gold leaf catches the light. It sits pressed onto a brick by a pilgrim who passed through before you. This is Jetavana, a quiet garden of ruins in northern India, and it is one of the most important places in the Buddhist world.

To understand why people travel so far to stand here, start with a single sentence. Many of the Buddha's recorded teachings open in almost the same way. They say that the Blessed One was staying near Shravasti, in Jeta's Grove, in the park of Anathapindika. That grove is this place. Thousands of times across the old texts, the name repeats like a return address. For many Buddhists, walking here feels like walking into the opening line of a scripture they have heard all their lives.

Shravasti itself was once a great city. It was a capital of the kingdom of Kosala, one of the powerful states of ancient northern India. Traders, kings, and wandering teachers moved along its roads. Today the old city lies mostly under low mounds of earth nearby. Locals know the two main sites as Saheth and Maheth. Saheth is the monastery ground where you stand now. Maheth is the ruined city a short distance away, with its own walls and stupas.

The story of how this garden became a monastery is the best-known tale of the place. A wealthy merchant named Sudatta lived in Shravasti. People called him Anathapindika, which means roughly 'the one who feeds the unprotected', because of his generosity. He wanted to give the Buddha a place to stay. The finest grove near the city belonged to a prince named Jeta, and the prince did not want to sell it.

According to the traditional account, the prince named an impossible price. He said he would sell the land for as many gold coins as it took to cover the ground. The merchant agreed... and began laying out coins, cart after cart. Legend has it that only a small patch near the gate stayed uncovered. Moved by this devotion, Prince Jeta gave that last piece himself and built a gateway there. That is why the place carries both names — Jeta's Grove, in Anathapindika's park.

Buddhist tradition says the Buddha spent more rainy seasons here than in any other single place. In ancient India, wandering monks stopped travelling during the monsoon months. They gathered in one spot to rest, study, and teach. So Jetavana became something like a home base. Many of the teachings that shaped Buddhism were, according to the texts, spoken in this grove. When you look around, imagine monks in simple robes sitting under trees much like these.

As you walk, you will see brick foundations laid out in rectangles and squares. These are the remains of monastery buildings, cells for monks, and small shrines. They come from different periods, as later generations rebuilt on earlier ground. The walls stand only a short height now, often to knee or waist level. They read almost like a floor plan drawn in brick. You can trace where rooms once stood, where doorways opened, and where courtyards gathered people together.

One structure draws most visitors. It is known as the Gandhakuti, which means the Fragrant Hut. Tradition says this was the dwelling of the Buddha, and devotees brought flowers so often that the air around it smelled sweet. Today it is a raised brick platform. Pilgrims gather around it to chant, meditate, and leave offerings. This is often the place where you will see the most gold leaf, the most candles, and the most silence. Another ruin nearby is linked by tradition to a hut called the Kosambakuti.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look for a large, old tree surrounded by offerings and coloured cloth. This is the Anandabodhi tree. According to tradition, Ananda, the Buddha's close attendant, asked for a way for people to honour the Buddha while he was travelling. A cutting was brought from the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya, the tree where the Buddha reached enlightenment. The tree you see is honoured as part of that living line. Pilgrims tie threads and prayer flags to it.

A second surprise lies in the written record. Long after the Buddha's time, travelling monks from China walked here on foot, across mountains and deserts. Faxian came around the fifth century. Xuanzang came in the seventh century. Both left written accounts of Shravasti. By then, parts of the monastery were already in ruins. Centuries later, in the nineteenth century, British archaeologists such as Alexander Cunningham used these old travel notes to help identify the site and begin excavations.

Now look at the people around you. On any given day, you may see monks in saffron robes from Thailand, maroon robes from Tibet, or deep red and brown from Myanmar and Sri Lanka. Groups sit in circles on the grass and chant together. Some walk slowly around the ruins in a clockwise direction, a traditional sign of respect. Nearby, several countries have built their own temples and monasteries. Together they make the wider area feel like a meeting point for the whole Buddhist world.

The atmosphere here is gentle, and visitors are welcome to share it. Walk quietly, especially near people who are meditating. Remove your shoes where you see others doing so. Do not climb on the ancient walls, because the brick is fragile. If a group is chanting, it is fine to sit at a respectful distance and listen. Many visitors find that the best way to experience Jetavana is simply to slow down and stay a while.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the shade under the trees and the warm colour of the old brick. Notice the birds, the soft voices, the small flicker of a candle on a stone ledge. More than two thousand years ago, people came to this grove to listen. They still do. As you walk back toward the gate, remember the old story of the last uncovered patch of ground — and the prince who chose to give it freely.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to hear pilgrim groups chanting.
Remove shoes where you see other visitors doing so.
Walk around shrines clockwise, following local custom.
Visit Maheth nearby to see the old city ruins.
Bring water and a hat; shade is patchy.
