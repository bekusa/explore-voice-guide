The guide

The story

Feel the stone first. Run your hand along the wall and it comes away faintly gritty, warm in colour even on a grey Cheshire day. This is red sandstone, quarried from the same ridge you are standing on. Around you, the woods move in the wind, rooks call from the trees, and the walls rise in towers and battlements. It looks like a fortress from the age of knights. But look again, and something feels slightly too complete.

That feeling is the first surprise of Peckforton Castle. This is not a medieval stronghold. It is a Victorian country house, built in the middle of the nineteenth century to look and work like a castle from the thirteenth century. No army ever besieged it. No knight ever rode out of this gatehouse to war. And yet it was built with a seriousness that sets it apart from many decorative mock castles of its time.

The man behind it was John Tollemache, a wealthy landowner who later became the first Baron Tollemache. He owned a large estate across this part of Cheshire. In the 1840s, he decided to build a family home on the Peckforton Hills. He did not want a classical mansion with columns and sash windows. He wanted a castle... and he wanted it done properly.

For the design, Tollemache turned to Anthony Salvin. Salvin was an architect who knew real medieval castles well. He had studied and repaired several of them, and he understood how they were laid out and how they were defended. At Peckforton, he did not simply add battlements to an ordinary house. He planned the whole building around a courtyard, with a gatehouse, towers, and a great hall, much as a medieval builder might have done.

This is why the castle feels so convincing. The walls are thick. The windows are narrow on the outer faces and wider where they look into the courtyard. The arrangement follows medieval logic rather than Victorian taste for display. Architects of the period argued about buildings like this. Some admired the scholarship. Others thought it was strange to build a working fortress in an age of railways and gas lighting. Both views make sense when you stand here.

Now turn your attention outward, towards the next hill. On a clear day, you can see another castle on a rocky crag nearby. That is Beeston Castle, and it is the real thing. It was begun in the thirteenth century by Ranulf, Earl of Chester, and today it stands as a ruin. Here is the second surprise: from parts of this ridge, you can see a genuine medieval castle and a Victorian imitation facing each other across the valley.

That view may have been part of the appeal for Tollemache. Building a castle within sight of Beeston connected his new home to the long story of this landscape. The sandstone ridge has been a place of lookouts and strongholds for centuries. A castle here does not look out of place. It looks as if it belongs, even though it is less than two hundred years old.

Tollemache was more than a castle builder. He was known as an improving landlord, someone who invested in his estate rather than simply collecting rents. Across the surrounding countryside, you can find estate cottages and farm buildings from this period. If you drive through the nearby villages, look for solid, well-built cottages with a similar character. They are part of the same story as the castle above them.

Inside, the castle was a family home for generations. It had to be warm, practical, and comfortable, which is a challenge in a building designed to look like a fortress. Salvin balanced the two. The great hall gave the house a grand, medieval heart, with a high space suited to gatherings. Around it, the family rooms, kitchens, and service areas were arranged for daily life. A large household of servants once kept all of this running.

In the twentieth century, life changed for great country houses across England. Running a building of this size became very difficult, and many families left their ancestral homes. Peckforton was no exception. Over the decades, the castle passed through several uses before finding a new life as a hotel and wedding venue. Today, the courtyard that once served one family welcomes guests, diners, and wedding parties.

The castle has also had a life on screen. Its towers and gatehouse look so medieval that film and television crews have used it as a stand-in for the real thing. That is perhaps the best compliment Salvin could receive. A building designed to imitate the thirteenth century now plays the part of the thirteenth century for cameras. Visitors who watch period dramas sometimes recognise the walls without knowing where they saw them.

The castle is now protected as a listed building of the highest grade in England. That status recognises its importance as a piece of Victorian architecture, not as a medieval relic. It is valued precisely because it shows how the Victorians thought about the past. They looked back at the Middle Ages with admiration, and some of them, like Tollemache and Salvin, tried to rebuild that world in stone.

If you have time, the landscape around the castle rewards a walk. The Sandstone Trail, a long-distance footpath, runs along this ridge through woodland and open hillside. From the higher ground, the Cheshire Plain spreads out below, flat and green, with farms and hedgerows in every direction. Nantwich lies out there too, a market town with its own long history, only a short drive away.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in front of the gatehouse. Listen to the trees and the birds. Look at the colour of the stone, the way the light changes it from rust to rose. You are standing in a Victorian dream of the Middle Ages, built with care and conviction. As you walk away, glance back once at the towers... and then towards Beeston on its crag, the real castle and the remembered one, side by side.
