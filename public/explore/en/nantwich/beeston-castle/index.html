The guide

The story

Put your hand on the stone here. It feels rough, slightly warm, and a little gritty, like coarse sandpaper. This is sandstone, the same rock that rises under your feet in a sudden cliff. Around you, the wind moves across open grass. Far below, the green fields of the Cheshire Plain stretch out in every direction. This is Beeston Castle. It stands on its crag a short drive north of Nantwich, and it has watched over this countryside for a very long time.

Before we talk about knights and sieges, look at the shape of the land itself. The plain around you is almost flat. Then, without warning, this crag rises out of it, with steep faces on several sides. Anyone standing up here can see a very long way. On a clear day, the hills of Wales sit along the western horizon. To the east, the land climbs slowly toward the Pennines. It is easy to understand why people kept coming back to this rock.

And they came back long before any castle existed. Archaeologists have found evidence that people lived and worked on this hilltop thousands of years ago. Bronze Age metalworkers left traces of their craft here. Later, in the Iron Age, people built defensive banks and ditches around the summit, making this a hillfort. So the medieval castle you are exploring sits on top of a much older story. Some of the earthworks near the outer walls follow lines that ancient builders first dug.

The stone castle came in the thirteenth century. It was the work of Ranulf, Earl of Chester, one of the most powerful men in England at the time. He began building in the 1220s, after returning from crusade in the Holy Land. Some historians think he was influenced by the fortresses he saw in the eastern Mediterranean. Whether or not that is true, the design here makes clever use of the rock. Nature does much of the defending.

Think about how the castle works as you walk. There is an outer ward, a large enclosed area on the lower slopes, protected by a curtain wall with towers. Then, higher up, there is an inner ward at the very top of the crag. To reach it, an attacker had to cross a deep ditch cut straight into the solid sandstone. Imagine the labour of that — men with hand tools, carving away bedrock, piece by piece, to make a dry moat.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look closely at the walls of that rock-cut ditch. You can often see the marks of the tools that shaped it, still visible after centuries of rain and wind. Notice, too, how the castle uses no large central keep, the great tower many people expect in a medieval castle. Instead, the cliff and the ditch do that job. The builders trusted the rock more than any single tower.

Earl Ranulf did not live long enough to enjoy his creation for many years. He died in 1232. His line ended soon after, and in 1237 the castle passed to the Crown, under King Henry the Third. From then on, Beeston was a royal castle. It served kings as a stronghold in this border region between England and Wales, a place where power and supplies could be held securely.

Now for a story you should treat as legend, not history. Locals say that King Richard the Second, who ruled at the end of the fourteenth century, hid a great treasure here. According to the tale, gold and jewels were lowered into the castle well and never recovered. People have searched over the years. No treasure has ever been found. But the legend has kept this hilltop in local imagination for generations, and children still ask about it at the well.

The well itself is real, and it is remarkable. It was cut down through the rock in the inner ward, and it is very deep. A castle under siege lives or dies by its water supply, and this well meant the garrison on the summit could hold out. When you stand near it, think of the patience it took to dig straight down through stone, far below the level of the plain, simply to reach water.

Centuries later, the castle saw war again. During the English Civil War of the seventeenth century, when the forces of King Charles the First fought those of Parliament, Beeston was held for Parliament at first. Then, in 1643, a small group of Royalist soldiers seized it in a surprise attack. The story goes that they climbed the steep side of the crag. The Parliamentary commander surrendered, and the castle changed hands with very little fighting.

What followed was much harder. Parliamentary forces besieged the castle for a long period. The Royalist garrison inside grew short of food. In 1645 they finally surrendered and marched out. Afterward, Parliament ordered the castle to be deliberately damaged, so it could never be used as a fortress again. This process is called slighting. Much of the ruin you see today is the result of that decision, not simply the slow work of time.

As you look out from the summit, find the other castle on the neighbouring ridge. That is Peckforton Castle. It looks medieval, but it is not. It was built in the nineteenth century as a grand country house in a castle style. So from this one spot, you can compare a real thirteenth-century fortress, worn and broken, with a Victorian dream of what a castle should be. The contrast says a lot about how later generations imagined the Middle Ages.

Today, English Heritage cares for Beeston Castle. The paths are steep in places, and the climb to the inner ward takes some effort. But that effort is part of the experience. Every step up shows you a little more of the plain. You begin to feel what a soldier or a lookout felt here — exposed to the wind, but able to see anyone approaching long before they arrived.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind in the grass and the trees on the slopes below. Look at the red and grey colours in the sandstone, softened by moss and weather. People have stood on this rock for thousands of years, for safety, for power, for the view. As you walk back down the path, notice how the plain slowly rises to meet you, and the crag grows tall behind you once again.
