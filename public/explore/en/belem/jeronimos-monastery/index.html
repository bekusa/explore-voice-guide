The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the wide pavement in front of the monastery and look at the stone. In the morning it is pale cream. By late afternoon it turns the colour of honey. This is lioz, a local limestone, and it holds the light of the Tagus River the way a sail holds wind. Behind you, traffic and trams pass. In front of you, a long, low façade runs for what feels like the length of a whole street, carved almost everywhere you look.

This is the Jerónimos Monastery, in the riverside district of Belém, just west of central Lisbon. It was commissioned by King Manuel the First at the very start of the sixteenth century. That timing matters. Only a few years earlier, Vasco da Gama had sailed from this shoreline and reached India by sea. Portugal suddenly sat at the centre of a new global trade in pepper, cinnamon and cloves. The monastery was the king's answer in stone: a thank-you, a tomb, and a statement of power.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The building was paid for, in large part, by the spice trade itself. Historians often describe a tax of around five percent on goods arriving from Africa and Asia, set aside for this project. People sometimes call it the pepper money. So when you look at this carved limestone, you are looking at cargo holds full of spices, turned into walls, columns and saints.

The site was not chosen at random. A small chapel once stood near here, used by sailors before they left on long voyages. Tradition holds that Vasco da Gama and his crew spent their last night ashore praying there before departing for India. Whether every detail of that story is true, the meaning is clear. This is where Portugal said goodbye to its sailors, and where it later chose to remember them.

The king gave the monastery to the Order of Saint Jerome, a community of monks known in Portuguese as the Hieronymites. That is where the name Jerónimos comes from. Their duties were partly spiritual and partly practical. They prayed for the soul of the king and his family. They also offered comfort to sailors heading out to sea. For more than three centuries, monks lived, studied and sang inside these walls.

Now walk toward the south portal, the large doorway facing the river. It is a tower of carved stone, crowded with saints, canopies and tiny figures. Between the two doors stands a statue of Henry the Navigator, the prince linked to Portugal's early voyages along the African coast. Above him, look for the Virgin Mary. The doorway was largely the work of João de Castilho, one of the master builders who shaped this monastery after its first architect, Diogo de Boitaca.

The style around you has its own name: Manueline, after King Manuel. Think of it as late Gothic, the pointed medieval style, but dressed for the sea. Stone is carved into twisted ropes, knots, coral, seaweed and shells. You will also see the armillary sphere, a globe made of rings used in navigation, which was the king's personal emblem. And you will see the cross of the Order of Christ, the same cross painted on Portuguese sails.

Step inside the church of Santa Maria and the noise of the street drops away. The air is cool. The nave is wide, and the columns are thin and tall, carved with patterns that climb toward a vaulted ceiling. Many people say the columns look like palm trees, their ribs spreading out like fronds. Here is a second surprise. When the great earthquake of 1755 destroyed much of Lisbon, this vault held. The monastery survived largely intact.

Near the entrance, on either side, are two tombs on carved stone supports. One belongs to Vasco da Gama, whose remains were brought here in the nineteenth century. The other honours Luís de Camões, the poet who wrote The Lusiads, the great Portuguese epic about da Gama's voyage. Historians are not fully certain that the poet's real remains lie inside. Still, the pairing is deliberate. The sailor and the man who turned his journey into poetry face each other across the nave.

From the church, find your way into the cloister. For many visitors, this is the heart of the monastery. It is a square courtyard ringed by two storeys of arcades, every arch filled with carved stone lace. Walk slowly. Look at the column bases, the window frames, the small creatures hidden in the decoration. Light falls through the arches in shifting patterns, and the garden in the centre gives the stone room to breathe.

In one corner of the cloister lies a more modern grave. It belongs to Fernando Pessoa, one of the most important Portuguese poets of the twentieth century. He wrote under many invented names, each with its own personality and style. His remains were moved here in the 1980s. So in one building you find the explorer, the epic poet of the sixteenth century, and a restless modern writer. It is a kind of national library made of tombs.

The monks' story ended in the 1830s, when Portugal dissolved its religious orders. The buildings then housed an orphanage and school for many years. In the nineteenth century a long new wing was added in a revival style that copies the old Manueline look. Today that wing holds the national archaeology museum and the maritime museum. And in 2007, European leaders signed the Treaty of Lisbon here, which reshaped how the European Union works.

Before you go, step back out onto the square and turn toward the river. Across the gardens stands the Monument to the Discoveries, and further west, the Tower of Belém. Together with this monastery, the tower forms a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Take one last look at the long façade. Notice how the stone has warmed or cooled since you arrived. Then perhaps follow the smell of cinnamon down the street, toward the bakeries, and the pastry that local tradition links to the monks who once lived here.
