The guide

The story

Listen first. Water runs somewhere ahead of you, splashing over stone. Under your feet, the path is uneven, and the walls on either side press close. Look down at the ground and at the low walls. Broken tiles, glazed fragments and rounded river stones are set into the concrete. The air is cooler here than on the open boulevards outside. You have entered a city that one man built in secret, piece by piece, out of what everyone else threw away.

That man was Nek Chand. He was born in a village in what is now Pakistan. After the Partition of India in 1947, he crossed into India, like millions of other people. He found work in the new city of Chandigarh as a road inspector for the Public Works Department. It was a modest government job. Nobody around him had any idea that he was about to start one of the strangest projects in modern India.

To understand this garden, you need to understand the city around it. Chandigarh was planned in the 1950s as a new capital for Punjab. The Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier led its design. He planned a modernist city of concrete, straight roads and numbered sectors. To make room for it, many old villages were cleared and demolished. Their bricks, pots, tiles and household objects became rubble. Nek Chand saw that rubble — and he saw something else inside it.

In the late 1950s, he began collecting waste on his bicycle. He carried it to a patch of forest land near Sukhna Lake, at the edge of the new city. This land was a protected government zone. Nothing was supposed to be built there. So he worked quietly, often in the evenings and at night, after his official shifts. Over many years, he cleared a hidden space and began to fill it with figures, walls and courtyards.

For almost two decades, the secret held. Then, in the mid-1970s, officials discovered what he had made. By then, the hidden garden covered a large area of forest. Legally, it was an illegal structure, and it could have been demolished. But the people who saw it were amazed. Public support grew quickly. Instead of tearing it down, the authorities decided to protect it. In 1976, the garden opened to the public.

Here is the first surprise. The city did not only forgive Nek Chand. It gave him a salary to continue his work full time, and it gave him a team of workers to help. A man who had been breaking the rules became the official builder of his own creation. Collection centres were set up around the city to gather waste for the garden. The rubbish of a growing city now had a destination.

As you walk on, look closely at the sculptures. From a distance, you see crowds of figures — dancers, soldiers, animals, birds and village women. Up close, the materials become clear. Some figures wear skins of broken ceramic plates. Others are covered in fragments of coloured glass bangles. Look for pieces of electrical fittings, old pipes and bits of toilet bowls and sinks. Every object had a first life somewhere in Chandigarh before it came here.

Notice how the figures stand together. Very few appear alone. They are arranged in rows and groups, sometimes dozens of them on a single slope. Each figure is slightly different in face, posture and colour. Together, they look like an audience, or a village crowd at a festival. Many visitors feel the figures are watching them. Take a moment to stand still and let your eyes move along the lines. The repetition is part of the effect.

Now pay attention to the paths themselves. The garden is a series of linked spaces, not one open park. Narrow passages connect the courtyards. In some places, the archways are low, and adults must bend their heads to pass. This design is deliberate. You move through tight, shadowed corridors, and then a wide courtyard opens suddenly in front of you. The squeeze and release changes how each new space feels. It is a small piece of theatre, built into the walls.

The garden grew in stages, and you can feel the change as you go deeper. The earlier sections feel intimate and enclosed, with winding lanes and small chambers. Later sections open up. You will find a large waterfall and pools fed by running water. Further on, there are tall arches and swings hanging from them. On busy days, you will hear children and adults laughing as they swing. The garden is meant to be touched and used, not only viewed.

Here is the second surprise. This garden does not only exist in Chandigarh. Nek Chand's work became known around the world. Over the years, his sculptures were shown in museums and galleries in Europe and America. Collectors and artists began to describe his work as outsider art — art made by self-taught people outside the formal art world. India also honoured him, and a postage stamp featuring the garden was issued in the early 1980s. Nek Chand died in 2015.

It is worth thinking about what this place says beside the city around it. Le Corbusier's Chandigarh is a city of order and plan. Nek Chand's garden is a city of curves, crowds and recycled memory. Many of the fragments here came from the villages that the modern city replaced. In a quiet way, the old life of the region survives inside these walls. The broken pots and tiles carry traces of homes that no longer stand.

Before you leave, stop somewhere quiet. Touch one of the walls and feel the rough concrete and the smooth edges of tile. Look at one single figure for a full minute. Notice the small pieces it is made from — a cup handle, a chip of blue glaze, a curved stone. As you walk back out toward the open roads, look at the ordinary waste of the city around you. You may see it a little differently now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for uneven paths and steps.
Arrive early to walk the narrow lanes before crowds.
Watch your head at the low archways between courtyards.
Bring water, especially on hot afternoons.
Combine your visit with a walk around nearby Sukhna Lake.
