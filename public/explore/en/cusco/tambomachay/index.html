The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look at anything, listen. Somewhere ahead of you, water is running over stone. It is a small sound, steady and clear, and it has been running here for centuries. The air up here is thin and cool, and it often smells of wet grass and eucalyptus. Under your feet the path is packed earth and loose gravel. You are standing in a quiet fold of the hills above Cusco, at a place built around one simple thing: water that never stops.

This is Tambomachay, an Inca site in the hills northeast of the city. It sits higher than Cusco itself, so if you feel a little short of breath, that is normal. Take the climb slowly. The site is part of a group of Inca monuments that circle the old capital, together with Saqsaywaman, Q'enqo and Puka Pukara. Many visitors see all four in a single morning. But Tambomachay has a different mood from the others. It is not a fortress. It is a place of water.

The name comes from Quechua, the language of the Inca and still the language of many families in these valleys. A tambo was a waystation, a place where travellers rested along the great Inca road network. The second part of the name is usually translated as a resting place or a cave. Put together, the name is often given as something like the resting place. Spanish speakers gave the site another name, El Baño del Inca, the Bath of the Inca. You will hear both names today.

Now look at the stone walls in front of you. They rise in a series of terraces, one above the other, set against the slope. The stones are grey limestone, cut and fitted together without mortar. Some are rectangular. Others have many sides, and each one locks into its neighbours like a puzzle piece. This style is called polygonal masonry. It was the most careful and most demanding kind of Inca building, and it was usually saved for places of special importance.

Here is something many visitors miss. The Inca did not build like this everywhere. Ordinary houses were often made of rough fieldstone set in mud. The fine fitted stonework you see here took enormous labour. Every face was shaped by hand, using harder stones as hammers. So the quality of this wall tells you something. Whoever built Tambomachay wanted it to be seen, and wanted it to last. And it has lasted — through earthquakes that brought down many Spanish buildings in Cusco.

On the upper terrace, look for the niches. They are shaped like trapezoids, wider at the bottom and narrower at the top. You will see this shape again and again in Inca architecture, in doors, windows and niches. Builders favoured it for a practical reason. A trapezoid spreads weight down and outward, which helps a wall stay standing when the ground shakes. In a region of frequent earthquakes, this was not decoration. It was engineering. What the niches once held, nobody can say for certain.

Now follow the sound of the water down to the lowest level. Here a stream comes out of the wall through a stone channel. Then it does something quietly remarkable. The single flow divides and falls as two streams, side by side, into a stone basin below. Watch them for a moment. They look almost identical. Water engineers still admire this kind of control. The Inca were able to guide water through hidden channels, at steady speed, using only stone and gravity.

Where does the water come from? It is fed by a spring higher up the hillside. The Inca built canals to carry it into the site, through the terraces and out at the fountains. Even today, the flow stays steady through dry months and rainy months alike. That reliability was precious. In the Andes, water meant life for crops, for animals and for people. It was also sacred. Springs, rivers and lakes were treated as living powers and honoured with offerings.

So what was Tambomachay for? The honest answer is that archaeologists are not completely sure. Many scholars believe it was a shrine linked to the worship of water. Others suggest it was also a royal retreat, a place where the Inca ruler and his court could rest away from the city. Some accounts link it to the emperor Pachacuti, the ruler who rebuilt Cusco, but this connection is a tradition rather than a firm record. Most likely, the site was several things at once.

There is a second detail worth noticing. Turn around and look across the road and up the slope. On the far side stand the reddish ruins of Puka Pukara, whose name means red fortress. Its walls are rougher and it had a lookout position over the valley. Many people think the two sites worked together, with Puka Pukara watching the route while Tambomachay served as a place of rest and ritual. Seeing them face each other helps you imagine this road as a living route.

Locals have their own stories about this water. According to one popular tale, drinking from the fountains or washing in them will keep you young. Another version says it brings luck in love. These are folk beliefs, not history, and nobody can promise you eternal youth. Still, you may see visitors cupping their hands under the stream. The water here is not treated for drinking, so it is wiser to simply touch it and feel how cold it is, fresh from the mountain.

As you walk the paths, notice the people around you. Women from nearby communities often sit near the entrance in bright woven clothing, sometimes with alpacas or llamas beside them. Many speak Quechua among themselves. Some sell textiles, and some will ask for a small tip if you take their photograph. It is polite to ask first. These families live in the same high country the Inca knew, and their presence connects the old stones to the present day.

Before you leave, stand still beside the basin one more time. Look at the two streams falling into the pool. Look up at the terraces, the grey stones tight against each other, the trapezoid niches in shadow. Listen to that same small sound you heard when you arrived. Long after the Inca court was gone, the water kept running. As you walk back toward the road, notice how quickly the sound fades behind you — and how quiet the hills become.

What to look for

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk slowly here because the altitude is higher than Cusco.
Combine this visit with Puka Pukara, Q'enqo and Saqsaywaman.
Check that your Cusco tourist ticket covers this site.
Bring a warm layer since the hillside wind feels cold.
Ask before photographing local women or their alpacas.
