The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the hum of tyres there is wind, and under the wind there is water slapping against concrete. Out here the sky takes up almost everything you can see. The sea changes colour as you watch it. Pale turquoise runs over the sandy shallows, and a deep, inky blue marks the channels where the current runs hard. You are standing at one of the longest bridges in the Florida Keys. It is a ribbon of road that seems to float between two worlds, the Gulf of Mexico on one side and the Atlantic on the other.

This is the Seven Mile Bridge. It links Knights Key, at the edge of the town of Marathon, with Little Duck Key to the southwest. And here is the first surprise. The bridge is not quite seven miles long. Measured end to end, it falls a little short of that. The name stuck anyway, because it is easy to say and it feels right. Few drivers notice the missing fraction. They are too busy looking at the water.

Look beside the modern road and you will see a second bridge. It is lower, older, and broken in places, running parallel like a shadow. That is the Old Seven Mile Bridge. Its story begins with a railroad. In the early twentieth century, the oil and hotel magnate Henry Flagler set out to carry trains all the way to Key West. Many people thought the idea was mad. Newspapers and critics called it Flagler's Folly.

The project became known as the Overseas Railroad. It was an extension of Flagler's Florida East Coast Railway, and it pushed south from island to island across open water. Workers faced heat, mosquitoes, and hurricanes. They had no fresh water nearby, so supplies came in by boat. The line reached Key West in 1912. For a few decades, passengers could ride a train across the sea, watching the waves pass beneath the carriage windows.

The long crossing here was one of the hardest parts of the whole line. Much of the old structure rests on concrete piers and arches, built to stand in moving water. Near the middle, over the deepest channel, there was once a section that swung open so tall boats could pass. When you look at the old bridge from a distance, you can see where its rhythm breaks. Pieces have been removed over the years, leaving gaps in the line.

Then came 1935. On Labor Day weekend, an extremely powerful hurricane struck the Upper Keys. It is remembered as one of the strongest storms ever to make landfall in the United States. Hundreds of people died, many of them veterans working on relief projects. Stretches of the railway were wrecked. The railroad company was already struggling with money, and it chose not to rebuild. The train to the sea was finished.

But the bridges survived. That is the second surprise. Engineers saw that the sturdy rail structures could carry cars instead. The old line was reworked into a road, and the Overseas Highway opened through the Keys in the late 1930s. On this crossing, cars drove on a narrow deck laid over the old railway bridge. Drivers remember it as tight and a little nerve-racking, with the open sea very close on both sides.

By the 1970s, the old bridge was showing its age. Traffic was heavier, and the narrow lanes were hard to maintain. So a new bridge was built alongside it, the one carrying traffic today. It opened in 1982. It is wider, smoother, and made of large concrete segments set on tall supports. Near the centre the deck climbs into a gentle hump. That rise gives boats enough room to pass beneath without any moving span.

That hump is worth noticing if you cross by car. As you climb, the horizon drops away and the view opens in every direction. On a clear day, you may see the low green line of islands, small boats tracing white lines across the flats, and pelicans gliding just above the water. Many people say this is the moment the Keys finally feel like the Keys. The mainland feels far behind you.

About two miles out from Marathon, the old bridge passes over a tiny island called Pigeon Key. It is easy to miss from the highway. During construction of the railroad, this island served as a camp for workers. Some of the wooden buildings from that era still stand. Today Pigeon Key is a historic site with exhibits about the railroad and the people who built it. Getting there needs a little planning, so check how visits work before you go.

The stretch of old bridge leading from Marathon toward Pigeon Key has been restored for people on foot and on bicycles. Walking it is a very different experience from driving. You hear your own footsteps on the concrete. You smell salt and sun-warmed stone. Looking down, you may spot fish moving through the clear water, or the dark shape of a ray. Anglers often line the rails, patient and quiet, waiting for a bite.

The bridge has also had a life on screen. Film crews have used it for its long, dramatic lines over open sea. Most famously, it appears in the 1994 action film True Lies, in a scene where part of a bridge is blown apart. The bridge has also become the course for an annual footrace. Runners cross the water from one end to the other, with nothing but sea and sky around them.

Standing here, you can read a century of choices in two lines of concrete. One bridge carried trains, then cars, and now walkers and cyclists. The other carries the daily life of the Keys: delivery trucks, holiday traffic, and residents heading home. Neither one fully replaced the other. They have simply learned to share the same stretch of sea, side by side.

So before you move on, take one more look. Follow the old bridge with your eyes until it breaks off and the water takes over. Then follow the new one as it curves toward the horizon. Notice the colour of the sea right now, because in an hour it will be different. And as you leave, listen again for that wind. It has been crossing this channel far longer than any bridge.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk or cycle the restored old bridge toward Pigeon Key.
Bring sun protection; there is almost no shade.
Check in advance how Pigeon Key visits are arranged.
Come early morning for calmer wind and softer light.
Pull over only at designated areas, never on the bridge.
