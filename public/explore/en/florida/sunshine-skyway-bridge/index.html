The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind runs off the open water and through the cables in a low, steady hum. Gulls turn above the channel. Out on the bay, the light changes from silver to green to pale blue, depending on the hour. And rising out of all that water is a long line of golden-yellow cables, fanned out like the strings of an enormous harp. This is the Sunshine Skyway Bridge, the gateway to lower Tampa Bay.

The bridge carries Interstate 275 across the mouth of Tampa Bay. It links Saint Petersburg, on the Pinellas County side, with Manatee County to the south. Before any bridge stood here, travellers had two choices. They could take a ferry across the bay, or they could drive the long way around its shore. A crossing at the mouth of the bay was a large ambition. The water is wide, and the shipping channel below must stay open to ocean-going vessels heading to the Port of Tampa.

The first Skyway opened in 1954. It was a steel cantilever bridge, a design that uses heavy trusses balanced on their piers. It carried traffic in both directions on a single span. As the Tampa Bay region grew, one span was not enough. In 1971, a second, parallel span opened beside it. From then on, northbound cars used one bridge and southbound cars used the other. For a time, the arrangement worked well.

Then came the morning of May 9, 1980. A sudden storm swept across the bay. Rain and wind cut visibility almost to nothing. A freighter called the Summit Venture was heading toward the port in those conditions. It struck one of the support piers of the southbound span. A large section of the bridge collapsed into the bay.

Thirty-five people died that morning. Several cars fell into the water, and so did a Greyhound bus. The loss shocked Florida and the whole country. One detail stays with many people who hear this story. A single car came to a stop very close to the broken edge of the roadway. Its driver survived. That image of a car halted just short of open air became one of the lasting memories of the disaster.

After the collapse, the state faced a hard question. Should it repair the damaged span, or build something new? Engineers and officials chose a new bridge. They wanted a crossing that was safer for drivers and safer for ships. The answer they chose was a cable-stayed bridge. In this design, tall towers hold up the roadway directly with straight steel cables, without the heavy trusses of the old bridge.

The new Sunshine Skyway opened in 1987. Look at how the cables work. They rise from a single line along the centre of the roadway, not from the edges. Drivers crossing the bridge have an open view of the water on both sides. The cables meet the two tall towers in a fan shape. Seen from a distance, the whole structure looks light, almost like a sail. Yet it holds up a heavy concrete deck high above the channel.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Around the main piers, near the shipping channel, you can see large concrete structures in the water. They look like small islands. Engineers call them dolphins. They are not decoration. They are there to stop a ship before it can reach the piers. Their purpose comes directly from the lessons of 1980. The bridge was built so that the same kind of accident could not happen again.

The main span between the two towers is roughly 1,200 feet long. The roadway also sits higher above the water than the old bridge did. That gives large ships more room to pass beneath. When a cargo ship or cruise ship slides under the deck, the scale becomes clear. Even a very large vessel looks small beneath those yellow cables. If you are lucky enough to see one pass, watch how slowly and carefully it moves through the channel.

In 2005, the bridge received a longer official name. It became the Bob Graham Sunshine Skyway Bridge. Bob Graham was governor of Florida during the years after the disaster, when the decision to build the new bridge was made. Most locals still simply call it the Skyway. For many people in the Tampa Bay area, the bridge is part of daily life. They see it on postcards, on licence plates of local imagination, and through their car windows on the drive to the Gulf beaches.

Now, a second surprise. The old bridges did not disappear completely. When the new Skyway opened, the old spans were taken down. But parts of their approaches were kept. Today, those long concrete ramps stretch out into the bay on both the north and south sides as fishing piers. They form the Skyway Fishing Pier State Park. Anglers come here day and night. They set up rods along the railings and wait for the tide to bring fish through the channel.

Walking out on one of those piers is a quiet experience. The old roadway ends suddenly, over open water, where the bridge once continued. From that point, you have a clear view of the new Skyway beside you. You see the old and the new side by side. The broken end of the past points toward the bridge that replaced it. Many visitors find this the most moving way to understand the story of this place.

The Skyway changes with the light. In the early morning, the cables can look almost white against a pale sky. In the late afternoon, the low sun turns them deep gold. After dark, lights along the cables and towers glow across the water. Storms also roll across the bay, especially on summer afternoons. On those days, dark clouds gather behind the towers, and the yellow cables stand out sharply against the grey.

Take a moment before you leave. Listen again to the wind in the cables, and to the water moving against the pilings. Watch the cars climbing the long slope toward the towers, small and steady against the sky. Every one of them crosses a bridge shaped by a hard memory and a careful answer to it. As you walk away, turn once more and look back. The cables will still be catching the light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stopping on the bridge itself is not allowed.
Visit the fishing piers for safe close-up views.
Come in late afternoon for golden light on the cables.
Bring sun protection; the piers have little shade.
Watch the sky for fast-moving summer afternoon storms.
