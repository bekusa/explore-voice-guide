The guide

The story

Put your hand on the rock beside the gate. It feels rough and grainy, almost like coarse sugar, and it is warm wherever the sun has touched it. The colour is a deep rusty red, streaked with ochre and pale cream. Above you, huge stone pillars rise straight out of the hillside. The fortress walls run between them, almost as if someone built a castle into the gaps of a giant's teeth.

Welcome to Belogradchik Fortress, in the far northwest of Bulgaria. You are close to the Serbian border, at the edge of the Balkan Mountains. The town below is small and quiet. The rocks around it are the real reason people come here. This fortress uses those rocks in a way that very few castles in Europe do. Over large stretches, the builders did not need to build a back wall at all.

That is the first surprise. The tall rocks behind the fortress are part of its defence. They are steep and smooth, and they rise far higher than any wall. Attackers could not climb them easily. So the builders put up walls only where the natural rock left an opening. As you walk, notice how the masonry stops and the cliff begins. Often the join is almost seamless.

The rocks themselves are much older than any human story here. They are made of sandstone and conglomerate, a rock that holds pebbles cemented together. Iron minerals give them the red colour. Over a very long time, wind, water and frost wore the softer layers away. The harder parts stayed standing. What remains is a forest of columns, towers and strange shapes that stretches for kilometres along the ridge.

People have used this natural stronghold for a very long time. The first fortifications here date back to the Roman period. The Romans valued the place as a watch point over the roads and valleys of this frontier region. They did not build a grand palace. They built a guard post, a place to watch and to signal. From the top, you can understand why. The view runs far across the countryside.

Many centuries later, in the fourteenth century, the fortress grew larger. This region belonged to the Tsardom of Vidin, one of the states that formed when the medieval Bulgarian kingdom broke apart. Its ruler, Tsar Ivan Sratsimir, strengthened Belogradchik as one of his important strongholds. At the end of that century, the Ottoman armies took the area. The fortress then passed into Ottoman hands, and it stayed under Ottoman control for nearly five hundred years.

Much of what you see today comes from the Ottoman period. In the nineteenth century, the Ottomans rebuilt and expanded the fortress in a major campaign, finished in the 1830s. Foreign military engineers are often said to have helped with the design. The walls became thicker, and the gun openings were adapted for firearms and cannon. So this is not a single moment frozen in stone. It is several eras, layered one on top of another.

The fortress has a dark chapter too. In 1850, local Bulgarians rose up against Ottoman rule in this region. The uprising was crushed, and the fortress played a role in the punishment that followed. Many visitors walk through without knowing this story. It is worth a quiet moment as you pass through the courtyards. These walls were not only a picture for the landscape. They were also a place of real fear.

The layout is simple to follow. The fortress has three courtyards, one above the other, each separated by its own wall and gate. You enter at the lowest level and climb upward. Each courtyard is smaller and steeper than the one before. The last stretch to the highest point uses steep steps and metal stairs fixed to the rock. Take your time, and hold the rails. The stone can be slippery, especially after rain.

Now look outward, at the rock pillars around you. Locals have given many of them names, because their shapes suggest people and animals. You may hear about the Madonna, the Monks, Adam and Eve, and the Horseman. Some shapes are easy to see. Others need a little imagination, and a helpful angle of light. Ask a local, or check the signboards, and try to match the names with the silhouettes on the skyline.

There is a well-known legend attached to these figures. According to one popular tale, a young nun named Vitina fell in love and broke her vows. As punishment, the story says, a storm struck the monastery, and the nun and the monks were turned to stone. Locals point to the Madonna and the Monks as the frozen figures of that story. It is folklore, not history. But it shows how long people here have looked at these rocks and seen faces.

The sound of this place is worth noticing too. On a calm day, the rocks block much of the wind. You hear birds, footsteps on gravel, and voices that carry strangely between the pillars. When the wind does rise, it moves through the gaps with a low, hollow sound. In the morning and late afternoon, the low sun deepens the red of the stone. At midday, the colour turns paler and flatter.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the highest point you reach. Look down at the walls below you, and follow them with your eyes as they meet the rock. Then look up at the pillars, which were here long before the first Roman soldier arrived. On your way down, touch the rough red stone once more. It has carried every chapter of this fortress, and it will still be here long after.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for the steep upper steps.
Come in early morning or late afternoon for warmer light.
Hold the handrails on the metal stairs near the top.
Bring water and sun protection for the exposed upper courtyard.
Allow extra time to explore the rock trails nearby.
