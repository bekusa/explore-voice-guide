The guide

The story

Underfoot, the ground is soft and pale. It is the colour of dry bread crust. Each step lifts a thin puff of dust. All around you, low walls of sun-dried mud brick rise only to knee or waist height. They run in long, straight lines across the hill. Look up, and the city of Hamadan surrounds you on every side. Traffic hums, rooftops crowd together, and on a clear day the long ridge of Mount Alvand stands to the south. You are standing on Hegmataneh Hill, the heart of ancient Ecbatana.

The name is old. Ecbatana is the Greek form. The Persian form, Hagmatana, is often translated as a place of gathering. Modern Hamadan carries that same name, worn smooth by many centuries of speech. That continuity is the first surprise of this place. Many ancient capitals became ruins far from any living town. Ecbatana never really stopped being a city. People kept building, farming, trading and burying their dead here, layer upon layer. So the modern city sits directly on top of the old one.

Ecbatana first rose to power as the capital of the Medes. The Medes were an Iranian people who built one of the earliest large kingdoms on this plateau. That was in the first millennium before the common era, centuries before the famous Persian kings. The Medes joined with Babylon in the late seventh century before the common era to bring down the Assyrian Empire. For a time, this high valley was one of the great political centres of the ancient world.

The most famous description of Ecbatana comes from Herodotus, the Greek historian. He wrote that a Median king named Deioces built a citadel with seven rings of walls. Each ring stood higher than the one outside it. According to his account, the battlements were painted in different colours: white, black, purple, blue, orange, and finally silver and gold at the centre. It is a vivid picture. But keep it in mind as an ancient story. Archaeologists have not found anything like those seven painted walls.

When Cyrus the Great defeated the last Median king, Ecbatana did not fade away. It became one of the royal seats of the new Achaemenid Persian Empire. The Achaemenids were the dynasty of Cyrus, Darius and Xerxes, who ruled from Egypt to the edge of India. Hamadan sits high on the plateau, and its summers are cooler than the lowland capitals. Ancient writers say the kings came here in the hot months. So this hill was, in effect, a summer residence for some of the most powerful rulers on earth.

Later Greek writers described the palace with a kind of awe. The historian Polybius wrote of woodwork in cedar and cypress, and of tiles covered in silver. He also noted that most of these precious metals were stripped away by later armies. That detail matters here. What you see on this hill is not polished stone or marble columns. It is mud brick, the humble material of this region. The gold and silver left long ago. The earth stayed.

Alexander the Great reached Ecbatana in 330 before the common era, while he was chasing the last Achaemenid king. He used the city as a treasury and a base. Years later, it became the setting of a personal tragedy. His closest companion, Hephaestion, died here in 324 before the common era. Ancient sources say Alexander's grief was extreme. Today, on another hill in Hamadan, there is a weathered stone lion. Locals have long linked it to this story, though its true origin is still debated.

Now look closely at the walls around you. The excavated area shows a careful grid. Straight streets cross at right angles, and square blocks of rooms sit between them. It looks almost like a planned modern town. Here is the second surprise. Many archaeologists think this grid is not the Median city of the old stories at all. They date much of it to later periods, possibly the Parthian era. The Parthians were another Iranian dynasty who ruled after Alexander's successors. The oldest layers may still lie deeper, beneath the modern streets.

That is the quiet frustration of Ecbatana. The richest layers are probably under the houses, shops and roads of Hamadan. Archaeologists cannot simply dig up a living city. Excavation on the hill has continued in stages over many decades, and parts of the site are protected with roofs and shelters. In some trenches you can see the brick courses clearly. Look for the thin lines of mortar between the blocks, and the places where the edges have crumbled into rounded shapes after centuries of rain and frost.

On the hill there is also a small museum. It holds objects found at the site and in the region. You may see pottery, tools, and pieces of decoration from different periods. The displays are modest, but they help connect the bare walls outside to the people who lived among them. Take a few minutes inside before or after your walk. The objects make the scale of human life here easier to picture than the open field of foundations alone.

Ecbatana is not alone in Hamadan. On the slopes of Mount Alvand, outside the city, two large cuneiform inscriptions are carved into a cliff face. The site is called Ganjnameh. The inscriptions belong to Darius the Great and his son Xerxes. Cuneiform is an ancient script made of small wedge-shaped marks. Inside the city you will also find the tomb traditionally linked to Esther and Mordecai from the Hebrew Bible, and the mausoleum of Avicenna, the great medieval physician and philosopher. All of them share this same long story.

Stand still for a moment and listen. The sounds here are mostly modern: car horns, voices, perhaps a call to prayer drifting from a nearby mosque. Wind moves across the open hill, and it often feels stronger than down in the streets. In winter, Hamadan is cold, and snow can lie on these ruins. In summer, the light is hard and bright, and the mud brick glows almost golden in the late afternoon. The same weather has shaped these walls since the first builders laid them down.

Before you leave, turn slowly in a full circle. On one side are the low, broken walls of an ancient capital. On every other side is a modern city of glass, concrete and traffic. The two are not really separate. The streets you walked to get here may cover rooms that no one has seen for two thousand years. As you step back down into Hamadan, notice the dust on your shoes. It is the same pale earth the Medes once built with.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon for warm light on mud brick.
Bring sun protection; the open hill has little shade.
Stop in the site museum before walking the trenches.
Wear sturdy shoes for dusty, uneven ground.
Combine your visit with Ganjnameh and Avicenna's mausoleum.
