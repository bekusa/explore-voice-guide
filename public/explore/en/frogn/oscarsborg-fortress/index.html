The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against smooth grey rock, and gulls call over the narrows. Underfoot, the ground is part granite, part old masonry, worn smooth by weather and boots. The air smells of salt and cut grass. Across the water, the white houses of Drøbak climb the shore. You are standing on a small island fortress in the Oslofjord, at one of the narrowest points between the open sea and Norway's capital.

That narrowness is the reason this place exists. The Oslofjord runs for a long way inland, but here at the Drøbak narrows it squeezes tight. Any ship heading for Oslo has to pass close to these islands. For centuries, military planners looked at this stretch of water and saw a gate. Whoever held the islands could decide which ships passed through. Oscarsborg was built to be that gatekeeper.

The fortress stands on the Kaholmen islands, a small cluster of rock in the middle of the fjord. The main works sit on Søndre Kaholmen, the southern island. Construction began in the middle of the nineteenth century, when Norway and Sweden shared a king. The fortress takes its name from that king, Oscar the First. Look at the heavy stone walls around you. They were built for an age of cannon and sail, and they show it in their thick, low, rounded forms.

Weapons changed quickly in the decades that followed. Iron ships and long-range guns made older defences look weak. So Oscarsborg was modernised again and again. Toward the end of the nineteenth century, the fortress received large guns made by the German firm Krupp. Here is the first surprise. Norwegian soldiers gave the three biggest guns biblical nicknames: Moses, Aron, and Josva, which is the Norwegian form of Joshua. Those names would become famous.

The fortress had another weapon, and most visitors never notice it. On the northern island, Nordre Kaholmen, engineers cut a torpedo battery into the rock. Torpedoes could be launched underwater from inside the hill, straight into the shipping channel. From the fjord, an enemy captain would see almost nothing. The battery was a quiet, hidden weapon. And by 1940, its torpedoes were already decades old.

Now to the night that made this place known around the world. In the early hours of the ninth of April, 1940, Germany invaded Norway. A German naval group sailed up the Oslofjord in darkness. At its head was the new heavy cruiser Blücher. On board were troops and officials who planned to take Oslo quickly. Their goal was to capture the king, the government, and the country's gold reserves before anyone could react.

The commander at Oscarsborg was Colonel Birger Eriksen. He had very little information, and very little time. Ships were coming through the narrows in the dark, and he could not be sure who they were. He made his decision. He gave the order to fire. A story often told about him says he accepted that he might be honoured or punished for that choice. Either way, he fired.

The old Krupp guns hit the Blücher at close range. Then the torpedoes from the hidden rock battery struck her hull. The cruiser caught fire and drifted. Explosions tore through her. Not far from where you stand, the Blücher rolled over and sank into the deep, cold water of the fjord. Many men died that morning, on a ship that was supposed to win a quick victory.

Here is the second surprise. The sinking did not stop the invasion. German forces still reached Oslo later that same day, by other routes. But the delay at the narrows changed everything. The king, the royal family, the government, and the national gold reserves had time to leave the capital. Norway's legal government continued to exist, and it later led the country's resistance from exile in Britain.

The wreck of the Blücher still lies on the bottom of the fjord near here. It is a war grave and a protected site. For the people who live along these shores, the ship is not only history. Oil from the wreck has been a long-running environmental concern, and efforts were made to deal with it. When you look out over the calm water, it is worth remembering what lies beneath it.

After the war, Oscarsborg stayed a military site for many years. Eventually its role came to an end, and the islands opened to the public. Today the fortress is a cultural heritage site. There is a museum about its history, old buildings now used for accommodation and dining, and open lawns where people picnic in summer. In the warmer months, the fortress has also hosted outdoor performances, with the fjord as a backdrop.

As you walk, notice the contrast. Old gun positions sit beside quiet grassy slopes. Tunnels and stone passages lead into the hillside, cool and damp even on a warm day. Rusted fittings and heavy doors remain where soldiers once worked. Children run along the ramparts, and sailboats glide past the same channel where the cruiser came through in the dark. The place carries both peace and war at once.

Take a moment now to look south and north along the fjord. See how close the shores are, and how any ship must pass right in front of you. That simple geography explains everything that happened here. Listen again to the water against the rock. When you take the ferry back toward Drøbak, watch the narrows behind you, and imagine the night when this small island held the line.
