The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here on the headland of Tindari, it comes off the Tyrrhenian Sea in long, steady pushes, carrying salt and the dry scent of wild herbs. Below you, the cliff drops sharply toward the water. Far out, on a clear day, the grey shapes of the Aeolian Islands sit low on the horizon. Close your eyes for a moment and listen. Gulls, the rustle of dry grass, perhaps a church bell. This is a place that people have climbed toward for more than two thousand years.

Tindari sits on a rocky promontory above the Gulf of Patti, on the northern coast of Sicily. It is two places at once. One is a Greek and Roman city, now an archaeological park of stone foundations and broken columns. The other is a sanctuary, home to a dark wooden statue of the Virgin Mary that draws pilgrims from across the island. Today you will walk through both, because on this hilltop they have always lived side by side.

Let us begin with the Greeks. The city of Tyndaris was founded in 396 BC by Dionysius the Elder, the powerful ruler of Syracuse. He settled it with exiles from Messenia, a region of southern Greece. These were people who had lost their homeland after long wars with Sparta. So imagine them arriving here — refugees, far from home, building a new life on a cliff above a foreign sea. That is the human story under every stone you see.

The name itself comes from Greek myth. Tyndareus was a legendary king of Sparta, and the father, in some versions of the story, of the twin heroes Castor and Pollux. These twins were protectors of sailors, so the choice made sense for a city that watched over the sea. It also carried a quiet irony. Exiles from Messenia, driven out by Sparta, named their new home after a Spartan king. Myth, it seems, belonged to everyone.

Walk into the archaeological park and look at the layout. The streets run in a regular grid, crossing at right angles. This was standard Greek town planning, and the Romans kept it when they took control. Between the streets you can trace the outlines of blocks of houses. Some rooms still hold fragments of mosaic floors and the lower parts of painted walls. Bend down and look closely. You are standing in the kitchens and dining rooms of ordinary families.

Now find the theatre. It was built by the Greeks and cut into the slope of the hill, so the audience faced out toward the sea. Later, under Roman rule, it was altered for different kinds of shows, possibly including the fighting games that Romans loved. Sit on the stone seats for a moment. Behind the stage, the view opens onto open water. Think about what it meant to watch a tragedy here, with the real horizon as the backdrop.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Tindari appears in the speeches of Cicero, the great Roman lawyer and orator. In the first century before Christ, he prosecuted Verres, a corrupt Roman governor of Sicily. Among his many crimes, Verres took treasured statues from Sicilian towns. Cicero describes how the people of Tyndaris lost a statue of the god Mercury. So this small hilltop city was part of one of the most famous corruption trials in Roman history.

The most striking ruin in the park is a large arched structure that locals have long called the Basilica. The name is a little misleading. Archaeologists now think it was a monumental gateway from the Roman period, leading toward the public heart of the city. Look at the heavy stone blocks and the vaulted passage. It is solid, practical, and built to impress. Walk through it and imagine the crowds moving to the forum beyond.

The city did not last forever. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder recorded that part of Tyndaris fell into the sea. The cliff edge here has always been unstable, and landslides and earthquakes reshaped the hill over the centuries. Look toward the edge and you can understand how it happened. Much of what once stood here is simply gone, lost to the rock and the waves below. What remains is only part of the story.

Now turn toward the sanctuary at the top of the hill. Inside is the Black Madonna of Tindari, a carved wooden statue of the Virgin holding the Christ child. Her face is dark. The style suggests an Eastern, Byzantine origin. Legend has it that the statue arrived by ship from the East, and that the vessel took shelter in the bay during a storm. According to the tale, the ship could not leave until the sailors carried the statue ashore and up the hill.

Beneath the statue, look for a Latin phrase taken from the Song of Songs in the Bible. It reads, in translation, I am black but beautiful. The present church is large and modern, built in the twentieth century beside an older, smaller church. Pilgrims come throughout the year, some walking long distances. You may see people praying quietly, lighting candles, or simply standing in silence before the Madonna.

Now walk to a viewpoint and look straight down. On the beach below, you will see pale sandbanks curving into shallow lagoons of green and blue water. These are the lakes of Marinello, now a protected nature reserve. Locals say they were formed by a miracle. In the popular story, a child fell from the cliff, and the sea pulled back to leave soft sand where she landed safely. The shapes shift slowly with the currents, so the view is never quite the same.

Before you leave, stand still once more and let the wind find you again. Behind you are the foundations of a Greek city built by people who had lost their home. In front of you is the church of a statue that, in the legend, refused to leave. Below are lagoons that the sea keeps redrawing. The Sicilian poet Salvatore Quasimodo wrote about the wind at Tindari. As you walk away, notice it on your skin. It is the same wind those first settlers felt.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start early to walk the ruins before midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone paths and slopes.
Dress modestly for entering the sanctuary church.
Bring water; the hilltop has little shade.
Choose a clear day to see the Aeolian Islands.
