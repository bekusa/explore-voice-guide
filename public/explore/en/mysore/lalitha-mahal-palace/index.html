The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the white. The palace seems almost too bright against the green lawns and the soft blue line of the Chamundi Hills behind it. In the morning the walls take on a faint cream warmth. By late afternoon they turn almost silver. Listen too. Out here on the edge of Mysore, the traffic noise drops away and birdsong fills the gaps. This is Lalitha Mahal, and it was built to make an impression from the very first glance.

Mysore is often called the City of Palaces, and with good reason. For centuries this was the seat of the Wadiyar dynasty, the royal family that ruled the Kingdom of Mysore. The great Mysore Palace sits at the heart of the old city, crowded with visitors and lit with thousands of bulbs on festival nights. Lalitha Mahal is a different kind of building. It stands apart, on raised ground at the foot of the hills, away from the busy centre.

The palace dates from the early twentieth century. It was commissioned by Maharaja Krishnaraja Wadiyar the Fourth, one of the most admired rulers in the dynasty's long line. Under his rule, Mysore became known across India for modern schools, hydroelectric power and careful public planning. Gandhi is often said to have called him a saintly king, though the exact wording of that remark varies from one telling to the next. Treat it as an affectionate legend rather than a precise quote.

Why build another palace when the family already had several? The answer is hospitality. Lalitha Mahal was built as a grand residence for very important guests, above all the Viceroy of India, the British Crown's highest representative in the country. In those years, a princely state like Mysore had to manage a delicate relationship with British power. A splendid guest house was a form of diplomacy, built in stone and plaster.

Now look at the shape of the building. It does not follow the Indo-Saracenic style of the main Mysore Palace, with its onion domes and pointed arches. Instead, it follows European Renaissance forms, the classical revival that spread from Italy across Europe. Notice the rows of paired columns along the facade, the balanced wings on either side, and the strict symmetry. Everything is arranged so that your eye travels inwards and upwards, towards the centre.

And at the centre is the dome. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The main dome is widely said to take its inspiration from Saint Paul's Cathedral in London, the great church designed by Christopher Wren. Once you know this, it is hard to stop seeing it. A London cathedral dome, reimagined in white, rises here above a South Indian lawn. It is a quiet reminder of how ideas travelled across empires in those years.

The design is usually credited to the English architect E. W. Fritchley, who worked in India during that period. Whoever drew the lines, the builders here were local. The craftsmen of the Mysore region had long experience with stone, plaster and fine decorative work. As you walk closer, look at the mouldings above the windows and along the cornices. The forms are European, but the hands that made them belonged to this place.

Inside, the palace was designed for ceremony. Visitors who have walked through the main halls describe a sweeping staircase, high ceilings, polished floors and heavy chandeliers. The grandest room is often described as a ballroom, with decorative ceiling work and coloured glass catching the light. If you go inside, pause under the central space and look straight up. The best decoration in this building is often above your head, not at eye level.

Here is the second surprise. For decades, this royal guest house has not been closed off as a museum. In the 1970s, the palace passed to the Indian government and became a heritage hotel. That means the building has had a second life. Guests have slept in rooms once prepared for viceroys, and wedding parties have filled the lawns. Arrangements for access have changed over the years, so check the current status before you plan a meal or a visit inside.

Spend a moment on the grounds. The palace sits on a gentle rise, and that height matters. From the front steps, the city spreads out to one side, and the hills rise on the other. On top of those hills stands the Chamundeshwari Temple, dedicated to the goddess Chamundi, the protective deity of the Wadiyar family. The palace and the temple face the same landscape. Royal power and sacred tradition share one horizon here.

Think about the journey a guest would have made a century ago. A carriage or an early motor car would come along a quiet road, with the white facade growing larger at every turn. The scale was meant to impress before a single word was spoken. Today, the approach still works in a similar way. Walk slowly from the gate if you can, and let the building rise in front of you, step by step.

The colour itself has a story too. Lalitha Mahal is almost entirely white, and that choice sets it apart from the warmer stone tones of many Indian palaces. White makes the classical details sharp in strong sunlight. Every column throws a clean, dark shadow. Photographers love the hour before sunset, when the facade turns golden and the shadows between the columns grow long. On cloudy days, the palace looks softer, almost like a drawing.

Before you leave, turn once more towards the building. Look at the dome against the sky, the long line of columns, the hills behind. Here a king tried to show the world that his small kingdom could host an empire with confidence and grace. Notice the quiet of the lawns, the birds in the trees, the white walls in the changing light. As you walk away, look back once from the road... the palace looks just as calm from there.
