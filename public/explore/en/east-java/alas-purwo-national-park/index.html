The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the trees, a low roar rolls in and fades, then rolls in again. That is the Indian Ocean breaking on coral reef. Closer, there is the dry rattle of leaves, the call of a bird you may not recognise, and the warm, damp smell of a forest that has been left largely to itself. You are standing near the far southeastern tip of Java, where the island narrows into a peninsula and reaches toward the sea.

This is Alas Purwo National Park, in the Banyuwangi region of East Java. The name is usually translated as the first forest, or the ancient forest. Alas means forest in Javanese. Purwo carries the sense of a beginning. That name is not just poetic. It holds a belief that many people here take seriously, and it shapes how visitors behave once they enter the trees.

Locals say that this corner of Java is where the land first rose out of the ocean at the creation of the world. In this telling, the forest is the oldest place on the island — the point where everything began. You do not have to share the belief to feel its weight. People speak quietly here. Some leave small offerings. Many Javanese consider the forest a place of strong spiritual energy, and that idea has drawn pilgrims for generations.

The park covers a broad sweep of the Blambangan Peninsula. Within it you will find lowland tropical forest, bamboo groves, mangrove channels, open grassland, and long beaches of pale sand. The ground underfoot changes as you move — soft leaf litter, then sandy track, then limestone rubble near the coast. Much of the interior is not easy to reach, and that is one reason the forest still feels so intact compared with the crowded farmland of central Java.

One of the best places to see wildlife is a grazing field called Sadengan. It is an open meadow cut out of the forest, with a viewing tower at its edge. Early in the morning or late in the afternoon, wild cattle called banteng often come out to graze. These are the wild ancestors of the cattle farmed across much of Southeast Asia. The bulls are dark, almost black, with white patches on their legs that look like stockings.

Watch the grass around them, too. Deer move along the edges of the field, and peafowl are common in the park. Here is a detail that many visitors miss — the peafowl of Java are green peafowl, not the blue birds you see in city parks around the world. Their feathers shine bronze and green in low sun. Green peafowl have become rare across much of their range, which makes a sighting here feel quietly special.

The forest holds larger, shyer animals as well. Leopards live in the park, and so do wild dogs called dholes, which hunt in packs. Most visitors will never see either. They keep to the deep forest and avoid people. But knowing they are there changes the feeling of a walk. Every snapped twig sounds a little louder. Rangers can tell you which signs of animals have been spotted recently, so it is worth asking.

Now turn toward the coast. Along the beach at Ngagelan, a conservation programme protects sea turtles that come ashore to lay their eggs. Several species nest on these sands. Staff collect eggs from exposed spots, guard them in a hatchery, and release the young turtles into the sea. If you arrive during a release, you may watch tiny hatchlings scramble across the sand toward the surf — each one small enough to sit in your palm.

Further along the southern shore lies Plengkung, a beach that surfers around the world know by another name — G-Land. The reef here produces long, powerful waves that peel to the left for a remarkable distance. Surfers began travelling to this remote coast in the 1970s, at a time when reaching it was a real expedition. Today small surf camps sit near the beach, but the waves are serious, and they are best left to experienced surfers.

Away from the waves, the limestone of the peninsula hides many caves. Some of them are used for meditation and prayer. Pilgrims come to sit in the darkness for hours, sometimes days. Visits often increase around the start of the Javanese new year, a month known as Suro, which many Javanese treat as a sacred and reflective time. If you enter a cave, move slowly, speak softly, and remember that for others this is a place of worship, not just a sight.

The forest is also home to a Hindu temple, Pura Luhur Giri Salaka, set among the trees not far from the park entrance. Its stone gateways and shrines follow the style of temples on Bali, which lies just across a narrow strait. Hindu worshippers from East Java and from Bali gather here for important ceremonies. On those days the air fills with incense smoke, the sound of chanting, and the bright colours of offerings woven from palm leaves and flowers.

That mix tells you something about this edge of Java. Islam, Hindu tradition, and older Javanese beliefs about spirits and sacred places all live side by side here. The same stretch of forest can hold a meditating pilgrim, a ranger counting turtle nests, a family on a day trip, and a surfer waxing a board. None of them cancels the others out. The forest seems large enough to hold them all.

A few practical thoughts. The dry season usually brings clearer tracks and more animals at the open grazing fields, as water becomes scarce in the forest. The wet season turns the green deeper but makes roads muddy. Bring water, insect repellent, and sturdy shoes. Mobile signal can be weak or absent. And the sun on the open beaches is strong, even when the forest shade feels cool.

Before you leave, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Notice the light — how it falls in narrow shafts through the canopy, or how it spreads white across the sand. Listen again for the ocean behind the trees. Whether or not this is truly the place where the world began, it is one of the few places on Java where the land still feels unhurried. Take that quiet with you as you walk back toward the road.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Sadengan early morning or late afternoon for wildlife.
Bring insect repellent, water, and sturdy walking shoes.
Expect weak or no mobile signal deep inside the park.
Ask rangers about recent animal sightings before setting out.
Speak softly and dress modestly near caves and temples.
