The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, on the north-western edge of Kaunas, the wind moves across open grass. Traffic hums from the highway nearby. Beneath your feet, the ground rises and falls in long earthen slopes. Those slopes are not natural hills. They were shaped by hand, and they hide brick and concrete underneath. You are standing at the Ninth Fort, one of the most solemn places in Lithuania.

The story begins with an empire's fear. In the late nineteenth century, Kaunas sat near the western frontier of the Russian Empire. Military planners turned the city into a fortress, ringed with a chain of separate forts. This one was built in the early twentieth century, as one of the later links in that chain. Its design followed the logic of the age — low profiles, thick earth cover, and vaulted corridors that could survive artillery fire.

Yet the fort never really served its first purpose. During the First World War, the Kaunas Fortress fell to German forces fairly quickly. The great defensive ring did not hold the city. And so these heavy walls, built to keep armies out, found a different and darker use. Over the following decades, they would be used to hold people in.

After the war, Lithuania became an independent state, and Kaunas served as its temporary capital. During those interwar years, the fort was turned into a prison. Its thick walls and isolated location made it practical for that role. Then, in 1940, the Soviet Union occupied Lithuania. Under Soviet rule, the fort held political prisoners. For many of them, it was a stop on the way east, toward labour camps deep inside the Soviet Union.

In the summer of 1941, Nazi Germany invaded, and the fort entered its most terrible chapter. The occupiers and local collaborators turned it into a place of mass killing. Most of the victims were Jews. Before the war, Kaunas had a large and lively Jewish community, with schools, synagogues, newspapers, and a famous tradition of religious learning. Within months, that community was forced into a ghetto across the river, in the district of Vilijampolė.

The single darkest day came on 29 October 1941. It is remembered as the Great Action. Historical records show that around 9,200 Jews from the Kaunas ghetto were marched up the road to this fort and shot in pits nearby. Nearly half of them were children. Survivors later described the long column of people walking past in the morning, and the silence that followed. Pause here, if you need to. Many visitors do.

The killing did not stop with Lithuanian Jews. This is a detail many visitors do not expect. Jews were also deported here from Western and Central Europe — from Germany, from Austria, and from France. They were often told they were being resettled for work. Instead, they were killed soon after arrival. In total, historians estimate that tens of thousands of people died at this site during the occupation.

In the later years of the war, the Nazis tried to erase the evidence. Prisoners were forced to dig up the mass graves and burn the remains. Then came an extraordinary act of courage. On Christmas night in 1943, a group of these prisoners escaped. Historical accounts put their number at around sixty-four. They had secretly prepared tools and a route out through the fort's structure. Some of them survived the war and later gave testimony about what had happened here.

When you go inside the casemates — the vaulted rooms within the fort — look closely at the walls. Prisoners scratched words into the plaster and brick. Some are names. Some are dates. Among them are inscriptions in French, left by men deported from France late in the war. These marks are small and easy to miss. But they are among the most personal traces in the whole complex.

Now turn toward the open field. The large stone memorial rises above the place where the mass graves lie. It was unveiled in 1984, during the Soviet period, and was designed by the sculptor Alfonsas Vilius Ambraziūnas. It stands about thirty-two metres tall. Its shapes are rough and broken, like rising stone. Some people see faces, raised fists, or bodies pressed together. The sculptor left it open, and every visitor reads it differently.

Here is a second thing worth knowing. For decades, under Soviet rule, the official story of this site rarely named the victims as Jews. Plaques spoke more generally of peaceful citizens and victims of fascism. Only after Lithuania regained independence in 1990 did the memory of the Holocaust here receive open and clear recognition. The museum today also covers the Soviet occupation, so the fort holds more than one layer of loss.

The museum itself is split between the old fort and a more modern exhibition space nearby. Inside, you will find documents, photographs, and personal objects. Give yourself time. This is not a place to rush. Many people walk the grounds slowly, in near silence. You may see small stones placed on the memorial. In Jewish tradition, leaving a stone is a way of honouring the dead and saying that someone remembers.

As you leave, stand once more at the edge of the field. Notice how quiet it is, even with the road so close. The grass grows over the mass graves now. The earthen slopes of the fort are soft and green. It is hard to match this calm landscape with what happened here. Perhaps that is the point. The ground looks ordinary... and that is exactly why remembering matters. Take one last look at the memorial against the sky. Then walk back slowly, and carry this place with you into the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress and behave respectfully; this is a mass grave site.
Bring a warm layer for the cold, damp casemates.
Allow at least two hours for the fort and museum.
Visit the Kaunas ghetto area in Vilijampolė for context.
Prepare children beforehand; some exhibits are deeply distressing.
