The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the grass on top of the earthen ramparts, and somewhere below, water drips inside a brick tunnel. The air near the old walls is cool, even on a warm day. It smells of damp stone, moss and soil. This is Kaunas Fortress. It was built as a ring of defences around a whole city, and today it rests quietly under trees and turf.

The first thing to understand is that Kaunas Fortress is not one building. It is a system. Forts, batteries, gun positions, ammunition stores, barracks and roads were spread in a wide circle around Kaunas. Wherever you are standing right now, you are inside one part of a much larger plan. The planners designed it so that each fort could support its neighbours with fire. On a map, the forts look like beads on a necklace around the old town.

Why here? Look at the geography. Kaunas sits where two rivers meet, the Nemunas and the Neris. In the late nineteenth century, this city was on the western edge of the Russian Empire, close to the border with Germany. Imperial planners worried that a German army might march east along these river valleys. So in the early 1880s, construction began on a fortress that was meant to stop that army here, before it could reach the interior of the empire.

The work continued for more than three decades, in several phases. Early forts were built mostly from red brick and earth. Later, engineers learned that new artillery shells could smash brick. So they turned to concrete, and they strengthened older positions. As you walk around, notice this change in materials. Warm red brick often marks the older work. Grey, heavy concrete usually marks the newer layer. You are looking at an arms race written in stone.

Now look at the shape of the ground. The forts were not built to stand tall and proud like medieval castles. They were built to hide. Thick layers of earth covered the walls and roofs to absorb explosions. Grass grew over them. From a distance, a fort could look like a low hill. Deep ditches surrounded many of them, and small firing positions covered those ditches from inside. Every curve here had a purpose.

Here is something many visitors miss. The fortress also changed the centre of Kaunas itself. The large church with the blue domes at the end of Laisvės alėja, the long pedestrian avenue downtown, was built as an Orthodox garrison church for the fortress soldiers. After Lithuania became independent, it became a Catholic church, dedicated to Saint Michael the Archangel. So the fortress is not only out here on the edges. Its story runs straight into the heart of the city.

The great test came in 1915, during the First World War. A German army attacked the fortress in the summer of that year. The defenders had big guns, many soldiers and decades of preparation. Yet the fortress fell in less than two weeks. Heavy German artillery broke through the defences on the western side. The Russian commandant left his post during the fighting, and he was later put on trial and harshly punished. Years of building ended in a few days of battle.

After the war, everything changed again. Lithuania became an independent state. Vilnius, the historic capital, was under Polish control, so Kaunas served as the temporary capital between the wars. The old fortress was no longer a defensive line. Its buildings were used as army stores, workshops and prisons. Some parts were simply left to the trees. The great system slowly became a patchwork of separate places, each with its own fate.

The darkest chapter belongs to the Ninth Fort, on the northern edge of the city. In interwar Lithuania it was a prison. Under Soviet rule it held political prisoners. Then, during the Nazi occupation in the Second World War, it became a place of mass murder. Tens of thousands of people were killed there, most of them Jews from Kaunas and from other parts of Europe. Today the Ninth Fort is a museum and memorial. If you visit, go slowly, and give it time.

One story from the Ninth Fort shows great courage. In late 1943, Nazi authorities forced a group of prisoners to dig up and burn the bodies of victims, to hide the evidence. Those prisoners planned an escape. On a winter night, a group of them broke out of the fort. Several survived and later gave testimony about what happened there. Their words helped the world learn the truth about this place.

Other forts tell gentler stories today. Some are now museums or education centres, where you can walk through tunnels, see old equipment and learn about military engineering. Others stand quietly in parks and residential districts. Local people walk their dogs along the ramparts, and children climb the grassy slopes. Some forts are closed or in poor condition, so they are best seen from the outside. Each one feels different, even though they come from the same plan.

Take a moment to notice the small details around you. Look for iron hooks and rings fixed in the walls. Look for narrow firing slits, and for brick arches over doorways. Look for drainage channels and air vents in the earth. Every one of these details was designed for life under fire. Soldiers needed light, fresh air and dry floors. Engineers solved these problems with simple materials and careful thinking.

Now stand still for one more moment. Feel the cool air near the walls, and hear the wind in the grass above you. More than a century ago, this place was built to stop an army. It failed at that task, and later it saw great suffering. Today it is quiet, and it belongs to the city again. As you walk away, look back once at the low green hills. Some of them are not hills at all.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan separate trips because the forts are spread across the city.
Allow at least two hours for the Ninth Fort museum.
Wear sturdy shoes for earthen ramparts and uneven tunnel floors.
Bring a light jacket since tunnels stay cool all year.
View closed or ruined forts only from the outside.
