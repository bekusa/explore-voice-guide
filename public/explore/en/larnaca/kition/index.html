The guide

The story

Listen first. A scooter hums somewhere down a side street. A dog barks behind a garden wall. Then you look down from the walkway, and the modern town seems to drop away. Below you lie pale foundations of stone, laid out in rectangles and corridors, warm under the Cypriot sun. You are standing over one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on the island. The town of Larnaca grew directly on top of an ancient city called Kition.

That is the first surprise. Kition is not a ruin out in the countryside. It is the buried root of the living town around you. Houses, schools and shops sit on layers of earlier streets. Archaeologists have uncovered only small windows into the old city, often between modern buildings. The area you see here is one of those windows. Much of Kition still lies under the pavements you walked on to get here.

The story begins in the Bronze Age. People were living on this coast long before the famous names of Greek history. By around the thirteenth century before Christ, Kition was a walled town with temples and workshops. Its position mattered. It faced a sheltered stretch of sea, with a natural harbour and a salt lake close by. Ships could reach Egypt, the Levant and the Aegean from this shore.

What made Cyprus rich in that age was copper. The island's name is closely tied to the metal in many languages. At Kition, excavators found copper workshops placed right beside the temples. This was not an accident. Religion and industry worked side by side here. The priests and the metalworkers shared the same sacred ground. Many scholars think the temples helped to control, and to bless, the valuable trade in metal.

Now look closely at the walls below you. Some of the lower courses are built from very large blocks of stone. In the late Bronze Age, newcomers from the Aegean world are believed to have settled here and rebuilt parts of the town. They raised strong walls and dressed stone with care. You can still see how carefully cut blocks sit against rougher fill. Try to picture these walls standing much higher.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. On one of the old temple walls, ancient people scratched drawings of ships into the stone. They are simple outlines of hulls and masts. Nobody knows exactly who made them. Perhaps sailors left them as offerings, asking for safe passage. Perhaps they were just marks made by people waiting by the temple. Either way, they bring the sea right into the sanctuary.

Around the ninth century before Christ, a new people arrived — the Phoenicians. They were seafaring traders from the coast of what is now Lebanon. They made Kition one of their most important footholds in the Mediterranean. On the ruins of an older temple, they built a large sanctuary to Astarte. She was a major Phoenician goddess, linked with fertility, love and the sea. Kition became a Phoenician city-kingdom with its own rulers.

For several centuries, Kition was one of the strongest kingdoms on the island. Cyprus at that time was divided into a number of small city-kingdoms, each with its own king. Kition often sided with the great Persian Empire against the Greek cities of Cyprus. Its kings minted their own coins. The city controlled trade in copper and, later, gained influence over inland territory. This was a confident, wealthy place.

That independence came to an end in the late fourth century before Christ. After the death of Alexander the Great, his generals fought over his empire. Ptolemy, who ruled Egypt, took control of Cyprus. The last king of Kition lost his throne, and the city-kingdom was absorbed into a larger state. Kition continued as a town, but it was no longer a kingdom with its own crown.

And now a second surprise. One of the most influential thinkers of the ancient world was born here. His name was Zeno of Kition. He moved to Athens, studied philosophy, and began teaching in a painted colonnade called the Stoa. His followers became known as the Stoics. Stoicism teaches calm acceptance of what we cannot control, and careful attention to what we can. Today people still read Stoic ideas, often without knowing they began with a man from this coast.

The old name has a long echo. Many scholars connect Kition with the word Kittim, which appears in the Hebrew Bible. It is used there for Cyprus, and sometimes more widely for peoples across the sea. Christian tradition also returns to the name. According to local belief, Lazarus, the man raised by Jesus, later came to Cyprus and became bishop of Kition. That story belongs to faith and tradition rather than archaeology, but it still shapes Larnaca's identity.

Modern exploration of Kition began properly in the twentieth century. A Swedish expedition worked on the island in the 1920s and 1930s and studied the area. Later, Cypriot archaeologists carried out long and careful excavations here. Their work uncovered the temples, the workshops and the city walls. Many of the finds, including pottery, figurines and inscriptions, now sit in museums. What remains on site is the plan of the place, open to the sky.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the walkway. Let your eye travel from the oldest stones to the balconies and rooftops just beyond the fence. Three thousand years of daily life are stacked here, one layer on another. Somewhere under your feet, a metalworker once fanned a fire, and a sailor once scratched a ship into stone. As you walk back into the streets of Larnaca, remember — the ancient city is still down there, quietly holding up the new one.
