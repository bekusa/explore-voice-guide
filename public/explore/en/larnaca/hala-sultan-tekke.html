The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Palm fronds rattle in the breeze. Somewhere beyond the garden wall, a jet climbs out of Larnaca airport. And in front of you, across a wide pale lake, the light comes back flat and silver, almost too bright to look at. The air here carries a faint smell of salt. You are standing on the western shore of the Larnaca Salt Lake, at one of the most revered Muslim shrines on the island of Cyprus.

The name itself tells you a lot. Hala Sultan is often translated as something close to the revered aunt, an honorific title. Tekke is a Turkish word for a lodge where Sufi dervishes once lived, prayed and welcomed travellers. Sufis are the mystical tradition within Islam, known for devotion through music, poetry and quiet contemplation. So this place is not only a mosque. It grew as a shrine, a resting place, and a home for a small religious community.

At the heart of it all is one woman. Her name was Umm Haram. Islamic tradition holds that she was a close relative of the Prophet Muhammad through his mother's family, and that the Prophet visited her home in Medina. She was married to Ubada ibn al-Samit, one of the Prophet's early companions. Her story is told in respected collections of the Prophet's sayings, which is part of why this shore matters so much to Muslims far beyond Cyprus.

In the middle of the seventh century, Arab forces launched raids on Cyprus. Umm Haram travelled with them, by then an elderly woman. According to tradition, she fell from her mule near this lake, was badly injured, and died. She was buried where she fell. That simple detail is the reason the shrine stands here — not in a city, not on a hilltop, but on a quiet shore of salt and reeds.

Now look at the building. The mosque you see today dates mostly from the Ottoman period, when the island was ruled from Istanbul. The present complex took shape in the early nineteenth century. It is modest in scale. The walls are pale stone, the dome is low and rounded, and a slender minaret rises above the palms. Nothing here shouts. The effect is calm, and the garden around it feels more like a courtyard than a monument.

Inside, the most important space is the tomb chamber. Here is something many visitors do not expect. The grave is marked by a structure of three very large stones — two standing upright, with a third laid across the top, like a doorway with no door. Archaeologists call this kind of arrangement a trilithon. It feels far older and heavier than the delicate Ottoman decoration around it.

Those stones carry their own legends. Locals say the great horizontal stone came from Mount Sinai, or even that it once hung in the air without support. These are tales, not recorded history. But they show how deeply people have loved this place. For centuries, pilgrims have come here to pray, to ask for help, and to sit quietly near the woman they consider a companion of the Prophet's household.

The tomb chamber holds a second surprise. Another woman rests here, much more recent. In 1929, a wife of Sharif Hussein of Mecca was buried at the shrine. Sharif Hussein led the Arab Revolt during the First World War, and this woman was the grandmother of the late King Hussein of Jordan. So in one small room, you have a grave from the seventh century and a grave from the twentieth century, linked by faith across more than 1,200 years.

In Ottoman times, the shrine was treated with great respect. A story is often told that Ottoman ships sailing past this coast lowered their flags in salute to Umm Haram. It is a lovely image, and widely repeated, but treat it as a tradition rather than a documented rule. What is certain is that the tekke became a focus of pilgrimage for Muslims across the region, and remains so today.

The ground beneath this area is older still. Close to the shrine, archaeologists have excavated the remains of a Late Bronze Age town, more than three thousand years old. It was a trading settlement, probably with a harbour connected to the sea before the lagoon closed off. Finds there show links with Egypt and the wider eastern Mediterranean. So long before the mosque, long before Umm Haram, people were already living and trading on this shore.

Turn back toward the water. The Salt Lake is not one single body of water but a network of shallow lakes. In the hot months, much of it dries out and leaves a white crust of salt that cracks underfoot. In winter, rain fills it again, and flocks of flamingos arrive. From the shrine, you may see a long pink line of birds feeding far out in the shallows. Their honking calls carry clearly across the water on still days.

This mix is what makes the place unusual. A sacred grave, a working mosque, an Ottoman garden, a Bronze Age town, a wetland for migrating birds — and an international airport just over the horizon. Planes pass low overhead while people pray inside. Families walk the lakeside path. Birdwatchers set up telescopes near the trees. Each group sees a different place, standing in exactly the same spot.

Before you leave, pause in the garden. Notice the shade of the palms and cypress trees, and the coolness of the stone underfoot. Look once more at the lake, white or blue depending on the season. Think of an old woman, far from home, coming to rest on this shore some thirteen centuries ago. As you walk back to the road, listen again — for the wind in the palms, the birds on the water, and the quiet that still surrounds her grave.
