The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wind and the traffic there is a low, steady hum. It comes from deep inside the concrete beneath your feet. Somewhere below, the Danube is falling through turbines, day and night, without a pause. The air here often smells of wet stone and river water. On a still morning, a thin mist can hang over the surface upstream, softening the hills on both banks.

You are standing at Iron Gate I, the largest dam on the entire Danube. It sits a short drive upstream from Drobeta-Turnu Severin. The structure stretches straight across the river from Romania to Serbia. That detail matters more than it first seems. This is not one country's dam. It is a shared project, built by two neighbours who had very different political paths during the Cold War.

Romania and Yugoslavia agreed to build it together in the early 1960s. Construction began in 1964, and the plant was completed in 1972. The design is almost perfectly symmetrical. There is a power station on the Romanian bank and a twin power station on the Serbian bank. Each side also has its own navigation lock for ships. The border runs through the middle, so in a sense, the dam is a handshake made of concrete.

Why build here? The answer is the name itself. For centuries, the Iron Gates were among the most feared stretches of the Danube. The river squeezed through narrow gorges between the Carpathian Mountains and the Balkan Mountains. Rocks, rapids and whirlpools sat just under the surface. Boats were wrecked, cargo was lost, and pilots who knew these waters were highly valued. Engineers in the nineteenth century cut channels to help ships pass, but the river was still dangerous.

The dam solved two problems at once. First, it raised the water level far upstream, drowning the worst rapids under a deep, calm lake. Ships can now move through the gorges with far less risk. Second, it turned the force of that falling water into electricity. For decades, this plant has been one of the most important power sources for both countries. It remains one of the largest hydroelectric plants in Europe.

Now for something many visitors miss. Look upstream and imagine the river before 1972. Out there, in the middle of the current, there was once an island called Ada Kaleh. It was home to a small community of Turkish people, a remnant of the long Ottoman presence in this region. Travellers remembered its mosque, its coffee houses, its fig trees and its sweets. When the reservoir filled, the island disappeared under the water.

The people of Ada Kaleh were moved away, many to other parts of Romania and some to Turkey. Planners tried to save part of the island's heritage. Some buildings and elements of the old fortress were moved to Șimian Island, downstream near Drobeta-Turnu Severin. The full plan was never really completed. So when you look at that smooth surface, remember that a whole small world lies beneath it, with its streets and its gardens now silent under the river.

Ada Kaleh was not the only loss. The old town of Orșova, a little further upstream, was also flooded. A new Orșova was built on higher ground, and its residents moved up the hillside. Villages along both banks shared the same fate. This is the quiet human cost behind every large dam. The electricity flowing from this plant came at the price of many homes and many memories.

Here is a second surprise. On the Serbian bank upstream, there is a Roman inscription carved into the cliff, known as the Tabula Traiana. It honours the emperor Trajan, who built a road along this gorge almost two thousand years ago. When the dam was planned, the rising water threatened to cover it. Engineers cut the inscription away from the rock and raised it to a higher position. So the Romans and the twentieth century meet in the same cliff face.

Trajan's name returns downstream, too. In Drobeta-Turnu Severin, you can still see the ruined stumps of Trajan's Bridge, a Roman bridge across the Danube from the second century. It was designed by the architect Apollodorus of Damascus and was one of the great engineering works of the ancient world. Think about that for a moment. Within a few kilometres, people have tried to master this river twice, once with Roman stone and timber, once with modern reinforced concrete.

Take a look at the dam itself. The concrete is pale and weathered, marked with long vertical stains where rain has run down the surface for decades. The lines are plain and functional, typical of large industrial works from that era. There is little decoration. Its power comes from scale. A road runs along the top, and this crossing is also an official border point between Romania and Serbia, so expect to see checkpoints, uniforms and queues of cars and trucks.

If you watch the water for a while, you may notice the difference between the two sides of the dam. Upstream, the lake is broad and calm. Downstream, the river comes out with more movement, swirling and churning as it leaves the turbines. Barges and riverboats sometimes wait to pass through the locks. Seeing a large vessel rise or fall inside a lock chamber is slow, patient and strangely satisfying to watch.

The landscape around you is part of the Iron Gates Natural Park on the Romanian side. The hills are covered with oak and beech forest, and limestone shows through in grey patches. Upstream, the Danube narrows into the Cazane Gorge, where the cliffs rise steeply from the water. Many travellers combine a visit to the dam with a boat trip through those gorges, or a drive along the riverside road toward Orșova.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen again to that hum. It is the sound of a river that once wrecked ships and now lights cities. Look upstream at the quiet water over Ada Kaleh, and downstream toward the Roman ruins at Drobeta. As you walk back, notice the border flags, the passing trucks and the mist on the hills. This is a place where nature, empire and modern ambition all meet in a single line of concrete.
