The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Below you, the Danube moves wide and heavy, brown-green in most light, with a low, steady rush against the bank. The ground here is uneven, grassy, scattered with worked stone. In front of you rises a rough, weathered mass of masonry. It does not look like much at first. A broken stump of brick and stone, softened by centuries of rain. But this is the end of one of the great engineering projects of the ancient world.

What you are looking at is a surviving pier from Trajan's Bridge. The Roman emperor Trajan ordered it built in the early second century, and it was finished around the year 105. It crossed the Danube from this bank, where the Roman town of Drobeta grew up, to the far shore, in what is now Serbia. For more than a thousand years afterwards, no one built a longer arched bridge anywhere in the world. That record alone is worth a pause.

Why here? Trajan was fighting the Dacians. They were a powerful kingdom north of the Danube, in lands that later became the heart of Romania. Rome had already fought one war against them. Trajan wanted a permanent, reliable crossing for soldiers and supplies before the next one. Boats and floating bridges could fail in floods or ice. A fixed bridge said something different. It said Rome intended to stay.

The man behind the design was Apollodorus of Damascus. He was a Greek-speaking architect and engineer from Syria, and he worked closely with Trajan. In Rome, he is also linked to the great Forum of Trajan. Here, his challenge was the river itself. The Danube is deep and fast at this point. It is also very wide. Building stone supports in moving water, without modern machinery, demanded careful planning and an enormous workforce.

The bridge had about twenty massive piers of stone and brick, set across the river bed. On top of them, the builders placed wooden arches that carried the roadway. So imagine a long line of heavy masonry columns marching across the water, linked by curving timber spans. The Roman historian Cassius Dio described the bridge with open admiration. He wrote about its scale and the difficulty of building it in such a powerful current. His account is one of our main written sources.

How exactly did the Romans set those piers in the river? Scholars still discuss this. A common explanation is that workers built temporary enclosures in the water, perhaps with wooden posts, and drained them to work on the river bed. Some researchers think the river may have been partly diverted. The honest answer is that the details are not fully certain. What is certain is the result. The bridge carried an army into Dacia.

Here is something many visitors miss. You can see this bridge in Rome. Trajan's Column, the tall carved column in the centre of the city, shows scenes from the Dacian Wars in a spiral band of relief. Among those scenes is a long bridge with stone piers and wooden arches, and the emperor making a sacrifice beside it. Historians generally take it as a picture of this crossing. So the structure in front of you was famous enough to be carved into Rome's own war memorial.

The second war ended in Roman victory. Dacia became a Roman province, and Drobeta grew into an important town around the bridgehead. Just beside you are the remains of the Roman fort of Drobeta. Its low stone walls and foundations are still visible. Soldiers guarded the bridge from here. Over time, a civilian settlement spread around the fort, with houses, baths and workshops. This was a frontier, but also a place where people lived ordinary lives.

The bridge did not stay in use for long. Trajan's successor, Hadrian, came to power in 117. According to Cassius Dio, Hadrian had the wooden superstructure removed. The stated reason was defence. Without the deck, raiders from the north could not use the bridge to cross into the empire. There is also a darker story. Some ancient writers claim that Hadrian and Apollodorus disliked each other. Whether that rivalry played any part here is uncertain, and it is best treated as tradition rather than proven fact.

So the stone piers were left standing in the river, without the timber spans between them. And they stayed there, for centuries. Travellers on the Danube recorded them long after Rome had gone. In the nineteenth century, during periods of very low water, people could see and count the old piers rising above the surface. Later, some of them were removed or reduced, because they were a danger to boats in the shipping channel. Others are still under the water today.

Now look across the river. On the Serbian bank, a matching stump of masonry marks the other end of the crossing. On a clear day, try to picture the line between the two. Every pier, every timber arch, every soldier marching over the water. The distance between the banks gives you the best sense of the scale. Here is a second detail many people overlook. Upstream, in the Iron Gates gorge, a Roman inscription cut into the rock records Trajan's work on the road along the river.

Back on this side, take a closer look at the pier itself. Notice the mix of materials, the rough faces of stone, the layers of brick. Notice where weather and plants have worn the edges. This is not a reconstruction. It is original Roman work, battered but still standing after nearly two thousand years. Nearby, the Iron Gates Region Museum displays finds from Drobeta and the area, and it adds useful context to what you see outside.

Before you leave, stand once more facing the water. The modern city of Drobeta-Turnu Severin lives around you now, with traffic, ships and the everyday sounds of a river port. Yet this rough block of stone is the reason the town is here at all. Listen to the current against the bank. It is the same river the Roman builders fought. As you walk away, look back once. The pier will look small again. You will know better.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the nearby Iron Gates Region Museum.
Walk the Drobeta fort ruins right beside the pier.
Visit on a clear day to see the Serbian shore.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven, grassy ground around the ruins.
Bring binoculars to study the far bank across the Danube.
