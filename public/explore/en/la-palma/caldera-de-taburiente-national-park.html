The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is wind moving through pine needles, a long soft hiss that rises and falls. Somewhere below, water runs over stone. The air smells of resin and warm earth, and in front of you the ground drops away into a huge bowl of rock and forest. Ridges fold into ridges. Cliffs glow red and ochre where the sun touches them. This is the Caldera de Taburiente, the green and stony heart of La Palma.

La Palma is one of the Canary Islands, the Spanish archipelago off the coast of north-west Africa. It is a small island with very steep land. And this great hollow sits almost at its centre. The rim runs in a rough horseshoe, several kilometres across. From the highest crest to the floor of the valley, the drop reaches well over a thousand metres in places. On the scale of the island, the effect is simple: you are looking into the middle of a mountain.

Here is the first surprise. The word caldera is now used by geologists all over the world for a large volcanic basin. That use owes a great deal to this place. In the early nineteenth century, the German geologist Leopold von Buch travelled in the Canaries. He studied this hollow, and he took the local Spanish word, which means cauldron, into the language of science. So every caldera on Earth, in a sense, carries the name of this valley.

The second surprise follows from the first. For a long time, people assumed this was a giant collapsed crater. Today, most geologists explain it differently. Much of the shape comes from erosion. Water, over a very long time, cut into the old volcanic mountain and carried the rock away. Landslides played their part too. So the bowl in front of you is less a crater and more a wound carved slowly by rain and streams.

All that water needs a way out, and there is only one. Look for the deep gorge that opens toward the south-west. It is called the Barranco de las Angustias, the Ravine of Anguish. Every stream inside the caldera eventually drains through it toward the sea. The name sounds dramatic, and the gorge earns it. When heavy rain falls, flash floods can run through this channel with real force. Walkers treat the barranco with respect for that reason.

Now look at the trees. Most of the forest is Canary Island pine, a species that grows only in this archipelago. Its long needles hang in soft bunches. Its bark is thick and plated, like armour. That bark matters. After a forest fire, the trunk can turn black, yet the tree often survives. Within months, fresh green shoots can push out along the scorched branches. Few pines in the world can do this so well.

On the high rim, the plants change. The air is thinner and the light is harsher. Here you may see low, cushion-like shrubs and, in late spring and early summer, the tall flower spikes of the tajinaste, a plant of the Echium family. Many plants in this park are endemic, meaning they grow naturally nowhere else, or only on La Palma and a few neighbouring islands. The protection of these plants was one reason the area became a national park in 1954.

Long before the Spanish arrived, people lived here. The original inhabitants of La Palma are known as the Benahoaritas. The caldera was one of their homelands, with water, grazing, and shelter. Archaeologists have recorded rock engravings around the island, often in spiral and circular patterns. Inside the caldera, one tall pillar of rock held special meaning. It is called the Roque Idafe, a thin needle of stone that rises from the valley floor.

Legend has it that the Benahoaritas feared the Roque Idafe might one day fall. If it fell, the story says, their world would end with it. To keep the rock standing, they brought it offerings of animal parts, and they sang to it. This is a tradition passed down through later accounts, so treat the details as story rather than precise history. Still, it shows how central this landscape was to the people who knew it first.

The Spanish conquest of La Palma came at the end of the fifteenth century. According to the chronicles, the caldera was the last stronghold of resistance. A Benahoarita leader named Tanausú held out here, protected by the cliffs and the single narrow entrance. The Castilian forces could not take the valley by direct attack. The accounts say Tanausú was finally captured after being drawn out to talks. Many islanders still remember him as a symbol of resistance.

Today, the visitor experience depends on where you stand. From the high viewpoints on the rim, you see the whole bowl at once. From La Cumbrecita, on the eastern side, the view opens straight across the valley to the far cliffs. Lower down, footpaths lead into the forest and toward the streams. One well-known spot is a small waterfall where iron-rich water has stained the rock orange, yellow, and green. Locals call it the Cascade of Colours.

Above you, on the northern rim, white domes may catch the light. These belong to the Roque de los Muchachos Observatory, one of the most important sites for astronomy in the Northern Hemisphere. The air here is clean and steady, and the island protects its night sky with strict lighting rules. On a clear night, the stars above the caldera are thick enough to make the dark look grainy. The Milky Way often appears as a pale, solid band.

Before you move on, stay still for one more minute. Notice how the clouds behave. Often they pour over the rim from the east and then dissolve as they sink into the warm bowl. Watch the shadows slide across the cliffs as the sun moves. Listen again for the water below. All of this — the pines, the red rock, the single gorge to the sea — is still slowly changing. When you walk away, you leave a valley that is still being carved, one rainfall at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check whether La Cumbrecita parking needs an advance reservation.
Arrive early, before clouds spill over the eastern rim.
Bring layers; the rim is much colder than the coast.
Avoid the barranco if heavy rain is forecast.
Carry plenty of water for any descent into the valley.
