The guide

The story

Before you see anything, you hear it. A low rustle drifts up from the forest floor, then a rising chatter, like rain on leaves. The air here is damp and green. It smells of moss, wet limestone, and earth that never fully dries. Under your feet, the path is soft and uneven. And just ahead, where the trees suddenly stop, the ground simply ends. You are standing at the edge of one of the deepest open-air pits on Earth.

This is the Sótano de las Golondrinas, known in English as the Cave of Swallows. It sits in the Huasteca Potosina, the lush eastern region of San Luis Potosí state, near the small town of Aquismón. The word sótano means basement or cellar in Spanish. Here, locals use it for a vertical shaft that drops straight into the ground. It is not a cave you walk into. It is a hole you look down into.

And what a hole it is. The opening is a rough oval, many tens of metres across. From the lower lip of the rim, the drop to the floor is commonly cited at more than three hundred metres of open air. That is a free fall with no ledges to break it. Visitors often compare it to a skyscraper. A very tall building could stand inside this shaft, and its roof would still sit below your feet.

Here is the first surprise. Despite the name, the famous birds of this cave are mostly not swallows. They are white-collared swifts — dark, fast birds with a pale band around the neck. Green parakeets nest here too, in the cracks and ledges of the walls. Early visitors saw clouds of small dark birds and called them swallows. The name stuck, even though the biology changed in the telling.

The birds give this place its daily rhythm. Every morning, soon after first light, thousands of swifts begin to circle inside the shaft. They do not fly straight out. Instead, they spiral upward in wide loops, gaining height together, round and round. The noise grows louder as the spiral rises. Then, group by group, they break free over the rim and scatter across the forest to feed.

The evening is the second act — and many people say it is the better one. As the light fades, the swifts return. They gather high above the pit. Then they fold their wings and drop, falling headfirst into the opening at remarkable speed. Just before they vanish into the dark, they open their wings again. From the rim, it can look like the forest is raining birds.

That is why timing matters so much here. The birds keep to the light, not to a clock, so the moment changes with the seasons and the weather. Most visitors aim for the dawn exit or the dusk return. Local guides can tell you what to expect on the day. Arriving early also gives your eyes time to adjust to the dim, misty light over the pit.

Now look at the walls themselves. The rock is limestone, the same pale stone that shapes much of this region. Over a very long time, slightly acidic water dissolved it from below and from above. Eventually the roof of a hidden chamber gave way, leaving this open shaft. The walls are streaked with green moss and hanging plants. Near the top, ferns and vines spill over the edge as if the forest wants to climb in.

The access to this place is in local hands. The land around the pit belongs to an indigenous Teenek community. The Teenek, sometimes called the Huastec people, have lived in this part of Mexico for many centuries. Members of the community manage the paths, the viewing area, and the guides. When you visit, you are a guest on their land, and the rules at the rim come from them.

One of those rules is quiet. At dawn especially, visitors are asked to keep their voices low and avoid sudden noise or bright lights. The idea is simple: do not disturb the birds before they leave. It also changes the experience. A group of strangers standing silently in the half-light, waiting for the first wings to rise, feels closer to a ceremony than a tourist stop.

The pit has drawn adventurers too. Cavers first descended the shaft by rope in the 1960s, and the long free drop made it famous among vertical explorers. Over the years, rappellers and parachute jumpers came as well. Today, access for these activities is controlled by the community and is far more limited than it once was. For most visitors, the view from the rim is the whole adventure — and it is more than enough.

The second surprise is how alive the bottom is. From above, the floor looks like a soft green blur, almost too far to read. But that floor holds its own small world of plants, insects, and debris that has fallen from above for a very long time. Very little sunlight reaches it directly. What you see as a faint glow down there is daylight that has travelled hundreds of metres to arrive.

The walk itself is part of the visit. A long stairway of stone steps leads down through the forest from the village to the rim. Going down is easy. Climbing back up, in humid air, is a real effort, so pace yourself. Along the way, listen to the forest around you. You will hear insects, distant dogs, perhaps a radio from a nearby house. Daily life here carries on beside one of nature's strangest places.

Before you leave, take one more look. Notice how the light changes on the far wall as the sun moves. Notice how small the circling birds look against the depth below them. Then turn back toward the trees. As you climb the steps, the sound of the pit will fade behind you. But the next morning, and every morning after, the spiral will rise again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive before first light to watch the dawn spiral.
Return near dusk to see swifts dive into the pit.
Keep voices low and flashlights off near the rim.
Save energy for the long humid climb back up.
Wear shoes with grip for damp, uneven stone steps.
