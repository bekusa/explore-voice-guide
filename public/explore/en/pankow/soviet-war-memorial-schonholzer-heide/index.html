The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Pankow fades behind the trees, and the gravel crunches softly underfoot. Pine needles and damp leaves give the air a resin smell. Ahead of you, the path opens into a long, quiet space. Hedges, lawns and dark stone all lead the eye toward one tall shape at the far end. This is the Soviet War Memorial in the Schönholzer Heide, and it is a cemetery before it is a monument.

Berlin has three large Soviet war memorials. Most visitors know the one in Treptower Park, with its giant soldier holding a child. Fewer find their way here, to the northern edge of the city. That is a pity, because this place feels more private. It is a burial ground first. More than 13,000 Soviet soldiers lie beneath these lawns, most of them killed in the final battle for Berlin in the spring of 1945.

That battle was short and brutal. In April 1945 the Red Army fought its way into the city from the east and north, street by street. Pankow was among the first districts the troops reached. Many thousands of soldiers died in the last weeks of a war that was almost over. After the fighting, Soviet authorities gathered many of the dead from temporary graves across the district and reburied them here.

The memorial was built in the late 1940s and completed in 1949. Germany was still in ruins at the time. The country was splitting into two states, and this district would become part of East Berlin. Stone was scarce. Labour was scarce. The Soviet military still chose to build on a grand scale. The size of this ground was itself a message: the sacrifice had been enormous, and it would not be forgotten.

Look at the layout as you walk. The design follows a strict axis, a single straight line from the entrance to the obelisk. On both sides, long walls frame the central lawns. This symmetry is typical of Soviet memorial architecture. It slows your pace, and it makes you walk the whole distance before you reach the focal point. Nothing here is accidental. The long approach is part of the experience.

The side walls carry bronze plaques. Many of them list names, written in the Cyrillic alphabet. Here is something many visitors miss: only a portion of the men buried here could ever be identified. Thousands of the dead had no papers. Some were too badly hurt to recognise. So the names you see are only part of the story. Many soldiers in these graves remain unknown.

Now turn toward the obelisk. It rises high above the trees, in dark polished stone. At its base stands a hall, and inside is the emotional centre of the whole memorial. A bronze figure of a grieving mother holds the body of a dead soldier across her lap. The pose recalls the pietà of Christian art, the Virgin Mary holding the body of Christ. Here, though, she is usually read as the Motherland mourning her son.

Stand quietly near the sculpture for a moment. The memorial does not show a victorious warrior raising a sword. It shows loss. The mother's head is bowed, and the soldier's body hangs heavy. For a Soviet monument of this period, that is a surprising choice. Most memorials of the era celebrated triumph. This one asks you to grieve.

The park around you has its own layered past. The Schönholzer Heide was once a popular place for Berliners to escape the city, with woods, paths and places for leisure. During the war, the heath also held a camp for forced labourers, people brought from occupied countries to work for the German war economy. So this ground carries more than one kind of wartime suffering, and the memorial sits on top of it.

During the Cold War, this corner of Pankow lay close to the border between East and West Berlin. Official ceremonies took place here on anniversaries of the war's end, with wreaths, flags and speeches. After German reunification in 1990, the question of what to do with these memorials could have become difficult. Instead, Germany agreed to protect and maintain the Soviet war graves. They remain under that care today.

The memorial was later restored. The stone was cleaned, the bronze was treated, and the paths and plantings were renewed. You may notice that some surfaces look fresher than others. That contrast is worth a closer look. Old weathering marks sit beside newer repairs, and together they show how a place like this needs constant attention to survive the Berlin climate.

On most days the memorial is very quiet. You may meet a dog walker, a jogger, or a family from the neighbourhood crossing the park. Around the anniversary of the war's end in early May, the mood changes. Flowers, wreaths and ribbons appear at the base of the obelisk. Many are left by people from countries of the former Soviet Union. Some families still come to remember grandfathers and great-uncles who never came home.

Before you leave, walk slowly back along the central axis and look over your shoulder once or twice. The obelisk grows smaller between the trees, and the sound of the city returns little by little. The lawns on either side look like ordinary grass, but you now know what lies beneath them. As you step back into Pankow, notice the birdsong, the rustle of the pines, and the ordinary life of the park around a very quiet field of graves.

Practical tips
