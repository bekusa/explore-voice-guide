The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere behind the trees the small River Panke runs over its stones. The palace in front of you is pale and calm, with tall windows and a modest front. It does not shout for attention. Yet within these walls, a queen spent decades of her life, a socialist state received its guests, and people argued about the future of a divided country. Welcome to Schönhausen Palace, in the Pankow district of northern Berlin.

Start with the shape of the building. It is Baroque, a European style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries that liked symmetry, order and calm proportions. Here that style is gentle. There are no towers and no gilded domes. Instead you see a long, balanced front, a raised central section, and rows of windows that let light deep into the rooms. It was built as a country house for royalty, not as a fortress. That difference still shows in how open and quiet it feels.

The central figure in this palace's story is Elisabeth Christine of Brunswick. She was the wife of the Prussian king Frederick the Second, known as Frederick the Great. Their marriage was arranged, and it was famously distant. Frederick lived mostly apart from her, at his own palaces in Potsdam. Soon after he became king in 1740, he gave Schönhausen to his queen. For the rest of her long life, this became her summer home and her own small world.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Elisabeth Christine was often treated as a lonely figure, but she was not idle. She read widely, wrote, and translated religious and moral texts from French into German. She kept a small court, received guests, and took a real interest in the gardens. Schönhausen gave her a space where she could set her own rhythm. She spent her summers here for more than half a century, until her death in 1797.

The palace did not have a peaceful time throughout those years. During the Seven Years' War, a major European conflict in the middle of the eighteenth century, enemy troops reached the Berlin area. The palace was damaged and looted. Afterwards it was repaired and partly rebuilt in the 1760s. Much of the building you see today carries the form it took after that rebuilding. So the calm front in front of you is, in part, a recovery from war.

Now turn toward the park. It runs along the Panke, the small river that gave Pankow its name. In the nineteenth century, the garden was redesigned in the English landscape style. That style replaced strict geometric beds with open lawns, curving paths and groups of trees that look natural. Walk a little way and you will notice how the views open and close. Each bend in the path shows you a different picture of the palace.

After the queen died, the palace lost its central purpose. Through the nineteenth century it was used only now and then by the royal family. Then, in the 1930s, it served a dark role. The Nazi regime used the building to store confiscated modern art, which it called degenerate. Some of these works were then sold abroad for foreign currency. It is a sobering thought: paintings now admired in museums around the world may once have rested in these rooms.

After the Second World War, Pankow lay in the Soviet zone of Berlin. In 1949 the German Democratic Republic, the socialist state of East Germany, was founded. Its first and only president, Wilhelm Pieck, took Schönhausen as his official residence. He lived and worked here until his death in 1960. Nearby streets became home to many senior East German officials. For years, people in the West used the word Pankow as a shorthand for the East German government itself.

Here is the second surprise. After Pieck's death, the palace became a guesthouse for the East German state. Foreign heads of state and official delegations stayed here during their visits. The rooms were refitted in the style of the 1960s, with furniture and fittings chosen to impress. Today some of these rooms are preserved. That means one building now holds two very different worlds: an eighteenth-century queen's home and a modern socialist reception house.

The story continues into the final days of East Germany. In the winter of 1989 and 1990, after the Berlin Wall fell, a forum called the Round Table brought together the old government and new opposition groups. They discussed elections, reforms and the country's next steps, and for a time they met here on the palace grounds. Talks linked to German reunification also took place at Schönhausen in 1990. The quiet garden once heard some of the most important conversations of that era.

After reunification, the palace stood largely empty for years. It was eventually restored and opened as a museum in 2009. It is cared for by the foundation that also looks after the Prussian palaces in Potsdam and Berlin. The restoration aimed to keep the layers visible rather than choose one period. Inside, you move from the queen's rooms to the rooms of the East German era, and the change in style is immediate.

As you walk through, pay attention to the small things. Notice how the light falls differently on painted walls and on later surfaces. Notice the scale of the rooms. They are elegant but not enormous, built for living rather than grand ceremony. Think about the different people who stood at these windows and looked out at the same trees. A queen in silk. A president in a dark suit. Delegates arguing late into the night about the shape of a new Germany.

Before you leave, stand once more in front of the palace and look back at it. The pale front, the tidy windows, the gravel at your feet. It seems almost too modest for its history. Then walk slowly toward the river and listen to the Panke again. The water has run past this house through war, empire, division and reunion. It is still running now, quietly, as you step back into the streets of Pankow.
