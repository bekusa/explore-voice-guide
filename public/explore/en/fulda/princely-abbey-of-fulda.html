The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the wide square in front of Fulda Cathedral. Feel the cobblestones under your feet, and look up at the twin towers. The stone is a soft, warm sandstone, and in afternoon light it glows almost pink. Pigeons move across the open ground. Somewhere nearby, a bell may be marking the hour. This calm baroque square sits on top of more than twelve centuries of history, and much of Europe's early Christian story passes through this exact spot.

The story begins in 744. A monk named Sturmius came into this forested valley beside the River Fulda. He was a follower of Boniface, an English missionary who spent his life spreading Christianity among the Germanic peoples. Boniface wanted a monastery here, a firm base deep in the interior of the land. Sturmius became its first abbot. At that time this was wild country, far from any large town. The city of Fulda you see today grew up around the monastery, not the other way around.

In 754, Boniface was killed on a missionary journey in Frisia, in what is now the northern Netherlands. He had asked to be buried at Fulda, and his body was carried back here. That decision changed everything. The tomb of a martyr drew pilgrims, gifts, and royal attention. Boniface became known as the Apostle of the Germans, and his grave turned this young monastery into one of the most important religious centres north of the Alps.

Here is something many visitors miss. The cathedral museum keeps an old manuscript linked to Boniface, known as the Ragyndrudis Codex. Its pages carry deep cuts. According to a popular tradition, Boniface raised this book above his head to protect himself when he was attacked. Historians cannot confirm that story. But the damaged book is real, and it has been treated with great respect here for centuries.

In the ninth century, the monastery became a powerhouse of learning. Its most famous abbot was Rabanus Maurus, a scholar so respected that later generations called him the Teacher of Germany. Under him, the abbey school trained monks, writers, and future leaders. The library grew into one of the largest of its age. Monks copied ancient texts by hand, and some classical works survived because a scribe in Fulda took the time to copy them.

One remarkable piece of early German writing is connected to this abbey. The Hildebrandslied, the Song of Hildebrand, is one of the oldest surviving poems in the German language. It tells of a tragic meeting between a father and his son on a battlefield. The fragment was written down at Fulda in the ninth century. Today it is kept elsewhere, but its words were first set on parchment here.

The abbey did not only grow in learning. It grew in land and power. Over the centuries the abbots received rich estates and special privileges from emperors. By the Middle Ages they held the rank of princes within the Holy Roman Empire, the loose union of German territories that lasted until the early nineteenth century. That is where the name comes from — the Princely Abbey. Its leader was both a churchman and a ruler of territory.

Now look again at the cathedral in front of you. It is not the medieval church. Early in the ninth century, the monks raised a vast basilica on this site, built to honour Boniface. It followed the model of the old church of Saint Peter in Rome. That great building stood for hundreds of years. In the early eighteenth century, it was taken down, and the baroque cathedral you see now rose in its place.

The architect was Johann Dientzenhofer, from a family of builders well known across Bohemia and southern Germany. He gave Fulda a bright, ordered church with a central dome and a facade framed by two towers. Step inside, and you will notice how much white and light there is. The decoration is rich, but the space feels open. In 1752, Fulda was raised to a bishopric, and this church became a cathedral.

Below the high altar lies the heart of the whole site — the crypt with the tomb of Boniface. Visitors often go quiet as they walk down. The tomb altar carries a carved relief showing his death. Every year, the Catholic bishops of Germany meet in Fulda and gather at this grave. This custom began in the nineteenth century, and it continues today. So this crypt is still a living place, not only a museum.

When you come back outside, turn toward the small church just beside the cathedral. This is the Church of Saint Michael. Its oldest part is a round chapel from the Carolingian era, the age of Charlemagne and his successors. It was built as a burial chapel for the monks. Inside, the low crypt and thick round columns feel very different from the bright baroque cathedral. Here you are standing in one of the oldest church spaces in Germany.

The prince-abbots did not live in a simple monastery cell. A short walk away stands the City Palace, their grand residence, also rebuilt in baroque style in the early eighteenth century. Its gardens and orangery show how these churchmen lived as princes. The rule of the abbey ended in the early 1800s, when Church territories across Germany were dissolved. Yet the buildings stayed, and they still shape the centre of Fulda.

Before you leave, take a slow look around the square once more. The towers above you, the round stone chapel beside them, the old city streets behind you — together they hold the outline of a monastery that once ruled a principality. Listen for the bells. Notice the colour of the stone as the light changes. When you walk away, you are walking through a town that began with one monk, one forest clearing, and one grave.
