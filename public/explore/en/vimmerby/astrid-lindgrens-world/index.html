The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere through the pines, a child is laughing. A wooden gate creaks on its hinges, and a voice is shouting lines in Swedish from a stage you cannot yet see. The air smells of resin, cut grass and warm timber. The ground under your feet is gravel and packed earth, not polished stone. This place does not feel like a theme park. It feels more like a forest that has been filled with stories... and you are walking straight into them.

Welcome to Astrid Lindgren's World, on the edge of the small town of Vimmerby, in the Swedish province of Småland. This is the home country of one of the most widely read children's authors on Earth. Her books have been translated into many dozens of languages. Yet the park does not show her stories behind glass. Instead, it builds the houses, farms and fortresses from her books at full size, and then lets actors bring the characters to life around you.

To understand why the park is here, start with the writer herself. Astrid Lindgren was born in 1907 on a farm called Näs, just outside Vimmerby. She grew up in a large farming family, surrounded by fields, animals, hay lofts and long summer evenings. Many readers believe that this childhood shaped almost everything she later wrote. The free, wild play of the farm children in her books came directly from the life she knew right here, in this landscape of red cottages and dark forests.

She became famous in the 1940s with a girl named Pippi Longstocking. Pippi is the strongest girl in the world. She lives alone with a horse and a monkey, and she ignores almost every rule the adults try to give her. At the time, some critics found her shocking. Children loved her immediately. Look around now, and you will probably see small visitors in red braids that stick out sideways — the clearest sign that Pippi is still very much alive.

The park itself grew up in the early 1980s, and it expanded slowly over the following decades. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The buildings were not designed by a theme-park company with a giant budget. They grew from the books, scene by scene, and the writer herself knew about the project during her lifetime. That is why the place feels handmade. The paint is a little faded. The fences lean. Nothing here is shiny, and that is exactly the point.

Follow the paths, and you will reach Villa Villekulla, Pippi's crooked house. It is painted in bright colours, with a veranda where her spotted horse usually stands in the books. Inside and around it, children climb, explore and listen to Pippi argue cheerfully with the local police. Notice how the house is a little odd, a little uneven. It looks like the kind of house a child would draw — and in a way, that is what it is.

Another part of the park takes you to Katthult, the farm of Emil of Lönneberga. Emil is a boy who never means to cause trouble, but trouble follows him everywhere. In the stories, he is often locked in the woodshed as punishment, where he carves little wooden men. Find the woodshed here and look inside. The rows of small carved figures are a quiet joke shared between the park and its most loyal readers.

Now for the second surprise. In some areas, the buildings are made at a reduced scale. Whole streets and little town houses are built smaller than normal, so that children stand at the right height and adults suddenly feel like giants. Stop for a moment and crouch down. From a child's eye level, the windows, doorways and steps make perfect sense. The park is quietly built for its smallest visitors first, and for the rest of us second.

Deeper in the forest, the mood changes. Here you will find the world of Ronja, the Robber's Daughter, with its rough timber fortress and its stony, mossy ground. This is one of Lindgren's darker and wilder books. It is about a girl who grows up among robbers, and about friendship across a family feud. The trees here feel taller, and the light becomes greener. Even the sound of the actors' voices seems to travel differently between the trunks.

You will also meet other stories along the way. There are the children of Noisy Village, known in Swedish as Bullerbyn, who live on three neighbouring farms. There is Karlsson, the small, round man who lives on a roof and flies with a propeller on his back. And there is The Brothers Lionheart, a serious and moving book about two brothers and courage in the face of loss. Each world has its own corner, with its own colours and its own pace.

The heart of the park is theatre. Throughout the day, short plays take place on outdoor stages and directly in the streets and farmyards. The performances are mostly in Swedish. Do not worry if you do not understand the words. The actors use big movements, music and clear emotion, and the stories carry well without translation. Watch the faces of the children around you. They are not watching a show... they are watching old friends.

It is worth remembering that this park is only one piece of the Lindgren landscape. In the town of Vimmerby, there is a statue of the writer, and her childhood home at Näs is preserved as a separate site, with its own exhibitions and garden. She died in 2002, but in this municipality her name is everywhere — on street signs, shop windows and café menus. The park is less a monument to her than a continuation of her work.

Before you leave, stand still for a final moment. Listen again for the creak of the gates, the laughter, the voices from the stages. Look at the red wooden walls, the uneven fences, the children running between them without any map. This is what Astrid Lindgren believed childhood should be: free, a little wild, full of stories. As you walk back toward the gate, notice how slowly the adults are moving now... as if, for one afternoon, they have remembered how to play.
