The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through dry grass. A dog barks somewhere in the village. Your shoes press into pale, dusty earth, and between the houses of Ballıhisar, blocks of carved marble lie in the fields. Some are half buried. Some have become garden walls. This quiet farming village sits on top of one of the most sacred places of the ancient world.

This is Pessinus. Today it belongs to the Sivrihisar district, in the province of Eskişehir, on the high, open plateau of central Anatolia. Around two thousand years ago, pilgrims crossed this same steppe to reach it. They were not coming to trade or to fight. They were coming to see a goddess. More exactly, they were coming to see a stone.

The goddess was the great Mother of the Gods. The Phrygians called her Matar, which simply means mother. The Greeks called her Meter or Cybele, and at Pessinus she was also linked with the name Agdistis. The Phrygians were the people who ruled much of inland Anatolia in the early first millennium before Christ. For them, she was the power of mountains, wild animals and the untamed land. And her most important home was here.

At the heart of her sanctuary there was no great statue. Ancient writers describe a stone, dark in colour and not very large, which people said had fallen from the sky. Many modern scholars think it may have been a meteorite. Ancient authors even played with the town's name. Some linked Pessinus to a Greek word for falling. That is a folk explanation, not proven history — but it shows how deeply the stone shaped the identity of this place.

Now comes the first surprise. That stone did not stay here. In 204 BC, Rome was exhausted by its long war against Hannibal, the Carthaginian general. Roman priests consulted their sacred books of prophecy. According to the ancient historians, the books said that the foreign enemy would leave Italy if the Mother of the Gods came to Rome. So Rome sent an embassy east, with the help of its ally, the king of Pergamon.

The sacred stone travelled from this plateau to the coast, and then by ship to Italy. Rome built a temple for her on the Palatine Hill, and she became known as Magna Mater, the Great Mother. A famous Roman story tells that the ship stuck in the mud of the Tiber River, and a noblewoman freed it alone with a simple rope. That tale is legend. But the arrival of the goddess in Rome is real history, and it began here, in this field.

Her priests were famous, and also a little frightening to outsiders. They were called the Galli. Ancient sources say they gave up their manhood in devotion to the goddess, in memory of Attis, her young companion in myth. They danced to drums and cymbals, and their processions were loud and wild. Greek and Roman writers often found them strange. At Pessinus, the high priests were powerful figures. For long periods, they acted almost like rulers of a small temple state.

Other peoples passed through this land as well. Legend links the sanctuary with King Midas of Phrygia, the ruler of the golden touch, and one ancient story says he founded the temple. Treat that as tradition, not record. More certain is the arrival of the Galatians in the third century before Christ. These were Celtic tribes who crossed into Anatolia from Europe. One of their tribes made this region its territory, and the goddess's town became part of their world.

Later, Rome took control of Anatolia, and Pessinus took on a Roman face. Under the early emperors, a new temple rose here. Belgian archaeologists from Ghent University began excavating in the late 1960s, and they found its foundations. What makes it unusual is what stood in front of it. A wide flight of steps doubled as rows of seats, like a small theatre facing the temple. Crowds could sit and watch the rituals take place before the sanctuary.

Here is the second surprise. Through the middle of the ancient town ran a stream, and the Romans did not leave it wild. They turned it into a canal, lined with walls of cut stone and marble. Small dams controlled the water. On dry summer days it was a shaded channel through the city. In spring, it could carry floodwater safely away. Few visitors expect this kind of careful engineering in a place so quiet today.

In Christian times, the goddess faded, but the town did not vanish at once. Pessinus became the seat of a bishop and an important church centre for this part of Anatolia. Slowly, though, the population declined. Earthquakes, changing trade routes and the general troubles of late antiquity took their toll. Over the centuries, houses rose over the ruins, and the old stones found new lives as building material in the village.

That is why this site feels so different from the great marble cities of the Turkish coast. Here, there are no tall columns standing against the sky. Instead, you find history scattered in fragments. A carved cornice beside a farmyard. An inscription in Greek set into a wall. Part of the finds are gathered in the village, displayed in the open air, so you can walk among them and read the stone up close.

As you walk, look at the land itself. The plateau is wide and almost treeless, with low hills on the horizon. The light here is strong, and in the afternoon it turns the grass gold. It is easy to imagine processions crossing this open ground, the drums heard long before the priests appeared. The landscape has changed less than the town.

So take a moment before you leave. Stand near the steps of the old temple, or by the line of the ancient canal. Listen again to the wind and the village sounds. Somewhere in Rome, the stone that gave this place its fame was once honoured on a hill above the Tiber. But its story started here, in this quiet field, under this same wide Anatolian sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, dusty ground between ruins.
Bring water and a hat; the plateau offers little shade.
Visit in late afternoon for softer, golden light.
Respect village homes; many ruins sit near private yards.
Combine your visit with nearby Sivrihisar town.
