The guide

The story

Feel the slope under your feet as you climb. The grass is short and springy, and the wind arrives from the west across open ground. Ahead, low walls of pale, weathered sandstone rise out of the turf like broken teeth. Rooks call from the trees below. This is Sandal Castle, on its hill above the south side of Wakefield, and it gives you one of the widest views in this part of Yorkshire.

Pause at the top and turn slowly. To the north, the city spreads out with its cathedral spire. Below you, the valley of the River Calder opens up in fields, roads and rooftops. Now you can see why someone wanted a fortress exactly here. Whoever held this hill could watch the river, the roads and the land for many miles in every direction.

The story begins soon after the Norman Conquest. In the early twelfth century, the de Warenne family, the powerful Earls of Surrey, held huge estates across this region. They raised a castle on this spot. At first it was built of earth and timber. It followed a design called a motte and bailey, an artificial mound for the main tower beside a larger enclosed yard for buildings, animals and people.

That mound is still the strongest shape on the site. Look for the steep, rounded hill inside the ruins. It is not natural. Workers built it up by hand, basket by basket, from soil dug out of the surrounding ditches. Those deep ditches, once a moat, still curve around the castle. Even after nine centuries, the earth shows the outline of the plan.

In the thirteenth century, the timber defences were replaced with stone. A keep with round towers crowned the top of the motte. A stone curtain wall, a great hall and other buildings filled the bailey. Then came a clever extra defence called a barbican — a separate fortified gateway built on its own island in the moat. An attacker had to break through the barbican before even reaching the main castle.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The barbican at Sandal was placed between the bailey and the keep, not at the outer edge. So even if enemies captured the yard, they still faced another moat and another gate before the final tower. Stand near those lower stones and picture the bridges, the drawbridges, and the defenders looking down from above.

The moment that made this castle famous came in the winter of 1460, during the Wars of the Roses. That was the long struggle for the English crown between the House of York and the House of Lancaster. Richard, Duke of York, was here at Sandal with his army over Christmas. On 30 December, he left the safety of the walls and fought a Lancastrian force on the low ground below. The Battle of Wakefield was a disaster for him.

Richard was killed in the fighting, and so was his young son Edmund. Historians still argue about why he left the castle at all. Was he tricked? Was he short of food? Did he misjudge the size of the enemy? Nobody can say for certain. But the result was clear. His head was later displayed above a gate in York, a grim message from his enemies.

And here is the second surprise. If you learned the colours of the rainbow at school with the phrase "Richard Of York Gave Battle In Vain", you may have been remembering this very man. The rhyme is popularly linked to Richard and his defeat near Wakefield. Some locals also connect the nursery rhyme about the Grand Old Duke of York, marching his men up a hill and down again, to this place. That one is folklore, but it is easy to imagine as you stand on the slope.

The story did not end with defeat. Richard's son became King Edward the Fourth, and another son later became King Richard the Third. In the 1480s, Richard the Third paid for new building work at Sandal. He seems to have planned it as a base for governing the north of England. But his reign was short. After his death at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485, the castle slowly lost its importance and began to decay.

It had one more war to see. During the English Civil War in the seventeenth century, royalist soldiers held Sandal Castle. Parliamentary forces besieged them, and the garrison surrendered in 1645. Afterwards, Parliament ordered the castle to be slighted. That means it was deliberately damaged so nobody could use it as a fortress again. Many walls came down then, and local people later carried away stone for their own buildings.

For centuries the site was a grassy ruin. Then, in the 1960s and early 1970s, archaeologists carried out major excavations here. They uncovered foundations, uncovered the layout of rooms and towers, and found thousands of objects, from pottery to weapons and coins. Today the site is cared for by Wakefield Council. Paths, bridges and information panels help you follow the plan, and a small visitor centre near the entrance tells more of the story.

As you walk, notice the quiet. Dog walkers pass by. Children run up the mound. Families sit on benches with the city below them. It is hard to believe this calm green hill once held a royal army, a siege and a bloody winter battle. The stones are low now, but the shape of power is still easy to read in the land.

Before you leave, stand on the highest point one more time. Look down towards the fields where Richard of York fought and fell. Feel the wind again, the same wind that blew across these walls in 1460. Then, as you walk back down the slope, watch the castle shrink behind you... and notice how the hill still keeps its watch over Wakefield.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the motte first for the clearest view of the layout.
Bring a windproof layer; the hilltop is very exposed.
Start at the visitor centre to understand the site plan.
Wear sturdy shoes; grass slopes get slippery after rain.
Visit late afternoon for long shadows across the ruins.
