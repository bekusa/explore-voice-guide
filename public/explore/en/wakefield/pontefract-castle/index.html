The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your feet. It is not flat. It rises and dips in soft grassy waves, and here and there a stub of pale, weathered stone pushes up through the turf. The wind moves easily across this hilltop. Below you, the roofs of Pontefract spread out in every direction. What you are standing on is not a ruin in the usual sense. It is the ghost of a fortress that was once one of the most powerful in northern England.

The story begins just after the Norman Conquest, in the late eleventh century. A Norman lord named Ilbert de Lacy was granted wide lands in Yorkshire, and he chose this rocky outcrop for his stronghold. The site made sense. It sat high above the surrounding country, close to important routes running north and south. The first castle was probably built of earth and timber. Over the following centuries it was rebuilt in stone, and it grew larger, stronger, and more important with each generation.

The town's name carries its own small puzzle. Pontefract comes from Latin words meaning broken bridge. Nobody is completely sure which bridge was broken, or when. Several local stories offer an answer, but none can be proven. In older writing you will often see the town called Pomfret, which is closer to how people once said it aloud. Keep that name in mind — it appears in one of the most famous plays in the English language.

By the fourteenth century, the castle belonged to the Earls of Lancaster, and later to the Duchy of Lancaster. This made it a royal possession of real weight. One of its lords, Thomas, Earl of Lancaster, led a rebellion against his cousin, King Edward the Second. He lost. After his defeat in 1322, he was brought back to his own castle and executed just outside it. Some people later treated him as a martyr, and pilgrims came to remember him.

Now for the darkest chapter, and the one that made this place famous. In 1399, King Richard the Second was overthrown by his cousin, Henry Bolingbroke, who became King Henry the Fourth. Richard was taken north and held here as a prisoner. Early in 1400, he died inside these walls. How he died is still debated. Many accounts say he was starved. Others suggest he was killed more directly. Whatever happened, a deposed king vanished from the world on this hill.

William Shakespeare made that moment unforgettable. In his play about Richard the Second, the fallen king is sent to Pomfret, and it is here that his final scene takes place. Shakespeare returns to the castle again in his play about Richard the Third, calling it a bloody place. That description was not only drama. In 1483, two relatives of the young Edward the Fifth were executed at Pontefract on the orders of the future Richard the Third.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The castle also has a place in the story of Henry the Eighth. In 1541, the king travelled north with his young fifth wife, Catherine Howard. During the royal stay at Pontefract, according to later evidence, Catherine met secretly with a courtier named Thomas Culpeper. Those meetings became part of the case against her. Within months, both were dead. So this quiet grass also carries the shadow of a Tudor queen.

The castle's final drama came in the English Civil War of the seventeenth century — the conflict between King Charles the First and Parliament. Pontefract was held for the King, and it was besieged three times. The fighting was long and hard, and the town around it suffered badly. Remarkably, Royalist soldiers were still holding the castle after King Charles had been executed in January 1649. It was one of the very last places in England to surrender.

When the garrison finally gave up, Parliament decided the castle should never be used again. The townspeople, who had endured years of war, were glad to see it go. The great walls and towers were pulled down. Much of the stone was carried away and reused in buildings around the town. That is why so little stands today. When you walk through Pontefract later, remember that some of its older walls may hold pieces of this fortress.

And yet, not everything was lost. Below ground, parts of the castle survive in surprising condition. The underground cellars, sometimes called the magazine, were used during the sieges to store supplies and to hold prisoners. If you visit them, look closely at the stone. Prisoners and soldiers scratched names, initials, and marks into the walls. These small carvings are among the most personal traces left on the site. They are the handwriting of people who waited here, not knowing how their war would end.

There is a sweeter story too, quite literally. Pontefract is known across Britain for liquorice. The liquorice plant once grew in the deep soil of this area, including land close to the castle. Local people turned the root into small round black sweets, stamped with a design, known as Pontefract cakes. The castle image has appeared on those stamps. It is a strange and lovely link — a site of royal death that also became the symbol of a children's sweet.

In recent years, the site has been carefully conserved. Paths now guide you around the remains, and a visitor centre explains what once stood here. Information panels help you picture the towers, the great hall, and the keep that rose on the highest point. Take your time with them. Try to imagine walls standing many times your own height, guards on the ramparts, and smoke rising from kitchen fires. Then look again at the empty grass.

As you walk on, notice how the town still curves around this hill. The streets below grew up in the shadow of the castle, and the shape of the place still remembers it. Listen to the traffic, the birds, the wind in the grass. Kings, rebels, soldiers, and prisoners all stood where you stand now. The fortress is mostly gone... but the hill it chose is still here, and so are you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven grassy slopes.
Ask at the visitor centre about guided cellar access.
Read the panels first to picture the lost towers.
Walk the town afterwards to spot reused castle stone.
Pick up Pontefract cakes from a local shop.
