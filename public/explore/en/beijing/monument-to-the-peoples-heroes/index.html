The guide

The story

Listen first. Around you, the open stone of Tiananmen Square carries the sound of thousands of footsteps, the crackle of a guard's radio, and wind with nothing to stop it. The paving is pale and smooth, worn by crowds. In the middle of all this flat space, one shape rises straight up. It is a tall, narrow obelisk of grey granite on a broad white base. This is the Monument to the People's Heroes, and it is the vertical anchor of the whole square.

The idea for this monument came before the People's Republic itself. On 30 September 1949, one day before the new state was proclaimed, delegates to the founding political conference gathered on this spot. Mao Zedong and the other leaders took part in a ceremony to lay the foundation stone. The country was not yet officially founded, but its leaders had already decided that the first thing on the square would be a memorial to the dead.

Building it took far longer than laying that first stone. Work began in the early 1950s and continued through most of the decade. The monument was finally unveiled on 1 May 1958, International Workers' Day. It stands roughly thirty-eight metres tall, about the height of a twelve-storey building. Thousands of blocks of granite and marble were cut, carved, and fitted together. Stone came from different parts of China, including a single great slab for the inscription panel.

Now here is the first detail most visitors miss. Look at which way the monument faces. Traditional Chinese palaces and temples face south, toward the sun. The Forbidden City behind Tiananmen Gate follows that old rule. But this monument turns its main face north. It looks directly at Tiananmen Gate, toward the place where the new state was proclaimed. The designers broke a rule that is many centuries old, and they did it on purpose.

On that north face, you can see eight large gold characters running down the shaft. They read: The People's Heroes Are Immortal. The calligraphy is in Mao Zedong's own hand, enlarged and carved into the stone. Chinese leaders often wrote inscriptions for important buildings, and handwriting carries real weight in Chinese culture. A person's brushstrokes are seen as a trace of their character. So the gold letters are not just words. They are meant as a personal mark.

Walk around to the south side, and the message changes. Here is a longer inscription composed by Mao and written out in the calligraphy of Zhou Enlai, the first premier. The text honours the dead in three layers of time. It remembers those who died in the three years of civil war before 1949. Then those who died in the thirty years of revolution before that. And finally, all those who fought for China's independence and freedom from 1840 onward.

That date, 1840, is the key to the whole design. It marks the start of the First Opium War with Britain. In Chinese history, that war opens a long period of foreign invasion, unequal treaties, and internal collapse. Many Chinese call it the century of humiliation. So this monument does not only honour Communist soldiers. It frames more than a century of struggle as a single story, ending in 1949.

That story is told in stone around the base. Look at the lower tier, and you will find large marble reliefs carved with crowds of figures. There are eight main panels, plus two smaller ones. They run in time order. The sequence begins with the burning of seized British opium at Humen, in southern China, in 1839. It moves through the Taiping rebellion, the 1911 uprising that ended the empire, and the student protests of May 1919.

The story continues with workers' strikes in Shanghai in the 1920s, the Nanchang Uprising of 1927, and guerrilla fighting against Japanese occupation. The largest panel, on the north side, shows the People's Liberation Army crossing the Yangtze River in 1949. Small scenes flank it, showing villagers bringing supplies to the front and welcoming the army. Many sculptors worked on these panels together. Look closely at the faces. Each figure has a distinct expression, not a copied mask.

The second surprise is the team behind the design. The architect Liang Sicheng, often called the father of modern Chinese architecture, led the design work. His wife, Lin Huiyin, was also an architect and a poet. She worked on the decorative patterns, including the carved wreaths of flowers around the base. Lin was seriously ill during these years. She died in 1955, before the monument was finished, and she is buried with one of her own wreath designs on her tomb.

Because it sits at the heart of the square, the monument has also been a stage for public feeling. In April 1976, during the Qingming festival, a traditional day for honouring the dead, huge crowds brought wreaths and poems here in memory of Zhou Enlai. The authorities cleared them by force. In 1989, student protesters again gathered around its base. Today, a barrier usually keeps visitors at some distance, and guards watch the area closely.

The monument still has a ceremonial role. Since 2014, China has marked Martyrs' Day on 30 September, the anniversary of that first foundation stone. National leaders come to lay flower baskets at the base. On national holidays, you may see large floral displays in the square nearby. Behind the monument, to the south, stands the mausoleum of Mao Zedong. In front, to the north, the flagpole and the gate complete a long ceremonial line.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment and look up along the shaft. Notice how the grey granite turns warmer in late light, and how the white balustrades below catch the sun first. Turn slowly in a circle. On every side, the square stretches away to the great halls and the old gate. This one column holds the centre. As you walk on, look back once — from a distance, it is easier to see how the entire square is arranged around it.
