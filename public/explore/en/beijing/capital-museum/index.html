The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the Capital Museum and look up. A long, flat roof stretches out over your head, far beyond the walls below it. The traffic of Chang'an Avenue hums behind you, one of the widest streets in Beijing. In front of you is a vast wall of glass. And behind that glass, something strange leans out of the building at an angle. It is a huge bronze cylinder, tilted as if it is slowly rising out of the earth.

That tilted form is the first surprise of this museum. It is not a sculpture standing in a hall. It is a hall. Inside that bronze shell are exhibition galleries, wrapped in a material that Chinese craftsmen have worked for thousands of years. The architects wanted the building to look like an ancient bronze vessel half-buried in the ground, just uncovered by a digger. Take a moment to notice how the cylinder pushes through the facade, breaking the clean grid of the glass.

The building you see today is fairly young. It opened in 2006, on the western stretch of Chang'an Avenue, close to Fuxingmen. Before that, the museum lived a very different life. For many years its collections were displayed inside the old Confucius Temple in the northeast of the city, among courtyards, cypress trees and stone tablets. Moving from a temple to this modern glass hall was a statement. Beijing wanted a museum that looked forward as much as it looked back.

The design was a collaboration between Chinese architects and a French team. You can feel both traditions in the building. The great overhanging roof is a modern echo of the deep eaves on traditional Chinese halls. Those eaves protected wooden buildings from rain and summer sun. The heavy stone base below the glass recalls the raised stone platforms of palaces and city walls. Old ideas, in other words, are rebuilt here in steel, stone and glass.

Now step inside, into the main atrium. The space is enormous and filled with light. Your eyes will probably go first to the bronze cylinder, which you can now see from within. But there is a second large form across from it — a rectangular block clad in warm wood. Bronze and wood face each other across the hall. Many visitors read this as a quiet conversation between the two oldest materials of Chinese craft.

Here is the second surprise, and it is easy to walk straight past it. Standing inside the atrium is a real memorial archway, the kind of decorated gateway called a pailou in Chinese. These gateways once stood over streets and temple approaches all over old Beijing. They honoured officials, virtuous families or sacred places. Most were taken down as the city widened its roads in the twentieth century. This one was saved and brought indoors, where it now stands under a glass sky.

Walk close to the archway if you can. Look at the painted beams, the brackets that lock together without nails, and the way the roof tiles sit in layers. Outside, a gateway like this would have weathered in dust storms and rain. Indoors it is protected, and you can study it at a closeness that was never possible on a busy street. It is a small piece of the old city, standing inside the new one.

The museum's core story is Beijing itself. One of the main exhibitions follows the city from early settlements through the many dynasties that made it a capital. You will see how a frontier town grew into Dadu, the capital of the Yuan dynasty, the Mongol rulers of China. Later it became the Ming and Qing capital, with the Forbidden City at its heart. Maps, models and objects help you picture streets that now lie under modern roads.

The collections go far beyond city history. The museum holds ancient bronzes, many of them ritual vessels once used to offer food and wine to ancestors. There are galleries of porcelain, with the cool blues and whites that made Chinese ceramics famous around the world. You will also find jade carvings, Buddhist statues, and paintings and calligraphy. The bronze cylinder itself holds some of these treasures, so the outside of the building gives a hint of what waits inside.

For many visitors, the most human gallery is the one about old Beijing customs. Here the museum shows everyday life in the city before the high-rises. There are scenes of family celebrations, festivals and the small rituals of daily routine in the hutongs. Hutongs are the narrow lanes lined with courtyard houses that once covered much of the city. This gallery feels less like a treasury and more like a family album, full of clothing, tools and toys.

As you move between floors, pay attention to the views. The glass wall lets you look out at Chang'an Avenue and the city beyond. Inside, you see objects that are hundreds or thousands of years old. Outside, you see buses, office towers and cyclists. The building is designed to keep both in your eye at the same time. It reminds you that the history on display happened on the same ground you are standing on.

A visit here can easily fill half a day, so give yourself time. The galleries are spread across several levels, and it helps to choose two or three exhibitions that interest you most. Then let yourself wander. The atrium works well as a meeting point, and it is a good place to sit for a moment between galleries. Look up again at the roof from inside, and notice how the light changes as clouds pass.

When you leave, turn back once more toward the facade. Find the bronze cylinder leaning through the glass, and the long roof shading the entrance. Think of the old archway standing quietly inside, far from the street it once crossed. Then listen to Chang'an Avenue around you, the same city the museum describes, still moving. You are walking out into the next chapter of the story those galleries tell.
