The guide

The story

Start with the sound of water. A soft slap against stone, the creak of a wooden boat at its mooring, and somewhere behind you the hum of a crowd moving slowly along a painted walkway. The air near the lake feels cooler than the city streets you just left. Ahead, a hill rises out of the water, stacked with yellow roofs and green tiles. This is the Summer Palace, and almost everything here was arranged so you would feel exactly this: calm.

In Chinese, the place is called Yiheyuan. The name is usually translated as the Garden of Preserved Harmony. It sits in the northwest of Beijing, well away from the Forbidden City and its heavy red walls. For emperors of the Qing dynasty, who ruled China until the early twentieth century, this was a retreat. It was a place to escape the summer heat, to rest, and to govern a little more quietly, away from the full weight of court ceremony.

The garden as a whole took its shape in the eighteenth century under the Qianlong Emperor. He built it in honour of his mother's birthday. He also had the lake dug wider and deeper, and the earth went into shaping the hill. So the two main features you see today, the lake and the hill, were not simply found here. They were engineered. Water and mountain are the two essential elements of a classical Chinese garden, and Qianlong wanted both on an imperial scale.

The lake in front of you is Kunming Lake. It covers roughly three quarters of the whole site, which surprises many first-time visitors. They expect a palace and find mostly water. The hill is Longevity Hill. On a clear day, look across the surface and you will see a long causeway lined with willows and small bridges. That causeway was modelled on a famous one at West Lake in Hangzhou, in southern China. Qianlong admired the south, and he brought its scenery north.

Now, a darker chapter. In 1860, during the Second Opium War, British and French troops looted and burned the imperial gardens around Beijing. This garden was heavily damaged. Much of what stands today is a rebuilding from the late nineteenth century. That reconstruction is tied to one woman above all others: the Empress Dowager Cixi. For decades she was the most powerful figure in the Chinese court. It was under her direction that the garden was restored and given the name it carries now.

The restoration carries a famous controversy. Many accounts say that money meant for China's modern navy was redirected to rebuild the palace. Historians still argue about how much money was involved and how directly it was moved. But the story has stayed attached to this place for well over a century. When China's fleet was defeated by Japan in the 1890s, many people connected that loss to the gardens and pavilions around you. Keep that tension in mind as you walk.

Which brings us to one of the most quietly ironic objects here: the Marble Boat. It sits at the western end of the Long Corridor, fixed at the edge of the water. Its base is stone. It will never sail. The upper decks were rebuilt in the late nineteenth century in a style that mixes Chinese and European details. For many visitors, a boat of stone that cannot move is a strange symbol for a court that was struggling to modernise.

The Long Corridor itself is the thread that ties the northern shore together. It runs for more than seven hundred metres along the foot of Longevity Hill. Walk a little way along it and look up. Almost every beam is painted. There are thousands of small scenes: landscapes, flowers, birds, and moments from classical novels and legends. Few people notice how varied they are. Pause under one section and try to find a story you recognise. Children often spot the Monkey King.

Above the corridor, the hill climbs toward the Tower of Buddhist Incense. It is an eight-sided tower on a tall stone base, and it crowns the whole composition. The climb up the steps is steep, and your legs will feel it. But from the terraces, the logic of the garden becomes clear. The lake spreads out below. The causeway draws a line across it. Far to the west, on a good day, the Western Hills sit pale and blue on the horizon.

Those distant hills are part of the design. Chinese garden builders used a technique often called borrowed scenery. It means framing a view so that something outside the walls appears to belong inside them. Here, a pagoda on a neighbouring hill and the mountains beyond were brought into the picture deliberately. So when you take a photograph from the hill, you are not just capturing a garden. You are capturing a view that designers planned more than two centuries ago.

On the eastern shore, a long bridge with seventeen arches crosses to a small island. Near its end stands a bronze ox, cast in the eighteenth century and placed to watch over the water. Legend links the ox to protection against floods. Locals also say that around the winter solstice, the low afternoon sun lines up with the bridge and lights all seventeen arches gold at once. Crowds gather for it each year, and photographers wait for hours.

There is one more human story worth knowing. Near the lake shore is a courtyard called the Hall of Jade Ripples. After a failed reform movement in 1898, the young Guangxu Emperor was confined within the palace grounds. Historical accounts say he spent long periods of that confinement here, under Cixi's control. Walls were built to close off the compound. It is a quiet space, and easy to pass by. Knowing what happened there changes the way the courtyard feels.

The garden suffered again in 1900, when foreign armies entered Beijing during the Boxer crisis. It was repaired once more. Later, after the end of imperial rule, it opened to the public. In 1998, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List. Today it belongs to everyone. On weekday mornings, you may see older residents doing slow exercises, singing, or writing characters on the paving stones with water brushes. In winter, people sometimes skate on the frozen lake.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Listen again to the water against the stone edge. Watch the willows move along the causeway. Notice how the roofs on the hill change colour as the light shifts. Emperors once looked at this same arrangement of water and mountain. They came here to slow down. Whatever brought you here today, the garden still does that same quiet work. Take your time leaving.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early on a weekday to avoid the heaviest crowds.
Wear comfortable shoes for the steep Longevity Hill steps.
Take a boat across Kunming Lake to rest your legs.
Allow at least half a day to see the main areas.
Bring water and sun protection in the summer months.
