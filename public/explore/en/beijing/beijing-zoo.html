The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the willows there is a soft, steady murmur of voices, the scrape of strollers on paving stones, and somewhere ahead a bird calling over the noise of the city. The air smells of damp earth, cut grass, and, now and then, the unmistakable scent of animals. Just beyond the trees, traffic moves around Xizhimen, one of the busiest corners of western Beijing. And yet, inside these gates, the pace slows. You have walked into one of the oldest public zoos in China.

This place did not begin as a zoo. In the final years of the Qing dynasty, the last imperial dynasty of China, these grounds were turned into an experimental farm and garden. It was called Wanshengyuan, often translated as the Garden of Ten Thousand Animals. The idea was modern for its time. The court wanted a place to study crops, plants, and livestock, in the spirit of reforms then spreading through the empire. Animals soon followed, and the garden opened its gates to visitors in the early twentieth century.

Some of the first animals arrived from far away. A Qing official returned from a tour of foreign countries and brought back a collection of animals bought in Europe. Imagine what that meant for people in Beijing at the time. Most had never seen a zebra, an ostrich, or a lion with their own eyes. Crowds came to stare. A walk through the garden was entertainment, education, and a glimpse of a wider world, all at once.

The imperial family came too. The Empress Dowager Cixi, who held real power at the Qing court for decades, visited the garden. For her, a Western-style pavilion was built inside the grounds. It is known as Changguanlou, and it still stands here today. Its arched windows, columns, and pale facade look surprising among the willows and Chinese roofs. It is a European design, built for a Chinese empress, in a garden of foreign animals. Few buildings in Beijing hold that mix so plainly.

As you move around, you may notice that water shapes much of the park. Ponds and streams run between the paths, and a canal passes along the edge of the grounds. In imperial times, this water route connected the city with the Summer Palace in the northwest. The court could travel by boat from near here toward the lakes and gardens there. Today, seasonal boat trips still follow part of that old route. Standing by the water, you can picture a royal barge sliding slowly past.

The twentieth century was hard on this garden. War, revolution, and long periods of neglect left it in poor condition. Many animals did not survive the difficult decades before 1949. After the founding of the People's Republic, the grounds were restored and expanded. In 1955, the park took the name it carries now, Beijing Zoo. From that point, it became a place for scientific work, breeding programmes, and, above all, family days out for millions of people in the capital.

And then there are the pandas. For many visitors, the giant panda house is the whole reason to come. Giant pandas live only in the mountain forests of central and southwestern China, and they became a national symbol in the twentieth century. Here, you can watch them do what pandas do best — eat bamboo, roll over, and sleep in surprising positions. Morning is often the liveliest time. Later in the day, they may be dozing high on a platform, barely moving at all.

Here is something many visitors miss. Pandas spend a large part of their day eating, because bamboo gives them very little energy. So the slow, lazy look is not laziness at all. It is a careful way of saving strength. Watch how a panda holds a stalk. It uses a special bone in its wrist almost like a thumb, gripping the bamboo while it strips the leaves. Once you see it, you will watch their paws differently.

Beyond the pandas, the zoo spreads into a long series of houses and enclosures. There are big cats, elephants, giraffes, primates, and many kinds of birds. Some areas are modern, with glass and open space. Others are older, and you can see how ideas about keeping animals have changed over the years. Inside the grounds there is also an aquarium, run as a separate attraction with its own entry. Give yourself time. This is a large park, and it is easy to walk for hours.

The human life of the zoo is worth watching, too. Grandparents push strollers along the shaded lanes. School groups move in tight lines behind a teacher with a small flag. Couples sit on benches by the ponds. On weekends and holidays, the paths fill with families from every part of the city and from across China. Many people here grew up visiting this place, and now they bring their own children. For a lot of Beijing residents, this zoo is a childhood memory.

The surroundings hold more history than you might expect. Just beyond the zoo, near the canal, stands the Temple of Five Pagodas, a Ming dynasty temple with an unusual stone tower crowned by five small pagodas. Across the main road is the Beijing Planetarium, popular with young science fans. And right outside the gates is a large metro station named after the zoo itself. In a few minutes, you can move from imperial gardens to stars to the hum of the modern city.

As you walk, pause once and look up. Old trees spread their branches over the paths, some planted long before most of today's enclosures were built. In spring, the willows turn a soft green. In summer, the shade is thick and cool. In autumn, the leaves turn gold against the grey walls. In winter, the paths are quieter, and the breath of the animals shows in the cold air. The garden changes with each season, and so does the mood.

So take a last look around you. The willows, the water, the European pavilion, the crowd gathered around the pandas — all of it belongs to one long story, from an empire's experimental farm to a family park in a modern capital. As you head back toward the gates, listen again. The bird calls, the voices, the distant traffic... The city is waiting just outside. But for a little while longer, the garden keeps its own slower time.
