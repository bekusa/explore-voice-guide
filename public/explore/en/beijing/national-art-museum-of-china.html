The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the steps for a moment and look up. Above you, rows of glazed tiles shine a warm, deep yellow. On a clear Beijing morning they catch the sun and glow almost like honey. Around you, the traffic of a wide avenue hums. In front of you rises a building that looks old but is not. This is the National Art Museum of China, and it holds one of the largest public collections of Chinese art anywhere.

The yellow tiles are a deliberate choice. In imperial China, yellow glazed roofs were reserved for the emperor and the most important buildings. You can see the same colour on the roofs of the Forbidden City, a short walk to the southwest. By using it here, the designers placed this museum in a long line of national buildings. The message is quiet but clear. This is a palace, but a palace for art.

The building belongs to a remarkable moment in Beijing's history. In the late 1950s, the new People's Republic launched a wave of large public buildings in the capital. The Great Hall of the People and the National Museum on Tiananmen Square came from the same burst of ambition. Work on this museum began in that period, and the building opened in the early 1960s. The architect most often credited with its design is Dai Nianci, one of the leading Chinese architects of his generation.

Look at the shape of the main hall. A central tower rises above the entrance, topped with a roof in the traditional style, with eaves that curve gently upward. Lower wings stretch out on each side. Open corridors with columns run along the front. The result mixes a modern concrete structure with the language of classical Chinese pavilions. It was a style many architects of the time explored — modern function inside, Chinese tradition on the outside.

Now find the calligraphy above the main entrance. The large characters give the name of the museum in Chinese. They were written by Mao Zedong himself. In China, a leader's handwriting on a public building carries real weight. It marks the place as officially important, almost like a seal. Many visitors walk under the sign without noticing whose brush made it.

Inside, the museum is organised around a series of exhibition halls on several floors. Unlike many great museums, much of the space here is given to changing exhibitions. One season you might find classical ink painting. The next, you could walk into contemporary sculpture, photography, or a large survey of a single living artist. So no two visits are quite the same. It is worth checking what is on before you come.

The permanent collection is enormous — well over one hundred thousand works. Its heart is Chinese art from the late nineteenth century to the present day. Here you can find ink paintings by masters such as Qi Baishi, famous for his shrimp, crabs, and small creatures drawn with a few quick strokes. You may also see work by Xu Beihong, a painter known for his galloping horses, who blended Chinese ink with lessons from European academic art.

Here is something many visitors miss. The museum also keeps a large collection of Chinese folk art. Think of paper cuts in bright red, woodblock New Year prints, embroidery, kites, and clay figures. These objects were made in villages, often by people whose names were never recorded. Their colours are strong and their stories are simple — wishes for luck, harvest, and children. If a folk art display is open during your visit, do not skip it. It shows a side of Chinese creativity that rarely reaches the big galleries abroad.

And a second surprise: the collection is not only Chinese. In the 1990s, the German collectors Peter and Irene Ludwig donated a group of Western artworks to the museum. Among them were works by Pablo Picasso. It is a reminder that this building has also served as a meeting point between Chinese and international art. When foreign exhibitions come to Beijing, they often come here.

The museum has also been the stage for sharp moments in Chinese art history. In early 1989, it hosted a landmark exhibition of experimental Chinese art, often called the China Avant-Garde exhibition. During the opening, the artist Xiao Lu fired a gun at her own installation inside the hall. Nobody was hurt, but the authorities closed the show for a time. Today, that act is remembered as one of the most famous performances in modern Chinese art.

As you move through the halls, notice the light. Many of the galleries are calm and high-ceilinged, with soft, even lighting that protects fragile ink and paper. Ink painting rewards slow looking. Step close and you can see how the brush pressed down, lifted, and trailed off. Step back, and the same marks become mountains, mist, or a bird on a branch. Give one painting a full minute, and it will change in front of you.

When you step outside again, look at the neighbourhood. The museum sits on a street named after the May Fourth Movement of 1919, when students in Beijing protested and demanded a new, modern culture for China. Wangfujing, one of the city's busiest shopping streets, lies just to the south. To the west are Jingshan Park and the old imperial heart of the city. So this museum stands between the old empire, the modern shops, and the memory of a student movement.

Before you leave, turn around once more at the bottom of the steps. Look again at the yellow roof against the Beijing sky, and at the brushstrokes above the door. Listen to the traffic, the voices of school groups, perhaps the click of a camera. This building was made to tell a story about China and its art. As you walk away toward the hutong lanes or the shops, you carry a small piece of that story with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current exhibitions before visiting; shows change often.
Book a reservation in advance if one is required.
Bring your passport for identity checks at entry.
Combine the visit with nearby Jingshan Park or Wangfujing.
Come early on weekdays to avoid school and tour groups.
