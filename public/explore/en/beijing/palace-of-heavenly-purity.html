The guide

The story

Stop for a moment at the top of the steps. Under your feet the stone is pale and worn smooth, polished by centuries of slippers, boots and now trainers. Ahead of you, a great roof of yellow glazed tiles rises in two layers, one set of eaves above another. When the sun is out, the tiles glow almost like honey. When the wind moves across the terrace, you may hear a small bell somewhere, or the murmur of a crowd pressing towards an open doorway.

This is the Palace of Heavenly Purity. It stands at the heart of the Inner Court of the Forbidden City, the private half of the imperial palace. Behind you lies the Outer Court, with its vast ceremonial halls, where emperors met officials in public. Here, the mood changes. The courtyards narrow. The buildings come closer together. You have crossed from the state's world into the emperor's household.

The palace was first raised in the early fifteenth century, when the Ming dynasty moved its capital to Beijing and built this whole walled complex. For much of the Ming era, this hall was the emperor's own residence. He slept here. He worked here. He received his closest ministers here. Fire, though, was a constant enemy in a city of timber. The building burned and was rebuilt more than once, and the hall you see today comes from a reconstruction at the very end of the eighteenth century.

Listen to the name for a moment. Heavenly Purity. A little further north stands its partner, the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, once linked with the empress. The pairing comes from an old line in the Daoist classic, the Dao De Jing, which says that heaven, by holding to unity, becomes pure, and earth, by holding to unity, becomes tranquil. The emperor was heaven. The empress was earth. Even the layout of these buildings carries that idea.

Now look up at the roof. The double eaves and the steep sloping sides mark this as one of the highest-ranked buildings in the palace. Only the most important halls received this form. Along the ridges, small glazed figures march in a line towards the edges. Their number also signals rank. The more figures you count, the more important the building. It is worth comparing these with the halls you pass later.

On the terrace around you, notice the bronze pieces. There is a tortoise and a crane, both symbols of long life in Chinese tradition. There is a sundial, its stone face angled towards the sky. There is also a vessel shaped as a standard grain measure. Together, the sundial and the measure told visitors something simple. The emperor controlled time, and he controlled fair weights and measures for the whole empire.

Look too for two small gilded pavilions standing on stone bases on either side of the terrace. They are easy to walk past. Each one looks like a tiny temple. Their meaning is grand, though. They stand for the land and the state, the mountains, rivers and altars of the realm. In other words, the whole empire placed in miniature, right outside the emperor's door.

Now step towards the open doors and let your eyes adjust to the dim interior. At the centre sits the throne, raised on a platform and surrounded by screens and incense burners. Above it, high in the shadows, a coiled dragon looks down from the decorated ceiling. And above the throne hangs a horizontal wooden board with four large characters. They read roughly as, be upright and bright, or be just and honourable. The calligraphy is credited to the Shunzhi emperor, the first Qing ruler to reign from this palace.

Here is the detail most visitors miss. That board was not just a motto. In the Qing dynasty, the Yongzheng emperor created a secret system for choosing his heir. He wrote the name of his chosen successor and sealed it inside a box. The box was placed behind this very plaque. A second copy was kept with the emperor himself. When he died, officials opened both and compared them. Only then did the court learn who would rule.

Why such secrecy? The Qing had seen bitter fights between princes over the throne. Yongzheng's own rise to power was clouded by rumours and rivalries. Keeping the name hidden meant no prince could be sure of his position. It reduced plotting at court. So, as you look at those four characters, remember that for many years the future of the empire was, quite literally, waiting in a box behind them.

That same emperor made another change. He moved his daily residence out of this hall to the smaller Hall of Mental Cultivation, a short walk to the west. After that, the Palace of Heavenly Purity became more of a ceremonial space. Emperors held receptions and banquets here. Two famous Qing rulers, the Kangxi and Qianlong emperors, hosted grand feasts for thousands of elderly men, to honour age and long life. When an emperor died, his coffin lay in state in this hall before the funeral.

The Ming years here were often less calm. Several dramatic episodes of court intrigue took place in and around this palace. One emperor of the early seventeenth century died suddenly after taking medicinal pills, and the event became one of the great scandals of the dynasty. Historians still debate exactly what happened. Standing here, it is easy to imagine whispered conversations in the corridors, and messengers hurrying across these same stones at night.

Before you move on, turn around and look south, back towards the Gate of Heavenly Purity. In the Qing period, emperors sometimes held early morning audiences right at that gate, hearing reports from officials in the cool light of dawn. Then look north. The path continues to the Hall of Union and the Palace of Earthly Tranquility, and beyond them to the imperial garden. The palace was designed as one long line, each building opening onto the next.

So take one last look at the throne in the shadows, and at the plaque floating above it. Feel the stone under your feet, cooler near the doorway where the sun cannot reach. As you walk on, notice how quiet the space becomes away from the crowd at the doors. Listen for the wind on the roofs. These walls held emperors, rivals, and one carefully hidden name. Now they hold you, for a few minutes, on your way north.

Continue north to finish your visit in the imperial garden.
