The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the quiet. Tiananmen Square is enormous and open, full of wind, footsteps, and the low murmur of tour groups. Yet as the queue edges toward this building, voices drop. People straighten their jackets. Somewhere nearby, a vendor holds out a bundle of chrysanthemums, the flowers many Chinese families bring to a grave. You are standing in line to see the preserved body of Mao Zedong, the man who founded the People's Republic of China.

Let's start with where you are. The hall stands at the southern half of the square, behind the tall stone Monument to the People's Heroes. Look north and you see Tiananmen Gate, with Mao's portrait hanging above the central arch. Look south and you see the old Qianmen gate towers. The memorial sits on the main north-south line that runs through the heart of the old imperial city. That placement is no accident. For centuries, this central line belonged to emperors.

Here is the first surprise. Before this hall existed, the spot held a gate. Under the Ming and Qing dynasties, a ceremonial gate stood here, renamed several times as the dynasties and then the republic changed. It was taken down in the twentieth century as the square was widened. So the memorial did not rise on empty ground. It replaced a doorway into the old imperial world with a tomb for the leader of the new one.

Mao died in Beijing on 9 September 1976, after a long period of failing health. The decision to preserve his body and build a hall came quickly. Construction moved at remarkable speed, with large numbers of workers and volunteers taking part. The hall opened to the public in 1977, close to the first anniversary of his death. Official accounts describe materials gathered from across the country, so that every region could claim a share in the building.

Now, a second surprise, and an important one. Mao himself did not ask for this. In the 1950s, he was among the senior leaders who signed a proposal that they should be cremated after death, as a modern and practical example to the nation. After he died, the leadership chose differently. They preserved his body for permanent display. Many visitors walk through the hall without knowing that the man inside once put his name to the opposite wish.

Take a moment to look at the building itself. It is a broad, almost square block, raised on a platform with wide steps. Tall square columns run around all four sides, giving it the feel of a classical temple. The roof has two layers, with a band of golden glazed tiles near the top edge. Gold and yellow were once colours reserved for the emperor. Here they frame a modern socialist monument, mixing the old language of power with the new.

On either side of the entrances, look for the large sculpture groups. They show crowds of figures, soldiers, farmers, workers, and students, many leaning forward with flags and raised arms. These scenes tell the official story of the revolution and the building of the state. They are carved in a heroic, muscular style that was common in Chinese public art of that era. Even if you cannot read the scenes in detail, notice the direction of movement. Everyone surges forward.

When you reach the doors, the pace changes. Inside the northern hall, a large white marble statue of Mao sits in an armchair, calm and relaxed, with a wide landscape scene behind him. This is where many visitors lay their flowers. Staff collect the chrysanthemums regularly, and locals say the same flowers often find their way back to the sellers outside. Treat that as a popular story rather than confirmed fact, but it is one you will hear often in the queue.

Then comes the viewing hall, and it is brief. The line moves steadily, and guards keep it flowing. Mao lies in a crystal coffin, covered by the red flag of the Communist Party, his face lit softly. You will pass in perhaps a minute or less. There is no stopping, no talking, and no photography. The silence here is strict, and for many Chinese visitors, especially older ones, this is a deeply emotional moment.

It helps to understand why feelings run so mixed. Mao led the revolution that unified the country in 1949 and ended decades of war and division. He is also tied to great suffering, including the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward and the turmoil of the Cultural Revolution. In 1981, the Communist Party issued an official resolution on his legacy. It praised his early achievements but named serious mistakes in his later years. That divided verdict still shapes how people speak about him today.

One more detail that many visitors miss. The hall is not only about Mao. In the early 1980s, memorial rooms were added on the upper level for other leaders of the revolutionary generation, including Zhou Enlai, Zhu De, and Liu Shaoqi, and later Deng Xiaoping. These rooms hold photographs and personal items, not bodies. They turn the building into a wider memorial to the first leaders of the People's Republic, though Mao remains its centre.

As you leave through the southern side, the mood shifts again. You often step out into a row of souvenir stands selling Mao badges, small busts, and red books. The contrast is sharp. One moment, a hushed tomb. The next, keychains and postcards. This, too, is part of the experience. It shows how Mao exists in China today, as a revered founder, a figure of debate, and a familiar face on everyday objects.

For many families from the provinces, this visit is a once-in-a-lifetime trip to the capital. Watch the people around you. Some bow. Some hold hands with grandparents. Some simply look curious, like you. Students, retirees, and soldiers on leave stand together in the same quiet line. Whatever you think of the man inside, the hall shows what he still means to millions of people in this country.

So pause for a moment before you move on. Turn and look back at the columns and the golden roof edge. Above you, the sky over the square is wide and open. Ahead of you, the traffic of Qianmen carries on as usual. Behind you lies one of the most visited tombs on earth, and also one of the shortest visits you will ever make. Notice how the noise of the city returns, step by step, as you walk away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a reservation in advance and carry your passport.
Leave bags and cameras behind before joining the queue.
Arrive early, as the line can grow very long.
Dress modestly and keep silent inside the halls.
Buy chrysanthemums outside if you wish to lay flowers.
