The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, white water is falling over stone, a steady roar that never quite stops. The air smells of pine resin, wet moss, and cold river spray. Underfoot, the path is a mix of sand, roots, and springy needles that give a little with each step. This is Oulanka National Park, in the far northeast of Finland, close to the Russian border and just south of the Arctic Circle.

Oulanka was established as a national park in 1956, and it has grown since then. It lies mostly within the municipalities of Kuusamo and Salla. On a map it looks like a long, winding corridor, and that shape is no accident. The park follows its rivers. The Oulanka River and its tributary, the Kitka, cut deep valleys through this landscape. Almost everything you see here, from the cliffs to the sandbanks to the plants, is shaped by moving water.

Here is the first thing that surprises many visitors. These rivers do not flow toward the Gulf of Bothnia, as most Finnish rivers do. They flow east, across the border into Russia, and eventually toward the White Sea. Just across that border lies Paanajärvi National Park in Russian Karelia. Together, the two parks protect one continuous wilderness, and the border line on a map means nothing to the bears and wolves who cross it.

If you are standing near the Oulanka Visitor Centre, you are close to Kiutaköngäs, the best-known rapids in the park. Here the river narrows and drops through a channel of rock. Look at the cliffs along the bank. Some of them are a warm reddish colour, unusual in Finland, where most bedrock is grey granite. This is dolomite and related rock, softer and richer in minerals, and it matters more than you might think.

That mineral-rich bedrock is the reason Oulanka is so special for plants. Limestone and dolomite make the soil less acidic, and certain rare species thrive here. The most famous is the calypso orchid, also called the fairy slipper — a small pink flower that blooms in early summer in shady spruce forest. Botanists come from far away to see it. Please stay on the paths, because these plants are fragile, and many grow right beside the trail.

The second surprise is about where these plants come from. Oulanka sits at a meeting point of different natural worlds. Some species here belong to the forests of Siberia and the east. Others belong to the mountains, or to the cold north, or to the south. This mix happens because the region sits on old migration routes for plants after the last Ice Age. In a single valley you can find species that normally live thousands of kilometres apart.

Now think about the people who walk here. Oulanka is home to much of the Karhunkierros, which means the Bear's Ring. It is one of the most popular long-distance hiking trails in Finland. The full route runs roughly eighty kilometres, from Hautajärvi in the north down to the Ruka area. Most hikers take several days, sleeping in open wilderness huts and lean-to shelters along the way. If you meet people with heavy packs and tired smiles, they are probably on that journey.

For those with less time, there is the Little Bear's Ring, a loop of around twelve kilometres that starts near Juuma. It is short enough for one day but still gives you the full character of the park. You cross swaying suspension bridges over fast water. You climb wooden steps up and down the valley sides. And you reach two of the park's most memorable places, Myllykoski and Jyrävä.

At Myllykoski, the name means mill rapids, you will find an old wooden mill standing beside the water. Grain was once ground here, using the power of the river. Today the mill is a quiet reminder that this wilderness was never completely empty. People lived along these rivers, fished them, and used the timber of the forests. Further along, Jyrävä is a powerful waterfall on the Kitka River, where the water drops steeply into a narrow gorge full of spray.

Look at the riverbanks as the valley widens. In some places, especially along the lower Oulanka, you will see soft sandbanks and sandy cliffs. The river constantly moves this sand, cutting away one bank and building up another. This means the shape of the river changes slowly from year to year. The meadows on the flat river terraces were once mown by local farmers for hay, and some are still managed today to keep them open.

Animals are part of this place too, even if you rarely see them. Brown bears, wolves, wolverines, and lynx live in these forests, though they usually avoid people. You are much more likely to meet reindeer. They are not wild; they belong to local herders, who let them roam freely across the land. In the rivers, salmon, trout, and grayling move through the cold, clear water, and birds like the Siberian jay often come close to hikers at the shelters.

The park also has a scientific side. The Oulanka Research Station, run by the University of Oulu, studies this landscape. Researchers look at rivers, forests, climate, and the way northern nature responds to change. It is a reminder that Oulanka is not only a place to enjoy, but also a place where scientists learn how northern ecosystems work. The changing seasons here are dramatic, from the midnight light of June to the deep snow and short days of winter.

Each season changes the experience. In summer, the paths are green and the evenings stay bright for a very long time. In early autumn, the Finns call it ruska, the birch leaves turn yellow and the ground plants turn deep red and orange. In winter, the rivers freeze in places, snow covers the trails, and the silence becomes almost complete, broken only by moving water that never fully freezes in the strongest rapids.

Before you move on, stop for a moment. Listen again to the water. Notice the colour of the rock beside you, the smell of the pines, the way the light comes through the trees. This river has been cutting this valley since the ice melted, long before any trail or bridge was built. As you walk away, take a last look at the current — it is carrying this water east, toward a sea far beyond the border.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the Little Bear's Ring from Juuma for a day trip.
Wear sturdy boots; wooden steps get slippery when wet.
Visit in early autumn to see the ruska leaf colours.
Bring insect repellent for mosquitoes in midsummer.
Book wilderness hut space ahead on busy summer weekends.
