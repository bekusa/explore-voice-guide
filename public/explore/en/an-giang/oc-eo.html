The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. There is wind moving through the rice, the dry rattle of sugar palm leaves, perhaps a motorbike somewhere along a canal road. The ground under your feet is flat, soft and green in every direction. Only one shape breaks the horizon, the low, wooded rise of Ba Thê mountain. It is a quiet, ordinary farming landscape. Yet under these fields lie the traces of one of the great trading cities of early Southeast Asia.

This is Óc Eo, in the An Giang province of Vietnam's Mekong Delta. Today the name refers to a village, an archaeological site and a whole ancient culture. Roughly two thousand years ago, this land belonged to a kingdom that Chinese writers called Funan. Historians generally place its height between the first and the sixth centuries of the common era. Funan was not a small chiefdom. It was a maritime power that connected the Indian Ocean with the South China Sea.

Here is the first surprise. Almost everything we know about Funan by name comes from China, not from Funan itself. In the third century, envoys from a southern Chinese kingdom travelled here and wrote down what they saw. Their accounts describe walled settlements, a king, a system of taxes paid in gold, silver and pearls, and people who could read and write. The original texts are mostly lost. Fragments survive because later Chinese histories copied them.

Those same records also carry a founding story, and it should be heard as legend, not history. According to the tale, a foreign man named Kaundinya arrived by sea, perhaps from India. A local queen, often called Liu Ye, met him. The two married, and their union began the royal line of Funan. Many scholars read the story as a symbol. It describes, in the language of myth, the meeting of local Mekong peoples with Indian traders and ideas.

For a long time, Funan existed only on paper. That changed in the twentieth century. French researchers studying aerial photographs of the delta noticed straight lines in the fields, the outlines of old canals and a large rectangular enclosure. In 1944, the French archaeologist Louis Malleret began excavations here. What his team found changed the understanding of the whole region's past.

They found the remains of a city built for trade. There were brick foundations, wooden post holes from houses raised above flood water, and workshops for making jewellery. They found thousands of beads in glass and semi-precious stone, gold ornaments, seals and rings. Canals once linked this city with the sea and with other settlements inland. Boats from far away could reach the delta, and goods from the delta could travel out again.

Now the second surprise, and it is a remarkable one. Among the objects at Óc Eo were Roman coins. One was a gold medallion bearing the image of the emperor Antoninus Pius, who ruled in the second century. Archaeologists also found Indian-style seals with Sanskrit inscriptions and bronze mirrors from China. Picture that for a moment. In these rice fields, objects from Rome, India and China lay together in the same mud.

The Roman pieces do not mean that Romans walked here. More likely, the coins passed from hand to hand along sea routes through India and the Gulf of Thailand. But they show how wide the trading world of Funan really was. Merchants waiting for the monsoon winds to change direction would stop in ports like this one. While they waited, they bought, sold, repaired their ships and shared their gods.

Those gods are still visible here. Look toward the slopes of Ba Thê mountain. On the hillside stand the brick foundations of ancient temples, now protected under modern roofs. Excavators found religious objects connected with Hindu worship, including stone lingas, symbols of the god Shiva, and statues of Vishnu. Buddhist images were found in the region too. Religion arrived along the same routes as beads and gold.

Near these ruins stands Linh Sơn pagoda, a working Buddhist temple. This is part of the texture of the place. Local people come to light incense, to pray and to leave offerings. Some ancient statues from the area are treated as living sacred objects, not only as museum pieces. You may see flowers, fruit and the curling smoke of joss sticks beside stones that are many centuries old. Please move quietly and dress modestly here.

Why did this city fade? No one can give a single answer with certainty. Shipping routes changed, and vessels began to sail more directly between India and China. Power in the region moved inland, toward the kingdoms that later became the Khmer empire, famous for the temples of Angkor. The delta is also a living landscape. Rivers move, silt builds up, and coastlines shift over time. Slowly, the port lost its place at the centre of the trading world.

Today the site is protected as one of Vietnam's special national relics. Excavation continues, and new finds still appear in the surrounding fields. A small exhibition space near the site displays copies and original objects found in the area. Many of the most important finds now sit in museums in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi. What remains here is the setting itself, the mountain, the low ground and the long lines of the canals.

Before you leave, look once more across the fields. Imagine boats coming up these waterways with cloth, spices and glass. Imagine the noise of a market where people spoke many languages. Then return to what is really in front of you, the green rice, the farmers on their bicycles, the calm hill above it all. As you walk back toward the road, notice how ordinary it all looks. That ordinary ground held a lost kingdom for more than a thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning to avoid the delta's midday heat.
Bring sun protection and water for walking between sites.
Wear modest clothing when visiting Linh Sơn pagoda.
Expect muddy paths during the rainy season.
Combine the trip with Ba Thê mountain's hillside ruins.
