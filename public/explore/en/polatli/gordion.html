The guide

The story

Wind moves across open steppe here. It carries dry grass, dust, and sometimes the smell of the Sakarya River, which runs slowly through the plain below. The land looks almost empty at first. Then you notice the hills. Some are too round, too smooth, too regular to be natural. You are standing among the burial mounds of Gordion, once the capital of the Phrygians, a kingdom that ruled much of central Anatolia almost three thousand years ago.

The Phrygians are not as well known as the Hittites before them or the Persians after them. They spoke an Indo-European language and wrote in an alphabet related to early Greek. Their kings ruled from this river valley during the Iron Age. One of those kings gave the world a story every child still knows. His name was Midas, and his capital stood on the low mound you can see across the fields.

Start with the largest hill, the one that fills the sky near the museum. Archaeologists call it Tumulus MM, short for Midas Mound. It rises roughly fifty metres above the plain. It is made of earth, clay, and rubble, piled by hand over a wooden tomb. For centuries, people walked past it without knowing what lay inside. Its sheer size was the only clue that someone very important was buried here.

In the 1950s, a team from the University of Pennsylvania Museum, led by the archaeologist Rodney Young, decided to find out. They drilled into the mound to locate the chamber, then dug a long tunnel through its side. In 1957 they broke through. Inside, they found a room built of massive timbers, sealed for thousands of years. The tunnel you can walk through today follows the route those excavators opened.

Here is the first surprise. The tomb is built of wood, not stone. Thick beams of pine and juniper form a chamber that is still standing. Because the timber survived, scientists could count and compare its tree rings. That method, called dendrochronology, dates the felling of the logs to around 740 BC. This makes the chamber one of the oldest surviving wooden buildings in the world. Look closely at the beams and you can still see the marks of ancient tools.

Inside lay the skeleton of a man in his sixties. Around him were wooden furniture with fine inlay, bronze bowls, belt fittings, and decorative pins. There was no gold. That quiet detail matters, because legend says Midas turned everything he touched to gold. Many scholars now think the man in the tomb was not Midas himself. A popular theory suggests he was Midas's father, possibly a king named Gordias, buried by his son.

The second surprise came much later, from the bottom of the bronze vessels. Researchers analysed residue left from the funeral feast. They found traces of a spiced stew of lamb or goat with lentils. The drink was even more unusual. It was a mixture of grape wine, barley beer, and honey mead, all in one cup. The mourners at this king's funeral ate and drank together, and science has recovered their menu.

Now turn toward the citadel mound, the long, flat hill near the village of Yassıhüyük. This was the heart of the city. Under its surface lie gates, courtyards, and halls called megarons. A megaron is a large rectangular hall with a porch and a central hearth, a building type found across the ancient Aegean and Anatolia. Kings received guests here, held feasts, and stored treasure. Workers ground grain and wove cloth in long service buildings nearby.

At some point around 800 BC, a great fire swept through the citadel. It was a disaster for the people living here, but a gift for archaeologists. The burning collapsed roofs and walls and sealed everything underneath. Pots, loom weights, ivories, and carbonised grain stayed where they fell. Archaeologists call this layer the Destruction Level. It gives an unusually clear picture of daily life in a Phrygian palace at a single moment.

Do not leave without visiting the small museum near the great mound. One of its quiet treasures is a floor of pebble mosaic from one of the megarons. Small river stones in red, white, and dark blue form geometric patterns. It is often described as among the oldest decorative pebble mosaics known. The pattern looks almost modern. Someone chose each stone by colour, then set it by hand to cover a royal floor.

Gordion is also tied to another famous legend. Ancient writers say an old wagon stood in the city, its yoke tied with a knot no one could undo. A prophecy promised that whoever loosened it would rule Asia. According to the story, Alexander the Great came here in 333 BC. Some versions say he cut the knot with his sword. Others say he simply pulled out the pin. Either way, the phrase Gordian knot still means a problem solved by bold action.

The city outlived the Phrygian kings. Lydians, Persians, Greeks, Galatians, and later Romans all left traces here. The Persians built a huge siege ramp against the old city walls, and parts of it can still be traced in the excavations. Over time the capital shrank into a village. In 2023, UNESCO added Gordion to the World Heritage List, recognising it as one of the most important Iron Age sites in the Near East.

Before you go, stand still for a moment and look across the plain. Count the mounds you can see. More than a hundred tumuli are scattered across this landscape, each one marking a burial. Listen to the wind and the distant tractors from the village fields. The same river still runs past the citadel. As you walk back toward the road, notice how the great mound stays on the horizon long after the museum disappears behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the museum before walking into the Midas Mound tunnel.
Bring a hat and water; the plain offers little shade.
Come early morning when the steppe light is soft.
Allow extra time to drive to the citadel mound nearby.
Wear sturdy shoes for dusty, uneven excavation paths.
