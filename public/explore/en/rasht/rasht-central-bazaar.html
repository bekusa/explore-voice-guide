The guide

The story

The smell reaches you first. Fresh fish on ice. Garlic and dried herbs in open baskets. Rain on warm stone. You are standing at the edge of the Grand Bazaar of Rasht, in the north of Iran. This is the largest market in the Gilan region. And much of it has no roof at all. Few markets in the country are quite like it.

Above you is open sky. This is on purpose. Rasht sits near the Caspian Sea, in a green and rainy part of the country. People here call it the Rain City. The market was built open so that sunlight could reach the lanes below. In a place of long gray days, that light matters to the people who work here every morning.

The bazaar spreads across about twenty-four hectares. That makes it the largest roofless market in the country. It is not one single hall. It is many smaller markets joined together over time. There is a Grand Bazaar and a Small Bazaar. There are four-sided crossings called Chaharsooq, where lanes meet and goods change hands. Each part has its own goods and its own crowd.

Let yourself get a little lost. The lanes do not run in straight lines. They bend and split and open into small squares. The stone underfoot is often wet and shining. Doorways lead into covered rooms, then back out into the rain. Part of the pleasure here is simply wandering and following whatever catches your eye.

Around you, the old brick buildings are caravanserais. These were resting places for traveling traders. Here there are fourteen of them. They were built across two long periods of Iranian history, the Qajar era and the Pahlavi era. The Qajar era ran from 1785 to 1925. The Pahlavi era followed, from 1925 to 1979.

Look up at the brick archways. The curves are simple and strong. Traders once parked their animals and stored their goods behind these walls. Today the rooms hold shops and small workshops. The brick has darkened with age and rain. But the shapes remain clear, and the work of the old builders is easy to see.

Now follow your nose to the food. Rasht is known across Iran for its cooking. The whole city carries the name of a Creative City of Gastronomy, given by UNESCO. That title is not about one famous dish. It is about a whole way of eating, built on the rice fields, the sea, and the gardens of Gilan.

In the market you will see the building blocks of that food. Garlic in heavy braids. Bundles of fresh green herbs. Olives in wide bowls. Sour fruits and pastes used in local stews. Sacks of rice grown in the flat fields outside the city. Smoked fish, salted and hung in rows.

The food of Gilan tends to be sour and full of garlic. It is different from the cooking in the dry center of Iran. Cooks here smoke eggplant, simmer beans with fresh dill, and press the juice from sour fruits into their stews. Walking past the stalls, you can guess the recipes just from the piles of raw ingredients.

The fish comes from the Caspian Sea, just to the north. Each morning it arrives fresh. Vendors lay it out on crushed ice. They call out their prices. Local families come early to choose the best of the catch. The smell of the sea is strongest right here. If you arrive in the morning, this is the market at its most alive.

Listen for a moment. You will hear bargaining, carts rolling over stone, and rain when it falls. The sound bounces off brick and open air at the same time. It is a market that has kept its old rhythm, even while the city around it has grown modern and loud. This is the everyday sound of Rasht at work.

If you want to eat now, you can. Small stalls and nearby shops sell Gilani food. Try whatever looks busy and freshly made. Carry some cash, because many small sellers do not take cards. And bring a light raincoat, because the weather here can change within minutes.

Take your time before you leave. Notice how the open sky changes the feeling of the place. Most old markets are dim and closed in. This one breathes. Light and rain both fall straight into it. The market has fed this city for a very long time. Standing here, in an ordinary morning that has repeated for generations, you are part of it too.
