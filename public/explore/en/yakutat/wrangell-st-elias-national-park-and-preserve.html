The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, near Yakutat, the loudest sound is often the wind coming off the ice. Then, without warning, comes a deep crack like distant thunder. That is a glacier moving. The air smells of salt, wet spruce, and cold stone. On a clear day, a wall of white peaks rises straight out of the sea. Behind them sits Wrangell–Saint Elias National Park and Preserve, the largest national park in the United States.

The size is hard to picture, so here is one way. The park and preserve together cover roughly 13 million acres. That is about six times the size of Yellowstone. It is larger than several entire countries. Yet it has very few roads, and none of them reach this coast. From Yakutat, the park is a place you enter by small plane, by boat, or simply by looking up at the horizon.

That horizon holds a surprising record. Nine of the sixteen highest peaks in the United States stand inside this park. Four great mountain ranges meet here: the Wrangell, the Saint Elias, the Chugach, and the eastern end of the Alaska Range. Few places on Earth pack so much vertical rock into one landscape. Mountains here rise from sea level to more than 5,000 metres in a short distance.

The giant of this coast is Mount Saint Elias. It rises to about 5,489 metres, making it the second-highest peak in the United States. Only Denali is taller. What makes Saint Elias special is the drop. Its summit stands only a short distance from the ocean. Few mountains anywhere show such a steep climb from the waterline to the top. When clouds lift, the whole mountain appears at once.

The mountain also carries an early chapter of written history. In 1741, the explorer Vitus Bering sailed this coast on a Russian expedition. The crew sighted the great peak, and the name of Saint Elias became attached to this region. But people knew these shores long before any European ship arrived. The Tlingit people have lived around Yakutat Bay for generations, fishing, hunting seals, and travelling these waters by canoe.

For the Tlingit, this land was never empty wilderness. It was home, with named places, family territories, and stories tied to rivers and ice. Today, Yakutat remains a Tlingit community. As you walk through town, you may notice fishing boats, smokehouses, and gear stacked by the harbour. Subsistence fishing and hunting still matter here. The park is not separate from local life. It is part of the same landscape.

Now turn your attention to the ice. West of Yakutat lies the Malaspina Glacier. It is often called the largest piedmont glacier in North America. A piedmont glacier is one that flows out of the mountains and spreads across flat land, like batter poured onto a pan. From the air, the Malaspina looks like a vast lobe of ice. Its surface carries dark stripes of rock, folded into strange curving patterns.

Those patterns are one of the park's great surprises. The stripes are moraines, which are long bands of rock and gravel carried by the ice. Different glaciers feed into the Malaspina, and each one surges at its own pace. The bands bend and twist into loops and zigzags. Seen from a plane, the glacier looks almost painted. Pilots who fly from Yakutat know these shapes well.

To the east, in Disenchantment Bay, the Hubbard Glacier reaches the sea. Its upper reaches flow out of the park's high mountains. Its face is a tall wall of blue and white ice, several kilometres wide. Large blocks break off and crash into the water. Locals call this calving. Unlike many glaciers in Alaska, the Hubbard has been advancing rather than retreating in recent times.

That advance has caused real drama. In 1986, and again in 2002, the Hubbard pushed forward and blocked the mouth of Russell Fjord. For a time, the fjord became a sealed lake. Water levels rose behind the ice dam. Each time, the dam later broke, and the water rushed out into the bay. Scientists watch this glacier closely, because another closure is possible in the future.

The park was first protected as a national monument in 1978. Two years later, in 1980, Congress made it a national park and preserve under a major Alaska lands law. The word preserve matters here. In preserve areas, sport hunting is allowed, and local residents keep traditional uses of the land. The park is also part of a larger World Heritage Site shared with neighbouring parks in Alaska and Canada.

Far from Yakutat, on the park's northern side, lies another story: Kennecott. In the early twentieth century, miners dug rich copper ore from these mountains. A company town grew there, with red wooden mill buildings climbing the hillside. The mines closed in 1938, and the town emptied. Today the old buildings still stand, a long journey from this coast, but part of the same protected land.

The first recorded climb of Mount Saint Elias came in 1897. An Italian expedition led by the Duke of the Abruzzi reached the summit after a long march over the ice. Even now, the peak is rarely climbed. Weather here changes fast. Storms from the Gulf of Alaska bring heavy snow and thick fog. Many visitors wait days for a clear view, and some never get one.

So take a moment now. Look toward the mountains, even if clouds hide them. Notice the cold edge in the wind and the grey-green colour of the water. Listen for the sound of ice, or a raven calling over the trees. Most of this park will stay out of reach. That is part of its meaning. As you walk away, carry the scale of it with you, quietly.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a flightseeing trip from Yakutat for glacier views.
Pack waterproof layers; coastal weather shifts within hours.
Build spare days into plans for fog and flight delays.
Bring binoculars to watch Hubbard Glacier calve from a boat.
Check with park rangers before heading into backcountry areas.
