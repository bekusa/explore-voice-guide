The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the limestone plateau, the wind moves through olive and pistachio trees, and somewhere beyond the walls a bell sounds. The stone under your feet is pale and warm. It is the same honey-coloured limestone that built almost every old village in this region. Before any history, notice the quiet. For more than sixteen centuries, people have chosen this ridge above the plains of Mesopotamia as a place to pray.

You are standing at Mor Gabriel, a short drive southeast of Midyat. Locals also call it Deyrulumur, from a Syriac phrase for the monastery of Umar. Tradition dates its founding to the year 397. It is widely described as the oldest Syriac Orthodox monastery still in use anywhere in the world. That means this is not a ruin or a museum. It is a working home for monks, nuns, and young students.

The founding story centres on two holy men. According to the monastery's own tradition, a monk named Mor Shmuel and his disciple Mor Shem'un settled here at the end of the fourth century. The word Mor is a Syriac title, roughly equal to Saint. Later, the community took the name of Gabriel, a seventh-century bishop of the region. He was remembered for strict discipline and a long life of prayer, and his name stayed with the monastery ever since.

The region around you has a name of its own — Tur Abdin. In Syriac, this means the mountain of the servants of God. For centuries, these hills held dozens of monasteries and churches, and many villages spoke Aramaic dialects. Syriac is a form of Aramaic, the language family spoken in this part of the Near East in the time of Jesus. Some families here still speak a living version of it, called Turoyo.

Now look closely at the main church. Its walls are thick, its windows small, and its lines are simple. Historians connect the building of this church to the early sixth century, with support from the Byzantine emperor Anastasius. That detail surprises many visitors. A Roman emperor, ruling from Constantinople far to the west, sent money to a monastery on the edge of his empire, close to the Persian frontier.

Inside the sanctuary, if it is open to you, raise your eyes to the vault above the altar. Here you may see one of the treasures of the place: early mosaics, often dated to the sixth century. Small cubes of coloured and gold glass form vine scrolls, vessels, and architectural shapes. Very few mosaics from this period survive in this part of the world. The light catches them gently, so give your eyes a moment to adjust.

There is a second surprise in the complex — a domed building often linked to the empress Theodora, wife of Justinian. Locals call it the Dome of Theodora. This connection belongs to tradition rather than firm record, so take it as a story the monastery treasures. Still, the structure is old and finely built. Its dome rises over a square base, a technique that early builders here handled with real skill.

As you walk, you will pass courtyards, stairways, and quiet passages. One chamber holds the tombs of saints and monks, and many visitors lower their voices as they enter. Over the centuries, the community suffered raids, plague, war, and long periods of decline. At times only a handful of monks remained. Yet the monastery never fully closed. Each generation repaired the walls, replanted the gardens, and continued the daily prayers.

Those prayers are still sung in Syriac. If you arrive during a service, you may hear the monks chant in a language that has carried Christian worship here since late antiquity. The melodies are slow and steady, without instruments. Stand at the back, keep still, and simply listen. For many travellers, this sound stays with them longer than any building or view.

Mor Gabriel is also the seat of the Metropolitan of Tur Abdin. A metropolitan is a senior bishop who leads the church in a region. The monastery runs a school where young people from Syriac Christian families study their language, liturgy, and faith. For a community that has seen many members emigrate to Europe and beyond, this teaching matters a great deal. It keeps an old tradition alive on its own soil.

In recent years, the monastery has also faced modern challenges. From the late 2000s, it was involved in long legal disputes over the ownership of its surrounding land. The cases drew attention from Syriac communities abroad and from international observers. Some land was later returned. For the people who live here, the walls and fields are not only property — they are the ground where their ancestors worked and prayed.

As a visitor, you are a guest in a living religious home. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Some parts of the complex are private, set aside for the monks and students, so follow the signs and the guidance of the staff. Photography may be limited in certain spaces, especially during prayer. A respectful question to the person at the gate usually opens more doors than a camera does.

Before you leave, step outside the walls and look out across the plateau. The fields, the scattered villages, the low stone houses — this is the landscape the founders saw. Notice the light on the limestone, how it turns from white to gold as the day moves on. Listen once more for the wind in the trees, or a bell from the courtyard. Then carry that quiet with you as you head back toward Midyat.
