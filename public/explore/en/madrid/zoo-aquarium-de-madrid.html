The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the city hum there is a different layer of sound. A peacock calls somewhere behind the hedges. Gravel crunches under strollers. Far off, a sea lion barks, short and rough. The air smells of pine resin and damp earth, because this zoo sits inside Casa de Campo, the largest public park in Madrid. You are only a few kilometres from the Royal Palace, yet the skyline has disappeared behind the trees.

That location matters. For centuries, Casa de Campo was a royal hunting estate, a private forest for Spanish kings. It opened to the public in the twentieth century, and today it belongs to everyone in the city. The zoo opened here in 1972. So when you walk these paths, you are walking on old royal ground. The holm oaks and stone pines around you are older than any enclosure.

Before this place existed, Madrid kept its animals in a much smaller spot. There was an old menagerie in Retiro Park, in the centre of the city, known locally as the Casa de Fieras, or House of Beasts. It was cramped, and it belonged to an older idea of a zoo, where cages simply put animals on show. The new zoo in Casa de Campo gave the collection far more space, and a chance to start again.

Now, the story that most visitors come for. In 1978, two giant pandas arrived in Madrid as a gift from China. At that time, pandas outside China were extremely rare. Then, in 1982, the pair had a cub named Chu-Lin. He was born through artificial insemination, and he became the first giant panda born in captivity in Europe. Madrid celebrated him as a small national star.

That panda connection has continued for decades. Several pandas have lived here since then, and cubs born in Madrid have later travelled to China, as the agreements require. A new pair arrived more recently, so the black-and-white bears are still part of daily life here. If you visit their area, watch how slowly they move. Pandas eat mostly bamboo, which gives them very little energy. Their calm is not laziness... it is careful saving of fuel.

Here is something many visitors miss. A giant panda has a kind of extra thumb. It is not a true finger, but an enlarged wrist bone that works like one. With it, the panda can grip a bamboo stem and strip the leaves with surprising skill. Watch the front paws during feeding. You may see the bear hold a stalk almost the way you would hold a pencil.

The zoo is laid out roughly by regions of the world, so a walk here becomes a slow journey across continents. One path brings you toward African grasslands, with giraffes lifting their heads above the fences. Another leads to Asian species, to big cats, to primates swinging on ropes. The paths are wide and mostly flat. Take your time. The pleasure here comes from stopping, not from rushing between the famous names.

As the name tells you, this is a zoo and an aquarium together. Step inside the aquarium building and the world changes at once. The light turns blue and dim. The temperature drops. Voices go quieter, almost without thinking. Fish move past in slow silver groups, and sharks glide close to the glass. Children press their hands flat against it. Adults often do too, when they think no one is looking.

There is also a dolphinarium, where bottlenose dolphins live, and an area for sea lions. Those barks you heard at the start probably came from there. Sea lions are loud, social animals, and they seem to enjoy an audience. Watch how they use their front flippers to lift their bodies out of the water. Unlike true seals, they can turn their back flippers forward and walk on land.

Look up as well as around. Birds are everywhere here, both inside the enclosures and outside them. Wild pigeons, sparrows and magpies from Casa de Campo come in to share the scraps. Some zoo birds of prey take part in flying demonstrations, when they are scheduled. Even without a show, the trees are full of movement. This zoo does not end neatly at its fence. The park around it keeps flowing in.

Like many modern zoos, this one now presents itself as more than a place for display. It takes part in breeding programmes for threatened species, and it talks about education and conservation. Those ideas would have seemed strange in the old House of Beasts in Retiro. Visitors today may still have mixed feelings about animals in captivity. That question is worth carrying with you as you walk. Good zoos invite it.

Getting here is part of the experience. Many people arrive by metro or bus, but there is also a cable car, the Teleférico, which runs from the edge of central Madrid across the treetops into Casa de Campo. From the cabin you see the Royal Palace, the cathedral and the long green carpet of the park. It shows you very clearly how close this wild-feeling place is to the heart of the capital.

Before you leave, pause on one of the shaded paths. Notice the dry, warm smell of the pines, and the red-brown Madrid earth at the edges of the gravel. Listen again for the peacock, the children, the sea lions. Somewhere nearby, a panda is probably eating bamboo with that clever wrist bone. As you walk back toward the city, the trees will close behind you... and the zoo will become a sound in the distance again.

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel paths.
Check the daily schedule for animal demonstrations.
Visit the aquarium during the hottest part of the day.
