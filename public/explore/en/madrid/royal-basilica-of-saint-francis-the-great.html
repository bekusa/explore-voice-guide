The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the steps for a moment and look up. A pale stone front rises above you, and behind it a great dome sits over the rooftops of old Madrid. The street is quieter here than in the city centre. Traffic hums along the wide avenue, swifts cut across the sky in summer, and the stone stays cool even on a hot afternoon. This is the Royal Basilica of Saint Francis the Great, and its size is the first thing almost everyone notices.

The story of this place begins with a legend. Locals say that Saint Francis of Assisi himself passed through Madrid in the early thirteenth century, on a pilgrimage to Santiago, and founded a small community on this hill. Historians treat that tale with caution. What is certain is that a Franciscan convent stood here for centuries, outside the old walls, in a modest form. The church you see today replaced it much later, as a far grander statement.

That new building belongs to the eighteenth century, the age of the Bourbon kings. They wanted Madrid to look like a true European capital, with broad avenues, academies, and buildings in the clean, calm style called Neoclassical. That style looks back to ancient Greece and Rome — columns, balance, and plain geometry instead of heavy ornament. The basilica was one of the largest religious projects of that ambition, and it took decades to finish.

Several architects shaped it, and the project did not run smoothly. A Franciscan friar, Francisco Cabezas, drew the first plans around a vast circular space. Building that dome proved difficult, and doubts about its stability slowed the work. Other architects followed. In the end the Italian-born Francesco Sabatini, a favourite architect of King Charles the Third, brought the building to completion in the 1780s. The front you face carries his sober, ordered hand.

Now think about that dome. Its diameter is often given at around thirty-three metres, which places it among the largest domes in Christian Europe. It is commonly ranked just behind Saint Peter's in Rome, the Pantheon, and Florence Cathedral. Many visitors walk past without realising this. From the outside it looks solid and heavy. From the inside, the effect is very different, as you will see.

When you step through the doors, the space opens in a single circle. Six chapels ring the central floor, and a deep chancel sits opposite the entrance. The eye goes straight up to the dome, which floats above you, painted and gilded. Sound behaves strangely here. A cough or a footstep travels around the walls and returns a moment later. Stand in the centre, look up, and let your eyes adjust to the gold.

Here is a surprise. Much of the rich decoration around you is not from the eighteenth century at all. In the late nineteenth century, under King Alfonso the Twelfth, the interior was transformed. A team of Spanish painters and craftsmen covered the walls and dome with murals, marble, and gilding. So the building has two personalities. The outside is calm and Neoclassical. The inside is a lavish nineteenth-century vision of royal and religious splendour.

That makeover had a purpose. For a time in the nineteenth century, the government chose this church to serve as a national pantheon, a resting place for great Spaniards. The plan did not last, and the remains moved elsewhere. But the ambition of that moment still fills the interior. The murals of saints and the heavy gold frames come from a country trying to tell a proud story about itself.

The most precious treasure is easy to miss. In one of the side chapels hangs a painting by Francisco de Goya, made early in his career. It shows Saint Bernardino of Siena preaching to a crowd. Look closely at the figures on the right. Tradition holds that one of the young men, turning to look out of the scene, is Goya himself. Art historians generally accept this as a self-portrait, a quiet signature from a young painter who would become one of Spain's greatest.

The basilica also holds works by Francisco de Zurbarán, the seventeenth-century master of calm, sculptural saints in soft light. And in the chancel and the old rooms beyond, look for the carved wooden choir stalls. They came from the Carthusian monastery of El Paular, in the mountains north of Madrid, and date from the sixteenth century. Their dark, detailed wood feels very different from the bright gold around them.

The church has a long Franciscan connection, and it still carries a link to the Holy Land. It is cared for under a royal foundation tied to the Franciscan custody of Christian sites in Jerusalem. That is why it holds the title Royal. For Madrid families, though, it is simply San Francisco el Grande, a parish church where people come for weddings, feast days, and a quiet prayer.

The building has not always looked this good. Years of wear, damp, and city pollution dimmed the paintings and stone. A long restoration stretched across the late twentieth century and finished around the turn of the new millennium. The colours you see now are much brighter than those older generations of Madrid residents remember. Some locals still speak of the scaffolding that stood here for years.

When you leave, walk a few steps toward the gardens of Las Vistillas nearby. From there the ground drops toward the Manzanares River, and on a clear evening the sky turns orange over the green Casa de Campo park. Turn back and look at the dome once more. Now you know what waits inside it — the gold, the echo, and a young Goya, still looking out at you from his crowd.
