The guide

The story

Listen first. Around you, traffic circles without pause. Buses, taxis, scooters, all looping around one island of white stone in the middle of the square. Above the noise, if you wait for a gap, you can hear water falling into a basin. That sound has carried across this crossroads for well over two centuries. In the centre sits a goddess on a chariot, pulled by two lions, calm as anything while Madrid rushes past her.

This is the Fountain of Cybele, and the square around her takes her name: the Plaza de Cibeles. Madrileños often just say "Cibeles", and everyone knows what they mean. The square sits where two of the city's great avenues meet, the Paseo del Prado and the Calle de Alcalá. For many locals, this is the true heart of Madrid. It is the place they meet, protest, and celebrate.

So who is she? Cybele was a mother goddess from ancient Anatolia, in what is now Turkey. The Greeks and later the Romans adopted her as a goddess of the earth, of fertility, and of nature's abundance. Look at her head. She wears a crown shaped like city walls and towers. That crown marks her as a protector of cities. In one hand she holds a sceptre, and in the other, a key. It is a fitting figure for a capital that wanted to show order, plenty, and confidence.

The fountain was born in the late eighteenth century, under King Charles III. He was a reforming king, and Madrileños still call him the city's best mayor. He wanted to turn this eastern edge of Madrid into an elegant promenade. The result was the Salón del Prado, a long tree-lined avenue for walking and being seen. The architect Ventura Rodríguez designed its fountains. Work on Cybele took place between 1777 and 1782.

Several artists shared the carving. The sculptor Francisco Gutiérrez made the goddess and her chariot. The French-born sculptor Roberto Michel carved the two lions. The work was planned as one piece of a larger scheme. Further down the Paseo del Prado, the Fountain of Apollo and the Fountain of Neptune belong to the same design. Together, the three fountains turned a dusty road into a stage for the city.

Now look closely at the lions. Here is a detail most visitors miss. They are not simply lions. In Greek myth, as told by the Roman poet Ovid, they were once two lovers, Atalanta and Hippomenes. Atalanta was a famous runner who refused to marry any man who could not beat her in a race. Hippomenes won by dropping golden apples to distract her. Later, the couple offended the gods inside a sacred temple. As punishment, they were turned into lions and tied to Cybele's chariot forever. Look at them again — side by side, yet facing forward, never able to look at each other.

The fountain was not only decoration. It was also part of the city's water supply. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Madrid relied on public fountains for drinking water. Professional water carriers, called aguadores, filled their jars here and sold water door to door. One spout served these workers, and another served ordinary residents. So this goddess was once a working part of daily life, surrounded by queues of people with jugs, not cameras.

The second surprise is that Cybele has moved. When she was first built, she stood closer to the edge of the promenade, facing her partner Neptune further down the avenue. In the late nineteenth century, the city reorganised the square for growing traffic. The fountain was shifted into the centre of the plaza and turned around. Today she looks up the Calle de Alcalá, towards the Puerta del Sol and the old heart of Madrid. Other decorative elements were added around that time as well.

Turn slowly and take in the four corners of the square. The enormous white building with towers is the Palacio de Cibeles. It was built in the early twentieth century as the city's main post office. Locals once joked that it looked like a wedding cake or a cathedral. Today it houses Madrid's City Hall and a cultural centre. Diagonally opposite stands the Bank of Spain, a heavy stone building that holds the national reserves. The other two corners hold the Palacio de Buenavista, now used by the army, and the Palacio de Linares, home to a cultural institute for Latin America.

The Bank of Spain gives us one of Madrid's favourite stories. Locals say that the bank's vault sits deep underground, beneath this square. According to a popular tale, if thieves ever broke in, a system would flood the vault with water — water linked to Cybele herself. It is almost certainly an urban legend, but it is a good one. It shows how closely the city connects this fountain with wealth, safety, and power.

Cybele has also lived through hard times. During the Spanish Civil War in the 1930s, Madrid was besieged and bombed. Residents protected the fountain by covering it with sandbags and a brick structure. The goddess spent the war hidden inside a protective shell. When the cover finally came off, she was still here. For many residents, that survival made the fountain even more precious.

Today, Cybele has a very different role. She belongs to Real Madrid football fans. When the club wins a major title, thousands of supporters pour into this square. Players climb up to the goddess, and the captain places a club scarf and flag on her. The night fills with chanting, horns, and white shirts. Fans of the rival club, Atlético de Madrid, celebrate at the Fountain of Neptune instead. So two stone gods, a few hundred metres apart, mark one of the city's oldest rivalries.

Because of these celebrations, the fountain is now fenced and carefully watched on big nights. Over the years, it has needed repairs and restoration. Celebrating crowds and city pollution both leave their marks on soft stone. When you look at the surface, you can see small differences in tone and texture. These are the traces of cleaning and repair. The goddess has been patched and polished many times, like the city around her.

Before you leave, look at her once more. Watch the light move across the marble, pale in the morning and warmer in the evening. Notice the lions straining forward, and the calm face above them. Then listen again for the water under the traffic. As you walk away, up Alcalá or down the Paseo del Prado, you are following the same paths that strollers and water carriers walked here more than two hundred years ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stay on the pavements; the fountain sits on a busy traffic island.
View it from the corner by the Bank of Spain.
Climb the Palacio de Cibeles rooftop viewpoint for an overhead shot.
Visit early morning for quiet streets and soft light.
Walk down the Paseo del Prado to see Neptune and Apollo.
