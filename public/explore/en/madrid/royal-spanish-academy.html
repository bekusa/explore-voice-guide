The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. On this quiet street behind the Prado, the traffic of central Madrid seems far away. You might hear footsteps on the pavement, a few birds in the trees, perhaps the bells of the church next door. In front of you stands a pale, formal building with columns and tall windows. It looks like a bank, or perhaps a small museum. In fact, this is the home of the Royal Spanish Academy, the institution that watches over the Spanish language.

Think about that for a second. Spanish is spoken by hundreds of millions of people, across Spain, the Americas, and beyond. Much of the shared reference work for that enormous language is shaped inside these walls. The official dictionary, the grammar, and the rules of spelling all come from the Academy and its partner institutions. Writers, teachers, journalists, and ordinary people argue about its decisions every year. And it all happens on this calm side street.

The story begins in 1713. A nobleman called Juan Manuel Fernández Pacheco, the Marquis of Villena, gathered a small group of scholars in his own house in Madrid. They were worried about the state of the language. They wanted to fix its words and its spelling, and to give it the same dignity that French had gained through its own academy in Paris. The next year, King Philip the Fifth gave the group his royal approval. That is where the word Royal in the name comes from.

The founders chose a motto that every Spanish student still knows. In Spanish it reads: Limpia, fija y da esplendor. In English, that means: it cleans, it fixes, and it gives splendour. The emblem shows a crucible, a small pot for melting metal, sitting in a fire. The idea is simple. Like a metalworker, the Academy heats the language, removes the impurities, and leaves something bright and lasting. Look for this image on the building and on the Academy's printed works.

The early academicians did not waste time. Their first great project was a dictionary, published in six volumes between 1726 and 1739. Today it is known as the Dictionary of Authorities. The name comes from its method. For each word, the editors quoted passages from respected Spanish writers to show how the word was really used. In the eighteenth century, this was a modern and serious approach. Later came a book of spelling rules in 1741, and a grammar in 1771.

For its first century and a half, the Academy had no permanent home of its own. It met in different places around the city. This building opened in 1894, late in the nineteenth century. Its design follows the neoclassical style, a style that borrows the calm columns and clean lines of ancient Greek and Roman temples. Notice how symmetrical the front is. Everything is balanced around the central entrance, as if the architecture itself wants to show order and rules.

Now look at the neighbourhood. You are standing in the area locals call Los Jerónimos. Just beside the Academy rises the church of San Jerónimo el Real, with its tall towers. A short walk away is the Prado Museum, one of the great art collections of Europe. A little further on, the green edge of the Retiro Park begins. So this building sits between painting, faith, and gardens. It is a fitting address for an institution that cares about culture.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The members of the Academy do not simply hold numbered seats. Each chair is marked with a letter of the alphabet — capital letters and small letters. One academician might sit in seat capital A, another in small b. When a member dies, a new person is elected to that same letter. Being given a letter here is one of the highest honours a Spanish writer or scholar can receive.

Many famous names have held those letters. Novelists, poets, philosophers, and linguists have all taken their seats here. New members give a formal speech when they join, and another academician replies. Over the years, these speeches have become a small library of their own. The members still meet regularly to debate words, definitions, and rules. It is slow, careful work. A single word can take a long time to enter the dictionary... or to leave it.

And here is a second surprise. The Academy does not work alone. In 1951, the language academies of the Spanish-speaking world formed an association, with its first meeting held in Mexico. Today, academies from Latin America, the Philippines, the United States, and other places work together with Madrid. The modern dictionary and grammar are shared projects. So the Spanish you hear in Buenos Aires or Bogotá also has a voice in the books that are produced here.

The dictionary itself has changed a great deal over time. It has gone through many editions, each one adding new words and removing old ones. In recent decades, it moved online, and today millions of people check it on their phones every day. Each update brings headlines in the Spanish press. Some people complain that new slang has been accepted. Others complain that the Academy moves too slowly. In a way, these arguments show how much people care.

It is worth knowing that this is a working institution, not a typical museum. The interior holds a library, meeting rooms, and portraits of past members, but it is not open every day like the Prado. The Academy sometimes organises special visits and open days. If you want to see inside, check the official website before your trip. Otherwise, the street view is the main experience — and it is a peaceful one.

Before you walk on, take one more look at the building. Behind those windows, people decide how words are spelled, what they mean, and how the language should be written. Every letter on a Spanish street sign, every word in a Spanish newspaper, connects in some way to this place. As you head toward the Prado or the Retiro, listen to the Spanish around you... and remember where so much of it is quietly recorded.
