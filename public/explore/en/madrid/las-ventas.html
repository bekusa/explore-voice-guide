The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along the wide avenue behind you, but in front of you the noise softens against a great curved wall of red brick. The brick is warm in colour, almost the shade of terracotta pots. Bands of glazed tile catch the light in blues, greens and ochres. Horseshoe arches repeat around the building. You are standing outside Las Ventas, the bullring of Madrid, and the largest bullring in Spain.

First, the name. It does not come from a famous matador or a king. It comes from the area itself. This eastern edge of old Madrid was once known for its ventas, the roadside inns where travellers stopped on the way into the capital. The neighbourhood kept the name long after the inns disappeared. So the most important bullring in the Spanish-speaking world carries the name of humble places where people once watered their mules and ate a simple meal.

The building you see dates from the early twentieth century. It was designed by the architect José Espelius, who died before it was finished. The work was completed by Manuel Muñoz Monasterio. The ring first opened in 1931, during a turbulent political period in Spain. Its formal inauguration followed a few years later, in 1934. Madrid had outgrown its older bullring, and the city wanted a venue that matched its role as the capital.

Look closely at the style. Architects call it Neo-Mudéjar. Mudéjar was the art of Muslim craftsmen who stayed and worked in Christian Spain during the Middle Ages. Their work used brick, ceramic tile and geometric patterns instead of carved stone. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Spanish architects revived this tradition as a kind of national style. So this modern building is dressed in the decorative language of medieval Iberia — a deliberate statement about Spanish identity.

Now let your eyes travel up the facade. Above the doors and along the walls, you can find ceramic panels with coats of arms. They represent the provinces of Spain. Many visitors walk straight past them on their way inside. But they turn the exterior into a kind of map of the country, set in glazed tile. The ring was meant to belong to all of Spain, not only to Madrid.

Inside, the ring holds roughly twenty-three thousand people. The seats rise in steep rows around a circle of sand. Seats are divided into sol and sombra — sun and shade. On a summer afternoon, this choice matters a great deal. The shaded seats cost more, and the sunny side can feel like an oven. The seating sections are numbered, and Madrid aficionados speak of them almost like neighbourhoods, each with its own character.

One section is famous among fans. Tendido seven has a reputation for the most demanding spectators in the bullring. Locals say a matador who pleases tendido seven has truly earned his applause. These fans whistle at weak performances and stay silent when they are not impressed. It is a reminder that this crowd is not passive. In Las Ventas, the audience judges, argues and remembers.

The high point of the year comes in spring, with the Feria de San Isidro. Saint Isidore is the patron saint of Madrid, and his festival in May brings a long series of bullfights. For several weeks, Las Ventas becomes the centre of the bullfighting world. A good afternoon here can make a young matador's career. A bad one can follow him for years.

The greatest honour has a physical form. If a matador performs exceptionally, the crowd may carry him on their shoulders out through the main gate, called the Puerta Grande. Leaving through this gate is the dream of every bullfighter. It does not happen often. When it does, the street outside fills with people and noise, and the moment is recorded in newspapers the next day.

Of course, bullfighting is deeply divisive, both in Spain and abroad. Supporters see it as an art form tied to music, painting and poetry. Opponents see cruelty to animals. Some Spanish regions have tried to restrict it, while Madrid has defended it as part of its cultural heritage. You do not need to settle that debate to visit. The building itself is a record of how central these spectacles once were to public life.

Here is a surprise. In 1965, The Beatles played a concert here. It was their show in Madrid during their European tour, and the arena was not even full that night. Over the years, Las Ventas has also hosted other concerts, boxing and tennis. So this sand has carried far more than bulls. The ring has quietly served as one of Madrid's great open-air stages.

Before you leave, walk around the outside. You will find statues of famous bullfighters, frozen in mid-pass with their capes. You will also find a monument to Alexander Fleming, the Scottish scientist who discovered penicillin. It may seem strange here. But bullfighters faced terrible goring wounds, and penicillin saved many lives. The bullfighting community honoured him in gratitude. Inside, a small bullfighting museum displays costumes, posters and other relics of famous matadors.

As you step back from the walls, notice the light again. In the late afternoon, the brick turns deeper red and the tiles glow. Listen to the avenue, the buses, the voices of people heading home. This building has heard roaring crowds and complete silence. Right now, it simply stands in the sun, patient and solid. Take one last look at the arches before you walk on.
