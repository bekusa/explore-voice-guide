The guide

The story

Stand at the iron railings for a moment. On the other side, gravel paths run between clipped hedges toward a long, pale facade. The traffic of Calle de la Princesa moves behind you, but the garden in front of you is quiet. You are looking at one of the last great aristocratic homes still standing in central Madrid. And here is the first surprise. Liria Palace is not a former residence. It is still a family home.

The family is the House of Alba, one of the oldest noble lines in Spain. Their titles go back centuries. Their name appears again and again in Spanish history, in war, in politics, and in the patronage of art. When people in Madrid say "the Alba palace", this is the building they mean. Its rooms still hold the paintings, books, and letters that generations of the family gathered.

The name itself has a story. Liria is the Castilian form of Llíria, a town near Valencia. The title Duke of Liria belonged to the family that built this house. That family, the Dukes of Berwick, had an unusual origin. The first Duke of Berwick was an illegitimate son of King James the Second of England. He became a marshal in the service of France and fought for the Bourbon side in Spain. So this very Spanish palace begins with an English royal bloodline.

It was his descendant, the third Duke of Berwick, who commissioned the palace in the eighteenth century. The work started under a French architect, Louis Guilbert. Later the Spanish architect Ventura Rodríguez took charge. Rodríguez was one of the leading builders of his age in Madrid. Look at the result. The style is neoclassical, which means it follows the calm, ordered rules of ancient Greek and Roman buildings. There are columns, a clear symmetry, and very little decoration for its own sake.

Notice the scale of the facade from here. It is long and low rather than tall. The central section steps forward slightly, and columns mark the main entrance. The stone has a warm, honey-grey tone that changes with the light. In the late afternoon it turns softer, almost golden. Early in the day it looks cooler and sharper. The garden in front was designed in a formal French manner, with geometric beds and straight lines that lead the eye to the house.

Later, two great noble houses joined. Through marriage, the Berwick line and the Alba line became one family. That union is why this palace became the home of the Dukes of Alba. It also explains the size of the collection inside. Two family inheritances came together under one roof. Paintings, tapestries, furniture, and archives from both lines now sit in the same rooms.

Now the second surprise. The building you see is not entirely the original. In 1936, at the start of the Spanish Civil War, the palace was bombed and burned. The interior was largely destroyed. Much of the art collection had been moved to safety before the fire, and that decision saved many works you can see today. After the war, the family rebuilt the palace. The outer walls follow the eighteenth-century design, but many interiors date from the reconstruction in the mid-twentieth century.

The most famous painting inside is by Francisco de Goya. He painted the Duchess of Alba of his time standing in a white dress, with a red sash at her waist and a small white dog at her feet. The portrait dates from the 1790s. The relationship between Goya and that duchess has fascinated people for two centuries. Many stories about it are rumour rather than fact. What is certain is that she was one of the most admired figures in the Madrid of her day.

The collection reaches far beyond Goya. Visitors on the guided tour see works by Titian, the Venetian master of colour, and by Rubens, the Flemish painter known for energy and movement. There are paintings by El Greco and other Spanish artists, and fine tapestries on the walls. The rooms are arranged as a home, not a gallery. A painting may hang above a sofa or beside a fireplace. That domestic setting changes how you look at the art.

The library holds what many consider the most remarkable treasures. The family archive contains documents connected to Christopher Columbus, including letters in his own hand. There are also rare early books and centuries of family papers. For historians, these records are a window into the Spain of exploration and empire. For visitors, it is simply striking to see such papers in a private house, a few steps from a busy Madrid street.

One more figure belongs to this place in the modern memory of Spain. Cayetana Fitz-James Stuart, the eighteenth Duchess of Alba, was born here in 1926. She held more noble titles than almost anyone in the world. She was famous for her independence, her love of flamenco, and her colourful personal life. She died in 2014. Many Spaniards still connect the palace with her face and her style.

For most of its history, the public could only see this house from the railings, as you are now. That changed in 2019, when the family opened parts of the palace for guided visits. The tour covers the main reception rooms, the library, and the art collection. Groups are small, and the visit has a calm, private feeling. Because people still live here, some areas remain closed, and the rules inside are strict.

The neighbourhood around you is worth a slow look too. Plaza de España is a short walk south. The Conde Duque cultural centre, a former barracks, is close by on the other side. The streets in between have small bars, bookshops, and quiet squares. After the formal silence of the palace, these streets bring you back to everyday Madrid life.

Before you walk on, look once more through the railings. See the straight paths, the pale stone, the windows in their even rows. Behind those windows hang paintings that survived a war, and papers that Columbus once held. And somewhere inside, a family still keeps its home. As you leave, listen to the city return around you... the traffic, the voices, the footsteps on the pavement.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a guided tour in advance; visits run in small groups.
Visit the garden railings in late afternoon for warmer light.
Check photography rules before the tour; they are strict inside.
Combine your visit with a walk to nearby Plaza de España.
Allow extra time to explore the Conde Duque neighbourhood afterwards.
