The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of the Moncloa interchange fades behind you, and the air changes. Pale stone, a long arcade, and a tall tower that looks less like a museum and more like a church on a colonial plaza. The ground underfoot is quiet here. This is the Museo de América, Spain's national museum for the art, archaeology and peoples of the Americas.

A quick note on place. The museum stands in Madrid's university quarter, the Ciudad Universitaria, in the district of Moncloa. If you have travelled up from Leganés or elsewhere in the south of the region, you have crossed most of the metropolitan area to reach it. That journey is worth it. Very few places in Europe let you see this much of the pre-Columbian world in one building.

Start with the building itself, because it tells you a lot before you enter. It was designed in the mid-twentieth century by the architects Luis Moya Blanco and Luis Martínez-Feduchi. They did not choose a modern glass box. They chose a historical style that echoes the churches and missions that Spain built across Latin America. The tower, the arches and the cloister-like layout are all deliberate. The architecture makes an argument about shared history before you see a single object.

That argument is important, and it is not a neutral one. The museum was created by decree in the early 1940s, in the years after the Spanish Civil War. The idea of a Spanish-speaking world tied together by language and faith was central to the politics of that era. The building took many years to finish and opened to the public in the 1960s. Later, in the 1990s, the galleries were renovated and the displays were reorganised with a more modern approach.

The collection is much older than the building. Its roots go back to the eighteenth century, to the Royal Cabinet of Natural History in Madrid. That was a royal collection of scientific and curious objects, built up under the Bourbon kings. Scientific expeditions to the Americas sent back plants, minerals, tools, textiles and sacred objects. Over the next two centuries, these pieces moved between different institutions before they found a permanent home here.

Inside, you will notice that the galleries are not arranged as a simple timeline. The displays are grouped around themes instead. One area looks at how Europeans came to know and imagine the Americas. Others look at the land itself, at social life, at religion, and at communication. This means a gold ornament from Colombia and a painting from Mexico can sit in the same conversation. It is a slower way to visit, and a more rewarding one.

Now for the first object that surprises most visitors. Somewhere in these galleries is the Madrid Codex, also called the Tro-Cortesiano Codex. It is a Maya book, painted on folded bark paper coated with fine plaster. Only a handful of Maya codices survived the colonial period. Most were destroyed. The others are in Dresden, Paris and Mexico. To stand in front of one of these books is to stand in front of something extremely rare. The pages hold calendars, rituals and sacred almanacs, written in glyphs that scholars are still working to read.

The second treasure is gold. The Quimbaya Treasure comes from a culture that lived in what is now central Colombia, long before Europeans arrived. Look closely at the pieces. Some are hollow figures with calm, closed eyes and smooth bodies. Others are flasks and ornaments. The government of Colombia gave the treasure to Spain in the late nineteenth century, after a large exhibition that marked four hundred years since the voyages of Columbus. Few visitors realise this gold arrived as a diplomatic gift, not as colonial loot.

The colonial galleries change the mood. Here you will find the casta paintings from New Spain, which was the colonial name for Mexico. They are sets of painted panels, each showing a father, a mother and a child of mixed ancestry. Each panel carries a label for the child's category. They look like gentle family scenes. In fact, they show a society trying to sort people into a strict social order based on race. Stand with them for a while. They are beautiful, and they are uncomfortable, and both things are true.

Nearby, keep an eye out for the enconchados. These are Mexican paintings with small pieces of mother-of-pearl set into the surface. When you move your head, the shell catches the light and shimmers under the paint. The technique drew on the lacquered and inlaid objects that came across the Pacific from Asia. It is a reminder that colonial Mexico was not only connected to Spain. It also sat on a trade route that reached all the way to the Philippines and China.

The museum also holds ethnographic material from many Indigenous peoples of the Americas, from the Arctic to the Amazon. You will see featherwork, textiles, masks, tools and ritual objects. Some pieces are delicate and have to be kept in low light, so do not be surprised if certain rooms feel dim. That darkness protects colours that were first dyed centuries ago. Let your eyes adjust before you move on.

It is worth thinking about the questions this museum raises today. Across Europe, museums are now discussing how their collections were acquired, and who has the right to tell these stories. The Museo de América is part of that conversation. As you walk, you might ask yourself whose voice each label uses. Is it the voice of the people who made the object, or the people who collected it? There is no single answer in these rooms.

When you step back outside, look again at the tower and the arcade. They will look different now. Across the open ground, you can see the tall modern viewing tower known as the Faro de Moncloa, and the large stone victory arch near the road. Each structure belongs to a different moment in the history of Madrid. Together, they make this corner of the city a quiet lesson in how a nation chooses to remember.

So take a last breath of the cooler air by the stone. Listen to the students passing on their way to lectures, and the distant sound of buses on the avenue. Behind you, a Maya book, a gift of Colombian gold and a room of painted families wait for the next visitor. Carry one of them with you as you walk back toward the city.

Practical tips
