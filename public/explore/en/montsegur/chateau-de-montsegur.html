The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Up here it rarely stops. It comes over the bare limestone and through the empty doorways, and it carries the faint smell of box shrubs and warm stone. Under your feet the rock is pale and polished by centuries of boots. Around you, the foothills of the Pyrenees fold away in blue and green layers. You have climbed a long way to reach these walls, and the mountain still makes you feel it in your legs.

This is the Château de Montségur, high above the village of the same name in the Ariège region of southern France. The castle sits on a steep rocky peak that locals call a pog, an Occitan word for a hill or summit. It rises to roughly twelve hundred metres. From a distance the peak looks almost like a sugarloaf, with sheer sides on most approaches. That shape is the whole reason a fortress was ever built here.

To understand why this hilltop matters, we need to talk about the Cathars. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, a Christian movement spread across this part of southern France. Its followers believed the material world was the work of an evil principle, while the spiritual world belonged to a good God. The Catholic Church saw this as heresy. Its most devoted members were called Perfects, or Good Men and Good Women, and they lived strict lives of prayer and fasting.

In 1209 the Pope called a crusade against the Cathars and the southern lords who protected them. It became known as the Albigensian Crusade, named after the town of Albi. For decades, northern French armies moved through the region, taking towns and castles one by one. As the pressure grew, Cathar leaders looked for a refuge that soldiers could not easily reach. Early in the thirteenth century, the local lord Raymond de Péreille agreed to fortify this summit for them.

For a time, Montségur became the spiritual heart of the Cathar church. A small village of houses clung to the slopes just below the walls. Perfects lived there, prayed there, and received visitors who climbed up from the valleys. Archaeologists have found traces of these dwellings on the terraces around the summit. Picture the hillside you walked up crowded with small stone and timber homes, smoke rising from cooking fires.

The end began with an act of violence. In 1242, armed men linked to the Montségur garrison killed a group of inquisitors at the town of Avignonet. The response was swift. In 1243 a royal and church army surrounded the mountain. The defenders, led by the knight Pierre-Roger de Mirepoix, held out through the summer, the autumn, and much of the winter. The steep cliffs did most of their work for them.

The siege lasted around ten months. The turning point came when attackers, said to be guided by local mountaineers, scaled the eastern ridge and captured a position close to the walls. From there they could bring a siege engine within range. In early 1244 the defenders asked for terms. There was a truce of about two weeks before the surrender. Here is a detail many visitors miss: during that truce, some of the defenders chose to join the Cathar faith, knowing what it would mean.

In March 1244, the castle surrendered. Those who would not renounce their beliefs were led down the mountain. Around two hundred people, among them the Cathar bishop Bertrand Marty, died in a great fire at the foot of the peak. The meadow where this is believed to have happened is still called the Prat dels Cremats, the Field of the Burned. On your way up, you passed a simple stone memorial there, placed in the twentieth century.

Now for the second surprise. The walls around you are almost certainly not the walls the Cathars knew. After the siege, the old fortress was largely pulled down. A new castle was built in its place by the Lévis family, the lords who received this land from the French crown. So the ruin you are standing in is mostly a royal military post from after the tragedy. Underneath it, and in the terraces around it, lie the fainter traces of the earlier settlement.

Take a moment to look at the shape of the building. The outer wall follows the edge of the summit, forming an irregular five-sided enclosure. At one end stands the keep, the strongest part of the castle, with thick walls and narrow openings. The stone is local limestone, rough and grey, with paler patches where it has been repaired. Inside, the courtyard is open to the sky. Notice how little space there is, and how close the cliff edge feels.

Montségur has also gathered many legends. One popular tale says that just before the surrender, a few Perfects slipped down the cliffs at night carrying a secret treasure. Some later writers claimed this treasure was the Holy Grail. There is no evidence for this, and historians treat it as folklore. Other people have suggested that the castle was aligned with the rising sun at the summer solstice. That idea is debated, but many visitors still come at dawn in June to watch.

Before you go down, walk slowly to the walls and look out. To one side you can see the village roofs far below, grey slate and stone tucked into the valley. Beyond them the land rises toward the high Pyrenees, often with snow on the peaks into spring. Down in the village, a small museum displays objects found during excavations on the summit, from everyday pottery to tools. It is worth an hour if your legs allow.

Stand still once more, and let the wind fill the silence. The people who lived here chose this rock because it was hard to reach and easy to defend. In the end, it could not protect them. Today the castle holds no garrison and no faith, only weathered stone and wide sky. As you begin the descent, watch your footing on the smooth rock... and when you reach the meadow below, you may want to pause by the memorial before you leave the mountain behind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes with grip for the steep rocky path.
Bring water, since the climb is hard in summer heat.
Visit the village museum to see finds from the excavations.
Start early to avoid midday sun on the exposed path.
Carry a windproof layer; the summit is often cold and gusty.
