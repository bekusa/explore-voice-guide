The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the wall beside you. The stone is warm, even in the early morning, and it is the colour of honey. Run your fingers along it and you may feel a fine, dusty grit. That is yellow sandstone, and the whole fort is built from it. In the late afternoon, when the sun drops low over the Thar Desert, the walls turn deep gold. This is why people call it Sonar Quila, the Golden Fort.

You are standing on Trikuta Hill, a ridge of rock that rises above the flat desert. From down in the town, the fort looks like it grows straight out of the hill. The walls follow the shape of the rock, curving and bending instead of running in straight lines. Count the round towers along the outer wall as you walk. Tradition says there are ninety-nine bastions in total. Each one once gave guards a clear view across the sand in every direction.

The story begins in the twelfth century. Tradition holds that a Rajput ruler named Rawal Jaisal founded the fort in 1156. The Rajputs were warrior clans who ruled much of this region, and Jaisal belonged to the Bhati clan. The city around you carries his name. Jaisalmer means, roughly, the hill fort of Jaisal. Legend has it that a holy man told him this ridge would make a strong home for his kingdom. Whatever the truth of that tale, the site was a smart choice.

Why build a city here, in the middle of a desert? The answer is trade. For centuries, camel caravans crossed this land, carrying goods between India, Persia, Arabia and beyond. Spices, silk, opium and textiles all passed through. Jaisalmer sat on these routes, and the rulers taxed the traders who stopped here. That wealth paid for the carved stone you see everywhere. When sea trade grew and the caravan routes faded, the city lost much of its income.

Now here is the first thing many visitors miss. This is not an empty monument. People live inside these walls. Families have stayed here for generations, in homes squeezed between the ramparts. You will pass doorways with laundry hanging above them, children on their way to school, and small shops selling tea and textiles. That is why Jaisalmer is often called a living fort. Very few forts of this age in the world still hold a working neighbourhood.

To enter, you pass through a series of heavy gates. Each gate is set at an angle to the one before it. This was deliberate. An enemy could not charge straight in, and war elephants could not build up speed to break the doors. As you climb the stone ramp, notice how the path turns and narrows. Defenders on the walls above could watch every step an attacker took. Even today, the climb slows you down.

Look up at the tops of the walls as you walk. In several places you may see large round stones resting on the battlements. These were weapons. In a siege, defenders could roll them down onto soldiers climbing below. Legends from this region also speak of terrible sieges in the medieval period, and of the women of the fort choosing death over capture. These stories are told with great pride locally, but the details belong more to folklore than to firm record.

At the heart of the fort is a wide open square, often called Dussehra Chowk. Dussehra is a Hindu festival celebrating the victory of good over evil. On one side rises the royal palace, the Raj Mahal, where the rulers of Jaisalmer once lived. Its upper floors have balconies with screens of carved stone, so fine they look like lace. Royal women could watch life in the square below without being seen. Much of the palace now works as a museum.

Walk a little further and you will find the Jain temples. Jainism is an ancient Indian religion built around non-violence and respect for all living things. These temples date roughly from the twelfth to the fifteenth centuries. Inside, nearly every surface is carved: dancers, animals, flowers and rows of calm, seated figures. The stone is the same yellow sandstone, yet here it feels almost soft. Please remember these are active places of worship, so move quietly.

Here is the second surprise. The fort is under real strain. For centuries, desert life meant using very little water. Today, with homes, hotels and restaurants inside the walls, far more water flows through the old stone. Some of it seeps into the foundations. Parts of the walls have cracked, and sections have collapsed in recent decades. Conservation groups and local residents now work on repairs. When you see fresh stone patched into old walls, you are seeing that effort.

In 2013, UNESCO added Jaisalmer Fort to its World Heritage List. It was listed together with other great hill forts of Rajasthan, the desert state you are standing in. Long before that, the fort found fame on screen. In the 1970s, the Bengali director Satyajit Ray made a children's adventure film called Sonar Kella, meaning the Golden Fort. Many visitors from eastern India still come here because of that film, and you may hear its name in the lanes.

Take a moment to notice the small things. The lanes are narrow, and they twist so that shade falls across them for much of the day. The carved windows let breezes through while keeping out harsh sun. Cows sometimes wander past. You may smell incense from a temple, or cumin and chilli from a kitchen. Listen, too. Bells, scooter horns, bargaining voices and the call of a vendor all mix together in these stone corridors.

If you can, find a spot on the ramparts and look out. Below you, the town spreads in the same golden stone. Beyond it, the land flattens into the Thar Desert, pale and wide. On a clear day, the horizon seems very far away. For the caravans that once crossed that sand, this hill was the first sign of water, shelter and trade after days of travel.

Before you go, touch the stone one more time. Feel how much warmer it has become since you arrived. Notice the colour too, because it changes with every hour of the day. As you walk back down the ramp and through the angled gates, look back over your shoulder. The fort will still be there, glowing above the town, with people living their ordinary lives inside its golden walls.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon to see the walls glow gold.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep stone entry ramp.
Remove shoes and leather items before entering Jain temples.
Walk the outer ramparts for wide views over the desert.
Carry water; the stone lanes get hot by midday.
