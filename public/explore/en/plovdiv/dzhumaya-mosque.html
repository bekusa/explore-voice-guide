The guide

The story

Start with the sound. Above the traffic of footsteps on the main pedestrian street, you may hear pigeons shifting on the domes, a fountain running, and the clink of small coffee cups from somewhere close by. The stone under your feet is smooth from heavy use. In front of you stands a low, broad building of stone and brick, topped with a cluster of grey lead domes. Beside it, a slender minaret rises in bands of red and pale stone. This is the Dzhumaya Mosque, one of the oldest Ottoman buildings in the Balkans. It is still a working place of prayer today.

The name gives you a clue to its role. Dzhumaya comes from the Turkish word for Friday, the day of the main congregational prayer in Islam. A Friday mosque is the central mosque of a town, the one large enough for the whole Muslim community to gather once a week. For centuries, this was that place for Plovdiv. Under Ottoman rule the city was known as Filibe, a name that grew out of its ancient Greek name, Philippopolis. The mosque sat at the commercial heart of Filibe, as it sits at the heart of Plovdiv now.

The Ottomans took the city in the fourteenth century. A first mosque is believed to have stood on this spot soon after the conquest. The building you see today is generally dated to the fifteenth century, to the reign of Sultan Murad the Second. Local tradition says an older Christian church once stood here, and that the mosque replaced it. That story is widely repeated, but treat it as tradition rather than settled fact. What is certain is that this corner of the city has been a place of worship and trade for a very long time.

Now look at the walls. They are not plain stone. Rows of cut stone alternate with thin courses of red brick, sometimes with brick set vertically between the stones. Builders across the Byzantine and early Ottoman world used this technique. It makes a wall stronger, and it also creates a pattern of warm and cool colours. Early Ottoman architects learned a great deal from the Byzantine buildings around them. Here you can see that conversation in the masonry itself.

Lift your eyes to the roof. Count the domes. There are nine, arranged in three rows of three. This layout is unusual. Later Ottoman mosques usually prefer one great central dome, like the famous mosques of Istanbul. The nine-dome plan belongs to an earlier stage of Ottoman building, before that single-dome ideal took over. So the roofline tells you something about the age of the building. It is a design from a time when the empire was still experimenting with form.

The minaret deserves a closer look. Its shaft carries a striking pattern of red brick and lighter stone, set in diagonal and zigzag bands. From some angles it looks almost woven. A minaret is the tower from which the call to prayer is given five times a day. Today a loudspeaker often does the work that a human voice once did from the balcony. If you are here at the right moment, you will hear the call carry across the square and down the shopping street.

If you go inside, remember that this is an active mosque. Dress modestly, remove your shoes at the entrance, and avoid visiting during prayer times. Women are usually asked to cover their hair. Inside, the space feels wider than you expect. Four heavy columns hold up the nine domes, so the room opens around you rather than above you. Carpets soften every step. The light comes in low through the windows, and the noise of the street drops away almost at once.

Look at the walls and the undersides of the domes. They are covered with painted decoration — flowers, vines, curling plant forms, and bands of colour. These paintings are much younger than the building. They are usually dated to the nineteenth century, a period when Ottoman interior design borrowed freely from European decorative fashions. So you are standing inside a fifteenth-century structure, wearing nineteenth-century clothes. That mix of layers is typical of Plovdiv as a whole.

Step back outside and turn toward the square. Here comes the first big surprise for many visitors. Just beside the mosque, below street level, you can see curved rows of stone seats. These belong to the Roman stadium of Philippopolis, built in the second century. Most of the stadium still lies hidden under the shops and the main street. What you see is only its rounded northern end. Romans once watched athletic contests here, and the track ran south for a long distance under the modern city.

Think about that for a moment. Within a few steps, you have a Roman sports arena, an early Ottoman mosque, and a modern shopping street full of phone shops and ice cream. Plovdiv is often described as one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Europe. On this square, you do not need to imagine it. The layers sit side by side, at different heights, and you can see them all from one spot.

The second surprise is a matter of level. Notice how the mosque seems to sit a little low compared to the street around it. Over the centuries, the city around it rose. Each generation built on the debris of the one before. Streets were raised, buildings were replaced, and ground level slowly climbed. The Roman stadium sits even lower. In effect, you are reading the city's history vertically — the older the layer, the deeper it lies.

The mosque has always been part of daily commercial life, not separate from it. Around it you will find small shops and cafés. Many visitors stop for a cup of thick, strong coffee or a piece of Turkish delight, a soft sweet dusted with powdered sugar. Sitting there, you can watch an ordinary rhythm. Worshippers come and go. Shoppers cut across the square. Children run along the edge of the stadium ruins. Older men sit on benches and talk.

The building has also survived difficult times. Plovdiv passed from Ottoman rule into the modern Bulgarian state in the late nineteenth century. Many Ottoman buildings in the region did not survive the changes that followed. This mosque did. It has been restored more than once, and it remains a reminder of the Muslim community that has been part of the city for centuries, and still is today.

Before you move on, stand still for a minute and take it in once more. The grey domes. The striped minaret against the sky. The rounded stone seats of the stadium below. The steady flow of people on the main street. As you walk away, notice how the street itself runs straight and long — it follows the line of the old Roman track beneath your feet. You are walking, quite literally, on top of history.
