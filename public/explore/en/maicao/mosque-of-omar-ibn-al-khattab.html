The guide

The story

Feel the heat first. In Maicao the sun presses down on the pavement, and the air carries dust from the dry plains of La Guajira. Somewhere nearby a motorbike engine rattles past. A street vendor calls out prices in Spanish, and perhaps a few words of Arabic drift from a shop doorway. Then you look up, and a white minaret rises above the low rooftops. It is not what most people expect to find in the far north of Colombia.

This is the Mosque of Omar Ibn Al-Khattab, one of the largest mosques in Latin America. It stands in a trading town close to the border with Venezuela, in a region better known for desert, salt flats and the Caribbean coast. To understand why a mosque of this size is here, you have to understand Maicao itself. This is a town that lives by commerce, and commerce brought people from very far away.

During the twentieth century, families from the eastern Mediterranean began to settle along Colombia's Caribbean coast. Many came from Lebanon, others from Syria and Palestine. Some arrived through Venezuela, and some came directly to Colombian ports. Maicao, with its open border and busy markets, became a natural place for merchants. Over the decades the Arab community here grew into one of the most visible in the country. Walk the nearby streets and you will see it in shop names, in faces, and in the food sold at small counters.

For a long time, this community gathered for prayer in more modest spaces. As families grew and businesses settled, the wish for a proper mosque grew too. The building you see now was completed in the late 1990s, funded largely by the local Muslim community. It was a statement of permanence. These were no longer travellers passing through — they were people who had decided that Maicao was home.

The mosque takes its name from Omar Ibn Al-Khattab, the second caliph of early Islam. A caliph was a leader of the Muslim community after the Prophet Muhammad. Omar is remembered in Islamic tradition for justice, simplicity and firm leadership during a period of rapid expansion in the seventh century. Naming a mosque after him is common across the Muslim world. Here, so far from the Middle East, the name links this desert town to a very old story.

Now look at the building itself. The walls are mostly white, a sensible choice under this fierce sun. White surfaces reflect heat, and they also make the structure stand out sharply against the blue sky. The minaret is tall and slender. In much of the Muslim world, a minaret is the tower from which the call to prayer is announced. Here it also works as a landmark. You can use it to find your way back through the grid of streets.

Notice the dome as well. Domes have a long history in Islamic architecture, and they often represent the vault of the heavens. Inside, a dome creates a wide open space without many columns, so worshippers can stand together in long straight rows. Those rows face Mecca, the holy city in Saudi Arabia. Inside the prayer hall, a small niche in the wall, called the mihrab, shows that direction. If you are allowed inside, look for it on the far wall.

Here is something many visitors miss. Islamic decoration usually avoids images of people and animals. Instead, artists use geometry, plant forms and Arabic calligraphy, which is decorative handwriting. Look closely at windows, arches and panels for repeating patterns. These designs can seem simple at first glance. Then your eye starts to follow one line, and it keeps going, folding back into itself. The idea is a pattern without a clear beginning or end.

A second surprise is the sound. Five times a day, the call to prayer can rise from this building over the town. In the Arabic language, it moves across streets where people also hear Caribbean music, Spanish conversation and perhaps Wayuunaiki, the language of the Wayuu, the Indigenous people of La Guajira. Few places in the Americas mix these sounds so naturally. If you are here at the right moment, stand still and listen to how the town absorbs it.

The mosque is more than a prayer hall. For the community, it is also a centre of education and gathering. Children learn Arabic and religious studies here or in schools linked to the community. Families meet during Ramadan, the month of fasting, when meals after sunset bring people together. Religious holidays fill the area with visitors in their best clothes. On an ordinary weekday, though, the place can feel calm, almost quiet, compared with the markets a few streets away.

If you would like to go inside, ask politely at the entrance. Visits are often possible outside prayer times, but permission is up to the people who care for the building. Dress modestly. Cover your shoulders and knees, and women may be asked to cover their hair. You will remove your shoes before entering the prayer area. Speak softly, and always ask before taking photographs of people at prayer.

Step back now and think about the town around you. Maicao has had a complicated reputation over the years, linked to border trade and its ups and downs. Yet this building tells a different part of the story. It shows how migrants built a life, kept their faith, and also became Colombian. Many families here have grown up with both arepas and kibbeh, the meat and bulgur dish of the Levant, on the same table.

Before you leave, find a little shade and look at the minaret again. Watch how the light changes on the white walls as clouds pass, or as the afternoon sun turns warmer. Listen to the street — the engines, the voices, the shop shutters. Then, as you walk away, notice how many small details in Maicao carry traces of this community. A sign in Arabic script. A bakery window. A greeting. The mosque is the largest sign, but it is not the only one.
