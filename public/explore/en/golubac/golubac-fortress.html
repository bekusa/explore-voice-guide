The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the walls, the Danube moves wide and slow, and the wind comes down the river carrying the smell of wet stone and reeds. Under your feet the path is uneven, worn rock and old mortar. Above you, grey towers climb the cliff one after another, as if the fortress is walking up the hill. This is Golubac, the gate to the Iron Gates gorge.

The position explains almost everything about this place. Here, on the Serbian bank, the Danube begins to narrow. It enters the long gorge that locals call Đerdap, where the river squeezes between the Carpathian and Balkan mountains. Whoever held this rock could watch every boat that came upstream or down. For centuries, that meant controlling trade, soldiers, and the border between empires.

Nobody knows exactly who laid the first stones. Historians generally place the fortress in the fourteenth century, and the first written mention usually cited dates from 1335, when it appears in Hungarian hands. Some scholars think an older structure stood here before that. What is clear is that the fortress grew in stages. Builders added towers and walls as weapons changed and as new owners arrived.

And there were many owners. Over the following generations, Golubac passed between the Kingdom of Hungary, the Serbian rulers, and the Ottoman Empire. It changed hands again and again, through sieges, treaties, and sales. In the fifteenth century the Ottomans took firm control, and they held the fortress for long periods afterwards. Each power left its mark, and you can see it in the stonework.

Look at the towers as you climb. Some are square and older in style. Others have rounded or many-sided outer faces. That change matters. When cannons arrived, flat walls became weak targets, and curved surfaces helped turn the shot aside. Ottoman builders also widened openings for guns. So the fortress you see is not one design. It is a record of military ideas laid side by side.

One of the most famous episodes happened here in 1428. Sigismund of Luxembourg, King of Hungary, laid siege to Golubac, and the siege failed. Among his knights was a Polish warrior known as Zawisza the Black, famous across Europe for his skill and his honour. He died near these walls. Polish tradition says he refused to flee and leave his men behind. That part is legend, but his memory is real, and Polish visitors still come here because of him.

Now turn toward the water and look for a rock rising out of the river. Locals call it Baba Kaj. According to one popular tale, a jealous Ottoman commander left a disobedient woman on this rock to die, and the name roughly means a call for the woman to repent. Another story links the fortress name to doves, because golub means dove in Serbian. Treat both as folklore. They tell you more about how people remember this place than about its documented past.

Here is something many visitors miss. The river you see today is higher than the river the builders knew. In the twentieth century, the large Iron Gate hydroelectric dam was built downstream, shared by Serbia and Romania. The water rose and covered the base of the lowest walls and the old shore below them. Part of the fortress now sits in the Danube. The scene looks timeless, but its waterline is quite modern.

There is a second surprise, and it concerns the road. For many years, the main road along the river ran straight through the fortress, under its gate and between its walls. Cars and trucks passed through a medieval stronghold every day. During a major restoration in the 2010s, a tunnel was built to carry traffic around the site. The walls finally became quiet again, and the fortress opened to visitors as a protected monument.

That restoration also changed what you can do here. Paths, stairs, and walkways now lead through the courtyards and up toward the higher towers. The upper sections are steep, and in places the climb is narrow. Take your time. With every level, the view opens a little further down the river, and you begin to understand why soldiers once stood exactly where you are standing.

From the higher walls, look across the water. The far bank is Romania. On a clear day you can follow the green hills on both sides as they close in around the river. This entire stretch belongs to Đerdap National Park, a protected area of forest, cliffs, and gorge. Birds of prey often circle above the slopes. Boats move slowly below, much smaller than you expect.

The light here changes the fortress through the day. In the morning, mist often lies on the Danube and the towers appear grey and soft. Later, the sun warms the stone to shades of brown and gold. Close up, notice the mix of materials: rough local rock, layers of mortar, and newer stone added during restoration. The new and old pieces sit together, and with a careful eye you can tell them apart.

It is worth thinking about the people who lived inside these walls. Soldiers kept watch through long winters, when wind from the gorge could be bitter. They depended on supplies brought up from the river. They waited for sieges that might come in any season. Today the same walls hold visitors with cameras, families on the stairs, and the sound of water far below.

Before you leave, pause at a low point near the river and look up once more. Count the towers rising up the hill toward the top of the cliff. Listen to the wind and the slow movement of the Danube. Kings, sultans, and knights fought for this rock. Now it is quiet, and the river keeps running east into the gorge, just as it did before the first stone was laid.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep and uneven stone stairs.
Climb the upper towers slowly; some passages are narrow.
Come early morning for mist and softer light on the river.
Bring water and sun protection for the exposed upper walls.
Combine your visit with a drive through Đerdap National Park.
