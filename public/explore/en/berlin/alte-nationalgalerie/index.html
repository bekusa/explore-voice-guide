The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the trams hum along the streets of Mitte. In front of you, the city goes quiet. A tall flight of stone stairs rises toward a building shaped like an ancient temple. It stands on a high base, set apart from the ground around it. Columns frame its front. The stone is pale and warm in the light. This is the Alte Nationalgalerie, the Old National Gallery, on Berlin's Museum Island.

The island is a narrow strip of land in the river Spree. Five great museums stand here, side by side, with water on both sides. Together they form a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Most visitors rush toward the ancient treasures in the larger museums nearby. This gallery is different. It holds paintings and sculpture from the nineteenth century, the century in which modern Germany was formed. The building is part of that story too.

The idea came from a king. Friedrich Wilhelm the Fourth of Prussia loved architecture, and he made sketches of his own. He imagined Museum Island as a quiet place for art and learning, apart from daily life. The architect Friedrich August Stüler turned the royal ideas into a real design. Stüler died before the work was finished. Another architect, Johann Heinrich Strack, completed the building, and it opened to the public in 1876.

Now look at the shape. It follows a classical temple, the kind the ancient Greeks and Romans built for their gods. Here, the god is art itself. That was a bold statement for its time. On the front, above the columns, a gilded inscription carries a short dedication in German. It translates as "To German Art", followed by the year 1871 in Roman numerals. That was the year Germany became one unified empire.

Here is a detail many people miss. The gallery did not begin with a king's collection. It began with a gift from a private citizen. In 1861, a Berlin banker and consul named Joachim Heinrich Wilhelm Wagener left his collection of paintings to the Prussian state. He asked that it become the core of a national gallery. One wealthy art lover's will helped give this building its reason to exist.

On the staircase, before you go inside, you meet the king himself. A bronze statue shows Friedrich Wilhelm the Fourth on horseback. He rides high above the steps and faces out toward the city. It is a little strange, if you think about it. The man who dreamed up the temple now stands guard at its door, forever on his way in, but never arriving.

Inside, the mood changes. The rooms are calm, with soft light and deep colours on the walls. The building has three main floors of galleries. You can move slowly from one century's idea of beauty to the next. The visit feels less like a march through a large museum. It feels more like a series of quiet rooms, each with its own conversation.

For many visitors, the heart of the collection is Caspar David Friedrich. He was a painter of the German Romantic movement. Romantic artists of that time looked to nature for strong feelings, mystery, and a sense of the spiritual. Look for The Monk by the Sea. A tiny figure stands on a shore under a huge, empty sky. There is almost nothing else in the picture. In its day, that emptiness shocked people. Today it still feels modern.

Nearby hangs another Friedrich painting, The Abbey in the Oakwood. It shows a ruined church among bare winter trees, with a funeral procession in the mist. The two paintings were made as a pair. Here is the surprise. The young future king of Prussia bought them both after seeing them in an exhibition. A teenage prince's purchase brought them into the royal collection, and that is part of why they are here today.

Then comes Adolph Menzel, a very different artist. He was small in height, sharp in eye, and endlessly curious. His painting The Iron Rolling Mill shows workers inside a factory, lit by glowing hot metal. Smoke, sweat, and noise fill the canvas. This was the new industrial Germany, painted with the seriousness usually given to kings and battles. Look closely, and you can find workers eating and washing during a break.

The gallery also holds a group of French Impressionist paintings, including works by Édouard Manet, Claude Monet, and Pierre-Auguste Renoir. In the years around 1900, the director Hugo von Tschudi bought French modern art for the collection. Not everyone approved. The emperor, Wilhelm the Second, disliked this new style, and the conflict became famous. Tschudi eventually left Berlin. Yet the paintings he chose stayed, and Berlin became one of the first public museums to collect them.

Look for sculpture too. One of the most loved pieces is a marble group by Johann Gottfried Schadow. It shows two young princesses, Luise and Friederike, standing close together in light, flowing dresses. One rests an arm on her sister's shoulder. The marble seems soft, almost warm. During the Second World War, the gallery was badly damaged, like much of Museum Island. It was repaired in stages, and after a major restoration it reopened in 2001.

When you leave, pause at the top of the stairs. Turn and look out. Colonnades run along the open garden beside you, and the domes and towers of Mitte rise beyond the trees. The river is close, though you may only hear it. Take a breath before you join the city again. For a short time, you stood inside one century's dream of what art could mean... and that dream still has its doors open.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with Caspar David Friedrich, then move to Menzel.
Walk the colonnade garden before or after your visit.
Consider a combined Museum Island ticket for several museums.
Visit early in the day for quieter gallery rooms.
Allow at least ninety minutes for the main collection.
