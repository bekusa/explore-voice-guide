The guide

The story

Listen first to the water. The Landwehr Canal runs slowly past this corner of Tiergarten, and the traffic of Klingelhöferstraße hums behind you. Then lift your eyes to the roofline. It is a row of white curves, rising and falling like a line of ship sails or a set of rounded saw teeth. Once you have seen that silhouette, you will not mistake this building for anything else in Berlin.

This is the Bauhaus Archive, also known as the Museum of Design. It holds what is widely described as the largest collection in the world on the history of the Bauhaus. That was the design school that changed how the twentieth century made chairs, lamps, typefaces, and houses. Everything here sits in a building drawn by the man who founded that school, Walter Gropius.

A quick word on the school itself. The Bauhaus opened in Weimar in 1919, just after the First World War. Gropius wanted to bring artists and craftsmen together under one roof. Students learned weaving, metalwork, carpentry, pottery, and printing, and they learned to design for industry rather than against it. The idea was simple to say and hard to do: good design should be useful, honest about its materials, and available to ordinary people.

The school never stayed still for long. Political pressure in Weimar pushed it to Dessau in 1925, where Gropius built its famous glass-walled home. Later directors were the architect Hannes Meyer and then Ludwig Mies van der Rohe. In 1932 the school moved here, to Berlin, as a small private institution. In 1933, under pressure from the new Nazi regime, its leaders closed it. The Bauhaus lasted only fourteen years.

Here is the first surprise. This archive did not begin in Berlin at all. It was founded in 1960 in Darmstadt, in West Germany, by the art historian Hans Maria Wingler. Gropius, then living in the United States, designed a building for it on a sloping site in that city. The plan in Darmstadt was never built. In the early 1970s the archive moved to West Berlin, and the design came with it.

That move created a puzzle. Gropius had drawn the building for a hillside, but this canal-side plot is flat. He died in 1969, before the building was finished. His long-time partner Alexander Cvijanovic adapted the plans for the new site, and the museum opened at the end of the 1970s. So the building you see is Gropius's idea, reshaped by other hands for ground he never designed it for.

Look again at those curved roof shapes. They are not just decoration. Each curve rises toward one side, where a tall band of glass lets in daylight from above. Architects call this a shed roof, or a north-light roof, and it is common in factories. It gives even, soft light without harsh sun on the exhibits. For a school that admired the factory, it is a fitting choice.

Now follow the lines of the walls. The building is pale and smooth, with long horizontal bands and very little ornament. There are no carved columns and no grand staircase at the front. Instead, a long ramp once carried visitors up into the complex, a trace of the original hillside plan. The whole composition is low, calm, and spread out. It feels more like a careful arrangement of boxes than a monument.

Inside, the collection covers the full range of Bauhaus work. There are pieces of furniture, metal objects, ceramics, textiles, and graphic design. There are paintings and drawings by teachers and students, alongside thousands of photographs. There are also letters, plans, and school documents. These papers are the second surprise for many visitors: much of the real treasure here is on paper, in the record of how ideas were taught and tested.

You should know that this site has been changing. For several years the museum has been closed for renovation and a major extension. The original Gropius building is a protected monument, so the work keeps its shape. Next to it, a new building has been rising, designed to give the collection far more room. Depending on when you visit, you may find open galleries, a construction fence, or something in between.

If the doors are closed, the walk around the outside is still worth your time. From the canal path, the roofline is clearest against the sky. From the street, you can see how the white volumes step back from the traffic. Notice how the old building and the new one sit side by side. One is low and curved. The other is taller and more transparent. Together they show two generations of thinking about the same school.

Take a moment to place this building in its city. Berlin lost the Bauhaus in 1933, and many of its teachers left Germany for good. Gropius and Mies van der Rohe both went to the United States. Their ideas spread across the world from there. So this archive is, in a quiet way, a homecoming. The school's papers and objects came back to the city where it ended.

Before you move on, stand still for a few breaths. Hear the water in the canal again, and watch the light move across those white curves. Look at the plain walls, the simple glass, the lack of anything extra. Then, as you walk away through Tiergarten, notice the chairs, lamps, and lettering around you. Many of them owe something to the people who studied here, a century ago, in a school that lasted only fourteen years.
