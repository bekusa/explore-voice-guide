The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the wide stone square and listen. Footsteps echo across the paving. Somewhere a tour group laughs. Above you, a pale drum of columns rises toward a green copper dome, and on its very top a gilded figure catches the light. This is the Neue Kirche, the New Church, on Gendarmenmarkt. Most people in Berlin know it by another name: the Deutscher Dom, the German Dome.

Here is the first surprise. Despite the name, this building was never a cathedral. In German, the word Dom usually means cathedral. But here it comes from the French word dôme, which simply means dome. So the Deutscher Dom is not a German cathedral at all. It is a church with a German-speaking congregation and a very large dome. Across the square, its twin carries the same kind of name: the Französischer Dom, the French Dome.

To understand why two domed towers face each other here, look back to the early eighteenth century. Berlin was growing fast. A planned district called Friedrichstadt was laid out on a grid, and this square sat near its heart. Many of the new residents were Huguenots, French Protestants who had fled persecution in France and found refuge in Brandenburg-Prussia. They brought crafts, trades and their own church life. A French church rose on one side of the square for them.

On this side, a second church was built for the German-speaking Protestant congregations of the district. It was finished in the early 1700s. The architect usually named is Martin Grünberg. The original church was modest compared with what you see today. Its floor plan was unusual — a five-sided shape, a pentagon, rather than a long nave. It was a practical building for preaching, not a grand statement. The grand statement came later.

That statement belongs to Frederick the Great, the Prussian king who loved architecture and Italian models. In the 1780s he ordered two tall domed towers to be added, one beside each church. Many historians link his idea to the Piazza del Popolo in Rome, where two matching churches frame the entrance to the city. The towers were designed by Carl von Gontard. Notice something important. The towers are not really part of the churches. They were added as showpieces, to give the square a skyline.

And now the second surprise. During construction, the tower on this side collapsed. Building such a heavy drum and dome on that ground was harder than expected. Work had to start again, and the tower was eventually completed in the mid-1780s. So the elegant structure in front of you is, in a sense, a second attempt. When you look up at those columns, you are looking at a lesson in engineering learned the hard way.

Let your eyes travel slowly upward. At ground level there are classical porticoes, with columns and triangular pediments, like the front of a temple. Above them sits the round drum, ringed with more columns. Then comes the dome itself, and finally the statue at the top. That figure is usually described as an allegory of Virtue. The sculptural decoration around the building shows biblical and allegorical scenes. It is worth walking all the way around to see how each side differs.

The square has seen moments of great drama. In March 1848, revolution broke out in Berlin. Citizens fought soldiers in the streets, and many people died. Afterwards, the coffins of those killed were laid out on the steps of this church, and crowds came to mourn. For a moment, this quiet place of worship became the center of a political awakening. Keep that image in mind. It connects to what the building holds today.

The twentieth century was far harsher. During the Second World War, bombing set the church on fire in 1943, and it burned out almost completely. For decades it stood as a ruin in East Berlin. Only in the 1980s did reconstruction begin, and the work was completed in the 1990s, after German reunification. The outside was rebuilt in its historic form. The inside, however, was not returned to a church.

Instead, the building now houses a permanent exhibition run by the German Bundestag, the national parliament. It tells the story of parliamentary democracy in Germany — its hopes, its failures and its long road back after dictatorship. So the place where the dead of 1848 lay in their coffins now explains how Germans finally built a lasting democracy. Few buildings in Berlin carry such a clear line between past and present.

Inside, the atmosphere changes. The noise of the square fades. Modern staircases and galleries climb through the old shell, and daylight falls from above. You move upward floor by floor, through documents, photographs and displays. It feels more like walking through a book than a church. Take your time. Even a short visit gives you a sense of how much effort democracy has demanded here.

When you step back outside, look at the whole square. The German Dome stands on one side and the French Dome on the other. Between them rises the Konzerthaus, the concert hall designed by Karl Friedrich Schinkel, the great Prussian architect of the nineteenth century. Together these three buildings form one of the most balanced public spaces in Europe. Many Berliners consider this square the most beautiful in the city, and it is easy to understand why.

Before you leave, find a spot where you can see both towers at once. Notice how alike they are, and yet how they belong to different stories — one built for refugees, one for local congregations, both crowned by a king's ambition. Listen again to the footsteps on the stone. Look up one last time at the figure of Virtue on the dome. Then walk on slowly, and let the square close behind you.
