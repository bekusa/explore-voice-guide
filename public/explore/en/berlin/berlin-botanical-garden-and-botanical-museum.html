The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your feet, a blackbird calls from a hedge, and somewhere ahead water drips from glass. The air smells of cut grass and damp soil. City traffic fades quickly here. Within a few steps, the noise of Berlin drops away, and the garden takes over. You are now standing in one of the largest botanical gardens in the world. It sits in the leafy southwest of the city, in the district of Steglitz-Zehlendorf.

The garden covers around forty hectares. That is enough ground for a full afternoon of walking, and still you will not see everything. Its collection is usually counted at around twenty thousand plant species. Some come from mountain slopes, some from deserts, and some from rainforests. Today the garden belongs to the Free University of Berlin. That makes it a working scientific institution as well as a place to relax. Many of the plants you pass are also living research material.

The story begins long before this site existed. In the late seventeenth century, the rulers of Brandenburg kept a kitchen and pleasure garden in what was then countryside outside Berlin. Over time, it grew into a scientific garden. By the late nineteenth century, the city had grown around it, and the old site in Schöneberg became too small and too polluted. That original ground still survives today as a park, called Heinrich von Kleist Park. So the decision was made to move everything here, to Dahlem.

The move took many years, around the turn of the twentieth century. It was guided by the botanist Adolf Engler. Engler was one of the leading plant scientists of his time. He was famous for building a system to classify the plant kingdom. His ideas shaped this landscape directly. He wanted visitors to understand how plants are related, and where on Earth they grow. So the garden is not only beautiful. It is also a kind of map that you can walk through.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. A large part of the grounds is arranged by geography. As you follow the paths, you move from one region of the world to another. You might pass plants from the Alps, then the Caucasus, then the mountains of East Asia or North America. Rock and soil are shaped to match each home landscape. In just a few minutes of walking, you cross continents. Watch how the colours and leaf shapes change as you go.

Another area is arranged by family relationships between plants. Engler's system is visible here in living form. Beds are grouped so that related species grow side by side. A rose may sit close to an apple or a strawberry, which surprises many people. All of them belong to the same broad family. The large arboretum, a collection of trees and shrubs, follows similar thinking. In autumn, these trees turn the slopes red, copper, and gold.

Now look for the glass. The greenhouse complex is the heart of the garden, and the Great Tropical Greenhouse is its centrepiece. It was completed in the early twentieth century. At that time it was one of the largest free-standing glasshouses anywhere. Its steel frame rises in a great curve, with no columns in the main hall to block the view. Over the years it needed serious repair. A major restoration in the early twenty-first century brought the structure back and improved its energy use.

Step inside, and the air changes at once. It is warm, heavy, and humid. Your glasses may fog for a moment. The smell is green and earthy, like rain on leaves. Above you, giant bamboo climbs toward the roof. Palms spread their fronds, and vines hang over the paths. The light comes down soft and filtered through the glass. In winter, this hall feels like a small holiday from Berlin's grey skies.

The tropical hall is only one of many glasshouses. Others hold cacti and succulents from dry regions, carnivorous plants, orchids, and water plants. Each house keeps its own climate. Moving between them is a little like changing countries again. Look closely at the carnivorous plants. Some have sticky hairs, and others have small traps shaped like jugs. Children often love this section most. Many adults stop here for longer than they expected, too.

Now for the second surprise. Near the garden stands the Botanical Museum. It is the only museum of its kind in Central Europe. It explains the world of plants through models, preserved specimens, and displays about how humans use plants. Behind the scenes is a vast herbarium, which is a library of dried and pressed plants. Researchers from around the world use it. It is one of the largest collections of its kind.

That collection carries a painful history. During the Second World War, bombing caused a fire that destroyed a large part of the herbarium and museum. Millions of specimens were lost, including many important reference plants for science. After the war, botanists slowly rebuilt the collection through exchange and new expeditions. When you see a pressed leaf in a glass case, remember that each one is a record. It shows a plant that grew in a certain place at a certain time.

The garden changes with the seasons, and each visit feels different. In spring, bulbs and flowering shrubs bring colour to the slopes. In summer, the ponds and wetland beds are full of life, with dragonflies over the water. Autumn belongs to the trees. In winter, the glasshouses become a warm shelter. Near the main entrance, a formal Italian-style garden offers a calmer, more structured space. Its neat beds and terraces contrast with the wilder regions beyond.

Before you leave, find a bench and stay still for a minute. Listen to the leaves moving above you. Notice the light on the glass of the greenhouses, and the many shades of green around you. Every plant here was chosen, labelled, and cared for, often across generations of gardeners. As you walk back toward the city streets, look again at the trees along the pavement. You may now recognise a few of them as old friends from the garden.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan at least three hours to see the main areas.
Visit the glasshouses on cold or rainy days.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel paths.
Come in autumn for the colours of the arboretum.
Combine your garden walk with the Botanical Museum.
