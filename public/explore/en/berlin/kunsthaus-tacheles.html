The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of Oranienburger Strasse for a moment and look up. The facade in front of you is heavy, grey, and deliberate, with tall windows and a deep entrance arch. Trams rattle past behind you. Around the old building, new glass and pale stone rise on every side. The contrast is the first thing most people notice here. One old survivor, wrapped in a brand-new city quarter.

This is the building Berliners still call the Tacheles. Its story runs through almost every chapter of the city in the twentieth century. It began as a temple of shopping. It became an office block, a ruin, and a squat. Then it became one of the best-known art houses in Europe, and finally a museum. Few buildings in Berlin have changed their identity so many times and still kept their walls.

The building went up in the first decade of the twentieth century as a shopping arcade. It was called the Friedrichstrassenpassage, because it linked Friedrichstrasse with the street where you are standing now. The idea came from Paris and other big cities. Covered passages lined with shops let customers browse in comfort, sheltered from rain and from horse-drawn traffic. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The structure used reinforced concrete, which was still a fairly modern technique in Berlin at the time.

The arcade was not a lasting commercial success. Within a few years the shops struggled, and the building passed through several owners. Later it was used for offices and technical exhibitions. During the Nazi period, government and party organisations used parts of it. The Second World War left the building badly damaged, like much of central Berlin. After the war, this district lay in the Soviet sector, and later in East Berlin.

For decades, the damaged structure stood half-used and half-forgotten. East German authorities eventually decided to clear it. Around 1980, a large part of the old arcade was demolished. The section you see today was also meant to go. Then history moved faster than the bulldozers. In November 1989 the Berlin Wall fell, and suddenly no one was quite sure who controlled empty buildings like this one.

In early 1990, a group of artists moved in. They occupied the ruin before the demolition could be finished. They called it the Tacheles. The word comes from Yiddish, and it means plain, direct speech. In German, to talk tacheles is to speak frankly, with no polite wrapping. The name fit the mood perfectly. This was a place for saying exactly what you thought, in paint, steel, sound, and performance.

Through the 1990s and 2000s, the Tacheles became a magnet. Artists from many countries worked in studios inside. The walls filled with graffiti, layer on layer, in every colour. Behind the building, a rough yard filled with metal sculptures, old car parts, and fire. There were concerts, a cinema, bars, and late-night parties. For many visitors, this was the image of post-Wall Berlin. It was raw, cheap, chaotic, and open to anyone with an idea.

Here is the second surprise. The Tacheles was never as untouchable as it looked. The land under it was valuable, right in the centre of a reunified capital. Ownership changed hands, debts piled up, and legal battles dragged on for years. Many of the artists accepted payments to leave. In 2012 the last remaining tenants were cleared out. For many longtime Berliners, the closing felt like the end of an era for the whole city.

After that, the building stood empty while a large redevelopment took shape around it. The new quarter is known as Am Tacheles. The Swiss architecture firm Herzog and de Meuron drew up the master plan for the area. New apartment and office buildings now fill the land where the old arcade and the sculpture yard once stood. The historic structure was protected as a listed monument, which is why it survived inside the new development.

The old building itself was carefully restored. Since 2023, it has been home to Fotografiska Berlin, a branch of a photography museum that started in Stockholm. The house that once hosted squatters now hosts exhibitions of contemporary photography, with a restaurant and a bar inside. Some people see this as a sad loss of the old spirit. Others see a fair way to keep an important building alive and open to the public.

Take a moment to look at the neighbourhood around you. This area is the Spandauer Vorstadt, one of the oldest parts of Berlin outside the medieval core. A short walk east along Oranienburger Strasse brings you to the New Synagogue, with its golden dome. The streets nearby are full of courtyards, small galleries, and old facades. Before the war, this was an important centre of Jewish life in Berlin. That history gives the Yiddish name of the art house an extra layer of meaning.

If you go inside, notice how the restoration has kept the scale of the old building. The ceilings are high, and the rooms feel solid and heavy, built for a different age of commerce. Outside, compare the old stonework with the clean new facades next door. The neighbours are polite and modern. The Tacheles still looks like a building that has argued with its century and survived the argument.

As you stand here, try to picture the layers at once. Shoppers in long coats under a covered arcade. Office workers. Ruins after the war. Then music, smoke, and spray paint in the early 1990s. Now quiet galleries and people with coffee cups. All of it happened inside the same walls. The building has held each version of Berlin for a while, and then let it go.

Before you walk on, look once more at the entrance arch and the long grey front. Listen to the trams on Oranienburger Strasse, the voices drifting from nearby cafes, the footsteps on the pavement. The artists are gone, but the name is still here, and it still means plain speech. Perhaps that is the right way to leave this place... looking honestly at a city that keeps rebuilding itself, one building at a time.

Practical tips

Book Fotografiska tickets ahead on busy weekends.

Explore the nearby courtyards of the Spandauer Vorstadt on foot.
Visit in the evening when the museum bar is livelier.
