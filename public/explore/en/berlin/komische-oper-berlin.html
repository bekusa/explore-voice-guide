The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on Behrenstraße and look at the building in front of you. The facade is calm and sober, with clean lines and a lot of glass. Traffic hums from Friedrichstraße nearby. A few steps to the north, Unter den Linden runs towards the Brandenburg Gate. Nothing on this street front suggests celebration. And that is the first secret of the Komische Oper. The outside and the inside belong to two very different centuries.

Behind that modern front sits a theatre auditorium from the late nineteenth century. Think of deep red seats, curved balconies, and gilded plaster ornament that catches the light. It is neo-Baroque, which means a late revival of the swirling, decorative Baroque style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Many first-time visitors walk in expecting something as plain as the facade. Then they reach their seats... and they look up. The contrast is part of the experience.

The story begins in 1892. This site opened as the Theater Unter den Linden, an entertainment house for a growing imperial capital. It was designed by Fellner and Helmer, a Viennese architectural firm. They built dozens of theatres across Central Europe, from small spa towns to major cities. Their halls were comfortable, practical, and full of decoration. Audiences here came for operetta, ballet, and light musical shows rather than serious tragedy.

Around the turn of the twentieth century, the house became known as the Metropol-Theater. Under that name it grew into one of Berlin's great homes of revue and operetta. Big productions, lavish costumes, and catchy songs filled the stage. In the 1920s, Berlin operetta was sharp, witty, and modern. Many of the composers, writers, and performers in that world were Jewish. After the Nazis came to power in 1933, many of them were forced out, and much of their music disappeared from German stages.

The Second World War brought heavy bombing to this part of Berlin. Much of the surrounding district was badly damaged. The theatre was hit too — but the historic auditorium largely survived. That is a rare piece of luck in central Berlin. When you sit inside today, you are sitting in a room that came through the war. Very few theatre interiors from this period in Mitte can say the same.

In 1947, the Austrian director Walter Felsenstein founded the Komische Oper here, in the Soviet sector of the divided city. The name can confuse visitors. It does not mean only funny opera. It points to a tradition that the French call opéra comique, a form of opera with spoken dialogue and a strong focus on story. Felsenstein wanted opera to feel like real theatre. For many years, works here were sung in German, so local audiences could follow every word.

Felsenstein's approach became known as music theatre. Singers were expected to act, not simply stand and sing. Rehearsals could run for many weeks, sometimes much longer than at other houses. Every gesture had to make sense in the drama. Some critics found this demanding and slow. But his ideas travelled. Directors from his circle went on to work across Europe, and his method shaped how many opera houses think about staging today.

Now, back to that facade. In the 1960s, during the years of the German Democratic Republic, the building received the modern exterior you see now. The old ornamented front was gone. The new design matched the sober taste of East Berlin at that time. And remember where you are standing. The Berlin Wall ran only a short walk to the west. For decades, this opera house sat close to one of the most tense borders in the world.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Komische Oper installed small translation screens in the backs of the seats. During a performance, you can choose a language and read the text quietly in front of you. The options have included German, English, French, and Turkish. That last choice reflects the large Turkish community in Berlin. It is a simple idea, but it changed who felt welcome in the house.

From 2012 to 2022, the Australian director Barrie Kosky led the company. He brought back the spirit of the old Metropol-Theater. Kosky staged forgotten Weimar-era operettas, many by Jewish composers whose work had been banned. The shows were bold, glamorous, funny, and sometimes painful. Music that had been silenced on this very site returned to its stage. For many Berliners, that felt like a quiet act of repair.

If you see scaffolding or construction fences today, there is a reason. In 2023, the building closed for a major renovation. The work aims to modernise the technical systems and the parts of the building around the historic auditorium. During this period, the company moved its performances to the Schiller Theater in Charlottenburg, in the west of the city. So the music continues — just in a different neighbourhood for now.

Look around the streets nearby. The building stretches back along Glinkastraße, a side street that runs north to Unter den Linden. The large Russian embassy stands on the boulevard close by. Further east along Unter den Linden is the State Opera, the grand royal house of the city. Berlin has long had several opera companies at once. The Komische Oper has always been the one that feels a little closer to the street.

So take one last look at the facade on Behrenstraße. The glass reflects the passing buses and the grey Berlin sky. Behind it, the old red-and-gold room waits for its audiences to return. As you walk away towards Unter den Linden, listen to the city around you. Operetta, war, division, and revival all happened within a few hundred metres of where you stand right now.
