The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the rustle of the Tiergarten trees, there is a faint, long tone of a violin. It does not build into a melody. It holds, and it fades, and it returns. In front of you lies a round pool of dark water, flat and still, framed by the trunks of old trees. The path under your feet turns from soft park ground to stone. The city is only a few steps away, but here the noise of Berlin drops back, and the water holds the sky.

This is the Memorial to the Sinti and Roma of Europe Murdered under National Socialism. It sits in the Tiergarten, Berlin's great central park, close to the Reichstag building, the seat of the German parliament. The location is deliberate. Germany placed this memorial within sight of the institution that now governs the country. The message is plain. The state takes responsibility for what an earlier German state did to these people.

Who were the victims? Sinti and Roma are related peoples whose ancestors came to Europe from northern India many centuries ago. The Sinti have lived in German-speaking lands for around six hundred years. Under the Nazi regime, both groups were classed as racially inferior. They were registered, measured, and photographed by so-called race scientists. Then they were stripped of their rights, deported, and murdered in ghettos, camps, and mass shootings across occupied Europe.

Historians still debate the exact numbers, because records are incomplete and many killings were never documented. The figure most often cited is up to 500,000 people. Many Roma use the Romani word Porajmos for this genocide, which means roughly the devouring. Others prefer Samudaripen, meaning the mass killing. Both words point to the same thing. Entire families and communities disappeared, and with them, much of a language, a music, and a way of life.

Here is the first fact many visitors miss. For decades after the war, this genocide was barely acknowledged. Survivors often met the same police officials who had once registered them. Courts and authorities treated their persecution as crime prevention rather than racial hatred. Germany formally recognised the killings as genocide only in 1982. Sinti and Roma activists fought hard for that recognition, including through public protests and hunger strikes.

The memorial itself took a long road. The German parliament supported the idea in the early 1990s. Then came years of disagreement about the site, the design, and above all the wording of the inscriptions. Even the name of the victim group was disputed. The memorial finally opened in October 2012, in the presence of the German chancellor and many survivors. Some of those survivors had waited most of their lives for this moment.

The design is the work of the Israeli artist Dani Karavan, known for large environmental works that use water, stone, and light. He chose not to build a tower or a statue. Instead, he made something low and quiet that you approach slowly. Look at the pool again. Its dark bottom makes the water act like a mirror. Lean over the edge, and you will see the trees, the clouds, and your own face.

In the centre of the pool sits a triangular stone. The shape is not decoration. In the Nazi concentration camps, prisoners wore coloured triangles of cloth sewn onto their uniforms, a code that marked the reason for their imprisonment. Sinti and Roma prisoners were forced to wear these badges too. Here, the triangle is turned into something else — a small platform that carries a single fresh flower.

And this is the second detail most people never notice. The flower is replaced every day. The stone can sink below the surface of the water, and it rises again with a new flower on it. The idea is simple and very human. Remembrance is not a single act carved once in stone. It is renewed, day after day, like someone visiting a grave and bringing fresh flowers.

Now walk slowly around the rim of the pool. Letters are set into the metal edge. They form a short poem called Auschwitz, written by the Italian Roma poet and musician Santino Spinelli. It is very brief, almost a list of words — a face, eyes, lips, silence, a broken heart, no breath, no tears. Read it once in a full circle. Notice how the poem has no beginning and no end on the round edge.

Around the pool, the stone paving holds more details. Many of the slabs carry the names of places where Sinti and Roma were persecuted and killed, from well-known camps to smaller sites in many countries. Some names will be familiar. Others may be completely new to you. That is part of the point. The genocide reached far beyond a few infamous locations and touched towns across the whole continent.

Near the entrance, tall glass panels carry a chronology of the persecution in German and English. Take a few minutes to read them. They trace the steps, one by one, from registration and exclusion to deportation and murder. The language is calm and factual. That calm makes it harder to read, not easier. This memorial does not shout. It asks you to stand, read, and think for yourself.

The memorial is also part of a wider landscape of remembrance in this district. Nearby stand the Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe and the memorial to the homosexual men and women persecuted under Nazism. Further along the park's edge is a site for the victims of the Nazi so-called euthanasia killings of disabled people. Walking between them, you see how Berlin chose to place its memory at the centre of the city, not at the margins.

Before you leave, stand still at the edge of the water one more time. Listen for the violin under the sound of the trees and the distant traffic. Look at the flower on its triangular stone, placed there by someone this morning. When you walk back out onto the path, the Reichstag dome will appear between the branches. Carry that view with you — the parliament, the quiet water, and the short space between them.
