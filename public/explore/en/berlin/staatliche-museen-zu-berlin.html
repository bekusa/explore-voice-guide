The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere nearby, the Spree is moving slowly past stone embankments. A tram bell rings across the bridge. Underfoot, the paving is worn smooth by millions of footsteps. If you are standing on Museum Island, you are surrounded by columns, domes and pale sandstone, all within a few minutes' walk. This is the heart of the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, the State Museums of Berlin. It is not a single building at all. It is a family of museums spread across the city, and it holds one of the largest public collections in Europe.

Here is the first surprise. Many visitors arrive looking for one museum called the Staatliche Museen. They find a whole network instead. The name covers more than a dozen museums, research institutes and archives. Some stand here on the island. Others are at the Kulturforum near Potsdamer Platz, in a former railway station, in the Humboldt Forum, and out in the leafy Dahlem and Charlottenburg districts. Together they hold art and objects that span thousands of years, from early Egyptian tombs to twentieth-century painting and contemporary installation.

The story begins with the Prussian kings. For generations, their art and curiosities sat in royal palaces, seen only by the court and its guests. In the early nineteenth century, ideas about education and public culture were changing across Europe. The Prussian state decided that its treasures should belong to its citizens. The result was a building just to your north, on the edge of the open lawn called the Lustgarten. Its name is the Altes Museum, which simply means the Old Museum.

The Altes Museum opened in 1830. Its architect was Karl Friedrich Schinkel, the leading builder of Prussian Berlin. Look at the long row of Ionic columns across the front. Ionic columns are the slender Greek style with scroll-shaped tops. Schinkel wanted the building to feel like an ancient temple of learning. Inside, a domed rotunda takes its form from the Pantheon in Rome. It was one of the first purpose-built public museums in Germany. Today it shows Greek and Roman antiquities.

After Schinkel, the island slowly filled with more museums through the rest of the nineteenth century and into the early twentieth. The Neues Museum, or New Museum, came next. Then the Alte Nationalgalerie, raised on a high base like a temple above a flight of steps. At the northern tip, where the river divides, stands the Bode-Museum with its rounded dome. It opened in 1904 under a different name, the Kaiser-Friedrich-Museum. The whole ensemble became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999.

Now for the twentieth century, which was hard on this place. Bombing in the Second World War damaged nearly every building on the island. Some collections were moved to safety, some were lost, and some were taken away by occupying forces. Then the city itself split in two. The island lay in East Berlin. Many objects that had been evacuated ended up in the West. For decades, the collections lived as two separate halves, in two separate states.

In West Berlin, a new foundation took charge of the former Prussian collections. It is called the Prussian Cultural Heritage Foundation, and it still oversees the State Museums today. After German reunification in 1990, the long work of bringing the halves back together began. Objects returned to their old homes. Departments merged. Buildings that had stood as ruins for decades finally received attention. Much of what you see around you is the result of that patient reunion, which continued well into the twenty-first century.

The clearest example is the Neues Museum. For years after the war it stood as a shell, open to the weather. The British architect David Chipperfield led its restoration, and it reopened in 2009. Here is the second surprise. He did not hide the damage. Inside, new walls of pale brick meet old painted plaster. Bullet marks and scorched surfaces remain visible beside the new work. The building shows its own history openly, scars and all.

The Neues Museum is also home to its most famous resident. The painted limestone bust of Nefertiti, an Egyptian queen who lived more than three thousand years ago, was found in 1912 at the ancient city of Amarna. A German expedition uncovered it there. She still keeps her calm gaze, her tall blue crown, and her single inlaid eye. The other eye socket is empty. Why it was never finished, or was lost, is still debated.

You may notice construction fences and cranes on the island. The Pergamonmuseum, best known for huge reconstructed ancient architecture, closed for a long renovation in 2023. Large parts of it will stay closed for several years. The work is part of a much larger master plan for the whole island. One step of that plan is already complete. The James-Simon-Galerie, a colonnaded entrance building also designed by Chipperfield, opened in 2019. It serves as a shared gateway and visitor centre.

That building carries a name worth knowing. James Simon was a Berlin businessman and collector in the decades around 1900. He gave many thousands of objects to the city's museums, and he funded excavations abroad. Because he was Jewish, his name was pushed out of public memory during the Nazi years. Naming the new entrance after him was a deliberate act of remembrance. Few visitors walking through it know the story behind the name.

If you have time beyond the island, the network rewards you. At the Kulturforum, the Gemäldegalerie holds European paintings from the Middle Ages to the eighteenth century. Next to it, the Neue Nationalgalerie is a glass and steel pavilion by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe. It opened in the late 1960s and reopened after restoration in 2021. Hamburger Bahnhof, a former railway terminus, now shows contemporary art. Each site feels completely different, yet all belong to the same family.

Before you go inside, take a slow look around. Notice how the buildings face each other across courtyards and colonnades, almost in conversation. Follow the river as it wraps around the island's edges. Watch the light move across the sandstone columns and the dark green dome of the cathedral beside the Lustgarten. People sit on the steps, students sketch, a street musician plays somewhere nearby. Kings once built this place for an idea of shared learning. Today, it belongs to everyone who walks across these stones.
