The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Trams rattle along Friedrichstrasse, bicycles hiss past, and then your eye catches the building. Its broad front is covered in coloured glass and patterned panels that shine softly, even on a grey afternoon. It does not look like an old theatre, and it does not look like a plain office block either. It sits between those two worlds, much like Berlin itself. You are standing outside the Friedrichstadt-Palast, one of the great revue theatres of Europe.

First, a word about what a revue is, because the idea is not familiar everywhere. A revue is a large stage show built from many separate scenes. There is music, dance, costume, acrobatics and spectacle, with no single serious plot to follow. Think of it as a sequence of bright, fast-moving pictures. Berlin loved this form in the early twentieth century, and this house carries that tradition forward today.

Here is the first surprise. The building in front of you is not the original Friedrichstadt-Palast. It opened in 1984, in what was then East Berlin, the capital of the German Democratic Republic. The first palace stood a short walk away, close to the river Spree. Many visitors assume the name and the building are the same age. They are not, and the older story is just as interesting as the new one.

That earlier building had a restless life. It began in the nineteenth century as a market hall. Later it became a circus, with a ring for horses and performers. Then, after the First World War, the director Max Reinhardt turned it into a vast theatre called the Grosses Schauspielhaus, which means the Great Playhouse. Reinhardt wanted huge, popular theatre that could hold enormous crowds, and this hall gave him the space.

The architect Hans Poelzig redesigned the interior for Reinhardt. His ceiling became famous. It was covered in hanging forms that looked like stalactites in a cave, the long stone shapes that grow down from cave roofs. Under the stage lights, the whole auditorium seemed to glow like the inside of a grotto. Photographs of that ceiling still appear in books about modern architecture, because nothing quite like it existed anywhere else.

After the Second World War, the old hall reopened and took the name Friedrichstadt-Palast. Friedrichstadt is the historic district around this street, laid out centuries ago as a planned extension of the city. Through the decades of divided Berlin, the palace became a place of glamour in the East. People came for feathers, sequins and long lines of dancers. For many East Germans, a ticket here was a special occasion, saved for birthdays and anniversaries.

But the old building had a hidden problem — the ground beneath it. Berlin sits on sandy, wet soil, and the river was very close. Over time, the foundations of the old hall became unsafe. The theatre closed around 1980, and the historic structure was later demolished. So the stalactite ceiling, the circus ring and Reinhardt's great hall are all gone. What survived was the name, the ensemble and the habit of spectacle.

The state then built this new palace on Friedrichstrasse. Look again at the facade. The decorated panels and the coloured glass give the long front a festive, slightly theatrical rhythm. Some people see echoes of the Art Deco style here, the geometric, decorative style of the 1920s and 1930s. It is a clear attempt to make a modern building feel like a pleasure palace, not only a functional box.

Now for the second surprise, which you cannot see from the pavement. Behind these walls is one of the largest theatre stages in the world. The stage machinery can raise and lower whole sections of the floor. The house was even built with a water basin in the stage area for aquatic scenes. The auditorium holds close to two thousand people, yet the designers aimed to keep every seat facing the action clearly.

The heart of any show here is the ballet line. Dozens of dancers stand shoulder to shoulder and kick in perfect time. This precision line, often called a kickline, is a signature of the house. When it works, the effect is almost mechanical, like one long, glittering machine. Dancers train for years to hold that timing, night after night, while wearing costumes that can be heavy and tall.

The palace also survived another great change. When the Berlin Wall fell in 1989 and Germany reunited, many East German institutions closed or lost their purpose. This one adapted. Today its shows draw audiences from across Germany and from abroad. Because the format relies on music, movement and image more than spoken words, visitors who speak little German can still follow and enjoy a performance.

Take a moment to look at the street around you. Friedrichstrasse runs north to south through Mitte, the central district of Berlin. To the south, toward the railway station and the river, you will find older theatres and the bridges over the Spree. Around here, new apartment blocks, cafes and offices mix with buildings from the East German years. The area has changed constantly, and the palace is one of its steadier landmarks.

In the evening, the scene shifts. The facade lights up, and people gather at the entrance in their best clothes, or in jeans and trainers. Couples, families and groups of friends wait together. You may hear several languages in a single queue. That mix feels right for a theatre that has worked through empire, republic, division and reunion, and still keeps the doors open for a night of colour.

So before you walk on, stand back once more. Look at the glass, the repeated panels and the long, low line of the building against the sky. Behind it, a stage crew may be testing lights, and dancers may be warming up. And somewhere near the river, under newer streets, lies the ground where the old palace once stood. Two buildings, one name... and a city that still loves a show.
