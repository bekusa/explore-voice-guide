The guide

The story

Gravel crunches softly underfoot as you walk in from the street. The noise of Berlin fades behind you. Ahead stands a plain brick wall, a quiet lawn, and a low building with tall windows. Birds move in the trees. There is very little here to look at, and that is the point. This small courtyard on the northern edge of Charlottenburg is one of the most important places of remembrance in Germany. It is the Plötzensee Memorial, built on the site where the Nazi regime executed thousands of people.

The prison itself is older than that dark chapter. It was built in the late nineteenth century, when Berlin was growing fast and needed a large modern jail on what was then the edge of the city. The name comes from the Plötzensee, a small lake just to the north. For decades it was an ordinary prison, a grid of brick cell blocks and workshops. Parts of the wider complex still serve as a prison today, which is why the memorial sits in its own fenced corner, separate from the active buildings.

Everything changed after 1933, when Adolf Hitler and the Nazi party took power. Plötzensee became one of the main execution sites of the regime. Between 1933 and 1945, close to three thousand people were put to death here. Many were sentenced by the so-called People's Court, a political tribunal created to deal harshly with anyone seen as an enemy of the state. Trials were often short. Verdicts were often decided in advance. And the sentence was very often death.

Who were these people? Here is something many visitors do not expect. A large share of them were not German at all. They included Czechs, Poles, French men and women, and people from many other occupied countries. Some had joined resistance groups. Some were forced labourers accused of small offences. Others were Germans who had spoken against the war, listened to foreign radio, or simply told the wrong joke to the wrong person. The range of reasons is part of what makes this place so disturbing.

Now turn toward the small brick building in front of you. This is the former execution shed, and it is the heart of the memorial. Inside, there are two plain rooms. The walls are bare. In the first years, executions were carried out with a guillotine, a heavy falling blade. That device is no longer here. It was removed after the war, and its later fate is unclear. What remains is the room itself — and that emptiness carries its own weight.

Look up, toward the ceiling. You will see a steel beam with iron hooks. This beam was installed during the war so that prisoners could also be hanged, several at a time. It is one of the few original objects left in the room. Many visitors stand in silence here, and you may notice people speaking in whispers without being asked. Take as much time as you need. Nobody will rush you.

One of the darkest episodes came in September 1943. Allied bombs hit the prison during an air raid and damaged parts of the buildings. In the chaos that followed, the authorities decided to carry out death sentences without the usual delays. Over several nights, more than two hundred and fifty prisoners were killed. Survivors and witnesses later called these the nights of blood. Some of the dead had not even had their final appeals heard.

Plötzensee is also closely tied to the German resistance. In December 1942, members of a resistance network that the Gestapo called the Red Orchestra were executed here. And after the failed attempt to kill Hitler on 20 July 1944, many of the people connected to the plot were hanged in this room. Among them were senior army officers, diplomats and civil servants. Field Marshal Erwin von Witzleben was one of them. Later, in January 1945, the lawyer Helmuth James von Moltke was also executed here.

In those final hours, the condemned were not entirely alone. Prison chaplains were allowed to visit them. The best known is the Protestant pastor Harald Poelchau. He sat with hundreds of prisoners before their deaths. He carried their last letters to their families, and he quietly helped persecuted people outside the prison walls. Many of the farewell letters written in these cells survive. Some are shown in the memorial's exhibition, and they are among the most moving documents of the period.

After the war, the city of Berlin decided to turn this site into a place of remembrance. The memorial opened in the early 1950s. Walk back to the large brick wall facing the courtyard. The inscription on it is dedicated to the victims of the Hitler dictatorship in the years 1933 to 1945. In front of the wall stands a stone urn. It holds soil taken from Nazi concentration camps, so that those other places of suffering are also present here.

The design is very restrained. There are no statues of heroes, no dramatic sculpture. This was a deliberate choice. The memorial does not tell you what to feel. It gives you a wall, a room, a lawn and a few words. In a city full of monuments, that simplicity stands out. It also reflects a difficult question that postwar Germany faced for decades — how do you honour victims when the killers were your own state?

If you have time after your visit, two churches nearby are worth knowing about. A short walk away is the Catholic church of Maria Regina Martyrum, built in memory of the martyrs of the Nazi era. There is also a Protestant community centre that holds a large series of paintings called the Plötzensee Dance of Death, by the Austrian artist Alfred Hrdlicka. The Dance of Death is an old European image of death leading people of every rank away. Here it is used to reflect on this place.

Before you leave, stand once more in the courtyard. Listen to the traffic on the far side of the wall, the wind in the trees, the gravel under other visitors' shoes. Life goes on just metres from this room, as it did then. Look at the names in the exhibition, the letters, the faces in old photographs. Many of these people were ordinary — teachers, workers, students, parents. As you walk back toward the street, you carry a small part of their story with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least forty-five minutes to read the exhibition panels.
Keep voices low; the execution room is a place of mourning.
Combine your visit with Maria Regina Martyrum church nearby.
Read the farewell letters on display before entering the shed.
Bring a jacket; the open courtyard offers little shelter.
