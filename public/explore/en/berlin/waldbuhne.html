The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through pine and birch at the edges of a deep bowl in the earth. Below you, row after row of seating drops down the slope toward a stage that looks small from up here. The ground feels firm and slightly sandy underfoot. On a quiet day you hear birds and the far hum of the city. On a concert night, about 22,000 people fill this hollow, and the trees still frame every one of them.

You are standing in the Waldbühne, which means the Forest Stage. It sits on the western side of Berlin, in the Westend part of Charlottenburg, close to the Olympic Stadium. The name is honest. This is a theatre cut into a natural ravine and wrapped in woodland. The audience sits on the hillside, the sky is the ceiling, and the forest forms the walls.

The shape comes from ancient Greece. Look at the curve of the rows. They wrap around the stage in a broad half circle, the way the open-air theatres of the Greek world did. Those theatres used a hillside to lift every seat above the one in front. The idea was simple and clever. With a slope and a curve, a voice on stage can carry a long way without help from machines.

The Waldbühne was built in the mid-1930s as part of the grounds for the 1936 Summer Olympics. The architect Werner March designed the wider Olympic complex, including this theatre. His task came from the Nazi regime, which wanted the Games to show a powerful, orderly Germany to the world. Some Olympic gymnastics events took place here. So this calm, green bowl was born inside one of the most carefully staged propaganda projects of the twentieth century.

The original purpose goes further than sport. The regime promoted a kind of mass open-air drama, sometimes called Thing plays, named after old Germanic assemblies. These performances mixed theatre, ritual and political message. The theatre first carried the name of Dietrich Eckart, an early Nazi writer. The movement behind those plays faded quickly, even before the war. But the stage remained, and so did the question of what to do with a place built for such ends.

Here is something many visitors miss. A short walk from these seats, in the nearby Murellen ravine, the German army executed soldiers during the Second World War. Many of them were deserters or men accused of undermining the war effort. Today a memorial marks the path through the woods, with a series of signs and mirrors set among the trees. It is worth knowing as you sit in this green place. The same forest holds two very different histories.

After the war, the Waldbühne found a new life as a venue for films, boxing, and above all, music. Then came a night in 1965 that Berliners still talk about. The Rolling Stones played here. The concert was very short, and the crowd turned on the venue. Seats were smashed, and damage spread to trains and streets nearby. For years afterwards, the theatre saw only limited use. It took a long time before big crowds came back.

When they did, they changed the mood of the place. From the 1980s, the Berlin Philharmonic began to play an open-air concert here near the end of its season. It has become a summer ritual. People arrive hours early. They bring blankets, cushions, bread, cheese, wine and candles. They claim a stretch of seating like a picnic spot in a park. When the music starts, a world-class orchestra plays to a crowd that might be sitting barefoot.

That concert usually ends with a small surprise. The final encore is often Berliner Luft, which means Berlin Air, a cheerful old tune by the composer Paul Lincke. It works almost like an unofficial song of the city. The audience whistles along, claps in rhythm, and in past years many people lit sparklers. Picture thousands of tiny lights scattered across this slope as the sky turns dark blue. It is one of the gentlest scenes this hollow has ever held.

Now look down toward the stage. Above it stretches a tent-like roof, a later addition that protects performers and equipment from Berlin weather. Notice how it contrasts with the stone and earth of the original design. The old theatre was meant to feel permanent and heavy. The roof feels light, almost temporary, like a sail. Between the two, you can read the change from a monument of power to a place for evenings out.

The acoustics still matter, even with modern speakers. Sound rises up the slope, and the trees soften the edges of it. On a still evening, even a quiet passage from a violin can reach the upper rows. The air smells of pine resin, cut grass, and, on concert nights, grilled sausages from the stands near the entrances. Rain is the great risk. There is no roof over the audience, so Berliners come with ponchos as well as picnics.

Over the decades, a long list of rock, pop and classical performers has played here. Big summer tours often book the Waldbühne for one simple reason. It feels intimate for its size. Even from the back, you look down into the stage rather than across a flat field. Performers often say they can see faces here. For the audience, the climb to the seats, the forest around them, and the open sky make every concert feel a little like an outing.

Take a moment before you leave. Turn and look at the trees along the rim of the bowl. Notice how the rows of seating bend to follow the land. Think of the different crowds this slope has held. Olympic spectators in 1936. Angry fans in 1965. Families with picnic baskets on a summer night. The stage has stayed in the same place. The people, and the meaning of the gathering, have kept changing.

As you walk back up and out through the woods, listen again. The voices of the stage fade fast behind the hill. The pines close in, and the city returns slowly, with the sound of trains and traffic in the distance. Somewhere nearby, the quiet memorial path runs through the same forest. Carry both pictures with you... the sparklers on the slope, and the stillness among the trees.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early on concert nights to claim good seats.
Bring a cushion; long sessions on seating get uncomfortable.
Pack a rain poncho because the audience area is uncovered.
Use public transport; parking near the venue fills quickly.
Visit the nearby Murellen ravine memorial along the forest path.
