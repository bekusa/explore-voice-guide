The guide

The story

Tilt your head back. Then a little further. The concrete shaft rises straight up from the square, smooth and grey, until it meets a silver ball that catches the light like a giant Christmas ornament. Around you, trams ring their bells, pigeons scatter across the paving, and someone is always taking the same photo you are about to take. This is the Berlin Television Tower, the Fernsehturm, and from almost anywhere in this city, it is watching you back.

So what exactly are you looking at? The tower stands about 368 metres tall, counting the red and white antenna at the top. That makes it the tallest structure in all of Germany. It was not built as a monument, at least not officially. It was built to broadcast television and radio signals across East Berlin and beyond. But anyone who stands here for a minute understands that it was also built to be seen.

The story begins in a divided city. In the 1960s, Berlin was split between East and West, and the government of East Germany, the German Democratic Republic, wanted a symbol. They wanted something modern, technical, and confident... something the West could not ignore. Construction ran through the second half of the decade, and the tower opened in October 1969. The timing was not an accident. It came just days before the twentieth anniversary of the East German state.

Look at that sphere again. Many people see a satellite in its shape, and that is no coincidence. In those years, the Soviet Union had launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, and space travel was a point of national pride across the Eastern Bloc. The architect Hermann Henselmann is often credited with an early version of the idea, and a team of designers and engineers turned it into steel and concrete. The silver ball was a promise of the future, placed high above the old city.

Now here is the first surprise. On a sunny day, look closely at the sphere. When the sun hits its steel panels at the right angle, the reflected light forms the shape of a bright cross. For an atheist state that had little love for the churches, this was deeply awkward. Berliners noticed straight away. They gave it a nickname that is still told with a grin today — the Pope's Revenge. Locals say officials were embarrassed, but there was no way to remove the sun.

The nickname tells you something about Berlin humour. People here have always given their buildings sharp, affectionate names, and the tower became part of daily life. Some called it by the name of a political leader. Others simply called it Telespargel, which means television asparagus. When you look at the thin shaft and the round head, you can see why. A symbol of state power, reduced to a vegetable... that is very Berlin.

At the foot of the tower, notice the low building that wraps around its base. Its roof folds into sharp, angled points, a little like paper that someone has creased and opened again. This is where visitors enter today. It is easy to walk past without a second look, but it belongs to the same design vision. The heavy, grounded base and the light, rising shaft were meant to be read together, one anchored to the earth and one reaching for the sky.

If you go up, you will ride one of the lifts inside the shaft. The journey is fast — well under a minute — and your ears may pop on the way. The doors open a little over two hundred metres above the ground, inside the sphere itself. Small windows run in a ring all the way around. On a clear day, the whole city spreads out below you, flat and wide, with green parks, red roofs, and the river Spree turning through the middle.

Just above the viewing floor sits a restaurant, and here is the second surprise. The restaurant turns. It rotates slowly, so that over the course of a meal, the view drifts past your table without you leaving your seat. You may not even feel it moving at first. Then you notice that the church spire you saw a few minutes ago has slipped out of sight, and a new part of the city has taken its place.

From up there, the old line of the Berlin Wall is no longer visible, but you can still sense the division in the city's shape. In the years before 1989, people in West Berlin could see this tower clearly over the Wall. It stood in their skyline every day, a reminder of the other side. After the Wall fell and Germany reunified, many expected the tower to lose its meaning. Instead, the whole city adopted it. Today it appears on postcards, logos, and souvenirs as a symbol of Berlin itself.

Back on the ground, take a moment to look around the square. To one side, the open space leads towards Alexanderplatz, with its famous World Clock and its constant flow of people and trains. Nearby stands the Marienkirche, Saint Mary's Church, one of the oldest churches in Berlin. Its dark brick walls look tiny beside the tower. The contrast is striking — medieval brick at your feet, space-age steel above your head, both sharing the same patch of sky.

Walk a little further and you will find the Neptune Fountain, with the sea god surrounded by figures that represent great rivers. Beyond it rises the Red Town Hall, the seat of the city government, named for its red brick. This whole area was rebuilt after heavy damage in the Second World War. The East German planners cleared wide open spaces, and the tower became the centre point of their new design. Much of what you see here was arranged around it.

So before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Feel the wind that moves across this wide square. Listen to the trams, the voices, the street musicians. Then look up once more at the silver sphere. It was built to send signals across a divided city. Today, it does something simpler. It tells people where they are. Wherever you walk in Berlin this afternoon, glance over your shoulder now and then. Chances are, the tower will still be there, quietly keeping watch.
