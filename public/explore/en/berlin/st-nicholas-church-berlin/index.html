The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The traffic of central Berlin fades here into the scrape of footsteps on cobblestones and the low murmur of café tables. Above you, two sharp spires rise against the sky. At the base of the church, look closely at the stone. It is rough, grey, and uneven. These are fieldstones, gathered from the land around the river, and they are some of the oldest building material in the city.

This is Saint Nicholas Church, known in German as the Nikolaikirche. It is generally considered the oldest church in Berlin. Its story begins in the thirteenth century, when Berlin was not yet a capital. It was a small trading settlement on the banks of the Spree, facing its twin town, Cölln, across the water. Merchants, fishermen, and boatmen lived here, and they built their church close to the river that fed them.

That choice of saint was not an accident. Saint Nicholas was the patron of merchants and sailors. Across northern Europe, trading towns along rivers and coasts often dedicated their churches to him. So the name itself tells you who prayed here first: people who made their living moving goods by water. The church stood at the heart of their market world, and the streets around it grew outward from this spot.

The first church on this site was a simpler building of fieldstone. Over the following centuries it was enlarged and rebuilt. In the fifteenth century it took the form you can still recognise today, a Gothic hall church made mostly of red brick. In a hall church, the side aisles rise to roughly the same height as the central nave. Inside, this creates one wide, bright space instead of a tall centre with dark corridors on either side.

Here is the first surprise. Look again at the lower part of the west front, beneath the towers. The heavy granite and fieldstone section there is a survivor from the medieval building. The brick above it belongs to later phases. And the twin spires, which look so old, are actually a nineteenth century addition. They were added during a restoration in the 1870s, when Berliners wanted their oldest church to look properly grand.

One of the most famous voices connected to this church belongs to Paul Gerhardt. He was a Lutheran pastor here in the seventeenth century, and he wrote hymns that are still sung in German-speaking churches today. His time here was not peaceful. He came into conflict with the ruling elector over religious policy, and he eventually lost his post. Yet his songs outlived the argument, and his name remains closely tied to this building.

The church was not only a place of worship. It also played a part in the political life of the city. In 1809, after reforms in Prussia gave towns more self-government, Berlin's first elected city assembly met here. In other words, this brick church helped host the beginning of modern local democracy in the city. It is an unusual role for a parish church, and most visitors walk past without knowing it.

Then came the Second World War. In the final years of the war, bombing and fighting left much of central Berlin in ruins. Saint Nicholas Church was badly damaged. Its roof and vaults were destroyed, and for decades the building stood as a shell. The surrounding old quarter was also largely lost. Many of the medieval lanes that once ran down to the river simply disappeared under rubble and later clearance.

The church you see now is largely a reconstruction. In the 1980s, East Germany rebuilt it as part of a project to recreate the old quarter around it, the Nikolaiviertel. The work was timed for Berlin's 750th anniversary in 1987. Builders used old images and plans to bring back the church's silhouette. The rest of the quarter was rebuilt in a mix of historic styles, often using modern concrete construction behind traditional-looking facades.

This is the second surprise. The cosy lanes around you, with their gabled houses and old-style lanterns, look centuries old. Many of them are not. They are a late twentieth century vision of what old Berlin felt like. That does not make them fake, exactly. It makes them a record of how a divided city, in the last years of the Cold War, chose to remember its own beginnings.

History returned to the building once more after reunification. In 1991, the newly elected parliament of a united Berlin held its first, opening session inside this church. So the same walls that saw the first city assembly in 1809 also saw the city's new beginning after the fall of the Wall. Today the church no longer serves as a parish. It is run as a museum, telling the story of the building and the city around it.

If you step inside, take time with the details. Much of what survived the war, such as old grave monuments and church furnishings, is displayed there. These carved memorials once honoured wealthy Berlin families. Notice how the light falls through the tall windows into the wide hall. The space is calm, and the acoustics are clear, which is why the church is also used for concerts from time to time.

Back outside, walk slowly around the building. The spires look different from each lane, sometimes framed between rooftops, sometimes reflected in a window. A few steps away, the river Spree still flows past, just as it did for the first merchants here. You can follow the water and imagine the barges, the fish markets, and the small wooden houses that once filled this ground.

Before you go, look up one last time at the two spires, and then down at the rough grey stones at their feet. Between them lie eight centuries of building, breaking, and rebuilding. The stone at the bottom is medieval. The spires are Victorian in age. Much of the rest was raised again from ruins. Together, they make a quiet portrait of Berlin itself... a city that keeps putting itself back together.
