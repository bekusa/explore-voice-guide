The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above the trees, an elevated train rattles past on its iron viaduct. Under your feet the ground is uneven, and if you look down you may spot a rail half-buried in the grass. This corner of Kreuzberg was never meant to be quiet. For generations it belonged to steam, coal smoke, and the clank of freight wagons. The German Museum of Technology did not arrive on an empty plot. It moved into the bones of an old railway yard, and those bones are still here.

Before we go inside, look up. On the roof of the modern glass-and-brick building, an aircraft sits at an angle, as if it has just touched down on the cornice. It is a Douglas C-47, the twin-engine transport that Berliners came to call a 'raisin bomber'. That nickname comes from the Berlin Airlift of 1948 and 1949. During those months, Western allied planes flew food, coal, and supplies into the blockaded western sectors of the city. Some pilots dropped sweets on tiny parachutes to children watching near the runways.

So the plane on the roof is more than a piece of engineering. For many older Berliners, it is a memory of survival. The building under it opened in the early 2000s as an extension to the museum. It holds the aviation and shipping collections. The decision to put a real aircraft on top was simple and clever. It turns the museum into a landmark you can recognise from the street, from the canal, and from the windows of passing trains.

Now turn your attention to the older part of the site. The museum began in 1982 under a different name, the Museum of Transport and Technology. It was renamed in the 1990s. Its home was the former railway works of Anhalter Bahnhof. Anhalter was once one of the great rail terminals of Berlin, sending trains south towards Leipzig, Dresden, and beyond. Today only a fragment of its grand station front survives, a short walk from here at Askanischer Platz.

The station itself was badly damaged in the Second World War and later demolished. But out here, behind it, the working side of the railway survived better. Here, locomotives were turned, cleaned, repaired, and parked. Two round engine sheds still stand on the site. Each one is arranged around a turntable, a rotating platform that swung a heavy locomotive towards its own bay. Walk into one of these halls and you will feel the curve of the building around you.

Inside the railway halls, the scale changes everything. Locomotives that look like toys in old photographs suddenly tower over you. You can see rivets the size of coins, wheels taller than a child, and cabs where the driver and stoker worked side by side in heat and noise. The smell is of oil, old timber, and cool brick. The light comes in from high windows and falls in long stripes across the tracks.

Here is something many visitors walk past without noticing. The rails on the floor are not decoration. Many of the vehicles stand on tracks laid where trains really ran. The turntables are part of the original railway logic of the site. In other words, you are not in a gallery built to look like an engine shed. You are standing in an engine shed that became a gallery.

The railway halls also carry a darker chapter, and the museum does not hide it. Among the vehicles is a freight wagon of the kind used by the German state railway during the Nazi era. Such wagons were used to deport Jews and other persecuted people to ghettos and camps. The display asks a hard question. What happens when efficient technology serves a criminal state? It is a quiet corner. Many people pause there longer than they expected.

Now for a second surprise, and this one is small, grey, and full of metal plates. In the computing section you can see a reconstruction of the Z1. This was a mechanical, programmable calculating machine designed by the Berlin engineer Konrad Zuse in the late 1930s. He built the original in his parents' apartment in Berlin. That first machine was destroyed during the war. Decades later, Zuse himself rebuilt it, and that reconstruction is on display here.

Look closely at the Z1 and you will see no screen, no glowing tubes, no chips. It is a dense stack of thin metal sheets, pins, and levers. It worked with binary numbers, the same ones and zeros that run the phone in your pocket. It is strange to think that a path towards modern computing passed through a family living room only a few kilometres from where you stand.

The museum spreads much further than one visit can cover. There are sections on printing, telecommunications, photography, film, textiles, and the making of everyday objects. Across the road, the Science Center Spectrum offers hands-on experiments with light, sound, electricity, and motion. It is the place where pressing buttons is encouraged. Many families use it as a lively break between the heavier historical halls.

Then step outside again, into the museum park. Here, nature has slowly reclaimed the old rail yard. Birch trees and wild grasses grow between the tracks. You may come across historic windmills, a reminder that technology did not begin with steam. This park links up with the larger Gleisdreieck Park, the name means 'track triangle', after the railway junction that once filled this ground. On a warm afternoon, joggers, cyclists, and children share the paths with the ghosts of freight trains.

As you walk back towards the entrance, take a last look around you. The brick of the engine sheds, the glass of the new wing, the plane on the roof, and the trains still running overhead. Each layer belongs to a different century, yet they sit together without much fuss. Listen again for the next train on the viaduct. It is the same sound this ground has carried for more than a hundred years... only now, you know where it began.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan separate time for the railway halls and aviation wing.
Use Science Center Spectrum as a break for children.
Photograph the rooftop plane from the Landwehr Canal side.
Walk on into Gleisdreieck Park after your visit.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven tracks in the park.
