The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Trams grind across Alexanderplatz, and voices drift from the fountain nearby. Then look at the red brick in front of you. It is rough, uneven, and dark in places where centuries of weather have settled into the clay. You are standing beside one of the oldest churches in Berlin, the Marienkirche, or Saint Mary's Church. Above it, the television tower rises like a needle into the sky. The two buildings stand only a few steps apart, but they come from completely different worlds.

Saint Mary's first appears in written records at the end of the thirteenth century. At that time, Berlin was a small trading town on the River Spree, paired with its twin settlement, Cölln, on the opposite bank. Merchants, craftsmen, and fishermen filled the narrow lanes around this church. It served the newer part of town, which was growing outward from the river. Today it is hard to picture that crowded medieval scene. Open squares, wide roads, and modern towers surround the church on almost every side.

That empty space around you has a story of its own. Before the Second World War, this church stood inside a dense quarter of houses, shops, and alleys. Bombing destroyed much of the old centre. Later, in the years of the German Democratic Republic, East German planners cleared the remaining blocks. They built a broad, open civic space with the television tower at its heart. So the church you see now stands almost alone. It is a survivor, left behind when the streets around it disappeared.

Look closely at the walls. This is brick Gothic, a style found across northern Germany and the Baltic coast. The region had very little good building stone, so builders fired local clay into bricks instead. They shaped the bricks into pointed arches, buttresses, and tall windows, following the Gothic ideas used in stone cathedrals farther south. The result is plainer and heavier than French Gothic, but it has its own quiet strength. Notice how the colour shifts from orange to deep red, brick by brick.

Now raise your eyes to the tower. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The lower part of the tower is medieval brick, but the pointed top is much younger. It was added in the late eighteenth century, and it mixes Gothic shapes with the taste of that later age. The architect Carl Gotthard Langhans designed it. You may know his more famous work in this city... the Brandenburg Gate. So the same mind shaped both the city's great ceremonial gate and this slender spire.

Before you go inside, look near the main entrance for a weathered stone cross. Its story is dark. In the early fourteenth century, an angry crowd of townspeople killed a church official here, a provost named Nikolaus von Bernau. The Church punished the whole town for this crime. As part of their penance, the citizens had to set up a cross of atonement. The worn stone you see is connected to that event. It is a small, quiet object, but it carries one of the oldest violent stories in Berlin's history.

Nearby stands a large statue of Martin Luther, the sixteenth-century reformer who started the Protestant Reformation. Saint Mary's has been a Protestant church for centuries, and it is still an active parish today. The statue was made in the nineteenth century, when Germans honoured Luther as a national figure. Look at his confident pose and the open book. Around the base, other figures from the Reformation once kept him company. The monument shows how strongly later generations linked this church to the Protestant tradition.

Now step inside. The air is cooler, and the noise of the square fades almost at once. The interior is a hall church, which means the side aisles rise to roughly the same height as the central nave. This creates one wide, open space instead of a tall centre with low sides. Light falls through tall windows onto white walls and slender columns. Take a moment here. Let your eyes adjust, and listen to the soft echo of footsteps on the floor.

Here is the second surprise. In the tower hall, near the entrance, a long painting runs across the wall. It is a Dance of Death, painted in the late fifteenth century, probably after an outbreak of plague. In the scene, figures of Death lead people from every rank of society — clergy, nobles, merchants, peasants — in a slow line. For a long time, this painting lay hidden under whitewash. It was rediscovered in the nineteenth century. The colours are faded, so stand close and look carefully.

The message of the Dance of Death is simple, and it was very real to medieval Berliners. Death comes for everyone, rich or poor, powerful or humble. Under the figures, old verses once gave each character a few words to speak. In a town where disease could empty whole streets, this painting was a daily reminder. It asked people to live well and prepare their souls. Today it is one of the rare medieval artworks to survive in the heart of Berlin.

Walk further in and find the pulpit. It is one of the finest objects in the church. The sculptor Andreas Schlüter created it at the start of the eighteenth century, in the Baroque style. Baroque art loves movement, drama, and rich decoration. Look at the carved figures, the sweeping curves, and the way the pulpit seems to float against its column. Schlüter was one of the leading sculptors and architects of Prussia in his day. This pulpit is a chance to see his skill up close.

Near the front, look for the old bronze baptismal font. It dates from the fifteenth century and is one of the oldest objects here. Look at its base. Small dragon-like creatures appear to hold the bowl up. Generations of Berlin children were baptised over this bronze. Then turn toward the west end and look up at the organ. Its Baroque case dates from the eighteenth century. When music fills the church during a service or a concert, the whole hall changes.

Before you leave, stand outside again and look at both buildings together. The old brick church sits low against the ground. The concrete tower rises high above it, with its silver sphere catching the light. One was built for prayer in a small medieval town. The other was built to send signals across a divided modern city. Between them lie more than seven centuries of Berlin. As you walk toward the square, glance back once more. Notice how the red brick still holds its place, steady and quiet, in the middle of everything.
