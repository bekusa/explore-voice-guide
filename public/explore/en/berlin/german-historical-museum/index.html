The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in front of the long pink-and-sand facade on Unter den Linden. Listen to the traffic on the boulevard and the trams further off. Above you, the roofline carries a crowd of stone figures: helmets, armour, flags, cannon. They look like decoration. They were put there as a warning. This building was made to store weapons, and it wanted everyone to know it.

You are looking at the Zeughaus, the old Prussian arsenal. Today it is home to the German Historical Museum, the national museum of German history. Its walls hold a story that runs from the early Middle Ages to the present day. But before we go inside, the building itself deserves your attention. It is the oldest structure on this famous boulevard. Many people walk past it without knowing that.

Construction began in 1695, under the Elector Frederick the Third of Brandenburg. A few years later he crowned himself Frederick the First, King in Prussia. The project passed through the hands of several architects. Johann Arnold Nering made the first plans. Martin Grünberg, Andreas Schlüter and Jean de Bodt followed. The work took decades, and the building was finished only around 1730. The result is Baroque in style — the grand, theatrical style of European courts at that time.

Notice how calm the facade looks. The lines are long and horizontal. The windows repeat in a steady rhythm. Then look up again at the trophies and sculptures along the top edge. This is a building with two voices. Below, order and discipline. Above, the noise and drama of war. A young kingdom wanted its arsenal to look both rational and powerful.

Now the first surprise. If you can reach the inner courtyard, look at the keystones above the arched windows. There you will find the faces of dying warriors, carved under the direction of Andreas Schlüter. There are twenty-two of them. Some are screaming. Some have their eyes half closed. Some seem frozen in the last moment of pain. For a building that celebrated military power, these masks are strangely honest. They show the cost of war, not the glory.

The courtyard itself is now covered by a glass roof. Light falls softly onto the grey stone and onto those suffering faces. It is a quiet place, even when the museum is busy. Many visitors stop here longer than they planned. Take a slow walk around the four sides. Each mask is different, and the details become clearer the longer you look.

The building has seen real violence, not only carved violence. During the revolution of 1848, crowds in Berlin stormed the arsenal and seized weapons. It was a short, chaotic episode. Still, it shows how close this building stood to the political life of the city. Later, in the late nineteenth century, the Prussian state turned the Zeughaus into a hall of fame for the army. The weapons store became a temple to military memory.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a dark one. In March 1943, Adolf Hitler came to the Zeughaus to view an exhibition of captured weapons. A German officer, Rudolf-Christoph von Gersdorff, carried explosives under his coat. He planned to die together with Hitler inside these rooms. But Hitler rushed through the exhibition in only a few minutes and left. Gersdorff managed to disarm the fuse in time. Few people who pass this facade know how close history came to turning here.

The war badly damaged the building. After 1945 it stood in the eastern part of the divided city. The East German state rebuilt it and used it for its own Museum of German History, told through a socialist lens. Meanwhile, in West Berlin, plans grew for a different national history museum. The German Historical Museum was founded in 1987. After reunification in 1990, the old arsenal and its collections passed to the new institution.

Walk around to the back of the building, toward the canal. Here you find a very different kind of architecture. This is the exhibition hall designed by the Chinese-American architect I. M. Pei, opened in 2003. Pei also designed the glass pyramid at the Louvre in Paris. His building here is light and geometric, made of stone and glass. A glass spiral staircase rises at the corner like a clear shell. The contrast with the heavy Baroque arsenal is deliberate.

Inside, the museum collects objects rather than just stories. Over the years it has shown weapons, uniforms, paintings, posters, coins, everyday tools and political propaganda. The idea is simple: history is made of things people actually held and used. Exhibitions change, and the Zeughaus has gone through major renovation work. So it is worth checking which halls are open on the day you visit. The Pei building often hosts temporary shows on difficult and surprising topics.

Step back onto Unter den Linden and look at the neighbours. Right next door stands the Neue Wache, a small classical building that now serves as Germany's central memorial to victims of war and dictatorship. Across the bridge lies Museum Island, with its domes and columns. Behind you runs the long avenue toward the Brandenburg Gate. The arsenal sits at the heart of this old royal quarter, where kings once wanted their power to be seen.

Before you leave, look once more at the roofline. The stone helmets and flags still stand against the sky, as they have for three centuries. Then think of the twenty-two faces in the courtyard behind them. One building, two messages: the pride of war on the outside, its pain on the inside. As you walk on along the boulevard, you might notice how many of Berlin's buildings carry the same double meaning.
