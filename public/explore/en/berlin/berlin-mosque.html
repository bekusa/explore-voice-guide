The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. A quiet residential street in Wilmersdorf, a tram bell somewhere in the distance, the rustle of trees in front gardens. Then, between ordinary apartment blocks, a pale dome rises over a low wall, flanked by two slender towers. It feels almost unreal, like a page from a book about northern India, placed carefully into a calm Berlin neighbourhood. This is the Berlin Mosque, and it has stood here for roughly a century.

Here is the first surprise. This modest building is widely regarded as the oldest mosque in Germany that is still standing. It was built in the 1920s, during the restless years of the Weimar Republic. That was the short democratic period between the end of the First World War and the rise of the Nazis. Berlin then was a city of cabarets, cinemas, inflation and political street fights. And in the middle of all that, a small religious community decided to build here.

The community behind the mosque came from the Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement. This is a reform-minded Muslim movement that began in British India, in what is today Pakistan. Its members saw Europe as a place for dialogue and missionary work. They sent a scholar to Berlin, and he led the project to build a permanent house of prayer. The aim was not only worship. It was also to explain Islam to curious Germans, in German, in their own city.

Look at the shape of the building. The design draws on Mughal architecture. The Mughals were the Muslim dynasty that ruled much of the Indian subcontinent for several centuries. They built the Taj Mahal, the great tomb in Agra. You can see that family resemblance here — the onion-like dome, the arched entrance, the paired towers at the sides. Of course, this is a much smaller and simpler version, adapted to a Berlin plot and a limited budget.

Notice how the mosque sits within its surroundings. It is not on a grand square or a major boulevard. It stands back from the street, behind a garden and a fence, among homes where people go about ordinary lives. Many visitors arrive expecting something monumental and find something intimate instead. That intimacy is part of the story. This was a small community, building with what it could afford, and choosing a quiet district rather than a showpiece location.

Berlin in the 1920s already had a surprising number of Muslims. There were diplomats, students, merchants and political exiles, many from India, Egypt, Turkey and further east. Some had come before the war, others after. The mosque became a meeting point for this scattered group. Over the years, its guest book and gatherings brought together people of many backgrounds. Some German converts to Islam also found their way here, and the community published writings in German.

Another surprising fact concerns what came before. During the First World War, Germany held many Muslim prisoners of war who had fought for Britain and France. A wooden mosque was built for them at a camp south of Berlin. It was a temporary structure, and it was later taken down. So this building in Wilmersdorf is not the very first mosque in the Berlin region. It is the first one built to last, and the one that survives today.

Then came the darkest chapter. In the final weeks of the Second World War, during the Battle of Berlin in 1945, fighting reached these streets. The mosque was badly damaged. The two minarets, the slender towers used in many mosques for the call to prayer, suffered heavily. For years afterwards, the building stood scarred, like so much of the city around it. Its survival at all is remarkable, given how much of Berlin was destroyed.

After the war, the community slowly repaired the building. The minarets you see today are reconstructions, rebuilt many decades after the damage. So when you look up at them, you are looking at a careful restoration, not the original towers from the 1920s. The dome and the main body of the mosque carry more of the original fabric. Today the building is protected as a historic monument, recognised for its place in the story of the city.

It is worth thinking about how rare this kind of continuity is in Berlin. This city has been rebuilt again and again. Palaces vanished, churches were bombed, a wall went up and came down. Yet this small mosque has kept the same purpose on the same plot for about a hundred years. Through democracy, dictatorship, war, division and reunification, people have continued to gather here to pray, to talk and to share meals.

If you are here at prayer time, you may see worshippers arriving, removing their shoes at the entrance. Please be respectful. This is an active place of worship, not a museum. Dress modestly if you hope to go inside, and women may wish to bring a scarf to cover their hair. Visits are not always possible, so it is wise to ask the community in advance. Even from the street, though, you can appreciate the building quietly.

Take a moment to compare the mosque with the houses around it. The neighbouring apartment blocks have the solid, practical look of early twentieth century Berlin, with balconies and plain façades. The mosque, by contrast, is all curves and symmetry. Yet it does not feel out of place. Trees soften the view, and the scale is gentle. Many local residents simply walk past it every day, the way you might walk past a familiar church.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the gate and look again at the dome against the Berlin sky. Listen to the street — a bicycle passing, children on their way home, a neighbour's window opening. A century ago, a small group of travellers from far away chose this spot to build something lasting. It is still here. As you walk on, notice how quickly the ordinary city returns around you... and how the two towers stay in view a little longer than you expect.
