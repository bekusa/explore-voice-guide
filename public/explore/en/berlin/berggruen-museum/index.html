The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on Schlossstraße and listen. Traffic hums along the wide avenue, and gravel crunches under someone's feet near the palace gardens. In front of you rises a pale, sober building with tall windows and a neat classical face. It looks like it was built to hold soldiers, not paintings. In a sense, that is exactly right. Behind these calm walls hangs one of the most personal collections of modern art in Berlin, gathered by one man over a long life.

This is the Berggruen Museum, part of the National Gallery of Berlin. Across the street, the long front of Charlottenburg Palace stretches out, all Baroque elegance and royal history. Turn around, and you will see a twin of this building on the other side of the avenue. The two buildings face each other like guards at a gate. That is no accident. They were designed as a matching pair to frame the approach to the palace.

Both buildings come from the architect Friedrich August Stüler, a favourite of the Prussian court, in the middle of the nineteenth century. They were built for officers of the royal guard, the elite cavalry that served the king. So the first surprise is simple. A space made for the discipline of the army now holds work by artists who broke almost every rule they were taught. Picasso and Klee, in an old barracks... there is a quiet joke in that.

The man behind the collection was Heinz Berggruen. He was born in Berlin in 1914 into a Jewish family. In the 1930s, as Nazi persecution grew, he left Germany and went to the United States. There he worked as a writer and in the art world in San Francisco. Many people do not know this next part. During those American years, he had a brief romance with the Mexican painter Frida Kahlo. It is a small detail, but it tells you how close he stood to the art of his time.

After the war, Berggruen settled in Paris and became an art dealer. He started small, with prints and works on paper. Over the decades he came to know many artists and the people around them, including Picasso. He sold a great deal of art, but he also kept pieces he simply could not let go. Slowly, those pieces became a private collection with a very clear personality — modern, intimate, and full of works he loved.

Then came the decision that brought it all here. In the 1990s, Berggruen returned the collection to his birthplace. It went on display in this building in 1996. A few years later, the Prussian Cultural Heritage Foundation acquired it, at a price widely described as far below its market value. For a man forced out of Berlin as a young adult, this was a deeply personal homecoming. Many visitors see the collection as a gesture of reconciliation with the city.

Inside, the centre of gravity is Pablo Picasso. The collection follows him across his long career. You can move from early, careful work through the sharp angles of Cubism — the style where an object is broken into flat shapes and seen from several sides at once. Later rooms bring bolder colour and freer lines. Walking through, you can almost watch one artist change his mind again and again over many decades.

Among the Picassos, look for the portraits of women from his circle, including Dora Maar, the photographer and artist who was his partner for some years. Faces turn in two directions at once. Eyes sit where you do not expect them. At first it can feel strange. Then, if you stay a little longer, the faces start to feel more alive than a normal portrait. That is part of the pleasure here. The rooms are small enough that you can take your time.

The second great voice in the collection is Paul Klee, the Swiss-born painter who taught at the Bauhaus, the famous German school of art and design. His pictures are often small. Up close, they are full of tiny lines, signs, arrows, and soft fields of colour. Some look like maps, some like music written in shapes. Klee works on paper are sensitive to light, so they are often shown in dimmer rooms. Let your eyes adjust before you lean in.

The collection also includes Henri Matisse, the French master of colour. Look for his late paper cut-outs, made when he worked with scissors and painted sheets instead of a brush. The shapes are simple — leaves, figures, waves — but the colours are strong and clean. You will also find sculpture by Alberto Giacometti, with his thin, stretched human figures. They seem to stand very far away, even when they are right in front of you. Works by Cézanne and Braque add to the story of how modern art began.

Notice the building itself as you move through it. At its heart is a central stairwell under a dome, with light coming from above. The rooms around it are modest in size, more like a large house than a grand museum hall. That scale suits the collection. Berggruen often lived with these works, and here they still feel close, almost domestic. In recent years the museum has gone through a major renovation, so parts of the layout may look different from older photos.

When you leave, remember the twin building across the avenue. It holds another private collection, focused on Surrealism and fantastic art, the Scharf-Gerstenberg Collection. Together, the two buildings make this corner of Charlottenburg a small district of modern art, facing a royal palace. Prussian power on one side, twentieth-century imagination on the other. It is a strange and very Berlin combination. And the palace gardens behind it all are a good place to sit and let the pictures settle.

So, before you walk on, look back once at the plain stone front. It does not announce what is inside. No bright banners, no dramatic glass. Just tall windows, a quiet door, and the sound of the street. Somewhere behind those windows, a Picasso face is looking two ways at once, and a Klee line is still wandering across its paper. Take a breath, listen to the gravel and the traffic... and carry a little of that colour with you as you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the museum's current status before visiting after its renovation.
Pair your visit with the Scharf-Gerstenberg Collection across the street.
Let your eyes adjust in the dimmer Klee rooms.
Walk the Charlottenburg Palace gardens afterwards to rest.
Come early on weekdays for quieter, smaller gallery rooms.
