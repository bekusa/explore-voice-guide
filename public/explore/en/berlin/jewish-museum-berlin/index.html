The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. On Lindenstrasse in Kreuzberg, traffic hums and bicycles tick past. Then your eye catches something that does not belong to the street. A long wall of grey zinc breaks into sharp angles, and its windows are cut like slashes across the metal. Beside it stands a calm, pale yellow Baroque palace with tall windows and a neat central doorway. These two buildings could hardly be more different. Together they form the Jewish Museum Berlin. They also tell one story about two thousand years of Jewish life in Germany.

Start with the older building, because that is where your visit begins. This is the Collegienhaus, built in the eighteenth century under the Prussian kings. For many years it housed a high court of justice. Its front is orderly and symmetrical, the kind of architecture that promises stability and rule. The building was badly damaged in the Second World War and later rebuilt. Today it holds the entrance, the ticket desks, a shop and a café. It feels like any grand city building. That feeling of normality is part of the design of the whole visit.

Here is the first surprise. From the street, you cannot see any door between the old palace and the new zinc building. There is no bridge and no visible link above ground. To reach the new wing, you walk down a staircase inside the Baroque house and continue underground. The architect, Daniel Libeskind, chose this on purpose. He wanted to show that Jewish and German history are tied together below the surface, even when the link is not visible from outside.

Libeskind, an American architect born in Poland, won the design competition in the late 1980s. The building was finished by 1999, before any exhibition was installed. For a time, the museum opened the empty building to visitors, and many people came just to walk through its bare halls. Some argued it should stay empty forever. The museum opened with its full exhibition in 2001. Seen from above, the building forms a broken zigzag. Some people compare it to a lightning bolt, and others to a shattered Star of David.

Now you are underground, on sloping floors with low ceilings. Three long corridors cross each other here. People call them the axes. One is the Axis of Continuity, which leads to a long, steep staircase up into the exhibition. The second is the Axis of Exile, which leads outside into a garden. The third is the Axis of the Holocaust, which ends at a heavy door. Along the walls, glass cases hold personal objects: letters, photographs and household items. Each object belonged to a real family whose life was broken by persecution.

The Axis of the Holocaust ends in the Holocaust Tower. It is a tall, empty room of bare concrete, and it has no heating. When the door closes behind you, the street noise almost disappears. A single narrow slit near the top lets in a thin line of daylight. You may hear faint sounds from outside, distant and out of reach. Many visitors stand in silence here for longer than they expect. There is no text and nothing to read. The room works only through cold, dark and height.

The Axis of Exile leads you out into the Garden of Exile. Here you find forty-nine tall concrete columns, set on a tilted square of ground. The floor slopes in two directions, so your balance shifts as you walk between them. Many visitors say they feel a little dizzy, and the architect intended that. It is a small physical idea of what exile means: the ground is no longer steady. Look up. Willow-leaf olive trees grow from the tops of the columns. Their silver-green leaves move against the sky above your head.

There is a detail about these columns that most visitors miss. According to the museum, forty-eight of them are filled with earth from Berlin. They stand for the year 1948, when the State of Israel was founded. The single column in the middle holds earth from Jerusalem, and it stands for Berlin itself. So the garden is not only about loss. It also carries a quiet idea of hope, with trees growing where you would not expect them to grow at all.

Inside the zinc building, the corridors are crossed by empty spaces that Libeskind called voids. These are tall concrete shafts that run through the building from bottom to top. You cannot enter most of them. You can only look into them from the bridges and doorways around. They stand for what is missing: the people, the culture and the lives that were destroyed in the Holocaust. As you walk the exhibition, you keep meeting these empty spaces again. They interrupt the story on purpose.

One void is open to visitors, and it is the second surprise. It is called the Memory Void. Its floor is covered with more than ten thousand round faces cut from heavy steel plates. Each face has an open mouth and empty eyes. This installation is called Fallen Leaves, by the Israeli artist Menashe Kadishman. Visitors are invited to walk across it. When you do, the steel plates knock and grind against each other. The sound rings up the concrete walls, loud and metallic. You hear the work before you understand it.

Upstairs, the core exhibition covers Jewish life in Germany from the Middle Ages to the present day. A new version opened in 2020. It moves through medieval communities, the age of the Enlightenment, rising hope in the nineteenth century, the catastrophe of the Nazi years, and Jewish life after the war. There are religious objects, art, films and listening stations. Some rooms feel playful and others feel heavy. The exhibition does not end in 1945. It also asks what Jewish life looks like in Germany today.

The museum continues beyond these two buildings. Back in the old palace, a glass roof now covers the inner courtyard. Libeskind designed it as well, inspired by the sukkah, the temporary hut used during the Jewish harvest festival of Sukkot. Across the street, a former flower market hall now holds the museum's academy, with its library and archive. Nearby you will also find ANOHA, the museum's children's world. It is built around the story of Noah's Ark, with a large wooden ark full of animals made from recycled materials.

Before you leave, stand on the pavement and look again at the zinc wall. When it was new, the metal was bright. Over the years, rain and air have softened it into a dull, uneven grey. The window slashes still cut across it in lines that do not follow any floor level. Behind you, the Baroque palace keeps its calm, regular face. As you walk back toward the street, listen again to the traffic and the bicycles. Daily Berlin goes on right beside this building, and the museum stands in the middle of it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least three hours for the core exhibition.
Expect airport-style security checks at the main entrance.
Bring a warm layer for the unheated Holocaust Tower.
Walk the Garden of Exile slowly if you feel unsteady.
Visit the axes first before climbing to the exhibition.
