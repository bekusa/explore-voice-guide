The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Below you, the Rusenski Lom river runs slowly through its green valley. Swallows cut across the cliff face. The limestone under your hand is pale, dry and slightly rough, warmed by the sun. Somewhere above you, cut directly into this rock, are rooms where medieval monks lived, prayed and painted. From the valley floor, many of them look like nothing more than dark openings in the cliff.

You are in northeastern Bulgaria, a short drive south of the Danube city of Ruse, close to the village of Ivanovo. These cliffs hold one of the most remarkable monastic sites in the Balkans. It is a network of churches, chapels and cells, carved by hand into the soft stone. In 1979, UNESCO added the Rock-hewn Churches of Ivanovo to its World Heritage List. The reason was mainly the wall paintings, which we will come to soon.

First, picture the setting as the monks saw it. The Rusenski Lom is a tributary of the Danube. Over a very long time, it cut a deep, winding gorge through the limestone plateau. The result is a valley of steep walls, small caves and natural ledges. For people seeking silence and distance from the world, this landscape offered something rare — shelter, water, and solitude, all within reach of a royal capital.

The story of the monastery begins in the thirteenth century. According to tradition and medieval sources, a monk named Joachim settled here and founded a community in these rocks. He later became Patriarch of Bulgaria, the head of the Bulgarian Orthodox Church. His rise from cliff hermit to church leader gave the site prestige. Over the following generations, more monks arrived, and more spaces were carved into the stone.

This was the period of the Second Bulgarian Empire, a medieval Bulgarian state with its capital at Tarnovo, further south. Bulgarian rulers took an interest in the monastery and supported it. Tsar Ivan Asen the Second, one of the strongest rulers of that era, is linked with its early growth. In the fourteenth century, Tsar Ivan Alexander became a major patron. Royal money meant better buildings and, above all, better painters.

The complex was never one single building. Think of it instead as a vertical village. Churches, chapels, monastic cells and small rooms were scattered along the cliff at different heights. Some were connected by paths, ledges and steps cut into the rock. Over the centuries, erosion and collapse destroyed many of the original walkways. Some rooms are now very hard to reach, and others have lost their front walls entirely.

Now to the heart of the visit — the Church of the Holy Mother of God. This is the church most visitors climb up to see, and it holds the best-preserved paintings. The frescoes date to the fourteenth century. Here is the first surprise for many people. These are not simple, rough images made by isolated hermits. They are sophisticated works, painted by highly skilled artists who clearly knew the leading art of their time.

Art historians connect these paintings with the Palaeologan style. That name comes from the Palaiologos dynasty, the last ruling family of the Byzantine Empire, based in Constantinople. Palaeologan painting is known for graceful figures, expressive faces and a sense of movement and depth. At Ivanovo, the figures turn, gesture and react to one another. Some scholars see a clear influence from classical, ancient Greek models in the way bodies and drapery are shown.

Look closely at the scenes from the life and Passion of Christ — the events leading to his death and resurrection. Notice the colours, too. Deep blues, warm ochres and soft reds still survive on the walls, even after roughly seven centuries. Among the paintings is a portrait of Tsar Ivan Alexander as the donor of the church. Medieval patrons were often painted this way, to record their gift and to ask for prayers.

Here is the second surprise. The monastery was not only a place of art. It was also a centre of spiritual thought. In the fourteenth century, it is associated with Hesychasm. This was a movement within Orthodox Christianity that focused on inner stillness, silent repeated prayer, and the search for direct experience of God. A remote cliff above a quiet river was, in many ways, the ideal home for such a practice.

The golden age did not last. In the late fourteenth century, the Ottoman Turks conquered the Bulgarian lands. Over time, the monastic community declined and the cliff churches fell out of regular use. Weather, moisture and time damaged many paintings. Visitors over the centuries also scratched names and marks into the plaster. Yet enough survived to show modern researchers how advanced this monastery once was.

Today, the site sits within the Rusenski Lom Nature Park. The surrounding landscape is protected for its rocks, forests, plants and birds. Walking trails run along the valley, and other rock-cut sites lie scattered through the wider area. As you climb toward the church, notice the shape of the cut openings above and around you. Each one may once have been a cell, a storeroom or a small chapel.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the edge and look back down into the valley. The river is the same river the monks heard every morning. The cliffs still hold their cool shade in summer. Above you, in a small room of stone, painted faces still look out from the walls. As you walk back down, count the dark openings in the cliff. Each one was once somebody's whole world.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for the rock steps.
Bring water, especially on hot summer afternoons.
Combine your visit with a walk in Rusenski Lom Nature Park.
Come early to see frescoes before groups arrive.
Allow time for the climb up the cliff path.
