The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, packed earth gives softly with each step. Somewhere ahead, a dog barks behind a low stone wall. Wood smoke drifts from a kitchen chimney, and the air carries a faint sour smell of fermenting soybean paste. Thatched roofs curve like loaves of bread against the hills. For a moment, nothing here looks like modern Korea at all. That is exactly the point. You are standing inside a village built to show how ordinary people once lived.

The Korean Folk Village sits on the edge of Yongin, a short ride from central Suwon. It opened in the 1970s as an open-air museum, a place where traditional houses and daily life could be seen at full scale. Many of the buildings are original structures moved here from other regions. Others were carefully rebuilt using old methods. Together they form a living picture of the late Joseon period, the dynasty that ruled Korea for about five hundred years until the early twentieth century.

Near the entrance, you may notice tall carved wooden posts with wide eyes and grinning teeth. These are jangseung, village guardian poles. In the past, communities placed them at the edge of a village to keep away disease and bad spirits. They also marked boundaries and sometimes showed travellers the distance to the next town. Their faces look fierce, but many visitors find them funny too. That mix of fear and humour runs through a great deal of Korean folk art.

As you walk, look closely at the houses. The village brings together homes from different provinces, so the styles change from one lane to the next. Some have thick thatch made of rice straw, renewed every year or two in the past. Others, belonging to wealthier families, have heavy grey clay tiles. In the south, roofs often sit lower against the wind. Notice how the shape of a house tells you about climate, money, and social rank.

One detail surprises many visitors. Find the houses built from dark, porous volcanic stone, with ropes tied over their thatched roofs. These come from Jeju, the island far off Korea's southern coast. Jeju is famous for strong winds, so islanders lashed their roofs down with a net of straw ropes. The black stone walls hold heat and break the gusts. Standing here, hundreds of kilometres from the island, you can see a completely different way of life.

Now look down, at the base of the walls and the raised floors. Many of these houses use ondol, the traditional Korean underfloor heating system. A fire in the kitchen sends hot smoke through channels beneath the stone floor of the next room. The floor itself becomes the heater. This is why Koreans traditionally sat, ate, and slept close to the ground. Even today, many modern Korean apartments use heated floors, a direct descendant of this old idea.

In many courtyards you will see rows of large brown earthenware jars, often on a raised stone platform. These are onggi, and the platform is called a jangdokdae. Families stored soy sauce, soybean paste, red pepper paste, and kimchi inside them. The clay lets air pass through slowly, which helps the food ferment. The platform was usually placed where it caught plenty of sun. In an old household, the state of these jars said a lot about how well the home was run.

The grand homes of the yangban, the scholar aristocracy, tell another story. Look for houses divided into separate quarters. Men received guests and studied in the outer rooms, while women managed the household in the inner rooms, further from the gate. Walls and courtyards controlled who could go where. Compare these spacious compounds with the small farmhouses nearby. The gap between them shows how strictly Joseon society was arranged by class and by gender.

The village also recreates places of public life. There is a government office complex, where local magistrates once heard disputes and collected taxes. You may see displays of old punishment equipment, which can feel harsh to modern eyes. There are also spaces for a village school, a blacksmith, and craft workshops. In some of them, artisans demonstrate skills such as weaving, pottery, or making traditional paper. Pause and watch their hands. Much of this knowledge passed from person to person, not through books.

Then comes the sound of drums and gongs. The village is well known for its performances, especially nongak, the farmers' music once played to lift spirits during hard work and to celebrate harvests. Performers spin long ribbons attached to their hats, drawing circles in the air. Other shows may include tightrope walking, horseback acrobatics, or a reenactment of a traditional wedding. Schedules vary by season and day, so check the information boards when you arrive.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. These lanes and courtyards have appeared in a large number of Korean historical dramas and films. Because the buildings are authentic in style and free of modern clutter, production teams have long used the village as a ready-made set. If you watch Korean period dramas, a gate or a stone wall here may look strangely familiar. Some fans come specifically to find the spots they remember from the screen.

When you feel hungry, follow the smell of oil and spring onions toward the market street. Traditional market stalls sell simple food such as savoury pancakes, noodles, and rice dishes, often with makgeolli, a milky rice wine. Sitting on a low wooden bench here feels close to how a market day might once have felt. Around the village, you may also find a separate area with modern rides and attractions, mainly for younger visitors.

Before you leave, stop for a moment beside a stream or under a large tree. Listen again. The water runs over stones, a wooden door creaks, and voices drift from a courtyard. Look at the line of the roofs against the hills, and the smoke rising into the sky. Everything here was made by hand, from earth, wood, straw, and stone. As you walk back toward the gate, notice how quiet the old lanes feel, compared with the road waiting just outside.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the boards on arrival for daily performance schedules.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven dirt paths and long walks.
Plan at least half a day to see the village properly.
Visit on a weekday morning for quieter lanes and courtyards.
Try savoury pancakes and makgeolli at the market street.
