The guide

The story

Push through the doors on Kildare Street and the first thing you notice is the floor. Small tiles, laid in curving patterns, run under your feet. Then the ceiling lifts away above you into a high round hall. Light falls from the top of the dome. Voices soften here. Footsteps echo for a moment, then fade. You are standing in the rotunda of the National Museum of Ireland, at its archaeology branch. This building holds some of the oldest made objects on the island.

First, a quick note on the name. The National Museum of Ireland is not one building. It is a family of four. There is this archaeology museum on Kildare Street, and the natural history museum around the corner on Merrion Street. Decorative arts and history sit in the old Collins Barracks by the River Liffey. A fourth branch, devoted to country life, is far to the west in County Mayo. This guide stays here, with the gold, the bronze and the bog.

The building opened in 1890. It was designed by the architect Thomas Newenham Deane, working with his son, Thomas Manly Deane. Look up at the dome again. Its shape draws on the Pantheon in Rome, the great round temple of the ancient city. That choice was deliberate. In the late nineteenth century, a public museum was meant to feel like a temple of learning. Here, the objects on display are the treasures.

Outside, the setting tells its own story. The museum faces a twin building across a wide courtyard. That twin is the National Library of Ireland, also by the Deanes. Between them stands Leinster House, which is now home to the Irish parliament. So as you walk in, you pass the place where the country makes its laws. Learning, memory and government sit side by side on one short street.

Now walk toward the gold. The exhibition of prehistoric Irish gold is one of the largest of its kind in Europe. The pieces are thousands of years old. There are flat crescents called lunulae, worn at the neck. There are heavy twisted bars called torcs, and thick collars. Many were found by farmers, turf cutters and labourers. People simply turned over the soil and saw a gleam. Gold does not rust, so it comes out of the ground still shining.

One small object stops most visitors. It is a tiny gold boat, part of the Broighter Hoard, found in County Derry in the 1890s. The boat has oars and benches, and even a mast. It dates to around the first century before Christ. Nobody knows exactly why it was buried. Many scholars think it was an offering, perhaps to a god of the sea. Bend down and look closely. The oars are thinner than matchsticks.

Next comes the room that many people remember most. The exhibition called Kingship and Sacrifice holds bodies from the Iron Age, found in Irish bogs. Bog water is cold, acidic and low in oxygen. So skin, hair and nails can survive for more than two thousand years. The display is quiet and carefully lit. Each body rests in its own enclosed space. You walk around them slowly, almost by instinct, and voices drop to a whisper.

Here is a detail that surprises many visitors. One of these men, known as Clonycavan Man, still has his hair styled upward. Researchers found that he used a kind of hair gel made from plant oil and pine resin. Some studies suggest the resin came from far away, possibly from southwest Europe. If that is right, it was a luxury product. And the theory behind this room is striking. These men may have been kings, or would-be kings, killed in rituals and placed at old boundaries between territories.

From the Iron Age, move forward into early Christian Ireland. This is the age of monks, monasteries and fine metalwork. Two objects here are famous across the country. The first is the Tara Brooch. Despite its name, it was not found at the Hill of Tara. It was found on the coast of County Meath in the mid nineteenth century. A dealer gave it the grand name to make it sound royal. The name stuck.

Look closely at the brooch, and you will see why craftspeople still study it. The surface is covered in gold filigree — wire so fine it looks like thread. There are tiny animals and twisting knots. Even the back is decorated, though the wearer would rarely see it. That was a matter of pride. A maker wanted the hidden side to be perfect too. The brooch dates to around the late seventh or early eighth century.

The second treasure is the Ardagh Chalice, a silver cup for the Christian Mass. Two boys found it in 1868 while digging potatoes near Ardagh, in County Limerick. Imagine pushing a spade into a field and hitting this. The chalice is wide and calm in shape, with bands of gold and studs of coloured glass. Nearby, you can also find the Cross of Cong, a tall processional cross from the twelfth century, made to hold a relic.

The story does not stop with monks. Upstairs and in other galleries, the museum turns to Viking Dublin. In the late twentieth century, excavations at Wood Quay, near the river, uncovered the streets of the Viking town. Archaeologists found houses, combs, shoes, tools and children's toys. These everyday things matter as much as gold. They show that Dublin began as a busy port. Its traders were linked to Scandinavia, Britain and beyond.

Before you leave, come back to the rotunda for a moment. Stand still in the middle of the floor. Look at the columns, the dome, the soft light from above. Think of the farmers, turf cutters and children who found so much of what you have just seen. Many of these objects lay underground for centuries, waiting. As you step back onto Kildare Street, notice the ordinary fields of Ireland in your mind... any one of them might still hold something like this.
