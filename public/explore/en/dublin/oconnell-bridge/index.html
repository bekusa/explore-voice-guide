The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the traffic through your feet. Buses roll past in a slow green and yellow stream. Gulls cry overhead, riding the wind that comes up the river from the sea. Beneath you, the dark water of the Liffey slides east toward Dublin Bay. You are standing at one of the busiest crossings in the city. This is O'Connell Bridge, and most people walk over it without realising they have crossed a bridge at all.

That is the first surprise. Look around you and the bridge does not feel narrow. It feels more like a small square than a river crossing. That impression is correct. The bridge is about as wide as it is long, which is unusual among the bridges of Europe. Dubliners like to point this out to visitors. Most bridges are long and slim. This one is almost a box, built to carry a wide, steady tide of people and vehicles from one side of the city to the other.

To understand why, look at what the bridge connects. To the north runs O'Connell Street, the broad main avenue of Dublin. To the south, the road splits into Westmoreland Street and D'Olier Street, which lead toward Trinity College and the old Parliament building on College Green. The bridge is the hinge between these two halves of the city. For more than two centuries, Dublin has funnelled its daily life through this one point on the river.

The first bridge here was built in the late eighteenth century. It was designed by James Gandon, the architect who also gave Dublin the Custom House and the Four Courts. At that time it carried a different name — Carlisle Bridge, after a British official who served in Ireland. Gandon's version was much narrower than what you see now. It had a humped profile, so carriages climbed up and then rolled down the other side.

By the nineteenth century, that old bridge could not cope. Dublin had grown, and traffic across the river had grown with it. In the 1870s the city rebuilt and widened the crossing, flattening the hump and giving it the generous width you are standing on today. When the new bridge opened, it took a new name. It was dedicated to Daniel O'Connell, the great nineteenth-century campaigner for Catholic rights in Ireland, often remembered as the Liberator.

If you turn to face north, you can see O'Connell himself. His monument stands at the foot of the street that carries his name, a tall bronze figure above a crowd of sculpted figures. Those lower figures represent the Irish people. Look closely at the monument when you walk past it. The bronze carries small holes and dents. These are marks from bullets fired during the fighting of the Easter Rising in 1916, when this street was at the centre of the rebellion.

Now look at the bridge itself. Lean over the parapet and you will see three stone arches carrying the road across the river. Along the edges run carved balustrades, and on the central islands stand tall, ornate lamp standards. These cast-iron lamps give the bridge much of its character after dark. At night their light falls in soft pools on the pavement, and their reflections break up on the moving water below.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a very Dublin one. Somewhere on this bridge, set into the stonework, there was once a small plaque. It honoured a man called Father Pat Noise, and claimed he had died in a carriage accident on the river. There was no such priest. The plaque was a prank, placed quietly by a pair of jokers in the early 2000s. For a while, nobody official noticed. When the city did notice, the story had already become part of local folklore. Ask a Dubliner about it, and you may get a grin.

Now turn and look along the river. To the west, upstream, you can see the delicate white arc of the Ha'penny Bridge, a slim iron footbridge from the early nineteenth century. Its name comes from the small toll once charged to cross it. Beyond it, the quays stretch away, lined with old houses, pubs and the dome of the Four Courts in the distance. It is one of the classic views of the city.

To the east, downstream, the view is different. A heavy railway bridge, the Loopline, crosses the river close by. Many Dubliners have complained about it for generations, because it blocks the view of the Custom House beyond. That building is another of Gandon's works, a long classical façade with a green copper dome. Walk a little way east along the quay and the view opens up again. Gandon's two works face the same water, one carrying traffic, the other once collecting the taxes of a trading port.

Pause and listen to the river. The Liffey here is tidal. Twice a day the sea pushes in and the water rises along the stone walls, then drains back out again. At low tide you may see mud banks and the green weed on the quay walls. At high tide the water sits close and full beneath the arches. The gulls follow the tide, and so does the smell of salt on a breezy morning.

This bridge has also seen the city's great public moments. Parades cross it on Saint Patrick's Day. Marches and protests pass over it on their way to Government buildings or the old Parliament. Crowds gather here for celebrations and for grief. Because it sits at the meeting point of north and south Dublin, it has long been a natural stage for the whole city. Even on an ordinary weekday, it is a good place to watch Dublin go about its business.

So before you move on, take one last look. Feel how wide the ground is under your feet, and remember that this is a bridge, not a square. Watch the lamps, the arches, the gulls, the green water heading for the sea. Then choose your direction — north toward O'Connell and his scarred bronze, or south toward Trinity. Either way, you are walking the same line Dubliners have walked for more than two hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross at the traffic lights; this junction is very busy.
Walk east along the quay for a clear Custom House view.
Visit after dark to see the lamps lit over the water.
Combine with Trinity College and the Ha'penny Bridge on foot.
Check the tide; high water looks fuller beneath the arches.
