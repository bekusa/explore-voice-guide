The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and tilt your head back. A thin line of brushed steel runs straight up from the pavement and seems to thin out into the sky. On a grey Dublin day it turns almost silver. On a bright one it catches the light and glows. All around you, buses sigh at their stops, footsteps cross the wide median, and the noise of O'Connell Street carries on. The Spire itself makes no sound at all.

You are standing beside the Spire of Dublin. Its official name is the Monument of Light, and in Irish it is called An Túr Solais. It rises about 120 metres from the centre of the city's main street. That makes it one of the tallest structures in central Dublin. Yet it is remarkably slender. At the base it is roughly three metres across. At the very top, it narrows to something closer to the width of your hand.

The material is stainless steel. Up close, you can see that the surface is not mirror smooth. It has a soft, brushed finish that takes on the colour of the weather. Grey sky makes it grey. A sunset can turn it gold. Look carefully at the lower section, near eye level, and you may notice a textured pattern worked into the steel. That texture gives your hand something to read. Many visitors touch it without thinking.

Now look at the very tip. The top part of the Spire is pierced with many small openings. After dark, lights inside shine out through them. The effect is a faint point of light hanging high above the street. On a clear evening it looks almost like a star that has settled on the city. On a misty night it softens into a glow. This lit tip is where the name Monument of Light comes from.

Here is something many visitors miss. The Spire stands on the site of an older monument. For well over a century, this spot held Nelson's Pillar. It was a tall stone column topped by a statue of Admiral Horatio Nelson, the British naval hero. For many Dubliners, a British admiral at the heart of their main street was a difficult symbol. The pillar was a meeting point, but it was also a reminder of empire.

In 1966, a bomb destroyed the upper part of Nelson's Pillar. The explosion happened at night, and the stone statue came crashing down. The attack came in the year Ireland marked fifty years since the Easter Rising. Afterwards, the damaged stump was removed by controlled demolition. That second blast caused more damage to the surrounding shopfronts than the first one did. For decades afterwards, the space stood empty.

And a small surprise. Nelson's stone head survived. It was later kept by the city, and it is held in the Dublin City Library and Archive on Pearse Street. So a piece of the old monument still exists, just a short walk across the river from where it once stood. If you enjoy layers of history, it is worth seeking out.

For years, the city debated what should fill the gap. In the end, an international design competition chose a proposal from the London firm Ian Ritchie Architects. The idea was simple and bold. Instead of a statue of a person, there would be no person at all. Just a clean vertical line of light and steel. It belonged to no single hero and no single cause.

The project was planned with the new millennium in mind. Legal challenges and planning delays pushed the work back. The steel sections were lifted into place and joined together, and the Spire was completed in early 2003. Some Dubliners loved it immediately. Others thought it was cold, or expensive, or simply strange. Opinions are still divided today, and that debate is part of its story.

Dubliners answered in the way they often do — with nicknames. You may hear it called the Spike. Locals also invented rhyming names, such as the Stiletto in the Ghetto and the Stiffy by the Liffey. These names are affectionate and cheeky at the same time. They show how quickly a new structure can become part of everyday conversation. If you ask a Dubliner for directions, they may simply say, meet me at the Spire.

Now turn and look across the street. The large building with the stone columns is the General Post Office. During the Easter Rising of 1916, rebels used it as their headquarters. Look closely at the columns and the stone of the front. You can still find marks left by bullets from that week. Standing here, you are at the centre of the street where modern Ireland was shaped.

O'Connell Street itself is broad and lined with statues. At the southern end, near the river Liffey, stands the monument to Daniel O'Connell, the nineteenth-century leader the street is named after. Closer to you, look for the figure of James Larkin, the labour leader, with his arms raised high. Just off the street, Henry Street leads to shops, and nearby Moore Street still has market traders calling out their prices.

Before you move on, step back a few paces and look up again. Watch how the Spire seems to disappear into the clouds on a low day. Notice how people use its base — waiting for friends, checking phones, catching a bus. The old pillar was a monument to one man. This one is simply a line pointing upward. As you walk away, glance back over your shoulder. You will probably still see it, rising above the rooftops.
