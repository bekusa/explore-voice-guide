The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beneath the voices of families and the rustle of old trees, you may catch something unexpected: the low call of a gibbon, a splash from the sea lion pool, or the rumble of an elephant somewhere beyond the hedges. This is the sound of Dublin Zoo, inside Phoenix Park. A short walk from the River Liffey, the city falls away and a much wider world begins.

Phoenix Park itself sets the scene. It is one of the largest walled parks in any European capital. It holds deer herds, wide grass plains, and long avenues of trees. The zoo sits in a corner of this green space. Its lake and wooded paths feel less like a city attraction and more like a garden that slowly filled with animals.

The story begins in the early nineteenth century. In 1830, a group of Dublin doctors, scientists, and gentlemen formed a society to study animals. They called it the Zoological Society of Dublin, and it later became the Royal Zoological Society of Ireland. The zoo opened to the public in 1831. That makes it one of the oldest zoos in the world still in operation.

Those first years were modest. The founders had little money, and the early collection was small. Visitors paid a small fee to walk the grounds, and Sunday visits became a popular habit for working Dubliners. For generations of families here, a trip to the zoo was a rite of childhood. Many people walking beside you today first came here holding a parent's hand.

Here is a surprise that many visitors miss. In the nineteenth century, Dublin Zoo became famous for breeding lions. Lion cubs born here were sent to zoos across Europe, and the zoo built a strong reputation for raising them. There is also a popular story that the roaring lion in the old film company logo of MGM was born in Dublin. The tale is often repeated, though it is hard to confirm, so treat it as local legend.

As you walk, notice the older buildings among the newer habitats. One Victorian building is Haughton House, named after Samuel Haughton, a scientist and doctor who was active in the society in the nineteenth century. Its red brick and decorative style belong to that earlier age. It is a reminder that the zoo was once a place of learned meetings as well as family outings.

The modern zoo looks very different from those early days. Over recent decades, small cages gave way to large, planted habitats. The aim is to give animals space, shade, and places to hide. Water, rocks, and trees now separate people from animals instead of bars. This means you may need patience. An animal resting deep in its habitat is a sign the design is working.

One of the largest areas is the African Plains. Here, open grassland stretches out under the Irish sky. You may see giraffes moving slowly across the ground, their heads level with the treetops. Zebras, rhinos, and other grassland animals share this landscape. It is a strange and pleasing sight: African animals grazing beneath the soft grey clouds of Dublin.

Then there is the Kaziranga Forest Trail, home to the zoo's Asian elephants. The name comes from Kaziranga, a national park in India known for its wildlife. The trail winds through bamboo and dense planting, so you see the elephants in stages. Watch how they use their trunks — to spray water, to pull at branches, to touch one another. A family group of elephants is a busy, social place.

The primate areas reward a slower pace. Gorillas live in a planted rainforest habitat, and you can often watch them sitting quietly, eating or grooming. Orangutans climb high ropes and platforms. Smaller monkeys and gibbons live on islands in the lake, and the water keeps them apart from visitors. Listen for the gibbons especially. Their calls can carry right across the zoo in the morning.

The lake itself is worth a pause. Waterbirds drift across it, and flamingos sometimes stand in pink groups near the shore. Willows lean over the water, and the light changes the colour of the surface through the day. From the paths around the lake, you can see several habitats at once. It is one of the calmest places to stand and simply watch.

Today, Dublin Zoo is one of Ireland's most visited family attractions, with around a million visitors in a typical year. But its work goes beyond the paths you are walking. The zoo takes part in international breeding programmes for endangered species. Many animals here belong to carefully managed groups shared between zoos. Each birth can matter for a species that is struggling in the wild. For children, the Family Farm offers a different kind of encounter. Here, the animals are familiar ones — goats, chickens, and other farm breeds. It gives younger visitors a chance to see animals up close. It also connects the zoo to rural Irish life, which is never far away, even in the capital.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Look at the old trees above you, some of them older than anyone here today. Listen again for the mix of sounds — the children, the birds, perhaps a distant call from the elephants. Nearly two centuries of Dubliners have walked these paths. And as you step back out into Phoenix Park, notice the wide open grass and the wild deer. The city has kept its own animals too.

Practical tips

Arrive early to hear the gibbons calling across the lake.
Bring patience; animals often rest deep within their habitats.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walking paths and slopes.
Pack a rain jacket for sudden Dublin showers.
Combine your visit with a walk through Phoenix Park.
