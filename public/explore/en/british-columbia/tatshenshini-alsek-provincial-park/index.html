The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wind there is a low, steady hiss. It is the sound of silt moving in fast grey water, millions of tiny grains of ground rock carried down from the ice. The air is cold even in summer, and it smells of wet gravel, willow, and something mineral, like a freshly split stone. You are in Tatshenshini-Alsek Provincial Park, in the far northwestern corner of British Columbia, where the province meets the Yukon and Alaska.

This is one of the largest protected wild areas in Canada. There are no towns inside it. There are almost no roads. Most of the park is mountain, glacier, and river valley, with ice that runs down toward the Pacific coast. The two rivers in its name, the Tatshenshini and the Alsek, cut through the coastal mountains. That is rare. Few rivers in this part of the world manage to break through such high ranges to reach the sea.

Because of those river corridors, this landscape works like a doorway. Moist air from the Gulf of Alaska pushes inland along the valleys. Plants and animals from the coast move inland too. The result is a meeting zone, where coastal rainforest species and northern interior species live close together. Within a single day on the water, a traveller can pass from dry interior slopes to dense, dripping coastal green, and then into a world of ice.

Here is the first surprise. This park exists largely because of a mine that was never built. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, a company proposed a very large open-pit copper mine at a mountain called Windy Craggy, inside this watershed. Conservation groups in Canada and the United States feared the effect on the rivers and the salmon. The campaign drew international attention. In 1993, the British Columbia government chose protection instead, and the park was created.

The recognition did not stop at the provincial border. In 1994, the park joined a group of neighbouring protected areas as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. UNESCO is the United Nations body that lists places of outstanding value to all of humanity. The shared site includes Kluane in the Yukon, and Wrangell-Saint Elias and Glacier Bay in Alaska. Together they form one of the largest international protected areas on Earth, crossing borders that the ice and the animals simply ignore.

People were here long before any of those borders. This is the traditional territory of the Champagne and Aishihik First Nations, whose ancestors travelled these valleys for trade, fishing, and hunting. Trails once linked the interior with the coast, carrying goods like fish oil and copper. Today the park is managed cooperatively by BC Parks and the Champagne and Aishihik First Nations. Their place names, stories, and knowledge are part of how this land is cared for and understood.

The second surprise came out of the ice itself. In 1999, a group of sheep hunters found human remains melting out of a glacier inside the park. The man had died several hundred years earlier, and the cold had preserved him, along with tools and a robe made of animal skins. He was given the name Kwäday Dän Ts'ìnchį, which means Long Ago Person Found. Scientists and First Nations communities studied the find together, and DNA testing later linked him to living people in the region.

The wildlife here moves on a large scale. Grizzly bears walk the gravel bars and river flats. Mountain goats hold the steep cliffs, small white shapes against dark rock. Bald eagles watch the water. And there is one animal that many visitors hope for and few see: the glacier bear. It is a black bear with fur of a pale blue-grey colour, a rare form found mainly in this coastal corner of the continent. Spotting one is a matter of luck.

The rivers are also salmon rivers. Each year, salmon swim up from the ocean to spawn in these cold waters. Their journey feeds the bears, the eagles, and the forest itself, because nutrients from the fish end up in the soil along the banks. For the people of this region, salmon have mattered for thousands of years. The concern for these fish was one of the strongest reasons people fought to keep the watershed protected.

Most visitors who travel deep into the park do it by raft. A classic trip starts in the Yukon and follows the Tatshenshini down into the Alsek, ending at Dry Bay on the Alaska coast. These journeys take many days. There are no bridges, no stores, and no easy way out. The river is cold, fast, and full of silt, so you cannot see the bottom. Travellers usually join experienced guides who know the channels and the weather.

Near the end of that journey, the river opens into Alsek Lake. Glaciers reach the water here, and large pieces of ice break away and float in the lake. Some are white. Some are a deep, clear blue, because dense old ice absorbs red light and leaves the blue behind. On a calm day you may hear a heavy crack, then a long rumble, as ice falls somewhere out of sight. The sound travels a long way across the water.

If you are not on the river, you can still touch the edge of this place. The Haines Highway runs through the eastern part of the park, between the Yukon and Haines in Alaska. From the road, you see wide valleys, braided channels, and snow on the peaks even in midsummer. Above the far ranges, out of view from most places, rises Mount Fairweather, the highest point in British Columbia, on the border with Alaska.

Weather shapes everything here. Clouds can drop low in minutes. Rain can turn to sleet at higher ground, even in July. Long summer days give the light a soft, lasting quality, and dusk can stretch late into the night. The scale is hard to judge. A slope that looks close may be hours away on foot. This is a place to move slowly, to respect, and to watch.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the colour of the water, that pale, milky grey. Every drop came from ice that has been here far longer than any road or border. Listen again for the hiss of silt, the wind in the willows, maybe a raven calling. When you turn away, the river will keep running to the sea, exactly as it did long before anyone thought to protect it.

Before you go

Practical tips

River trips need a permit; most people join licensed outfitters.
Pack waterproof layers even for a summer visit.
Use the Haines Highway for a roadside view of the park.
Carry bear spray and learn how to use it.
Keep binoculars ready for goats on the cliffs.
