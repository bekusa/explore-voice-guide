The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, trucks and cars rush along the main highway between Tbilisi and the west of Georgia. In front of you, the ground rises into a quiet, grassy hill. Under your feet is dry earth, loose stone and short, tough grass. The wind comes up from the river valley and carries the smell of warm soil. The traffic and the old hill make a strange pair. Ordinary roadside land on one side. On the other, one of the most important archaeological sites found in Georgia in recent decades.

This is Grakliani Hill, in the Shida Kartli region, near the town of Kaspi. Shida Kartli means Inner Kartli, the old heartland of eastern Georgia. The Mtkvari River runs through this broad valley, and you will know it by its other name too, the Kura. For thousands of years, people moved along this corridor between east and west. Traders, farmers, soldiers and priests all passed this way. The hill stood beside that route. From its top, a person could watch the river, the fields and the road all at once.

The discovery itself is part of the story. For a long time, this was simply a hill beside the road. Then, in the late 2000s, work began on upgrading the highway. Construction crews and archaeologists surveyed the land, as they must before major building in Georgia. What they found changed the plans. Pottery, walls and burials started to appear. Excavations followed, led by archaeologists from Tbilisi State University. Professor Vakhtang Licheli became the name most closely linked with the site. Digging has continued season after season since then.

Here is the first surprise. Grakliani is not one site from one period. It is many sites stacked on top of each other. Archaeologists describe layers of human activity that reach back to the Stone Age and run forward through the Bronze Age, the Iron Age and into later centuries. Think of the hill as a tall pile of pages. Each generation wrote its own page on top of the last. Excavation means turning those pages slowly, one thin layer of soil at a time, and recording everything before it is removed.

As you walk, notice how the hill is shaped. It is not a smooth dome. It rises in terraces, like broad steps cut into the slope. People levelled these platforms to build houses, workshops and sacred buildings. On the upper parts of the hill, excavators uncovered the remains of a temple complex. Low stone foundations still mark where walls once stood. Today they look modest, only knee-high in many places. But they outline rooms, passages and spaces where rituals once took place.

The temple is one of the reasons Grakliani matters so much. Archaeologists found altars, offerings and figurines connected with worship. Many researchers link the sanctuary to a female deity, perhaps a goddess of fertility and the harvest. The exact name of that goddess is still debated, so treat any single answer with caution. What is clear is that this hilltop was a holy place. People climbed here with gifts, and they returned for generations.

Now the second surprise, and the find that brought Grakliani into newspapers far beyond Georgia. In 2015, the team uncovered an altar with an inscription carved into its base. The letters do not match any known script from the region. Excavators dated it to the first millennium before Christ, many centuries before the Georgian alphabet developed. Some researchers suggested it might be among the earliest local writing ever found in this part of the Caucasus. The script has not been deciphered. Nobody can yet read what those carved signs say.

Pause on that idea for a moment. Somewhere on this hill, someone carved a message in stone. They expected it to be read. Perhaps it named a god, a donor, or a prayer. And now, more than two thousand years later, the message survives, but its meaning does not. Scholars continue to study it. Some think it may be related to scripts used elsewhere in the ancient Near East. Others urge patience until more examples are found.

Grakliani also shows how connected this valley was to the wider ancient world. In the middle of the first millennium before Christ, much of the region sat on the edge of the Persian Empire of the Achaemenid kings, the great dynasty of ancient Iran. Later, Greek styles and ideas spread across the Near East after Alexander the Great. Finds from Grakliani carry traces of both worlds. Jewellery, ceramics and building styles show local traditions mixing with influences from far away.

The burials found here add a human scale to all of this. Archaeologists excavated graves with personal objects placed beside the dead. These included jewellery, vessels and tools. Each grave is a small, private decision made by a grieving family. What to send with someone. How to lay them down. Which way to face them. Studying these choices helps researchers understand beliefs about death and status. It also reminds us that the people here were individuals, not just a culture in a textbook.

Take a look at the excavation areas if they are open to view. You may see neat square trenches with straight walls, string lines and labelled tags. This careful grid is how archaeologists keep track of exactly where each object came from. Location matters as much as the object itself. A bead found inside a temple room means something different from the same bead found in a rubbish pit. In summer, students and volunteers often work here, kneeling in the dust with small trowels and brushes.

There is also a lesson here about modern Georgia. The highway that revealed the site still runs right beside it. Engineers and archaeologists had to find a balance between a busy road and a fragile past. That tension exists in many countries. At Grakliani, you can see both sides at once. The future roars past on asphalt. The past waits quietly under a few centimetres of soil.

Before you leave, climb to a high point if you can safely do so. Look along the valley. The river line, the distant ridges and the green or golden fields are the same basic landscape that ancient people saw. The crops and the roads have changed. The shape of the land has not. It is easy to understand why people chose this hill. It offered height, water nearby, good soil and a clear view of anyone approaching.

So take a last look at the low stone walls around you. Feel the wind again, and listen to the mix of birds, grass and distant traffic. As you walk back down, watch the ground. Small fragments of red and brown pottery sometimes lie on the surface of sites like this. Leave them exactly where they are. They belong to the hill's long story... a story that is still being read, one layer at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for loose stone and uneven terraces.
Bring water and sun protection on exposed slopes.
Stay outside fenced trenches and marked excavation zones.
Visit in summer to possibly see archaeologists at work.
Combine with a stop in nearby Kaspi or Gori.
