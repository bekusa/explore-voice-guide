The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves through the long grass, and somewhere below you the River Boyne slides past in a slow, wide curve. The ground here rises and falls in soft green swells. Some of those swells are natural. Others were built by human hands, stone by stone, more than five thousand years ago. You are standing in one of the oldest ritual landscapes in Europe.

The name Brú na Bóinne is Irish. It roughly means the palace, or the dwelling, of the Boyne. The river bends here in a great loop, and inside that loop Neolithic farmers raised a cluster of monuments. The Neolithic is the New Stone Age, the period when people first settled down, cleared forests, and grew crops. Three great mounds stand above the rest. Their names are Newgrange, Knowth and Dowth. Around them lie dozens of smaller tombs, standing stones and earthworks.

Here is the first surprise. The great mound at Newgrange is usually dated to around 3200 BC. That makes it older than the stone circle at Stonehenge in its familiar form. It is also older than the great pyramids of Giza. When Egypt was still centuries away from its first pyramid, people in this valley were already hauling huge stones across the fields. In 1993, UNESCO recognised the whole bend of the river as a World Heritage Site.

Look at Newgrange from the outside. The mound is round and broad, with a flattened top covered in grass. Its front is faced with a bright wall of white quartz, studded with darker rounded stones. That wall is a modern reconstruction. Archaeologists rebuilt it in the late twentieth century, based on the quartz they found scattered at the foot of the mound. Not every scholar agrees that the original looked like this. So, as you admire it, remember that you are seeing one careful interpretation of the past.

Now walk your eyes along the base of the mound. A ring of large flat stones runs all the way around it, nearly a hundred of them, laid end to end. These are the kerbstones. They hold the mound in place, like a belt. In front of the entrance lies the most famous of them. Its surface is carved with deep spirals, lozenges and curving lines. Nobody knows what the patterns meant. Perhaps they were symbols of the sun, of water, or of the journey of the dead. The honest answer is that the meaning is lost.

Above the doorway sits a small rectangular opening, like a stone window. This is the roof-box, and it holds the second surprise. At dawn around the winter solstice, near the twenty-first of December, the rising sun shines straight through this gap. A thin beam travels down the long passage and lights the floor of the inner chamber. It lasts only a short while, roughly a quarter of an hour. Then the light withdraws, and the chamber returns to darkness for another year.

The builders had no metal tools and no written language. Yet they aligned this passage to the sun with great precision. They also had to account for the slope of the ground, because the passage floor rises as it goes inward. The roof-box lets the light enter high and still reach the back. The modern rediscovery of this effect came in 1967, when the archaeologist Michael O'Kelly stood inside the chamber at dawn and watched the light arrive.

If you go inside, the passage is narrow and low in places. You may need to turn sideways. The stones on either side lean in close, and some carry their own carvings. Then the space opens into a chamber shaped like a cross, with three small side recesses. Look up. The roof is built from overlapping slabs, each one stepping inward until a single capstone closes the top. This kind of roof is called corbelling. After five thousand years, it still keeps the rain out.

A short distance west lies Knowth. Its great mound is similar in size to Newgrange, but it feels different. It holds two separate passages, one facing east and one facing west. Around it cluster many smaller satellite tombs, like moons around a planet. Knowth also holds one of the largest collections of megalithic art in Western Europe. Megalithic simply means made with very large stones. Many of the carved kerbstones here are covered edge to edge in spirals, circles and wavy lines.

Knowth did not fall silent after the Stone Age. People returned to it again and again. In the early medieval period, it became a royal seat, with houses and defences built on and around the old mound. Later still, the Normans used it, after they arrived in Ireland in the twelfth century. So the site carries layers of very different lives, one on top of another, spread across thousands of years.

The third great mound, Dowth, stands to the east. It is the least excavated of the three and is not part of the guided tours. You can view it from the roadside. Its top is scarred by an old crater, the result of careless digging in the nineteenth century. In a way, Dowth shows you what these monuments looked like before modern archaeology. It is quiet, overgrown, and still keeps many of its questions.

This valley also lives in Irish mythology. Locals and old stories say that Brú na Bóinne was the home of the Tuatha Dé Danann, a race of gods in Irish legend. According to these tales, the great god known as the Dagda lived here, and later his son Aengus, a figure linked with love and youth. These are legends, not history. But they show how the people of later centuries saw the mounds — as doorways to another world.

As you stand here now, let your eyes travel across the fields. Notice how the mounds sit on the higher ground, looking down toward the water. Notice the cattle, the hedgerows, the low grey sky or the soft gold light. The river still runs where it ran when the first stones were dragged up this slope. And when you walk away, look back once. The mound will still be there, holding its dark chamber, waiting for the short winter dawn.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book your tour well ahead, especially in summer.
Start at the Visitor Centre; monuments are reached by shuttle.
Allow several hours to see both Newgrange and Knowth.
Bring a waterproof layer; the fields are exposed to weather.
Skip the Newgrange chamber if tight spaces worry you.
