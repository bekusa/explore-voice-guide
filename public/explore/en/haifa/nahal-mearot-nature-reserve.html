The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the foot of the cliff. The limestone above you is pale and pitted, streaked with grey and rust. A breeze comes up the valley from the coast, carrying the smell of dry grass and sage. Below, the coastal plain stretches flat toward the Mediterranean. The four dark openings in the rock face behind you have looked out over this same view for an extraordinarily long time... and people have been looking out from them for almost as long.

This is Nahal Me'arot, on the western slope of Mount Carmel, south of Haifa. The Hebrew name means the Stream of the Caves. In Arabic, the valley is called Wadi el-Mughara, which carries the same meaning. In 2012, UNESCO added these caves to its World Heritage List. The reason is simple to say and hard to absorb. This small valley holds one of the longest records of human life found anywhere on Earth.

The cliff itself is part of the story. Mount Carmel is built largely from limestone, rock formed on an ancient sea floor long before any humans existed. Water slowly dissolved the softer layers, and caves opened in the rock face. Their position was ideal. The caves sit where the valley meets the plain, with fresh water, shelter, and a wide view of the land below. Hunters could watch for animals moving across the flatlands from a safe, high place.

The modern discovery of the caves began in the late 1920s. The British Mandate authorities needed stone for building the port of Haifa, and quarrying started near here. Archaeologists became concerned that the caves would be lost. Between 1929 and 1934, a team led by the British archaeologist Dorothy Garrod carried out major excavations. What they found changed how scientists thought about early humans in this part of the world.

Dorothy Garrod deserves a pause of her own. She was a pioneer in a field that was dominated by men. A few years after her work here, she became the first woman to hold a professorship at the University of Cambridge. Here is a detail many visitors miss — much of the digging at these caves was done by local women from nearby villages. Garrod hired them and trained them. Old photographs show them working in long dresses, carrying baskets of earth out of the caves.

The cave with the deepest record is Tabun. Its name means oven in Arabic. Layer upon layer of sediment built up inside it, and each layer held traces of the people who lived there. Archaeologists believe the human story in Tabun stretches back roughly half a million years. That makes it one of the longest continuous sequences of human activity ever studied. Stone tools change slowly from layer to layer, like pages in a very thick book.

Tabun also held a famous burial. In one layer, the excavators found the skeleton of a Neanderthal woman. Neanderthals were a separate human species, close relatives of our own, who lived across Europe and western Asia. Finding one here, at the southern edge of their range, was important. It showed that Neanderthals had walked these same slopes, sheltered in this same rock, and watched the same coastline that you can see today.

Now look along the cliff toward the cave called Skhul. Here the team found something different — the remains of early modern humans, people of our own species, buried around one hundred thousand years ago. Some of these burials appear to have been made with care. So within a short walk, you have evidence of two kinds of humans. Scientists still debate how these groups lived in the region over time. Did they meet? Did they take turns? The questions remain open.

The largest of the caves is el-Wad. It is a long, deep cavern, and the trail allows visitors to walk into its cool interior. The air changes as soon as you step inside. The temperature drops, and the light from the entrance fades into shadow. Near its mouth, archaeologists found remains of the Natufian culture. The Natufians lived in this region from roughly fifteen thousand to eleven and a half thousand years ago.

The Natufians matter because they stood at a turning point in human history. They were still hunters and gatherers, but they began to stay in one place for longer periods. They built stone structures, buried their dead in groups, and harvested wild cereals. Many researchers see them as the people just before farming began. The ideas that led to villages, fields, and eventually cities were taking shape among communities like the one that lived here.

The fourth cave, Jamal, is the smallest. Today it holds a reconstruction that helps visitors imagine daily life in prehistoric times. Figures and objects suggest how people might have worked, cooked, and rested in a space like this. It is a good place to slow down. Try to picture the smoke from a fire, the sound of stone being shaped into tools, and voices echoing against the rock.

Outside the caves, the reserve has walking trails through Mediterranean scrubland. Low oaks, shrubs, and wild herbs cover the slopes. In spring, the hillside turns green, and wildflowers appear among the rocks. In summer, the land is dry and gold, and the limestone holds the heat of the day. Some trails climb steeply, with steps cut into the slope. From higher points, the view opens across the plain toward the sea.

Before you leave, turn and face the cave mouths one more time. The same light that falls on the rock now fell on it when Neanderthals sat here, and when early modern humans buried their dead in Skhul. As you walk back down the path, notice the sound of your own footsteps on the stone. You are the latest visitor in a line that reaches back hundreds of thousands of years... and the cliff is still here, looking out to sea.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep trail steps and rocky paths.
Bring water, especially on hot summer afternoons.
Visit in spring to see the hillsides green and flowering.
Walk deep into el-Wad Cave to feel the cooler air.
Climb the higher trail for views across the coastal plain.
