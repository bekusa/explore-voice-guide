The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Up here on the hill above Magadan, it rarely stops. It pushes in from the Sea of Okhotsk, cold and salty, and it moves through the grass around your feet. In front of you rises a huge concrete face. It is grey, rough, and heavy. It looks down toward the city and the water beyond. Before you read any plaque or hear any date, just stand for a moment. Let the size of it settle on you.

This is the Mask of Sorrow. It was unveiled in 1996, a few years after the end of the Soviet Union. It stands on Krutaya Hill, which means steep hill, and it is about fifteen metres tall. The monument remembers the prisoners of the Kolyma labour camps. Hundreds of thousands of people were sent to this region during the Soviet years. Many of them never returned home. Magadan was the gateway through which most of them passed.

The sculptor was Ernst Neizvestny, one of the most famous and independent Russian artists of the twentieth century. His surname, by chance, means unknown. He fought in the Second World War and was badly wounded. In 1962, he argued openly with the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev at an art exhibition in Moscow. That was a dangerous thing to do. Years later, after Khrushchev died, his family asked Neizvestny to make his tombstone. The artist later emigrated and worked abroad for many years.

Now look closely at the face itself. From a distance, it seems simple. Up close, it is full of detail. Below the left eye, tears are falling. But these tears are not drops of water. Each tear is a small mask, a little human face. They run down the cheek like a stream of people. Many visitors see this only after several minutes. Once you notice it, you cannot stop seeing it.

The right eye is different. It is not an eye at all. It is a window with bars, like the window of a prison cell. Through it, you can see the sky behind the monument. This is one of the quiet surprises of the place. The face both weeps and looks out from behind bars. It is a mourner and a prisoner at the same moment.

Walk around to the back of the monument. Many people stay at the front and miss this side completely. Here, the sculpture shows other figures. You will find a young woman who is weeping. There is also a headless man on a cross. The back of the mask feels more private than the front. It faces away from the city, and it is often quieter here, out of the strongest wind.

Inside the structure, there is a small room made to look like a prison cell from the Stalin era. It is simple and bare, with a narrow bed and a barred window. The space is cramped on purpose. When you stand inside, the outside world feels very far away. Take a moment here if it is open during your visit. It turns the history from something abstract into something you can feel with your own body.

To understand why this monument stands in Magadan, think about geography. Kolyma is a remote region in the far northeast of Russia. In the Soviet period, it was rich in gold and other metals. The state used forced labour to dig them out. Prisoners came by train across the country, and then by ship across the sea. Many of them landed at Nagaevo Bay, the harbour you can see below the city.

From Magadan, prisoners travelled inland on a long road into the hills and the frozen valleys. This road is often called the Road of Bones. According to a common local belief, people who died building it were buried inside the roadbed itself. Historians still discuss how literal that story is. But the name has stayed, and many people in the region use it today. It tells you how deeply this past runs through the landscape.

One of the most famous voices from Kolyma is the writer Varlam Shalamov. He spent many years in the camps here. Later, he wrote a series of short stories known in English as Kolyma Tales. They are spare, cold, and very direct. Many readers say they understand this monument better after reading even one of his stories. If you travel with a book, his would be a fitting companion on this hill.

Around the base of the monument, look for the stones and markers. Some of them carry the names of camps from across the region. Others carry the symbols of different faiths. People of many nations and religions died in Kolyma. The monument does not belong to only one group. It was made for all of them. Sometimes you will find flowers or small personal objects left here by visitors.

Now turn and look out at the view. Below you is the modern city of Magadan, with its apartment blocks, its streets, and its port. Beyond it is the sea, often grey, sometimes silver when the light breaks through. The contrast is strong. Ordinary daily life continues below. Up here, the hill holds the memory of the people who built much of that city under force.

The weather on Krutaya Hill changes quickly. Fog can come in from the water and cover the face in minutes. In winter, snow gathers in the folds of the concrete and on the small masks of the tears. In summer, the light can stay late into the evening. Each visit looks different. The monument was made from plain concrete, and it carries every change of light and weather clearly.

Before you walk away, look one more time at the barred eye. Notice what you can see through it right now — a patch of cloud, perhaps, or a piece of blue sky. Listen again to the wind across the hill. Then, as you walk down toward the city, look back once. The great face will keep watching the harbour, as it does every day, long after you have gone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk all the way around to see the back figures.
Bring a windproof layer even in summer.
Come in clear weather for views over the harbour.
Read one of Shalamov's Kolyma Tales before visiting.
Allow quiet time inside the reconstructed prison cell.
