The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here on the slope above the Ilm, the city noise thins out. You hear leaves, a few birds, perhaps footsteps on the path. And in front of you is a white box. It has no gables and no decoration, and its corners are sharp and clean. A taller square block rises from its centre like a lantern. On a quiet street of older villas, it still looks a little startling, and that was exactly the point.

This is the Haus am Horn, named after the street and the hillside it stands on. It was built in 1923 as the model house for the first great public exhibition of the Bauhaus, the art and design school founded in Weimar in 1919. The school had been promising a new way to live. Critics and the city were asking to see results. This small house was the answer, and it was the first building ever made to Bauhaus ideas.

The design came from an unexpected person. Georg Muche was not an architect. He was a painter, and at the Bauhaus he was a master in the weaving workshop. His design was chosen for the exhibition house. The practical building work was then handled through the office of Walter Gropius, the school's director, with his partner Adolf Meyer. So a painter's idea of a home became concrete, steel and plaster on this hillside.

Look at the shape again and imagine the floor plan. The heart of the house is one large square living room. All the other rooms sit around it like a ring: the kitchen, a dining area, bedrooms, a children's room, a bathroom, a small study. There are no long corridors. Each room has one clear job. The central room is taller than the rest. Its high windows sit above the surrounding roofs and let daylight fall from above.

Here is something many visitors miss. Because of those high windows, the living room has very little wall space at eye level for looking out. Muche wanted the centre of the house to feel calm and protected, a room for gathering rather than watching the street. The views belong to the smaller rooms around it. Stand in the middle, look up, and notice how the light changes as clouds pass over Weimar.

The exhibition motto was 'Art and Technology, a New Unity'. The Haus am Horn was meant to prove it in everyday life. The Bauhaus workshops furnished the interior together. Weavers made the textiles. Metal and woodworkers made lamps, fittings and furniture. Marcel Breuer, then a young student, designed furniture for one of the rooms. Alma Buscher created the children's room, with colourful pieces that a child could rearrange and play with.

The kitchen deserves a slow look. Benita Otte and Ernst Gebhardt planned it around the work a person actually does there. There is a long, continuous work surface under a window, with cupboards above and below. Everything is within reach, and the cook works in daylight. Today this feels completely normal. In 1923, it was a quiet revolution. Historians often describe it as an early ancestor of the fitted kitchen that later spread across Europe.

The building also tested new materials and methods. The goal was a house that could be built quickly and economically, and perhaps repeated many times. The Bauhaus had larger hopes for this hillside. Plans existed for a whole settlement of Bauhaus houses here on the Horn. Those plans were never realised. So this single house stands alone, a sample of a neighbourhood that only ever existed on paper.

The reaction in 1923 was mixed. Many visitors came to Weimar to see the exhibition, and the house drew curiosity as well as criticism. Some people praised its logic and its light. Others found it cold, strange or too small. Weimar was a conservative city, proud of Goethe and Schiller, and the Bauhaus never had an easy relationship with local politics. Within a few years, the school left Weimar and moved to Dessau.

After the exhibition, the house became a private home. Over the following decades, owners changed it to suit their own needs, and some of the original character was lost. That is the normal life of a house, and it is also part of this story. Later, careful restoration work brought the building back towards its 1923 appearance. It reopened to the public in 2019, the year the Bauhaus celebrated its hundredth anniversary.

In 1996, the Haus am Horn became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, together with other Bauhaus places in Weimar and Dessau. That listing was later extended to include Bernau, near Berlin. It is a large honour for a modest single-storey house. But the value here is not size. It is the moment it records, when a group of artists tried to design ordinary life from the ground up.

Turn and look down the slope. Below you lies the Park an der Ilm, with its meadows, trees and winding paths. Somewhere down there is the small garden house where Goethe once lived and worked. Think about that distance. A short walk separates a poet's retreat from a laboratory of modern living. Weimar holds both these ideas of home, the classical and the radical, almost side by side.

Before you leave, take one more look at the white walls and the raised square centre. Notice how simple the outline is, and how much thinking sits inside it. Many homes you know, with their open kitchens and clear rooms, carry small traces of experiments like this one. As you walk back down towards the park, listen again to the leaves and the quiet street, and picture this hill as the Bauhaus once imagined it.
