The guide

The story

The road climbs away from the river plain and into low, dark hills. Trees close in. The air cools a little. Then the walls appear above you, grey stone against the green slope. This is Kondapalli Fort, set in the hills a short drive from Vijayawada, in the state of Andhra Pradesh. Bamboo and dry scrub cover the rock, and the heat of the plain gives way to shade.

The fort is old. It was founded in the fourteenth century by rulers of the Reddy line. For hundreds of years it guarded the routes between the coast and the inland country. Whoever held these hills could watch the roads below. So the fort was wanted, fought over, and held by one power after another. Control of the fort meant control of the trade that moved below.

Its history is a list of changing hands. After the Reddy kings came other southern dynasties. Later the Qutb Shahi rulers of Golconda took control. Later still, the British made the fort their own. Each new ruler left some mark on the stone. The fort you walk through today is a layering of all of them.

Under the British, the fort served as a military base. Soldiers trained and lived within these walls. You can still find the long, plain rooms that once held troops and stores. The thick walls that kept out attackers also kept the heat at bay. Stand inside one of these rooms and the noise of the world drops away. The stone stays cool even when the day outside turns harsh.

The way in is through a great stone gateway. It is cut from large blocks of granite, wide and high enough for men and animals to pass. Gates like this were the weak point of any fort, so they were made strong and watched closely. Pause here and look up at the weight of the stone above you. Marks of old hinges and bars can still be seen in the rock.

Climb a little further and the walls reveal their plan. They do not run in straight lines. Instead they follow the natural shape of the hill, rising and dipping with the rock. This made the fort harder to attack and easier to defend. The builders worked with the land rather than against it.

From the higher points, the view opens wide. Below you stretch fields, roads, and the distant shine of the river valley. On a clear day you can see for a long way. It is easy to understand, standing here, why this hill was chosen. Anyone approaching could be seen long before they arrived. Traders, armies, and messengers all moved across the land you now see.

The hills around the fort are quiet now. Birds call from the trees. Wind moves through gaps in the broken walls. Where soldiers once stood watch, there are now only lizards on warm stone and grass pushing up between the blocks. The fort has slipped from war into rest.

The town at the foot of the hill carries its own fame. Kondapalli is known across the region for its wooden toys. For generations, families here have carved and painted small figures from soft, light wood. Gods, animals, farmers, and village scenes all take shape under their tools. The craft has passed from parent to child for many generations.

These toys are made by hand, step by step. The wood is cut, shaped, and joined. Then it is painted in bright, simple colors. Each piece is small enough to hold in your palm. Together they tell the everyday story of village life in southern India, in wood and color.

If you have time, visit a workshop in the town below. You can watch the carving, smell the cut wood, and choose a piece to carry home. Buying directly supports the families who keep this old craft alive. A single painted figure makes a light and meaningful gift.

Back on the hill, walk the walls slowly before you leave. Touch the warm granite. Look through a gap where a watchman once looked out. The fort asks little of you. It simply stands, holding centuries of guard duty in its stone, open to anyone willing to climb. There is no grand museum here, only the bare stone and the wind.

When you start back down the slope, turn once for a last look. The walls settle again into the green of the hill. Soldiers, kings, and traders have all passed through and gone. The stone remains, patient and grey, keeping its long watch over the quiet land below.

Bring water, as shade and stalls are limited
Visit in cooler morning hours to avoid heat
Combine the trip with a toy-making workshop in town
