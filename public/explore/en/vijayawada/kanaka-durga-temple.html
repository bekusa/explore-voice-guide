The guide

The story

The climb begins with sound. Bells ring somewhere above you. Pilgrims murmur as they pass on the worn stone steps. Far below, the wide Krishna River moves slowly past the city. You are on Indrakeeladri Hill, in the middle of Vijayawada. Above you sits one of the most visited goddess temples in southern India.

This is the temple of Kanaka Durga. Her name joins two ideas. Kanaka means gold. Durga is the fierce mother goddess. Together they name a deity who is both shining and strong. Many people here simply call her Amma, which means mother. For this region, she is the center of daily faith and feeling.

The hill itself carries old stories. Its name points to Indra, the king of the gods. One tale ties this rock to Arjuna, the great warrior of the Mahabharata epic. The story says he prayed here to Lord Shiva and received a powerful weapon. You may read this as history or as legend. Either way, seekers have climbed here for many centuries. Pilgrims have come in good times and in hard ones alike.

The goddess is shown in a form of power. She has eight arms. Each hand holds a weapon or a sacred sign. She stands on a demon, the buffalo-headed Mahishasura, at the moment of her victory. This is Durga as the destroyer of evil. The image is small, but its force fills the shrine. Worshippers believe it appeared on its own.

There are two ways to reach the goddess. Many walk up a long flight of stone steps, pausing at small shrines along the way. Others ride the winding ghat road by car or bus to the top. Whichever you choose, the climb is part of the prayer. The effort of the body is offered along with the words of the heart.

She does not stand alone on the hill. Close by is a shrine to Malleswara Swamy, a form of Lord Shiva. In many temples the goddess stands to the left of her lord. Here, people note, she stands to his right instead. Worshippers read this small difference as a sign of her special strength at this place.

Move through the temple slowly. The corridors are narrow and close. The air is warm and full of incense and camphor. Priests work quickly, marking foreheads and passing trays of flame. A bell is struck again and again. The pace can feel fast and pressing. Let the current of people carry you, rather than fighting to rush ahead.

The most powerful season here is autumn. During Navaratri, the nine nights of the goddess, the temple fills beyond counting. Each day she is dressed as a different form of the divine mother. The closing days, called Dussehra, mark the victory of good over evil. Lamps, color, song, and crowds cover the whole hill in those days.

Water plays its part as well. During festivals, the goddess is carried on a decorated float on the Krishna River below. This is the float festival, known as Theppotsavam. Lit by many lamps and moving on dark water, the image draws great crowds to the river bank. They come simply to watch, to pray, and to be near her.

From the temple terraces, turn and look outward. The Krishna River spreads wide and pale beneath you. You can trace the long line of the Prakasam Barrage crossing it. The city presses close on every side. The hill stands like a green and crowded island, lifted above the slow river and the steady noise of traffic.

Notice the small acts of devotion around you. A coconut broken open on stone. A thread tied to a railing to hold a wish. Sugar, flowers, and red powder offered in cupped hands. These are not grand gestures. They are the steady, ordinary prayers of people who return here through every season of their lives. Birth, marriage, loss, and hope all bring people back to this same hill.

For a visitor, this is a living place of worship first, and a sight second. Dress with care and cover your shoulders and legs. Expect long lines, and expect to leave your shoes outside. The faith here is warm and welcoming. It is also daily and serious. Move gently, and you will be met gently in return. Photography is often limited inside, so keep your phone away near the inner shrine.

When you step back onto the steps, pause for a moment. Below you the river keeps moving, as it has through every story told on this hill. The bells fade slowly behind you. For a breath, the gold of the goddess and the grey of the water seem to hold the whole city quietly between them.
