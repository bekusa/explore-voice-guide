The story

You hear the water before you see it. A steady rush, low and wide, rising from the river. Then the long line of the barrage opens in front of you, crossing the broad Krishna River. Cars move along its top. Below, the water pours through gate after gate. This is the Prakasam Barrage, in the heart of Vijayawada. Spray hangs in the air, and the smell of river water is everywhere.

A barrage is a kind of low dam. It does not hold a deep reservoir behind a high wall. Instead it spans the river with a row of gates. The gates can be raised or lowered. In this way they control how much water flows on, and lift the level upstream for the canals.

This structure was completed in 1957. It stretches more than a kilometre across the river, linking the land on both banks. Along its top runs a busy road, joining two districts that the river would otherwise divide. So the barrage is a bridge and a water gate at the same time. Thousands of vehicles cross it every single day.

It is named after Tanguturi Prakasam, a leader much loved in this region. His name was given to honor his service to the people of Andhra. Many public works here carry the names of such figures. The name turns a piece of engineering into a kind of memorial as well.

The barrage you see is not the first to stand here. In the nineteenth century, under the British engineer Arthur Cotton, an earlier dam was built across the Krishna. That older work first turned the river's flow toward the fields. The modern barrage carries on the same task with stronger and wider gates. The river has been worked and reworked here for well over a century.

The purpose of all this is water for farming. The gates lift the river and send it into canals. Those canals carry it across the wide, flat delta of the Krishna. There the water feeds rice and other crops over a great spread of land. Many lives downstream depend on what these gates decide. In the dry months, that decision can shape an entire harvest.

Stand at the middle of the barrage and look upstream. The river spreads into a wide, calm sheet, held back by the gates. The water is pale and still on that side. It can look almost like a lake reaching toward the far hills. Light rests on it gently in the early morning and evening.

Now look downstream. Here the water is alive and broken. It pours through the open gates in white ropes and falls to the lower river. The sound is constant. On the rocks and shallows below, birds gather to fish. The contrast between the two sides is the whole story of the barrage in one view. Spend a few minutes here and the rhythm of falling water settles your mind.

To the north, a green hill rises above the city. On it stands the temple of Kanaka Durga, one of the most visited shrines in the region. From the barrage you can see the hill clearly. River and temple, water and worship, sit within one wide view of Vijayawada.

The barrage is also a daily part of city life. People cross it to work and home. In the cooler hours, families walk along the riverside nearby. Vendors sell snacks and tea. The great engine of water and the small pleasures of an evening stroll share the same stretch of river. Children point at the gates while elders talk over cups of tea.

Evening is the best time to come. As the sun lowers, the gates and the road catch the warm light. Lamps switch on along the span and shine across the water. The river takes on color. The scene is calm and open, and it costs nothing to stand and watch it.

Take care as you walk here. The road stays busy with traffic, and there is little room to spare. Keep to the side. Hold children close. The reward for a little caution is a long, open view of the river and the city it has fed for generations. Take a moment when the traffic thins to feel the river beneath your feet.

Before you leave, watch the water once more as it falls through the gates. It has come a long way down the river, and it has far still to go. For a moment you are standing on the exact line where a city decides what its great river will do next.
