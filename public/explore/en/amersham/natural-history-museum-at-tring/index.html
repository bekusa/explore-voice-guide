The guide

The story

Listen first. Floorboards creak softly underfoot, and the glass of old cabinets catches the light from tall windows. Rows of animals look back at you from behind that glass. They are still, patient, and strangely calm. The air carries a faint scent of polished wood. Before any history, notice how quiet this room is. It feels less like a modern museum and more like a private collection. That is exactly what it once was.

A quick note on where you are standing. This museum is in the market town of Tring, in Hertfordshire. It sits a short drive from Amersham, across the county line in the Chilterns. Many visitors come from Amersham or the surrounding villages for the day. The road winds through chalk hills and beech woods, and the town itself is small. That small-town setting matters. It is part of why this museum feels so personal.

The man behind it all was Walter Rothschild. He was born in 1868 into the famous banking family. His family expected him to follow them into finance. Walter had other plans. From childhood, he collected insects, birds, and almost anything else that had once been alive. By early adulthood, his collection had grown far beyond a hobby. It needed a building of its own.

That building is the one around you. It opened to the public in 1892 as Walter's own museum, on the edge of his family's estate at Tring Park. Local people could walk in and see animals from every continent. For a quiet English town in the late nineteenth century, this was extraordinary. Many visitors had never seen a zebra, a kangaroo, or a polar bear, even in a picture.

Walter was not simply a collector of curiosities. He was a serious zoologist. He paid collectors around the world to send him specimens. He studied them, described them, and published his findings. Many species and subspecies carry his name. One example you may already know is Rothschild's giraffe. It is named after him, and it is now a rare animal in the wild.

Now look at the cases themselves. The museum keeps much of its original Victorian character. The animals stand in tall wooden cabinets, packed closely together. There are no screens and very few modern displays in the main galleries. Instead, you see animal after animal, arranged by group. This style of display was normal in Walter's time. Today it is unusual, and that is part of the charm. You are seeing a museum as people saw museums over a century ago.

Here is the first surprise most visitors miss. Somewhere in these galleries are dressed fleas. Yes, fleas, wearing tiny costumes. These are a traditional Mexican folk craft. The fleas were dressed by hand in miniature clothing. They are so small that you need to lean in close to the glass. Walter had a particular passion for fleas. His brother Charles became a leading expert on them, and the family helped build a major flea collection.

Walter also liked living animals, and the stories about him are lively. He kept giant tortoises, and a well-known photograph shows him sitting on the back of one. He also trained zebras to pull a carriage. According to a popular tale, he once drove this zebra carriage to Buckingham Palace. Whether every detail of that story is true, the zebra carriage itself is well documented. It tells you a lot about the man.

The second surprise is a gallery devoted to domestic dogs. Many people do not expect this in a natural history museum. Here you can see preserved dogs from many different breeds. Some were famous in their own time, such as champion show dogs and racing greyhounds. Look at how certain breeds have changed shape over the years. A breed you know today may look different here. It is a quiet lesson in how humans shape animals.

Walter's story has a difficult chapter too. Collecting on this scale cost an enormous amount of money. In the early 1930s, he sold most of his vast collection of bird skins to the American Museum of Natural History in New York. It was a painful decision. Even so, the mounted animals here in Tring remained. They are the part of his life's work that you can still see today.

Walter Rothschild died in 1937. In his will, he left the museum and its collections to the British Museum. Over time, the site became part of the Natural History Museum, the national collection based in London. So this building is now a branch of a great national institution. Yet it still carries Walter's personality in every room. The scale is human. The cases are crowded. The feeling is private.

The museum is also more than a display space. A separate building nearby holds the Natural History Museum's national bird collection. This is a working research collection, used by scientists from around the world. It includes skins, skeletons, nests, and eggs. Most visitors never see it, because it is not a public gallery. But it means that Tring is still a place of real science, as Walter would have wanted.

As you move through the galleries, take your time. Look up, because some animals are placed high on top of cases. Look down, because small creatures sit near the floor. Children often spot things that adults walk past. Notice the old labels, too. Some show handwriting and naming styles from an earlier age. Each case is a small window into how people once tried to understand the whole living world.

Before you leave, pause in one gallery and simply stand still. Hear the creak of the boards and the murmur of other visitors. Look at the rows of glass and the patient animals behind it. One man gathered all of this, out of pure curiosity. When you step outside into the streets of Tring, you may look at a passing bird a little differently... and that would make Walter very happy.
