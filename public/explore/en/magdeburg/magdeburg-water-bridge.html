The guide

The story

Stand on the walkway and listen. Below you, a long way down, the Elbe River moves north in wide brown curls. Beside you, at almost the same height as your shoulder, there is a second body of water. It is flat, calm and green, and it is held inside a steel trough. You are standing between two waterways that cross each other at right angles. One runs on the ground. The other runs through the air.

This is the Magdeburg Water Bridge, a navigable aqueduct on the northeastern edge of the city. It carries the Elbe-Havel Canal over the Elbe River itself. It is about 918 metres long, and it is often described as the longest navigable canal bridge in Europe. Barges, cargo ships and small pleasure boats sail across it. Below them, river traffic passes under. It is a junction for ships, built in steel and concrete.

To see why it exists, picture the map of Germany's inland waterways. To the west lies the Mittelland Canal, a major east-west shipping route across the north German plain. To the east lies the Elbe-Havel Canal, which leads on toward Berlin. For most of the twentieth century, the Elbe sat between them like a gap in a road. Ships could not simply cross it. They had to go down into the river, travel along it, and then climb back up.

That detour was slow and unreliable. Boats coming from the west were lowered into the Elbe by the Rothensee boat lift, a large mechanical lift built in the 1930s. Then they travelled a stretch of river and rose again through a lock further north. The trip added roughly twelve kilometres. Worse, the Elbe is a moody river. In dry summers its water level falls, and loaded barges could run aground or had to wait. A canal that depended on the river's mood was a weak link.

Here is the first surprise. The idea of a bridge over the Elbe is far older than the bridge you are standing on. Planning began early in the twentieth century. In the 1930s, engineers even started building parts of the crossing. Then the Second World War stopped the work. After the war, Germany was divided, and this stretch of river ended up in East Germany. The project sat unfinished for decades, as if the plan had simply been put away in a drawer.

German reunification in 1990 opened the drawer again. Germany began a large programme to improve transport links between east and west, and this waterway junction was part of it. Construction of the bridge started in the late 1990s. In October 2003, the water bridge opened to shipping. So a crossing first sketched before the war was finally finished in the twenty-first century. Few structures carry such a long gap between idea and reality.

Now look closely at the trough beside you. It is a long steel channel, filled with water deep enough for full-size commercial barges. Think about the weight. A loaded cargo ship is heavy, but the water it sits on is heavier still. And here is the second surprise. When a ship sails onto the bridge, the bridge does not carry any extra load. The ship pushes aside a mass of water equal to its own weight. That water flows away along the canal. The total weight on the piers stays almost the same, whether the trough is empty of boats or full of them.

The structure has two parts. The section directly over the river has longer spans, so river ships have room to pass below. The longer section crosses the low, flat floodplain on the river's banks. Here the piers stand closer together. Walk along and notice how the rhythm of the supports changes beneath you. The bridge is built for a river that floods. When the Elbe rises in spring, water can spread far across these meadows.

At the eastern end, the canal meets the Hohenwarthe locks, named after the nearby village. These are twin locks. They lower ships from the height of the bridge down to the level of the Elbe-Havel Canal. If you have time, walk toward them and watch a ship go through. Gates close, water drains, and a huge vessel sinks slowly, as if it is going down in a lift without any cables.

The bridge was built for ships, but it is also built for people. Footpaths run along both sides of the trough. Walkers, joggers and cyclists cross here every day. There are no cars, so the loudest sounds are often the wind, the cry of a gull, and the low hum of a diesel engine. When a barge passes, its wake slaps gently against the steel walls. Stand still and you can feel how quiet engineering can be.

The landscape around you matters too. This is not a dense city scene. It is open country, with river meadows, willow trees and wide skies. The Elbe valley here is a home for birds, and in the wet seasons you may see herons standing in the shallows. On clear days the light lies flat across the plain. In autumn, morning fog can fill the river valley while the canal above it stays clear. Then the bridge seems to float.

The old route has not disappeared. The Rothensee boat lift still stands to the south, a reminder of the time when ships had to go down to the river and climb back up. Together, the old lift and the new bridge tell one story. It is the story of a waterway that waited a long time for a missing link. Many visitors cross the bridge and never learn how long that wait was.

Before you leave, stand in the middle once more. Look down at the Elbe, then across at the still canal, and let your eyes follow the two lines of water until they disappear. Listen for the next engine coming from the west. As you walk back toward the bank, notice the pale steel railings, the reeds below, and the wide sky above the meadows. Water is crossing water here. Most of the time, it happens in complete calm.
