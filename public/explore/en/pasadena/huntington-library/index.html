The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches softly underfoot, and somewhere a sprinkler ticks across a lawn. The air carries something green and slightly sweet, part cut grass and part warm eucalyptus. Old oaks throw wide pools of shade, and between them the Southern California light falls clean and bright. Before you see a single painting or book, the place feels like a very large private garden. In a sense, that is exactly what it was.

Welcome to the Huntington. Here is the first surprise. Most people call it a Pasadena attraction, but the grounds actually sit in San Marino, a small, quiet city right next door. The full name is long: the Huntington Library, Art Museum, and Botanical Gardens. That long name is honest, though. This is three institutions in one, and each could fill a full day of your time.

The story begins with Henry E. Huntington. He was a businessman of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. He made his fortune in railways and real estate. His streetcar lines helped connect the towns of greater Los Angeles, and land values rose along the tracks. Henry was the nephew of Collis Huntington, one of the powerful railroad men who built routes across the American West. Henry learned the business from his uncle, and later he moved into his own ventures.

Henry bought this land when it was a working ranch, with orchards and open fields. Over time he turned it into an estate. He built a grand house in the early twentieth century, and today that house holds part of the art collection. Henry married Arabella Huntington, his uncle's widow. Arabella was a serious art collector in her own right. Her taste shaped much of what you see indoors, especially the European furniture and paintings.

In 1919, the couple signed the papers to create a trust. With that step, their private estate was set on the path to become a public institution for research and education. Arabella died in 1924, and Henry died in 1927. They are buried together on the grounds, in a classical marble mausoleum. Its designer was John Russell Pope, the architect later known for the Jefferson Memorial in Washington. Few visitors walk out to see it, so it often feels very still.

Now, the library. This is the heart of the place, and many visitors miss how important it is. Henry collected rare books and manuscripts with enormous energy. Scholars still come here from around the world to study the collection. Its strengths include British and American history and literature. Only a small part is on public display at any one time. Behind those exhibition cases sit millions of items, kept safe for researchers.

Among the treasures on display, look for a Gutenberg Bible printed on vellum, which is fine animal skin. This is one of the earliest books printed in Europe with movable metal type, from the fifteenth century. Nearby, you may see the Ellesmere Chaucer, a beautifully illustrated early manuscript of The Canterbury Tales. The pages show small painted pilgrims riding along the margins. Early editions of Shakespeare are also part of the collection. Displays rotate, so what you see today may differ from what a friend saw last year.

Then there is the art. The most famous painting here is The Blue Boy, by the English painter Thomas Gainsborough. It shows a young man in a shimmering blue satin suit. Huntington bought it in the 1920s, and its departure from Britain caused real public sadness there. Across the gallery hangs Pinkie, by Thomas Lawrence, a girl in a white dress against a stormy sky. The two portraits face each other. Visitors often treat them as a pair, though different artists painted them decades apart.

Step outside again, and the gardens begin. They spread across a large part of the estate, and they are organized into themed areas. Each one has its own climate, mood, and plant world. You could walk here for hours and still not see everything. It helps to choose two or three gardens and give them real attention, rather than racing through all of them.

The Desert Garden is the second surprise for many visitors. It holds one of the oldest and largest collections of cacti and succulents in the world. Golden barrel cacti sit in round clusters, like soft green cushions with sharp spines. Tall columns rise beside twisted aloes and spiky agaves. Henry's gardener had to convince him that desert plants were worth growing. Today this garden is one of the most unusual landscapes on the whole estate.

The Japanese Garden brings a different feeling. A curved bridge crosses a pond, and koi fish move slowly under the water. There is a traditional house, a raked gravel garden, and a grove of bamboo. Not far away is the Chinese Garden, called Liu Fang Yuan, which means the Garden of Flowing Fragrance. It opened in the early twenty-first century. Its pavilions, stone bridges, and rocks frame a central lake. Its design follows classical gardens from the Chinese city of Suzhou.

There is also a rose garden, a jungle garden, an herb garden, and a children's garden where families can play with water and light. In the greenhouse, the gardens sometimes grow a giant plant called the corpse flower. When it blooms, it smells like rotting meat. That smell attracts its pollinators in the wild. Crowds have lined up to experience it here. It is not every visitor's idea of romance, but it is unforgettable.

As you walk on, look back at the house and the long lawns. The scale is grand, but the details are small. There is the shadow of an oak branch on a marble statue, or a hummingbird near the flowers. Somewhere a scholar is reading a manuscript that few people have touched in centuries. Outside, a child is pointing at a cactus. Take a breath of that warm green air... and choose where your feet will carry you next.
