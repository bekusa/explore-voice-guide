The guide

The story

The wind comes first. Up here on the Longwood plateau, the trade winds blow steadily from the southeast. They push low cloud across the grass and bend the trees in one direction. The air often feels damp, even on a bright morning. A soft mist can roll in within minutes and hide the hills. Stand still for a moment and listen. You may hear the wind in the leaves, a bird calling, and almost nothing else. This quiet, remote spot became the last home of Napoleon Bonaparte.

You are standing on Saint Helena, one of the most isolated inhabited islands on Earth. It sits in the South Atlantic, far from both Africa and South America. Jamestown, the small capital, lies down in a deep valley on the coast. Longwood is a drive of several kilometres inland and uphill from the harbour. The British chose this island because escape from it seemed almost impossible. After his defeat at Waterloo in 1815, Napoleon was sent here as a prisoner. He would never leave.

Napoleon arrived in October 1815. Longwood was not ready, so he stayed for a few weeks at a pavilion at The Briars, the home of the Balcombe family. That short stay is remembered fondly in many accounts, partly because of his friendship with the family's lively young daughter. In December 1815 he moved up to this house. It had been a modest farm building, later used as a country residence by island officials. Workers hurried to extend it for the fallen emperor and his household.

The house you see is long and low, with pale walls and green shutters. Look at the simple single-storey shape. This was no palace. It was a working house enlarged in a hurry. The rooms lead from one to the next in a line. Napoleon and his companions complained about the house often. They described damp walls, mould, leaking roofs and rats. The plateau's wind and mist kept the rooms cool and wet for much of the year. For a man who had lived in Europe's grandest palaces, the contrast was sharp.

Napoleon did not come alone. A small court followed him into exile. Among them were General Bertrand and his wife, the Count of Montholon, General Gourgaud, and the Count of Las Cases. There were also servants and a valet. Las Cases spent long hours taking down Napoleon's conversations. Those notes later became a famous book about the emperor's life and ideas. In a real sense, much of the Napoleon legend was shaped right here, in these small rooms, as an old soldier told his own story.

Inside, one of the first rooms is the billiard room. Napoleon is said to have used the table less for games than for spreading out maps and papers. Here he received visitors and worked on his memories of his campaigns. Look closely at the shutters in the house. Tradition says Napoleon cut small holes in them. Through these openings he could watch the guards and the garden without being seen. Whether every hole is original or not, the story says much about life under constant watch.

And the watch was real. The British governor, Sir Hudson Lowe, held strict control over the prisoner. Soldiers were posted around the estate. Visitors needed permission. Letters were read. Napoleon and Lowe disliked each other deeply, and they met only a few times. Each side blamed the other for the tension. Today historians still argue about how fair or harsh Lowe really was. Standing here, you can understand both views. The island was secure, but the prisoner felt he was being slowly worn down.

In his later years, Napoleon found a new interest. He turned to the garden. With his household and servants, he dug beds, planted flowers and shaped paths. Some accounts describe him working in the soil wearing a wide straw hat. Sunken paths and earth banks gave him some privacy from the guards' eyes. Today the gardens have been restored, and they are full of colour in the right season. Walk slowly through them. They are one of the most personal traces of the man.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The British actually built a new, larger house for Napoleon close by. It was meant to be more comfortable. But it was completed only around the time of his death, and he never lived in it. That later building no longer stands in its original form. So the house before you is the one he truly knew, with all its faults. Its smallness is the point. This is the scale of a real life in exile.

Napoleon's health declined over the years. He died in this house on 5 May 1821. He was buried in a quiet valley on the island, a walk away from here, near a spring he had liked. For almost two decades his grave stayed on Saint Helena. Then, in 1840, his remains were taken back to France. Today he rests in Paris, under the great dome of Les Invalides. The empty tomb site here on the island is still kept with care.

Another surprise: you are, in a way, standing on French ground. In the 1850s, under Emperor Napoleon the Third, France acquired Longwood House and the tomb site from Britain. Since then, these places have been owned by the French state, on a British island. A French official based here looks after the properties. Before the bicentenary of Napoleon's arrival, the house went through a major restoration. Many rooms were returned to how they may have looked in his time.

As you move through the rooms, notice the furniture, the portraits and the objects on display. Some pieces have direct links to the emperor's years here. Others recreate the setting. Pay attention to the bedrooms and the small study. These were private spaces, not grand halls. Look out of the windows too. The view across the plateau shows how far the house sits from town and sea. That distance shaped every day for the people who lived here.

Before you leave, step back outside into the wind. Look at the long green shutters and the low roofline once more. Notice how the mist moves over the grass and the trees. This is the same weather Napoleon walked through every day for more than five years. As you head back down the winding road towards Jamestown, watch the landscape change from damp green uplands to bare rock. Remember the quiet house behind you, where one of history's loudest lives came to a very quiet end.
