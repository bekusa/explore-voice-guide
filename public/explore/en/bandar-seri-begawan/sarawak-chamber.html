The guide

The story

Your headlamp beam leaves your forehead, travels forward, and then simply stops. It does not hit a wall. It thins into the dark and disappears. Around you there is the sound of dripping water, the crunch of boots on wet stone, and the cool, mineral smell of rock that has never felt sunlight. You are standing in Sarawak Chamber. Here, the darkness has no visible edge.

First, a small but important point about where you are. Your trip may have started in Bandar Seri Begawan, the capital of Brunei, but this chamber is not in Brunei. It lies across the border in Sarawak, a state of Malaysia, inside Gunung Mulu National Park. Many travellers in this part of Borneo pass through Brunei and the Malaysian city of Miri on their way here. So think of Brunei as a neighbour. The chamber belongs to the limestone mountains of northern Sarawak.

The chamber sits deep inside a cave system called Gua Nasib Bagus. In Malay, that name means the Good Luck Cave. The name is fitting, as you will hear in a moment. The journey usually begins in the rainforest of the national park. You walk along a jungle trail, with humid air and the calls of insects and birds around you. Then, at the cave entrance, the green light of the forest fades behind you, and the temperature drops.

The route to the chamber is real adventure caving. It is not a paved walkway. Visitors follow an underground river. In places they wade through water, scramble over slippery rock, and hold fixed ropes along narrow ledges. The sound of the river echoes off the walls of the passage. Headlamps pick out wet surfaces, ripples, and the backs of the people in front. Everything outside the circle of light belongs to the dark.

The chamber was not known to the outside world until 1981. That year, a British caving expedition was surveying the caves of Mulu. Three of its members, Andy Eavis, Dave Checkley, and Tony White, pushed into Good Luck Cave and came into a space that their lights could not cross. Accounts of the expedition describe how hard it was to understand what they had found. They could not see the far side. They only knew that the space kept going.

This is the first surprise for many visitors. The largest known cave chamber on Earth, measured by floor area, was found by people who could barely see it. They had to work out its size the slow way, by following its edges and surveying it with instruments. Even now, no single light can show you the whole room. You build a picture of it in your mind, piece by piece, as beams sweep across the stone.

So how big is it? Published surveys put the chamber at roughly seven hundred metres long and around four hundred metres wide. Its ceiling rises at least seventy metres above parts of the floor. Guides like to give a comparison: the chamber could hold many jumbo jets parked side by side. Some caves in China may hold more total volume. But for sheer floor area, Sarawak Chamber is still the one that cavers talk about.

The size is easier to understand through your body than through numbers. Notice how your voice behaves here. In a small cave, sound bounces back quickly. Here, a call seems to go out into the dark and fade away. The air feels still and very large around you. Your eyes keep searching for a wall or a ceiling to rest on, and they find nothing. Many visitors say this is the strangest part — the space feels huge, yet you see very little of it.

How does a room like this form? The mountains of Mulu are made of limestone. This is rock built up long ago from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures. Over a very long time, slightly acidic water seeped through cracks and dissolved the rock. Underground rivers carved out passages. As those spaces grew, parts of the ceiling lost their support and collapsed. The chamber you are standing in is partly the result of that slow dissolving and that sudden falling.

You can see the evidence under your feet. The floor of the chamber is not flat. It is a steep slope covered with huge boulders, many of them the size of cars or small houses. These are pieces of ceiling that fell long ago. Walking here means stepping carefully from rock to rock, following your guide's line. Look at the edges of the nearest boulders in your light. Some are sharp, and some are rounded and smoothed by water.

Life is scarce this deep inside the mountain, but it is not absent. Mulu's caves are known for their bats and swiftlets, although most of these live closer to the entrances, where they can reach the forest. In the deep, dark parts of caves, smaller creatures such as cave crickets and spiders can survive without sunlight. If you see something moving at the edge of your light, leave it alone. These animals live in a fragile world, and every visitor passes through it.

This fragility is one reason the area is protected. Gunung Mulu National Park was added to UNESCO's World Heritage List in 2000. UNESCO recognised its caves, its karst landscape, and its rainforest. Karst means a landscape shaped by water dissolving rock. Access to Sarawak Chamber is only possible with licensed guides, and trips are limited and carefully managed. The rules exist to keep visitors safe and to keep the chamber as undamaged as it was when cavers first entered it.

It is worth being honest about the effort involved. A trip to the chamber and back is a long, physically demanding day. There is wading, climbing, and many hours on uneven ground. Your clothes will get wet. Your legs will feel it the next day. But people who make the journey often say the effort becomes part of the memory. The long approach makes the arrival feel earned. The chamber does not show itself quickly, so the slow journey suits it.

Before you turn back, try one thing, if your guide allows it. Switch off your headlamp for a moment, together with your group. The darkness here is complete — darker than any night outside. Listen to the drip of water and the breathing of the people around you. Then switch your light back on and watch it fade into that great space once more. As you walk out toward the river and the forest, remember that this room has stayed dark for a very long time, and it will stay dark after you leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a licensed park guide well before your visit.
Train for a long day of wading and climbing.
Carry a spare headlamp with fresh batteries.
Wear grippy shoes that can get completely soaked.
Bring your passport when travelling over from Brunei.
