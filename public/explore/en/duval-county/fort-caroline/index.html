The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the live oaks, and somewhere below the bluff the broad, dark water of the Saint Johns River slides toward the Atlantic. The air is thick and salty, and the ground under your feet is soft with sand and fallen leaves. Long before this place had a park sign, people stood on this riverbank and watched for ships. Some of them were hoping a ship would come. Others feared that one would.

You are standing at Fort Caroline, on the edge of Jacksonville in Duval County, Florida. This is where one of the earliest European attempts to settle what is now the United States took shape. It happened in 1564, decades before the English arrived at Jamestown. The settlers were not Spanish, as many visitors expect. They were French, and most of them were Protestant. That single detail shaped everything that followed.

These French Protestants were known as Huguenots. In the middle of the sixteenth century, France was falling into bitter religious wars between Catholics and Protestants. Some French leaders saw the New World as a possible refuge for Huguenots. They also saw it as a strategic prize. A French base on this coast could watch the Spanish treasure fleets that sailed north along Florida on their way home to Europe.

The story begins two years earlier, in 1562. A French captain named Jean Ribault sailed into the mouth of this river. He called it the River of May, because he arrived in that month. Ribault set up a stone column carved with the arms of the French king, to claim the land for France. He then sailed north and left a small garrison in what is now South Carolina. That outpost did not survive.

In 1564, a second expedition arrived, led by René de Laudonnière, who had sailed with Ribault before. The colonists built a small fort near the river and named it La Caroline, in honour of King Charles the Ninth of France. Historical accounts describe a triangular earth and timber fort. It was modest, built in a hurry, and far from home. Soon the settlers were struggling with hunger, illness, and quarrels among themselves.

The French were not arriving in an empty land. This river belonged to the Timucua, a group of Native peoples who lived across much of northeast Florida. A local leader the French called Saturiwa ruled villages near the river mouth. The Timucua fished, farmed, and traded, and at first they helped the newcomers. But the French were drawn into local rivalries, and they leaned heavily on Native food stores. Over time, that relationship grew strained.

Here is something many visitors miss. Much of what the world knows about the Timucua comes from this short-lived colony. An artist named Jacques Le Moyne travelled with the French and recorded the land and its people. Years later, engravings based on his work were published in Europe by Theodor de Bry. Historians treat these images with care, because they were reworked for European audiences. Still, they remain some of the earliest European pictures of Native life in North America.

Spain watched all of this with alarm. Spain claimed Florida, and a French Protestant fort on the treasure route was a direct threat. In 1565, the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés arrived on the coast. He founded Saint Augustine, about forty miles to the south. Around the same time, Ribault returned with reinforcements for the French. The two sides were now only a few days apart, and both prepared to strike.

Ribault sailed south to attack the Spanish by sea. Then a powerful storm hit the coast and wrecked his fleet. While the French ships were scattered, Menéndez marched his soldiers overland through rain and swamp. In September 1565, they attacked Fort Caroline. Most of the defenders were killed. A small number escaped, including Laudonnière and Le Moyne. The Spanish took the fort and renamed it San Mateo.

The story grows darker still. Many French survivors of the shipwrecks surrendered to the Spanish further south, and most were put to death, including Ribault himself. The inlet where this happened is still called Matanzas, a Spanish word that means slaughters. A few years later, in 1568, a French captain named Dominique de Gourgues attacked the Spanish garrison here in revenge. After that, France gave up its claim to Florida.

Now for a second surprise. Nobody knows exactly where the original fort stood. Over the centuries, the river shifted, the banks changed, and the timber and earth simply disappeared. Some historians believe the site was lost to erosion, while others still debate its location. The fort you can see here today is an exhibit, not a ruin. It was built to give visitors a sense of the fort's scale and shape, based largely on those early drawings.

As you walk the grounds, take a moment with the reconstruction. Notice how small and exposed it feels against the forest and the water. Then follow the trails under the oaks and palms. The memorial is part of the Timucuan Ecological and Historic Preserve, a wide area of marsh, river, and coastal forest. On the bluff nearby, a stone monument recalls the column that Ribault raised on his first voyage. From there, the view stretches across the river.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute and look out at the water. This river carried French sails, Spanish soldiers, and Timucua canoes, all within a few short years. Today you might see a fishing boat, a heron, or just the light moving on the surface. The fort is gone, but the river and the bluff remain. As you walk back under the trees, listen again to the wind... it is the same sound the settlers heard.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the visitor centre first for helpful context.
Bring insect repellent for the shaded forest trails.
Walk to the bluff for wide views over the river.
Wear sturdy shoes on the sandy, root-crossed paths.
Come early on summer days to avoid the heat.
