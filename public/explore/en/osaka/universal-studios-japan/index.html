The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you hear it: a brass fanfare from a hidden speaker, the hiss of a ride launching somewhere behind the trees, and a thousand conversations in Osaka dialect rising and falling around you. The air smells of caramel popcorn and warm butter. Underfoot, the pavement is clean enough to notice. You are standing at the edge of Osaka Bay, at Universal Studios Japan.

The park opened in 2001. It was the first Universal theme park built in Asia. Its location says a lot about modern Osaka. This stretch of waterfront in the Konohana ward was once industrial land, home to shipyards and factories that served the busy port. When heavy industry moved away, the city looked for a new use for the shoreline. The answer was a film-themed park, with a short train line to bring the crowds in.

That train line is part of the experience. Most visitors arrive at Universal City Station on the JR Yumesaki Line. Some of the trains on this line are painted with characters from the park. The walk from the platform leads through Universal CityWalk, a strip of hotels, restaurants and souvenir shops. It works like an airlock. By the time you reach the gates, the everyday city has fallen away behind you.

At the entrance, look for the large rotating globe with the Universal name across it. It is the most photographed spot in the park, and on busy mornings people queue patiently to stand in front of it. Notice how orderly that queue is. Japanese visitors are known for their careful etiquette, and you will see it everywhere here, from the tidy lines to the way people clear their own tables at lunch.

Now look at what people are wearing. This is one of the things that surprises many first-time visitors. Groups of friends often dress alike, in matching sweatshirts, headbands or even full costumes. Popcorn buckets hang from shoulder straps like handbags, and many are shaped like characters. For local fans, the park is not only a place for rides. It is a social stage, a place to dress up, take photos together and share them.

The first section most people walk through is styled after Hollywood. The facades borrow from old American movie studios and city streets, with painted signs and neon lettering. Above your head, you may hear screams from Hollywood Dream, a roller coaster that runs over the area. Riders choose their own soundtrack before the ride begins. It is a small detail, but it shows the park's focus on personal touches.

Further in, the scenery shifts again and again. There are streets modelled on New York and San Francisco, a lagoon, and a jungle area built around the Jurassic Park films. One of the coasters there, The Flying Dinosaur, holds riders face-down beneath the track, so they swing through the air like prey in a dinosaur's claws. Even if you do not ride it, stand nearby for a moment and watch it pass overhead.

Then comes a change in mood. In 2014, the park opened The Wizarding World of Harry Potter. You enter through a dark forest path, and the village of Hogsmeade appears with steep, crooked roofs covered in permanent snow, even on a hot August afternoon. Above it rises a replica of Hogwarts castle. Inside the castle, the main ride carries visitors through scenes from the stories. In the village, the drink to try is Butterbeer, sweet and creamy, served with a foamy top.

The biggest change to the park came in 2021, with the opening of Super Nintendo World. Here, the theme is not a film but a video game. You enter through a green warp pipe, and you come out inside a world of bright blocks, spinning coins and floating platforms. Many visitors buy a Power-Up Band, a wristband that lets them hit blocks and collect digital coins as they explore. The central attraction is a Mario Kart ride that uses augmented reality headsets.

This area grew again at the end of 2024, when Donkey Kong Country opened beside it. Its mine-cart coaster is designed to look as if the cart jumps over broken sections of track. The Nintendo areas are often very crowded. On busy days, entry can be controlled with timed tickets, so plan this part of your visit before you arrive. It is worth the planning. Few places in the world feel this close to standing inside a game.

The park also changes with the seasons. Autumn brings Halloween events, and the evening hours are known for scary performers who walk among the crowds. Winter brings lights and holiday decorations. Locals often return several times a year because the experience is different each time. This is a big reason why so many visitors here are from Japan itself, not only from abroad.

One more detail is easy to miss. Look for the gaps between the themed areas. Behind a fence or past a corner, you may see the cranes, warehouses and water of the working port. The park sits right on the bay, and from some spots you can catch a breeze carrying the smell of the sea. It is a quiet reminder that all this fantasy was built on old industrial ground.

So pause for a moment where you are standing now. Look at the faces around you, and listen to the mix of music, screams and laughter. Notice the people in matching outfits taking their tenth photo of the day, and the staff waving to guests on passing rides. As you walk on, look up at the rooftops, the painted signs and the sky above the bay. This place is built from imagination, but the joy you see here is completely real.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive by train to Universal City Station.
Check if Nintendo areas need timed entry tickets.
Visit the entrance globe early to avoid queues.
Consider an Express Pass for the busiest rides.
Buy a Power-Up Band before entering Nintendo World.
