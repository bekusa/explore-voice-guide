The guide

The story

Listen for the gulls first. Their calls drift up from the mouth of the River Tyne, just a short walk away. Underfoot, the grass gives way to low courses of pale stone, laid out in neat rectangles. Around you are rows of red-brick terraced houses, chimneys, washing lines. And rising out of this ordinary street, there is a Roman gateway, with tall towers, timber gates and a walkway along the top. Welcome to Arbeia.

This fort sits on a hill above South Shields, on the south bank of the river where the Tyne meets the North Sea. The Romans chose the spot with care. From here, soldiers could watch every ship that entered or left the river. The fort was built in the second century, in the decades after Emperor Hadrian ordered his great wall across northern Britain. Arbeia did not sit on the wall itself. It sat at the eastern end of that frontier, and guarded the sea road into it.

The name sounds unusual for northern England, and it is. Many scholars believe Arbeia means something like the fort of the Arabs. The name likely comes from a unit that served here late in the Roman period. A Roman document lists a company of bargemen from the Tigris, the great river of Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. Picture it. Boatmen from the Middle East, working the cold grey waters of the Tyne. This is the first surprise of Arbeia. The Roman frontier was a far more international place than most people imagine.

For a large part of its life, the fort had a very practical job. In the early third century, the Roman army was campaigning in Scotland, and those soldiers needed food. Arbeia became a supply base. Ships brought in grain, and the grain was stored here before it moved north and west. Excavations have found the remains of many granaries inside the walls, far more than a normal fort would need. Look at the long stone footings across the site. Some of those rectangles once held grain for thousands of men.

Granaries had a clever design. Their floors were raised on low stone pillars or supports. Air could move underneath, which kept the grain dry and made it harder for rats and damp to spoil it. Thick walls with strong buttresses held up heavy roofs. In a damp coastal climate, this mattered a great deal. When you walk among the foundations, watch for the narrow parallel walls and the gaps between them. These are the bones of a Roman warehouse district, sitting quietly under the open sky.

Now turn to the gateway. This is a modern reconstruction, built in the 1980s on the original Roman foundations. Archaeologists and builders used evidence from Roman forts across the empire to decide its height, its windows and its towers. Nobody can be completely certain how the original looked. But the gate gives you a real sense of scale. Climb inside if it is open. From the upper level, the view runs over the rooftops of South Shields toward the river and the sea, which is the same view a Roman sentry was watching.

Near the gate, you will find more reconstructions. One is a barrack block, where ordinary soldiers lived. The rooms are small. Several men shared each one, with their kit, their bedding and their cooking things. Another building is the house of the commanding officer, and it is very different. It has a courtyard, painted walls and a dining room. The contrast tells a clear story about rank in the Roman army. A common soldier had a crowded room. The commander had a comfortable Mediterranean-style home at the edge of the North Sea.

Inside the museum, look for one of the most moving objects from Roman Britain. It is a tombstone for a woman called Regina. Her name means queen. The inscription tells us she was from the Catuvellauni, a British tribe from the south of England. She had been an enslaved woman. Her husband, Barates, freed her and married her. Barates came from Palmyra, a great trading city in the Syrian desert. Regina died young, in her thirties, and Barates paid for her memorial.

Here is the second surprise. Below the Latin text on the stone, there is a second line in a completely different script. It is Palmyrene, a form of Aramaic, the language of Barates' home city. It says, in short, Regina, freedwoman of Barates, alas. Very few people in South Shields could have read those words. Barates seems to have written them for himself, in his own language. On the stone, Regina sits in a chair with her wool basket and her jewellery box, like a respected lady of Palmyra.

Another tombstone in the collection belongs to a young man called Victor. The inscription describes him as a Moor, which means he came from North Africa. He had also been enslaved, and he was freed by a cavalryman from a Spanish regiment. Victor died at about twenty years old. His former master gave him a funeral and a carved memorial. The sculpture shows Victor lying on a couch at a banquet. Together, these two stones describe a frontier where people from Britain, Syria, Africa and Spain lived and died side by side.

The fort did not stay the same through the centuries. It was rebuilt and changed several times. Some granaries later became barracks, and the layout shifted as the army's needs changed. Archaeologists have dug here since the nineteenth century, and work has continued in modern times. Each season of excavation adds small details. They find coins, pottery, pieces of armour and the everyday rubbish of Roman life. The site today is part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire World Heritage Site, together with Hadrian's Wall.

After Rome left Britain, the fort slowly disappeared under the town. The stone was reused, and the ground was covered with fields, then streets. That is why Arbeia feels so unusual. There is no wild hillside here, and no lonely moorland. Instead, there is a Roman fort in the middle of a working town, with buses, schools and front doors all around it. Local people walk their dogs past the walls. Children grow up a few streets from a Roman commander's house. In South Shields, the ancient and the everyday share the same hill.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the gateway and look toward the river. The wind here usually comes off the sea, and it can be sharp even in summer. Think of the Tigris bargemen hauling cargo in this weather. Think of Regina, far from her tribe, and Barates, far from his desert city. Then walk back out between the terraced houses. Notice the stone foundations beside the modern pavement. For a few steps, two very different towns sit on the same ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the reconstructed gateway for views toward the river.
Bring a windproof layer; sea winds cross the hill.
Visit the museum first to understand the foundations outside.
Combine with a walk down to the Tyne riverside.
Allow extra time to read the tombstone inscriptions closely.
