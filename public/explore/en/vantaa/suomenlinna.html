The guide

The story

The ferry pulls away from the market square, and the city slips behind you. Salt air moves across the deck. Gulls follow the wake. In about fifteen minutes, low green islands rise from the water, ringed with grey stone walls. This is Suomenlinna, a sea fortress built across a group of islands at the mouth of Helsinki's harbor. The same ferry is part of the city's public transport, used by residents and visitors alike.

Work on the fortress began in 1748. At that time, Finland was part of the kingdom of Sweden. The Swedish crown wanted a strong base to guard against Russian expansion in the Baltic Sea. The man who led the project was Augustin Ehrensvärd, a soldier and military engineer. He shaped much of what you see today, and his ideas guided the builders for years after his death.

The first name of the fortress was Sveaborg, which means fortress of Sweden. Soldiers and workers cut stone, raised walls, and dug into the rock for decades. The plan was bold. Some called it the Gibraltar of the North. Cannons pointed out to sea, ready to meet any fleet that tried to pass. Building such a place was slow, costly work, and it stretched across the reigns of more than one king.

History did not follow the plan. In 1808, during a war with Russia, the fortress surrendered after a siege. Control passed to the Russian empire. For more than a hundred years, Russian soldiers lived here, and the islands became part of their defenses. The walls you walk along served three different rulers across their long life.

Finland became independent in 1917. The next year, in 1918, the fortress took a new name: Suomenlinna, which means castle of Finland. The change marked a fresh chapter. What had once guarded foreign powers now belonged to a young nation finding its own feet. The new name carried pride, a sign that the islands now served Finland alone.

As you walk inland, look for the King's Gate. This stone gateway was the ceremonial entrance to the fortress from the sea. Ships of state once arrived here. The gate is set into the rock and framed by heavy walls, and it remains the symbol of Suomenlinna today.

The paths lead past grass embankments and old barracks painted in soft yellow and red. Children run along the ramparts. Cannons sit quiet on their mounts, dark with age. The sound shifts as you move: wind on the open side, then sudden calm behind a thick wall. In summer the lawns fill with people on blankets, and in winter snow softens the grey stone.

Near the center of the islands lies the tomb of Augustin Ehrensvärd. He died in 1772 and was buried on the fortress he built. A stone monument marks the spot, designed in part by a Swedish king who admired his work. Visitors pause here, where the builder rests inside his own creation.

Suomenlinna is not only a monument. Around 800 people live here all year. They take the same ferry to work and school. Washing hangs between old buildings. A small shop sells bread and milk. Artists keep studios here, drawn by the quiet and the light off the water. The fortress is a living neighborhood as much as a piece of the past.

One of the most visited sights is the submarine Vesikko. Built in the 1930s, it served in the Second World War and now rests on dry land as a museum. You can climb inside and feel how narrow the world of a submarine crew really was. In all, six museums tell the story of the islands, from the years of war to daily life on the water.

The fortress also holds one of the oldest dry docks still in use anywhere. It was carved in the eighteenth century to build and repair warships. Wooden ships were floated in, the water was drained away, and crews worked on the bare hulls. Today the dock still serves boats, a working link to the first builders.

In 1991, Suomenlinna was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list. It was honored as a rare example of military building from its time, shaped by several nations over the years. The recognition helps protect the walls and houses for the people who come after us.

Stand at the outer wall and look toward the open sea. The stones under your hand were laid by hands long gone. The water is the same water those builders watched. A fort is made to keep the world out, yet this one now invites the world in. Take a slow breath of the salt air before the ferry calls you back.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the ferry from the Market Square downtown.
Buy a return ferry ticket before you board.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven stone paths.
Allow at least three hours to explore the islands.
