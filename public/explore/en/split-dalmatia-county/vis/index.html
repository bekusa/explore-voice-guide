The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The ferry engine drops to a low hum, and the water in the harbour turns from deep navy to a clear, glassy green. Stone houses line the shore. Their shutters are faded by salt and sun. A rope creaks against a bollard, and somewhere above the town a dog barks once. Welcome to Vis... the farthest inhabited island of the central Dalmatian coast, and one of the quietest places on the Croatian Adriatic.

Vis sits well out to sea, beyond the larger islands that most visitors see from Split. The crossing takes time, and that distance has shaped everything here. The island stayed apart from the mainland for long stretches of its history. Sometimes by choice, often by force. Today that distance gives it a slower rhythm. There are two main towns, Vis in the east and Komiža in the west, and a hilly interior of vineyards, pine and dry stone walls between them.

The story of the island begins with the ancient Greeks. In the fourth century before Christ, settlers sent from the Greek city of Syracuse in Sicily founded a colony here. They called it Issa. It became one of the most important Greek settlements in the Adriatic, and it founded colonies of its own on the coast. On the slopes near the modern town of Vis, archaeologists have found the remains of town walls, graves and a theatre. The Romans later took control, and they left baths and villas behind.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of the most valued finds from Issa are small objects, not grand buildings. Amphorae, coins and pottery have come out of the ground and the sea around the island. The local archaeological museum, inside an old Austrian fortification near the harbour, holds a collection of Hellenistic pieces. Among them is a bronze head of a Greek goddess, often identified as Aphrodite or Artemis. It is a quiet reminder that people have shipped wine and oil from this harbour for well over two thousand years.

The island's position made it valuable to every navy that wanted to control the Adriatic. Whoever held Vis could watch the sea lanes between the eastern and western shores. That is why the island appears again and again in naval history. Two major sea battles took place in these waters, both known by the island's Italian name, Lissa. They came in the nineteenth century, fifty-five years apart, and each one changed how people thought about war at sea.

The first battle came in 1811, during the Napoleonic Wars. A small British squadron under Captain William Hoste defeated a larger French and Venetian force off the island. For a few years afterwards, the British used Vis as a base. They built forts on the headlands around the harbour, and some of these stone walls still stand. Locals say the British sailors also brought cricket to the island. This story is popular, though how much cricket was really played here is uncertain.

The second battle came in 1866. The Austrian fleet, led by Admiral Wilhelm von Tegetthoff, met the Italian navy near the island and won. It was one of the first large battles between ironclad warships, ships protected by iron armour. In one famous moment, an Austrian flagship rammed and sank an Italian ironclad. For years afterwards, navies around the world added ramming bows to their ships because of this battle. Few people standing on this calm shore imagine that naval design changed here.

The twentieth century brought a different kind of conflict. During the Second World War, Vis became a key base for the Yugoslav Partisans, the resistance movement led by Josip Broz Tito. Allied forces worked alongside them here. Tito used a cave high on Mount Hum, the island's highest peak, as his headquarters for a time in 1944. The cave can still be reached on foot. From the slopes, the view opens across the sea toward the smaller islands to the west.

After the war, the island's strategic value kept it closed. Yugoslavia turned Vis into a military zone. Tunnels, bunkers and submarine pens were cut into the rock. For decades, foreign visitors were not allowed to come here. The ban only ended in the late 1980s. This long isolation is the second surprise of Vis. While other islands filled with hotels, this one stayed small and quiet. Many visitors now take guided tours of the old military tunnels.

Now cross the island to Komiža, on the western side. This is a fishing town, and its life has always turned toward the open sea. The fishermen here sailed a special boat called the falkuša, a fast, narrow sailing boat built for long trips to rich fishing grounds far offshore. On the harbour stands a sixteenth century Venetian tower called the Kaštel. Inside it, a fishing museum tells the story of these boats and the families who sailed them.

Food here follows the sea and the dry hills. Look for viška pogača or komiška pogača, a flatbread baked with salted fish, onion and tomato. It is simple, salty and filling, made for fishermen on long days at sea. The island also has a long wine tradition. Vugava is a local white grape, and plavac mali, a red grape found along the Dalmatian coast, grows on the sunny slopes. Small family producers often sell wine straight from their cellars.

The coastline is full of small coves. The best known is Stiniva, a pebble beach enclosed by tall cliffs that almost close above the water. From Komiža, boats also run to the nearby island of Biševo and its Blue Cave, where sunlight enters through an underwater opening and turns the water an electric blue. In more recent years, Vis has also appeared on screen. Parts of the musical film Mamma Mia! Here We Go Again were filmed on the island.

So take a breath. Feel the warm stone under your hand, and look at the water again. The same harbour that sheltered Greek traders, British frigates and wartime submarines now holds small fishing boats and swimmers. As you walk on, notice the old walls between the vineyards, the closed shutters in the afternoon heat, the smell of pine and salt. Vis stayed apart from the world for a long time... and it still lets you feel that distance, quietly.
