The guide

The story

Run your hand along the wall here. The surface is cold and uneven, made of thousands of flint nodules split open and set in mortar. Some are grey, some nearly black, and a few catch the light like dark glass. Above you, the wind moves across the top of a steep, grassy mound. Below, the roofs of an old market town fall away towards the river. This is Lewes Castle, and it sits a short train ride inland from Brighton.

Lewes is not part of Brighton itself. It is the county town of East Sussex, a separate place with its own long story. Many visitors to the coast come here for an afternoon, and it rewards the trip. The town grew up on a chalk spur above the River Ouse. That high ground, with water below and the Downs around it, is exactly why someone chose to build a fortress here.

That someone was William de Warenne. He was one of the Norman lords who crossed the Channel with William the Conqueror in 1066. After the conquest, he received huge estates in Sussex. A Norman lord needed to hold new land quickly and visibly — so he raised a castle. Its first form was earth and timber, built to control the river crossing and to show the local Saxon population who was now in charge.

Here is the first surprise. Most early Norman castles of this kind had a single mound, called a motte, beside an enclosed yard, called a bailey. Lewes has two mounds. Only a very small number of castles in England share this layout. You are standing near one of them now. The second mound lies to the east, and the old bailey stretches between them, though houses and gardens have long since filled much of that space.

The Warenne family soon replaced the wooden defences with stone. On top of the western mound they built a shell keep. This is a ring of stone walls that encircles the summit, rather than a single solid tower. In the thirteenth century, polygonal towers were added to the shell. Those towers still stand, and they give the keep its distinctive outline against the sky when you see it from the streets below.

The Warennes did more than build walls. William de Warenne and his wife, Gundrada, also founded a priory in Lewes, dedicated to Saint Pancras. Its ruins lie in the south of the town today. Castle and priory together show how a Norman lord thought about power. One building protected his body and his lands. The other was meant to protect his soul. Both were statements written in stone for everyone to see.

The most dramatic moment in this castle's story came in 1264. England was divided. King Henry the Third faced a rebellion of barons led by Simon de Montfort. The two armies met on the Downs just outside Lewes. The royal side had used the town and its castle as a base. The battle went badly for the king. Henry was captured, and for a time de Montfort held real control over the kingdom.

That battle matters beyond Sussex. In the months that followed, de Montfort called a parliament that included representatives from towns, not only great lords and churchmen. Historians often point to it as an early step towards the idea of a parliament that speaks for wider society. So the hills you can see from the top of this keep are linked to a long, slow change in how England was governed.

Now turn to the gatehouse, which visitors call the Barbican. It was added in the fourteenth century, in front of the older Norman gate. Look at the face of the wall. The flints here are knapped — split and shaped so their flat, dark faces sit neatly in rows. It is fine craftsmanship. High above the entrance, you can see projecting stonework with openings in the floor. Defenders could drop objects or pour liquids through them onto attackers below.

Here is the second surprise. Within the area of the old bailey lies a bowling green. Locals have played bowls on this patch of level ground for centuries. Picture it. Where knights once trained and soldiers gathered, people now roll wooden bowls across smooth grass on summer evenings. It is one of the quietest and most English details of the whole site, and many visitors walk straight past it.

As the centuries passed, the castle lost its military purpose. The Warenne line ended, and the buildings slowly decayed. In the nineteenth century, the Sussex Archaeological Society took responsibility for the site, and it still cares for it today. The society also runs a museum in Barbican House, next to the gatehouse. Inside, you can see objects from across Sussex history, from prehistoric finds to Roman and medieval material.

Climb to the top of the keep if you can. The steps are steep, but the view explains everything. To one side, the River Ouse runs through the valley towards the sea. All around, the smooth green shoulders of the South Downs rise and fall. Below you, the town spreads out in red tile, flint, and brick. From here, it is easy to understand why a Norman lord wanted this exact spot.

Lewes has a strong sense of its own identity, and the castle sits at the centre of it. Every year in early November, the town holds its famous bonfire celebrations. Torch-lit processions fill the narrow streets. Part of the tradition remembers seventeen Protestant martyrs burned in Lewes in the 1550s. If you visit in autumn, you may notice posters and preparations in the lanes around the castle.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind on the mound and the faint noise of the town below. Look again at the flint under your hand, shaped and set by people nearly a thousand years ago. As you walk down the lane and through the gatehouse, notice how the street bends around the old line of the walls. The castle still shapes the town — quietly, in every step you take.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the short train from Brighton to Lewes station.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep steps up the keep.
Combine your visit with the museum in Barbican House.
Walk to the Priory ruins afterwards for the Warenne story.
Choose a clear day for the widest views over the Downs.
