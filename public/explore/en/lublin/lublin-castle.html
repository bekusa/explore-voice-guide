The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the long ramp and look up. The walls are pale and smooth, almost like cake icing, with neat battlements along the top. On a bright day the stone glows a soft cream colour against the sky. Under your feet, the path climbs gently over an old earth embankment. The sounds of the Old Town fall away behind you. Ahead, the castle waits on its hill... and it looks younger than it really is.

That first impression is a small trick. The building you see from the ramp is mostly from the nineteenth century, built in a neo-Gothic style, a revival of medieval forms. But this hill has held a fortress for far longer. In the early Middle Ages, a wooden stronghold stood here, guarding the river valley and the trade routes across eastern Poland. Over the centuries, wood gave way to stone. Each layer of building sits on top of the last, so the hill is a stack of different eras.

The oldest layer still above ground is the round tower, called the donjon, or keep. It is usually dated to the thirteenth century, which makes it the oldest standing building in Lublin. Its walls are thick and heavy, built in the Romanesque tradition. Romanesque is the solid, rounded style of building that came before the pointed arches of Gothic. Look at how little decoration it has. This tower was built for one purpose: to survive an attack. Today you can climb it for a view across the city.

In the fourteenth century, King Casimir the Great turned the hill into a proper stone castle. He was known for building fortresses across his kingdom, and Lublin was an important point on the road east. Later, the Jagiellonian dynasty, the royal family that ruled Poland and Lithuania together, used the castle often. Kings stayed here. Royal children were educated here. For a time, this quiet hill sat close to the centre of power in a very large state.

And that brings us to the most famous moment in this castle's story. In 1569, nobles from Poland and Lithuania met in Lublin and agreed to join their two countries into one. This agreement is known as the Union of Lublin. It created the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, one of the largest states in Europe at that time. Inside the castle museum, you can see a huge painting of this event by Jan Matejko, Poland's best-known history painter. Take a moment in front of it. The faces, the fabrics, the drama... Matejko painted history as theatre.

Now for the real treasure. Tucked against the castle is the Chapel of the Holy Trinity. From outside, it looks like a modest Gothic church, with tall, narrow windows and brick walls. Many visitors walk past it. Please do not. Inside, almost every surface is covered with medieval paintings. The walls, the vault, the pillar... all of it is colour. Ochre, deep red, blue, and gold surround you from floor to ceiling.

Here is the surprise. The building is Catholic and Gothic, in the Western style. But the paintings are Byzantine in style, the art tradition of the Orthodox Christian world. They were made by painters from Ruthenia, the lands to the east, during the reign of King Władysław Jagiełło. An inscription inside the chapel gives the year the work was finished: 1418. So in one small room, two branches of Christian art meet. That combination is very rare in Europe.

Look up at the ceiling. The ribs of the vault spread out from a single central pillar, a little like the branches of a palm tree. Then look for the king himself. Jagiełło appears in the paintings more than once. In one scene he rides a horse. In another, he kneels in prayer. These are portraits of a real ruler, painted during his lifetime. It is not often that you can stand this close to a medieval king.

The paintings also survived by a kind of accident. For a long time they were hidden under layers of plaster. They were found again in the late nineteenth century, and careful restoration work continued through the twentieth century. The chapel is small and the paintings are fragile, so the number of visitors inside at one time is limited. Move slowly. Let your eyes adjust to the dim light. Details appear the longer you stand still.

Now we come to the darker chapter. By the early nineteenth century, the old royal castle was in ruins. In the 1820s, under Russian control, the authorities rebuilt it — not as a palace, but as a prison. That is the neo-Gothic building you saw from the ramp. The pretty battlements were decoration on a place of confinement. For well over a century, these walls held prisoners, many of them political.

During the Second World War, the German occupiers used the castle as a prison. Thousands of people passed through it. In July 1944, just before the Germans left the city, many prisoners were killed here. After the war, the prison continued under the new communist authorities, who used it against their own opponents. It finally closed in 1954. A few years later, the building became a museum. Memorials around the castle remember those who suffered inside.

Before you leave, walk to the edge and look down at the open square below the castle. Today it is a wide, quiet space with grass and paths. Before the war, this area was full of streets and houses. It was the heart of Jewish Lublin, a large and long-established community. The district was destroyed during the war, and its people were murdered in the Holocaust. The emptiness you see is not natural. It is a gap where a whole neighbourhood once stood.

So pause for a moment on the ramp as you go. Behind you are a medieval tower, a painted royal chapel, and the walls of an old prison. In front of you is the Old Town, with the Grodzka Gate leading back into its narrow streets. Notice the quiet. Notice the cream walls catching the light. This hill has seen kings, treaties, prisoners, and loss. And now it is a place where people walk slowly, look closely, and remember.
