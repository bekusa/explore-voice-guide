The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against stones at the shore, a soft and steady rhythm. A breeze moves through old trees and carries the cool, green smell of the lake. Somewhere a boat engine hums and then fades. Through the branches you can see pale walls and a steep roof. This is Possenhofen Castle, on the western shore of Lake Starnberg, just south of Munich in Bavaria. It is a quiet place today, but it was once full of children, dogs, horses and laughter.

The castle stands in the municipality of Pöcking, a small community spread across gentle green hills above the water. Lake Starnberg is long and narrow, and on clear days the Alps rise along its southern horizon. For centuries, wealthy families from Munich came to these shores to escape the heat of the city. They built villas, hunting lodges and country houses. Possenhofen is one of the oldest of these lakeside residences. Its roots go back many centuries, and the building was rebuilt and changed many times over the years.

The chapter that most visitors come for begins in the nineteenth century. In 1834, Duke Maximilian in Bavaria bought the castle. Note the small word in his title. He was a duke "in" Bavaria, not "of" Bavaria. That word marked a side branch of the royal Wittelsbach family, the dynasty that ruled Bavaria for centuries. This branch had royal blood, but it had little political duty. That freedom shaped everything that happened in this house.

Duke Max was an unusual aristocrat. He loved folk music and played the zither, a flat stringed instrument common in the Alpine countryside. He travelled widely, he wrote, and he was happiest among ordinary people in the villages and taverns. He brought that relaxed spirit to Possenhofen. His family spent their summers here, and life at the castle was famous for its informality. The children rode, swam, climbed and ran across the fields with very little ceremony.

One of those children was a girl named Elisabeth, born in Munich in 1837. Her family called her Sisi. Today the world knows her as Empress Elisabeth of Austria. Here, though, she was simply a lively daughter who loved horses, long walks and the open air. She is said to have called this castle by a fond nickname, "Possi". For the rest of her life, this lakeshore stood for freedom and for a childhood that she never stopped missing.

Now comes the turn in the story. In 1853, Sisi travelled with her mother and her older sister to the spa town of Bad Ischl in Austria. The plan was for her sister to meet the young Emperor Franz Joseph. But the emperor fell for Sisi instead. They married the following year, when she was still a teenager. In a very short time, the girl from Possenhofen became empress of one of the largest states in Europe.

The Viennese court was the opposite of life here. It had strict rules, endless ceremony and constant observation. Sisi found it very hard. Over the decades she became restless, and she travelled constantly. She sought out the sea, the mountains and distant places. But she returned to Lake Starnberg again and again. Her letters and the memories of the people around her show a deep attachment to the landscape of her youth. Her life ended in Geneva in 1898, when an assassin attacked her on the lakeside promenade.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Across the water, on the eastern shore, stands Berg Castle. That was a residence of Sisi's cousin, King Ludwig the Second of Bavaria. He is the dreamy king famous for Neuschwanstein, the fairy-tale castle in the Alps. Ludwig and Sisi shared a love of solitude and poetry. And in 1886, Ludwig died in the shallow water of this same lake, near Berg. The circumstances are still debated today.

Look toward the small island in the lake, closer to the nearby town of Feldafing. That is the Rose Island, once a royal retreat with a little villa and a rose garden. According to a popular local tale, Sisi and Ludwig left secret letters for each other there. It is a romantic story, and people love to tell it. But it is best treated as legend. What is certain is that the two cousins knew each other well, and both of them loved this water.

After the age of the dukes, the castle had a difficult century. Ownership changed, and the twentieth century, with its wars, brought hard times. For a long period the building stood neglected, and it fell into serious decay. Then, in the late twentieth century, it was restored and divided into private apartments. That is the most important practical fact for you today. The castle is a private residence, and you cannot tour its rooms.

So enjoy it as Sisi's family often did — from the outside, and from the shore. Walk the paths along the water and look back at the pale facade framed by trees. Notice how the building sits low and close to the lake, as if it belongs to the water rather than to the hill. The surrounding parkland and lakeshore are where the real atmosphere lives. Families picnic here, swimmers walk down to the water, and in summer the meadows smell of cut grass.

If you want to see objects connected to Sisi, there is a small museum nearby. It is dedicated to Empress Elisabeth and is housed in a historic building near the old Possenhofen railway station. The railway made this lakeshore easy to reach from Munich in the nineteenth century, and royal visitors used it too. Today the trains still bring day-trippers from the city, just as they did more than a century ago. The short walk between station, museum and shore makes a natural loop.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the edge of the lake. Watch the light change on the surface. In the morning it can be silver and flat; in the late afternoon it turns golden and soft. Look at the far shore, where Berg lies among the trees. Then look back at the pale walls behind you. A young girl once ran down to this water, long before crowns and courts. The lake has not changed much since then... and as you walk away, the sound of the small waves will follow you up the path.

Before you go

Practical tips

View the castle from the lakeside paths, not the interior.
Arrive by the regional train from Munich to Possenhofen station.
Combine your visit with the nearby Empress Elisabeth museum.
Bring swimwear in summer for the public lakeshore meadows.
Come late afternoon for warm light over the lake.
