The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest reaches you through sound. Dry leaves crack under a wheel or a boot. A peacock calls from somewhere out of sight. Then a sharp, barking cry cuts through the trees, and your guide may go very still. In this forest, sound is often the first sign that something large is moving nearby.

You are standing in Sanjay National Park, in the Sidhi district of Madhya Pradesh, in central India. This is not a park built around one famous view. It is a place of long, quiet distances. The trees thin and close again. The ground rises into low hills and drops toward stream beds. The forest stretches far beyond what the eye can follow.

The dominant tree here is the sal. It is a tall hardwood with a straight trunk and broad, leathery leaves. In much of central and eastern India, sal forest covers huge areas of land. When the leaves fall, they cover the ground in a brown, papery layer. That layer crunches loudly. It is one reason why animals here can hear people long before people see them. Between the sal trees, look for clumps of bamboo and open patches of grass.

Here is something most visitors do not know. The park you are visiting is only part of a much larger original park. When it was first established, Sanjay National Park covered a far wider stretch of forest. Then, in 2000, the state of Chhattisgarh was created from the eastern part of Madhya Pradesh. The new border ran straight through the park. The larger share went to Chhattisgarh, where it became Guru Ghasidas National Park. The forest did not change. Only the lines on the map moved.

Today, this park forms the heart of the Sanjay-Dubri Tiger Reserve. It is joined with the neighbouring Dubri Wildlife Sanctuary. Together they protect a corridor of forest that links with other wild areas across central India. That word, corridor, matters a great deal. Tigers need room. A young male may walk a very long way to find territory of his own. Connected forests let him make that journey without crossing too many villages and roads.

So, is there a tiger behind those trees? Perhaps. But be honest with yourself about the odds. Sanjay is a quieter, less visited reserve than some of the famous parks of Madhya Pradesh. Tiger sightings here are possible, but they are never promised. Many visitors leave without seeing a single stripe. And many of those visitors still say the day was worth it. The pleasure here is in the searching, and in everything else you see along the way.

And there is a lot else to see. Spotted deer, called chital, move in herds through the open ground. Their coats are reddish brown with white spots. Sambar, the largest deer in India, are darker and heavier, and they prefer thicker cover. You may see nilgai, a large antelope with a blue-grey coat on the males. Wild boar dig along the forest edge. Langur monkeys, grey with black faces, watch from the branches above.

Now, back to that barking cry. In Indian forests, deer and monkeys give alarm calls when they see a predator. A chital gives a high, sharp bark. A langur gives a deep, coughing call from the treetops. Experienced guides read these sounds like a message. If the calls repeat and move, a tiger or a leopard may be walking just out of view. This is the second surprise of the forest. Often the best tracker in the jungle is a monkey.

The predators here are not only tigers. Leopards live in these hills too. They are smaller, quieter, and very good at staying hidden. Sloth bears move through the forest at dawn and dusk, digging for termites with long, curved claws. Their shaggy black coats make them look larger than they are. Look also at the soft sand of the track. Guides often stop to read pugmarks, the paw prints that show which animal passed, and how recently.

The forest also matters to the people who live around it. Villages sit along the edges of the reserve, and many families have deep ties to this land. One tree tells part of that story. The mahua tree drops pale, sweet-smelling flowers in the dry season. Local people gather these flowers for food and other uses. Bears and deer love them too. If you smell something sweet and heavy in the warm air, a mahua tree may be close by.

The season changes this place completely. In the cooler months, the mornings are misty and soft, and the light comes in low through the trees. In the hot months before the rains, the forest becomes dry and open. Animals gather near the remaining water, so sightings can become easier. Then the monsoon arrives. The whole landscape turns green, the streams fill, and the tracks turn to mud. Every visit here feels different from the last.

A few words on how to watch. Keep your voice low, even when you are excited. Avoid bright colours, which stand out against the brown and green. Hold your camera ready, but do not spend the whole drive behind the lens. Sometimes the best moment is simply watching a deer lift its head and turn its ears toward a sound you cannot hear. Listen to your guide. Every person in the vehicle helps by staying patient and still.

Before you leave, take one quiet minute. Turn off your phone screen. Look at the trunks of the sal trees around you, the dust on the track, the light coming down through the leaves. Listen for birds, for the wind, for one more alarm call. You may not have seen a tiger today. But a tiger may well have seen you. And as you drive away, the forest will close behind you, exactly as it has always done.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book safari permits well ahead through official forest department channels.
Choose early morning drives for cooler air and active wildlife.
Wear muted greens or browns instead of bright colours.
Bring binoculars for distant deer, birds, and treetop langurs.
Check seasonal closures before planning a trip in monsoon months.
