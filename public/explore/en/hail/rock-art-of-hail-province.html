The guide

The story

Run your hand near the rock, without touching it, and feel the warmth coming off the sandstone. The surface is dark, almost burnished, like old bronze. Then the light shifts, and pale lines appear in that dark skin. A camel. A long-horned animal. A human figure with raised arms. Around you the desert is quiet except for the wind moving over the sand. Thousands of years ago, someone stood exactly where you are standing and cut these shapes into the stone.

This is the Rock Art of Hail Province, in the north of Saudi Arabia. In 2015 it became the country's first World Heritage Site to be recognised for its rock art. The listing covers two separate places. One is Jabal Umm Sinman, a rocky mountain beside the town of Jubbah, northwest of the city of Ha'il. The other is a pair of sites near Shuwaymis, to the south: Jabal al-Manjor and Raat. Together they hold one of the largest collections of petroglyphs in the region.

A petroglyph is an image cut, scratched or pecked into rock, rather than painted onto it. Here, the method was simple and clever. Over many centuries, the desert sun and minerals coat sandstone with a thin dark layer. People call it desert varnish. When an artist chipped through that layer with a harder stone, the lighter rock underneath showed through. The result is a picture in pale lines on a dark ground... and it can last for thousands of years.

Now the first surprise. The desert around Jubbah was not always desert. Scientists who study the ancient climate have found that a lake once lay at the foot of Jabal Umm Sinman. In wetter periods, this basin held water, plants and animals. People came to its shores to hunt, to gather and to rest. The mountain rose above the water like a landmark. And on its rocks, generation after generation left a record of the world they knew.

Look closely at the animals, and that lost world comes back. Some carvings show cattle with long, curving horns. Cattle need grass and water, so these images point to a greener time. Other carvings show ibex, the wild mountain goat, and oryx, the straight-horned antelope of Arabia. Ostriches appear too. Over time, the cast of animals changes. The camel and the horse arrive, and they become more and more common.

That change is one of the most valuable things about these rocks. They are not a single gallery made in one moment. They are layers of images made across a very long span, often described as around ten thousand years. Some figures were carved on top of older ones. Some older figures are darker, because the varnish has had more time to grow back over the cut lines. The rock works almost like a history book written in pictures.

The human figures are the second surprise. At Jubbah, some people are shown close to life size. They stand tall and narrow, sometimes with arms lifted, sometimes with what look like belts or decorated clothing. Nobody knows for certain what these large figures meant. Were they gods, ancestors, hunters, or dancers? Researchers offer different ideas. It is honest to say the meaning is still open, and that is part of the pull of standing in front of them.

Hunting scenes appear often across the province. You may see figures with bows, sometimes with dogs at their side, following animals across the rock. Some scenes seem to tell a small story: a chase, an encounter, a group moving together. Others are single animals, carved with care and placed alone on a smooth panel. The artists knew their subjects well. A curved neck or a set of horns can be recognised at once, even after so many centuries.

The rocks carry words as well as pictures. Many panels include short inscriptions in ancient scripts of North Arabia. Scholars often group them under the name Thamudic, a broad label for early alphabets used across the Arabian Peninsula before the Arabic script spread. Many of these lines are thought to be names, short messages or prayers. Imagine travellers stopping here, reading the older carvings, and adding their own names beside them.

Shuwaymis feels different from Jubbah. Jubbah sits at the edge of the Nafud, a great desert of red and orange sand. Shuwaymis lies further south, among sandstone outcrops and valleys, and it feels more remote. The carvings there are also rich in animals and people, including scenes with cattle that again suggest a wetter past. If you visit both, notice how each landscape shapes the experience of the art.

These places were never truly lost. Local people in Hail Province have known the carved rocks for generations, and Bedouin travellers passed them for centuries. In the nineteenth century, European travellers crossing northern Arabia wrote about the carvings and inscriptions at Jubbah. Modern archaeological work came later, as Saudi and international teams studied, mapped and recorded the panels. Protection now matters as much as study, because a single careless scratch cannot be undone.

So, how do you look at rock art well? Slow down. Walk along a panel and let your eyes adjust. Low light, early or late in the day, throws shadows into the shallow grooves and makes faint figures appear. In the hard light of midday, many images almost disappear. Step back to see the whole panel, then step close to see the marks of the tool. Each tiny dent was one blow of stone on stone.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look out from the rock instead of at it. See the line of the land, the colour of the sand, the empty sky. The people who carved these figures saw a different view, with water, grass and herds below. Then turn back to the dark stone. The camels, the ibex and the tall human figures are still there, holding their places... as they have for thousands of years, waiting for the next person to notice them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon for raking light.
Arrange site access through official channels before travelling.
Never touch the carvings, skin oils damage the varnish.
Carry plenty of water and sun protection for desert walks.
Hire a local guide to find the faintest panels.
