The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see much at all, you hear water. A steady, layered rush rolls down the slope ahead of you. Underfoot, the gravel crunches. The air carries a cool dampness, even on a bright Northumberland day. This is how the Alnwick Garden greets most visitors. It opens with sound and movement, not with quiet flower beds. Take a moment where you are. Let your eyes follow the water down the hill before we start walking together.

That moving water is the Grand Cascade, the centrepiece of the garden. It is a wide staircase of stone weirs, and water tumbles from one level to the next. At intervals, jets shoot upward from the sides and arch across the channels. The pattern changes through the day, so the cascade never looks quite the same twice. Children often stand at the edges, waiting for the next burst. Adults pretend they are not doing the same thing.

Here is the first surprise. This garden is young. It is not a centuries-old estate garden, preserved under glass. The modern Alnwick Garden was created from the late 1990s onward and opened in the early 2000s. The driving force was Jane Percy, the Duchess of Northumberland. Her family has lived at Alnwick Castle, just next door, for many generations. She wanted a garden that people from the region could use and enjoy, not only admire from behind a rope.

The site itself is older than the planting. The Dukes of Northumberland had a walled garden here long before. It was used for growing food during the Second World War, when Britain urged people to dig for victory. Later it became a nursery, and then it slowly fell out of use. For decades, much of this hillside was neglected. So when you walk these paths, you are walking on an old foundation with a new design laid over it.

The design came from Belgian landscape architects Jacques and Peter Wirtz, a father and son. Their style relies on strong lines and clipped green shapes. You can see it in the hedges of hornbeam, a hardy tree that takes clipping well, framing the cascade. The structure is formal and almost architectural. Yet the plants inside it soften those hard edges. Look along the hedges and notice how they guide your eye straight up the slope.

Now for the garden's most talked-about corner. Behind a pair of black iron gates sits the Poison Garden. The gates carry a skull and crossbones, and they stay locked. You can only enter with a guide. Inside grow plants that can harm, and in some cases kill. Some are familiar from ordinary gardens, which is part of the point. The guides tell stories of poisonings, real and legendary, and they are good at it. Please do not touch, smell, or taste anything inside.

The idea for the Poison Garden reportedly came from the Duchess's interest in Renaissance Italy, where noble families were linked with poison. Whether or not every tale is true, the effect works. People who would never join a talk about botany listen closely here. It is a clever way to teach. You learn which plants to respect, and why some of them also have a place in medicine. A single leaf can be both a cure and a danger.

If you climb toward the top of the cascade, you reach the Ornamental Garden. This is the walled part of the site. Here the mood changes. The rush of water fades, and the space feels enclosed and calm. Beds are packed with flowering plants, and fruit trees are trained flat against frames. In summer the colours are deep and layered. Bees work the borders. It is a good place to sit for a few minutes and simply let the noise of the cascade fall away.

Nearby is the Rose Garden. Its fragrance often reaches you before the flowers do. Many of the roses here are old-fashioned English types, with full, cupped blooms. They climb over pergolas and arches, so you walk under them as well as past them. On a warm afternoon the scent becomes heavy and sweet. If you visit early in the season, you may only see buds. Come in high summer and the arches can be thick with flowers.

The second surprise sits among the trees. The Treehouse is a large wooden structure raised on stilts and walkways, with rope bridges between its parts. It is among the biggest wooden treehouses in the world. Inside there is even a restaurant, warmed by a log fire on cold days. Walking across the bridges, you feel the boards flex slightly under your feet. Look up, and you see turrets and pointed roofs. It looks like something from a storybook, built at full adult scale.

Other corners reward slower walking. There is a bamboo labyrinth, where tall green stems close around you and the paths twist. There is also the Serpent Garden, set among topiary. Here, sculpted water features play with surface tension, reflection, and flow. Some appear perfectly still until you look closely and see the water moving. Children like to test them with a fingertip. Adults often stand longer than expected, trying to work out how each one works.

In spring, many visitors head for the cherry orchard. Hundreds of white-flowering cherry trees grow here, planted in loose rows. When they bloom, the petals gather in drifts across the grass. Swings hang from some of the branches. It is one of the quieter places in the garden, and people tend to lower their voices. Outside blossom season it is still a pleasant green space. But for a short window each year, it turns almost entirely white.

Before you go, return to where you can see the Grand Cascade again. Notice how the slope, the hedges, and the water all point toward the same place. The garden was built as a gift to its community, and that idea still shapes it. As you walk away, listen once more. The rush of water will follow you for a while... then fade behind the hedges. Let it go slowly. Then step back into the town of Alnwick.
