The guide

The story

Listen first. Beech leaves move overhead, and somewhere below, the low hum of traffic rises from the valley. Then the trees open, and three round towers appear. Each one wears a pointed conical roof. The stone has a warm, rusty tone, and you can almost feel why people named this place Castell Coch. In Welsh, that simply means the Red Castle. It stands on a steep hillside above the village of Tongwynlais, between Cardiff and Caerphilly.

At first glance, this looks like a medieval fortress from a storybook. In fact, it is mostly a Victorian creation. It is a nineteenth-century dream of the Middle Ages, built on real medieval ruins. That double life is the key to the whole place. Part of what you see is old stone. Part of it is an imagined past, designed by two very unusual men. Let us meet them.

The first was John Crichton-Stuart, the third Marquess of Bute. Coal and the docks at Cardiff had made his family enormously rich. He was a scholar with deep interests in history, religion, and languages. The second man was the architect William Burges. Burges loved medieval art with a passion that bordered on obsession. Together, they had already transformed Cardiff Castle. Castell Coch became their second, smaller, and perhaps more personal project.

But the story begins much earlier. A castle stood on this ridge in the Middle Ages, probably in the thirteenth century. Historians often link it to the powerful de Clare family. These Norman lords also built the vast castle at Caerphilly, just over the hills. The site made sense. It guarded a narrow gorge where the River Taff runs south toward the sea. Over the centuries, though, the castle fell into ruin. By the Victorian era, only broken walls and overgrown foundations remained.

Burges studied those ruins carefully before he drew anything. He produced a detailed report and a set of beautiful drawings. Then, from the 1870s, the rebuilding began. Burges kept the old foundations and raised new walls on top of them. He studied castles in France and elsewhere for ideas. The tall conical roofs, for example, owe more to continental Europe than to Wales. Most Welsh castles never had roofs like these. That is the first surprise many visitors miss.

Now look at the entrance. A real drawbridge crosses the dry ditch, and a portcullis hangs in the gatehouse. These are not just decoration. The mechanisms were built to work, in the spirit of a true medieval defence. Burges wanted the castle to feel believable, not like a stage set. As you cross, notice how narrow the approach is. Any attacker would have been forced into a tight, exposed space. The design follows real military logic, even if no army ever came.

Step into the courtyard and the mood changes. The space is small and enclosed, almost intimate. Timber galleries run along the walls, and stone steps climb toward the towers. The three towers have names: the Keep Tower, the Kitchen Tower, and the Well Tower. Each one has a different job. From here, the castle feels less like a fortress and more like a private retreat. That is exactly what it was meant to be.

Inside, the colour arrives all at once. The Drawing Room is the showpiece. Its walls are covered with painted plants and animals, many drawn from the ancient fables of Aesop, the Greek storyteller. High above, the ribbed ceiling is painted with birds, butterflies, and stars. Over the fireplace stand sculpted figures of the Three Fates. In Greek myth, these three women spin, measure, and cut the thread of every human life. Take your time here. The details reward patience.

Here is the second surprise. William Burges died in 1881, long before the interiors were finished. His team, led by his assistant William Frame, carried on the work using his designs and his spirit. So some rooms show Burges at his boldest, and others show his followers completing his vision. Look for the small differences in style as you move between rooms. The castle is, in a quiet way, also a memorial to its architect.

Upstairs, Lady Bute's Bedroom feels like the inside of a jewel box. A dome rises above the room, painted and gilded. The bed itself is a striking piece of furniture, with crystal balls topping its posts. The room is round, soft, and dreamlike. By contrast, Lord Bute's own bedroom nearby is much simpler. That contrast says something about the couple, or at least about how Burges imagined them. Yet for all this effort, the family visited only rarely.

Before you leave, consider the hillside itself. Lord Bute planted a vineyard on the slopes below the castle in the Victorian period. It was an ambitious experiment in the cool, damp climate of South Wales. For a time, it actually produced wine. The vineyard did not survive into the modern age, but the idea tells you a lot about Bute. He loved a challenge, and he had the money to try almost anything.

The woods around you matter too. The castle sits within a large beech forest, one of the finest in this part of Wales. In autumn, the leaves turn copper and gold, and the castle almost matches them. In spring, the floor of the wood fills with fresh green. Walking trails lead out from the castle into the trees. Today, the Welsh heritage body Cadw cares for the building and keeps its painted rooms protected.

As you walk back across the drawbridge, turn around once more. Look at those three towers against the sky. Remember that you are seeing two ages at once — medieval foundations and a Victorian fantasy built on top. Listen again to the leaves, and to the valley below. Then take the path down slowly. Somewhere through the trees, the red stone will catch the light one last time.
