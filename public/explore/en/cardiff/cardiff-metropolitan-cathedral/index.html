The guide

The story

Step in from Charles Street and the sound changes almost at once. Behind you are buses, footsteps and the hum of Cardiff's shopping streets. In here, the air is cooler and the noise drops away. Your shoes tap on the floor and the echo rises toward the roof. This is the Metropolitan Cathedral of Saint David, the mother church of Roman Catholics in Cardiff and much of South Wales. It does not stand on a hill or in a green close. It stands right on a city street, among offices and shops.

That setting tells you something about how this church began. In the nineteenth century, Cardiff grew very fast. Coal from the valleys of South Wales came down to the docks here, and the town became one of the busiest coal ports in the world. Work drew people from across Britain and beyond. Many of them came from Ireland, and many were Catholic. They needed churches close to where they lived and worked. So this building grew out of a working city, not out of a royal or noble plan.

The church was built in the 1880s, and it opened in 1887. Its designers were Pugin and Pugin, a family firm of church architects. The name may sound familiar. Augustus Pugin, the father of the firm's founders, helped shape the Gothic Revival style in Britain. That style looked back to the pointed arches and tall windows of medieval churches. His sons carried the family style into many Catholic churches across the country. Look for the pointed arches here. They belong to that tradition.

At first, this was a parish church, not a cathedral. That changed in 1916, when Cardiff became the seat of a Catholic archdiocese. An archdiocese is a large church region led by an archbishop. The word metropolitan in the cathedral's name comes from that role. It means this is the main church of a wider group of dioceses. So a church built for the dock workers and their families became the head church of a whole region of Wales.

Now for the hardest chapter. During the Second World War, Cardiff was a target for German bombers, because of its docks and industry. In March 1941, during a heavy air raid, bombs struck this cathedral. Fire swept through the building and destroyed much of the interior. Imagine the people of the parish coming here the next morning. They would have seen smoke, broken glass and an open roof. Much of what you see around you today is the result of that loss.

The rebuilding took many years. Britain after the war was short of money and materials, and homes came first. The restored cathedral reopened in 1959. This history explains a feature that surprises many visitors. The interior feels calm, light and fairly plain, rather than heavy with Victorian decoration. Part of the old building survived, but much of what you see is the work of the post-war years. When you look at the walls and the roof, you are looking at a church that was rebuilt by its community.

Take a moment to look at the name above the door. The cathedral is dedicated to Saint David, the patron saint of Wales. In Welsh he is called Dewi Sant. He was a bishop in the sixth century, in the far west of Wales. Wales celebrates his feast day on the first of March. On that day you may see people wearing daffodils or leeks, two national symbols of Wales. A cathedral named for him in the Welsh capital is a natural fit.

One saying is closely linked to Saint David. According to tradition, his last words to his followers included the advice, do the little things. In Welsh, it is gwnewch y pethau bychain. We cannot be sure he said these exact words, because the story comes from a life of the saint written centuries after his death. But the phrase is well loved in Wales. It suits this building, too. This is not a grand palace of a church. It is a place of small, steady acts of faith.

Here is a detail that confuses many visitors. Cardiff has more than one cathedral, and it has many places named after Saint David. Llandaff Cathedral, in the north-west of the city, belongs to the Church in Wales, which is Anglican. This cathedral is Roman Catholic. Close by you will also find Saint David's Hall, a concert venue, and a large shopping centre with the same name. So if a local gives you directions to Saint David's, ask which one they mean.

Music is a large part of life in this cathedral. Wales has a strong tradition of choral singing, and the cathedral has its own choir. If you visit during a sung service, you will hear how the voices fill the space. The hard surfaces of stone and plaster carry the sound and hold it for a moment after each phrase. Even outside service times, you may hear an organist practising. Stand still and listen to how long each note stays in the air.

Remember that this is a working church, not a museum. People come in during the day to pray, to light a candle or to sit quietly for a few minutes. Some are on a lunch break from nearby offices. Others are travellers like you. Move slowly, keep your voice low and switch your phone to silent. If a Mass is taking place, it is best to sit at the back or come back later to look around. The quiet here is something people share.

As you walk around, notice the side chapels and the statues of saints. Each one is a small place of personal devotion. You may see rows of candles burning in front of a statue. Each candle usually stands for a prayer, often for someone who is ill or someone who has died. Notice the way coloured light from the windows moves across the floor as clouds pass over Cardiff. On a bright day the colours are strong. On a grey Welsh day they are soft.

Before you go, stand for a moment near the back and look toward the altar. Think about the long story of this place. Dock workers and their families built it. The war almost destroyed it. A new generation rebuilt it in the years of rationing and repair. Now step back out onto Charles Street. The noise of the city returns, and the shops are only a few steps away. Notice how close the quiet and the busy street really are.
