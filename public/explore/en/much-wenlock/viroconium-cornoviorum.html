The guide

The story

Start with the ground beneath your feet. It is short grass, low walls of rough grey stone, and the outlines of rooms open to the sky. Then lift your eyes to the one thing that does not fit. A tall, ragged wall stands alone in the field, patched with red tile and pale rubble. Rooks call from the trees. The wind comes across flat Shropshire farmland. This is what remains above ground of Viroconium Cornoviorum, a Roman city that once held thousands of people.

First, a word about where you are. The ruins sit beside the village of Wroxeter, in the countryside between Shrewsbury and the Much Wenlock area. The River Severn runs close by. On a clear day you can see the long dark shape of the Wrekin, a hill to the east. That hill matters to this story. Before the Romans came, the local people, a tribe the Romans called the Cornovii, are thought to have used the hilltop as a fortified stronghold.

The name of this place carries that history. Viroconium is a Latin form of an older, local name. Cornoviorum means 'of the Cornovii'. So the full name tells you this was the town of that tribe. When Rome took control of this region in the first century, it did something it often did across its empire. It moved local power off the hilltops and down into a planned town, where it could be watched, taxed, and slowly made Roman.

But the town did not begin as a town. It began as a military base. In the middle of the first century, Roman soldiers built a legionary fortress here, close to the river crossing. A legion was a large unit of professional infantry, several thousand men. From here, the army pushed west towards Wales. When the soldiers eventually moved on, the fortress did not disappear. A civilian settlement grew where the army had been, and it kept growing.

At its height, Viroconium became one of the largest cities in Roman Britain. It is often described as the fourth largest. Here is the first surprise. What you see around you is only a small corner of it. Most of the city still lies under the surrounding fields, never dug. Surveys using instruments that read beneath the soil have mapped streets, houses and public buildings under the crops. Farmers have been ploughing over a Roman city for centuries.

Now look again at that tall wall. Locals call it the Old Work. It is one of the largest pieces of free-standing Roman wall still standing in Britain. It once formed part of the public baths. It separated the bathing rooms from a great covered exercise hall. Notice the large opening in the middle. Bathers passed through that doorway, moving from exercise to the warm rooms. You are looking at a door frame that people walked through nearly two thousand years ago.

The baths were the social heart of a Roman town. People came to wash, but also to meet, talk business, gamble and gossip. On the ground near the wall, look for the rows of short stone or tile pillars. These held up the floors of the heated rooms. Hot air from furnaces flowed through the space beneath, warming the floor from below. The Romans called this system a hypocaust. Walk slowly here, and you can trace the plan of each room.

Across the road stood the forum, the market square and centre of local government. Here is the second surprise. Excavators found a large stone inscription that once stood above its entrance. It dedicates the building to the emperor Hadrian, the same ruler who built the famous wall in northern England. That dates the forum to around the 130s. It shows that this town on the edge of the empire had a direct link to the emperor himself.

The story does not end when Roman rule ended. Archaeologists working here in the later twentieth century found traces of large timber buildings on top of the old baths area. They date from after the Roman army had left Britain. Someone was still organising life here, in the fifth and perhaps sixth centuries. It suggests the city did not simply fall overnight. It faded slowly, as people reused the ruins in new ways.

After that, the stone itself moved on. For hundreds of years, local people treated the ruins as a quarry. Dressed Roman stone was useful, and it was free. If you visit the church in Wroxeter village, look closely at its walls. Roman stone is built into them. At the churchyard entrance, the gateposts are made from Roman columns. In a quiet way, the old city is still standing. It has just changed its shape.

Back on the site, you will also find a reconstructed Roman townhouse. It was built in recent years using Roman-style methods and materials, as part of a television project. It shows what a comfortable house in Viroconium might have looked like when it was new. The bright painted walls may surprise you. Roman buildings were not grey ruins to the people who lived in them. They were colourful, warm, and full of everyday noise.

The site is cared for by English Heritage, and a small museum displays objects found here. Look for the pieces of ordinary life. Pottery, coins, tools and jewellery tell you more about real people than any grand wall can. These were the belongings of shopkeepers, craftsmen and families. Some were Roman newcomers. Many more were probably Cornovii, whose grandparents had once lived on that hill to the east.

Before you leave, stand facing the Old Work once more. Behind it, imagine the streets running out across the fields, lined with shops, houses and workshops. Imagine the smoke from the bath furnaces and the sound of carts on the main road. Then come back to what is really here. The grass, the wind, the rooks in the trees. As you walk away, watch the fields. Somewhere under them, the rest of the city is still waiting.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the site museum before walking the ruins.
Wear sturdy shoes; the grass can be wet and uneven.
Walk to Wroxeter church to see reused Roman stone.
Bring a layer; the open fields catch the wind.
Look east on clear days to spot the Wrekin.
