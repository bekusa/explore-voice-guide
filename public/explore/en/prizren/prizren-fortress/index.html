The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the river runs over its stones, steady and low. A call to prayer drifts up from the old town, then another, slightly out of time with the first. The wind moves through dry grass and loose rock. Up here on the hill, the city sounds small and far away. It feels as if you have stepped above the noise into a quieter layer of the place.

This is Prizren Fortress, known locally as the Kalaja. It sits on a steep hill above the old town, watching over the river Bistrica, which locals also call the Lumbardhi. From these walls, the whole valley opens out. Rooftops of red tile spread below you. Minarets and church towers rise between them. And beyond the city, the land climbs towards the Sharr Mountains in the south.

The position explains almost everything about this fortress. Whoever held this hill controlled the valley and the roads through it. Routes between the Adriatic coast and the interior of the Balkans passed close by. So for centuries, different powers wanted this rock. The walls around you are not the work of one builder or one age. They are layers, added and repaired and rebuilt by people who came, ruled, and moved on.

The earliest story of the hill is the hardest to see. Historians believe a fortified site stood here in the late Roman or early Byzantine period. Byzantine sources mention a town called Petrizen in this region, and many scholars link that name to Prizren. Archaeological work on the hill in recent years has uncovered earlier layers beneath the later walls. Much of that ancient story is still under the ground you are standing on.

In the Middle Ages, Prizren became one of the most important cities of the medieval Serbian state. Rulers of the Nemanjić dynasty spent time here, and the city grew rich through trade. Merchants from Dubrovnik, on the Adriatic coast, kept business ties with the town. The fortress protected all of this. It was a guard post for a city that was, for a time, close to the centre of royal power in the region.

Then, in the fifteenth century, the Ottoman Empire took the city. The Ottomans ruled here for more than four hundred years, and they shaped both the town and the fortress. They strengthened and adapted the walls for a new age of warfare. Down below, they built mosques, baths, and the old stone bridge across the river. Much of the old town you see from here today carries that Ottoman imprint.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look down at the old town and find the large domed mosque with its tall, slender minaret. That is the Sinan Pasha Mosque, built in the early seventeenth century. From the fortress, you see its dome from above, which is an angle most people never get. Now look for church towers nearby. In one small frame, you can see centuries of different faiths living side by side.

If you walked up from the old town, you probably passed the ruins of the Church of the Holy Saviour along the path. It is a small medieval church, now roofless, with old brick and stone walls. It sits quietly on the slope, almost a halfway mark on the climb. Many people pass it without stopping. It is worth a pause on the way back down, when your breath is easier.

Prizren also holds an important place in Albanian history. In 1878, Albanian leaders met in this city to form what became known as the League of Prizren. This was a political movement that pushed for Albanian rights and unity in the late Ottoman period. The meeting took place down in the town, not here on the hill. But from these walls, you look over the streets where that chapter of history unfolded.

Now turn your attention to the walls themselves. Notice the stone. It is rough, uneven, and laid in many different ways. In some places the blocks are large and carefully set. In others, smaller stones are packed tightly with mortar. Those differences are clues. They show where one period ended and another began, and where later hands patched older damage. You are looking at a wall built over many lifetimes.

The fortress has suffered over the centuries. Wars, earthquakes, and simple neglect have all left their marks. For long stretches, parts of the hill stood in ruin. In recent years, conservation and archaeological teams have worked to stabilise the walls and study what lies beneath them. Walk carefully. The ground is uneven, and some stones are loose. Paths have been improved, but this is still an old fortress, not a polished museum.

The best moment up here is often late in the day. As the sun drops, the light turns warm and low. It catches the tiled roofs and the stone of the mosques, and the river shines between the houses. Then the lights of the town begin to come on, one window at a time. The calls to prayer at dusk travel up the hill clearly. Many visitors stay until the sky turns dark blue.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the edge of the walls. Look down at the river and follow it with your eyes through the old town. Find the stone bridge, the domes, the little squares where people sit with coffee. Soon you will be walking among them again. And when you do, look up once. The fortress will be there above the rooftops, where it has stood for centuries.
