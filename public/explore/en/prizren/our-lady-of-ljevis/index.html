The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the walls in front of you. Thin red bricks run in bands between pale blocks of stone. Each stone sits in its own frame of brick, like a tile set into mortar. In the afternoon the whole surface turns warm, almost pink. Somewhere nearby, traffic hums and a café radio plays. Yet this church has stood in the centre of Prizren for more than seven hundred years.

This is the Church of Our Lady of Ljeviš, a Serbian Orthodox church dedicated to the Virgin Mary. The name Ljeviš is usually linked to an old local term for this spot or its holy image. The building you see now dates from the early fourteenth century. It went up in the years around 1306 and 1307, during the reign of the Serbian king Stefan Uroš the Second Milutin. He was one of the great church builders of medieval Serbia.

Milutin did not start from empty ground. An older church already stood here, and his builders kept parts of it. They raised a new and grander structure on top of those foundations and walls. So the stones under your feet belong to more than one age. Look closely at the lower courses of masonry and you may notice the change in rhythm, where older work meets newer work.

The builders are not anonymous, which is rare for this period. An inscription on the facade names a master builder called Nikola. This is one of the few signatures of a medieval architect that survives in the region. Inside, the paintings are linked to the workshop of Michael Astrapas and Eutychios. These were Greek painters from Thessaloniki, and they worked for King Milutin on several churches. Their style belongs to the late Byzantine world, when painters gave saints real weight, real faces, and real emotion.

Now look up at the roofline. The church carries five domes, a central one with four smaller ones around it. This plan follows Byzantine models, the eastern Christian tradition centred on Constantinople. Above the western entrance rises a tall bell tower. It was built over an outer porch, which architects call an exonarthex. Together, the domes and the tower give the building a vertical lift that you notice from the streets around it.

Here is the first surprise. For several centuries, this was not a church at all. After the Ottoman Empire took control of the region in the fifteenth century, the building was turned into a mosque. It served as the main Friday mosque of the town. A minaret stood beside it, and the Christian paintings inside were covered with plaster. For generations, the people who prayed here never saw the saints on the walls.

And this leads to the second surprise. The workers who prepared the walls for plaster first cut thousands of small marks into the paintings. These chisel marks helped the new plaster hold to the old surface. When restorers removed the plaster in the twentieth century, the frescoes came back to light, but covered in these little cuts. Today the marks are part of the story. The saints look out through a fine net of scars.

The restoration work began in earnest after the Second World War. The minaret was removed, and specialists uncovered the medieval paintings layer by layer. Much was lost, but much survived. Among the most admired images is a painting of the Virgin holding the Christ child, often called the Virgin of Ljeviš. It is older than the main fresco cycle, and many scholars consider it one of the finest works of its kind in the Balkans.

The paintings also include portraits of the royal family. Medieval Serbian rulers liked to appear on church walls as founders and protectors. A painted founder holding a model of his church was a common way to show who paid for the building. These portraits are a direct link between the art and the politics of the age. They told every visitor who had built this house of prayer, and why it mattered.

The recent history of the church is painful. During unrest in Kosovo in March 2004, the church was set on fire and badly damaged. Some frescoes suffered further harm from the flames and smoke. Conservation work followed in the years after. In 2006, the church joined the UNESCO World Heritage site called Medieval Monuments in Kosovo. UNESCO also placed that site on its list of World Heritage in Danger, which reflects concern for its long-term protection.

Because of this history, access to the church is not always simple. The building is often locked, and visits inside may need to be arranged in advance. Even from outside, though, there is plenty to see. Walk slowly around the walls. Notice how the bands of brick change direction around the windows. Notice the small decorative patterns built into the masonry, made only from brick and mortar. A medieval builder planned every one of those lines.

The neighbourhood around the church tells its own story. Prizren is a town where several faiths and languages have lived side by side for centuries. Within a short walk, you can find mosques, an Ottoman stone bridge over the Bistrica river, and the old fortress on the hill above. The church sits in the middle of this mix. Its walls have seen markets, processions, wars, and ordinary days without number.

As you stand here, think of how many times this building has changed its meaning. First a church, then a mosque, then a monument, then a wounded relic, and now a protected heritage site. Through all of it, the brick and stone have stayed in place. The domes still catch the evening light. The bell tower still rises above the street.

Before you walk on, look once more at the facade. Find one single stone framed by its thin lines of brick. Someone placed it there with care, more than seven centuries ago. Then turn and listen to the town around you... the voices, the footsteps, the river not far away. The church keeps its quiet watch over all of it.
