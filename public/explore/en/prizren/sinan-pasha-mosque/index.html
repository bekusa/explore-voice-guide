The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The river runs over its stones somewhere below you, cafe cups clink on saucers, and voices drift across the square in Albanian, Turkish, and Serbian. Then your eye climbs. A grey stone dome sits above the rooftops, and beside it a slender minaret rises against the green slope of the hills. This is the Sinan Pasha Mosque, and for four centuries it has watched over the heart of Prizren.

The mosque was built in 1615, during the long Ottoman period. The Ottoman Empire was a Turkish-led state that ruled much of the Balkans for roughly five hundred years. Its patron was Sofu Sinan Pasha, a high-ranking official in the imperial administration. Do not confuse him with Mimar Sinan, the famous architect of Istanbul. That Sinan had died decades earlier. Here, the name belongs to a governor and patron, not to a builder of domes.

Stand back and look at the shape of the building. It follows a classic Ottoman plan. A single large dome covers a square prayer hall. In front, a covered porch with columns and smaller domes welcomes worshippers before they enter. The minaret, the tall tower used for the call to prayer, stands at one corner. The design is simple and balanced. Nothing is crowded. Every part has a job, and every part supports the weight of the dome.

Now look closely at the stone itself. The walls are built from cut blocks of local stone, laid in neat, even rows. Here is something many visitors walk past without knowing. Historians widely believe that some of this stone came from the Monastery of the Holy Archangels. That was a large medieval Serbian monastery in the gorge just upstream from the city. By the time this mosque was built, the monastery lay in ruins, and its stone was reused in new buildings across the town.

That detail tells you a great deal about Prizren. This is a city of layers. Byzantine, Serbian, Ottoman, Albanian, and Yugoslav histories all sit on top of one another here, sometimes literally in the same wall. A church tower stands within sight of minarets. A Turkish bathhouse sits near Orthodox chapels. The mosque is not separate from that story. It is built out of it, stone by stone.

Walk toward the entrance, and you will notice that the mosque sits slightly raised above the street. Steps lead up to the porch. This small rise gives the building presence in a crowded centre. From the porch, you can look out over the square and the river. In the past, this was where people paused, took off their shoes, and left the noise of the market behind them. Many still do exactly the same today.

If you go inside, the first surprise is the colour. The interior walls carry painted decoration — flowing floral patterns, vines, and geometric bands in soft reds, blues, greens, and ochres. In Islamic tradition, mosques avoid images of people and animals. Instead, artists used plants, shapes, and calligraphy to fill the space with beauty. Under the dome, the painted surfaces seem to lift the eye upward, toward the light from the windows.

Look for the mihrab, the decorated niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca. Worshippers face it during prayer. To its right stands the minbar, a tall, narrow staircase with a small platform at the top. The imam speaks from there during Friday sermons. Notice how quiet the room is, even when the square outside is full. The thick stone walls hold the sound of the city back.

The mosque has not always looked this cared for. Over the centuries it went through hard times. Earthquakes, neglect, and the political changes of the twentieth century all left their mark. For long periods, parts of the painted interior were faded or damaged. In recent years, a major restoration repaired the structure and brought much of the decoration back. What you see today is the result of careful, patient work by conservators.

Step outside again and turn toward the river. The Lumbardhi, also called the Bistrica, runs through the middle of town, and an old stone bridge with a single arch crosses it close by. On warm evenings, the riverbanks fill with people walking, eating ice cream, and meeting friends. This walk along the water is one of the oldest habits in Prizren. The mosque has stood as a backdrop to it for generations.

Now lift your eyes to the hill above the town. On the summit stand the walls of the fortress, known locally as Kalaja. From up there, the dome and minaret of Sinan Pasha sit among the red roofs below like a fixed point on a map. If you climb up later, look back down and find this building. It is one of the easiest ways to understand how the old town fits together.

Prizren also has a strong modern life. Every summer, the city hosts a well-known documentary film festival, and screenings sometimes take place in open-air spaces near the river. Young people fill the cafes around the square late into the night. Yet several times a day, the call to prayer still rises from this minaret over all of it. Old and new do not cancel each other out here. They share the same few streets.

Before you move on, stand still once more in front of the porch. Look at the stones, some perhaps carried down from a ruined monastery four centuries ago. Look at the dome, the columns, the people climbing the steps. Listen again for the river. As you walk away, notice how often this minaret appears between buildings, at the end of lanes, above the trees. Prizren keeps showing it to you.

Key facts

What to look for

The carved mihrab niche facing toward Mecca.
The tall narrow minbar staircase beside the mihrab.
Small domes crowning the columned front porch.
Even rows of cut stone blocks in the walls.
Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly and cover shoulders before entering.
Remove your shoes at the entrance to the prayer hall.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers.
Climb to the fortress later for the best view.
Walk the riverbank at dusk for evening atmosphere.
