The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water laps against stone somewhere below you, and the wind off Belfast Lough carries a faint smell of salt and rust. In front of you sits a small ship with a black hull and a white upper deck. A buff-coloured funnel rises from her middle. She looks modest beside the angular buildings of the Titanic Quarter. But this little vessel has a claim no other ship in the world can make.

This is the SS Nomadic. She is the last surviving ship of the White Star Line, the company that owned the Titanic. She was built here in Belfast at the Harland and Wolff shipyard, in the same years and by the same workforce that built the Olympic and the Titanic. So when you look at her riveted plates and her polished brass, you are looking at the craftsmanship of those famous liners, on a smaller scale.

Why build a little ship at all? The answer lies in France. The great White Star liners were simply too large to come alongside the quay at the port of Cherbourg. Passengers had to be carried out to them in the open harbour. The Nomadic was built for exactly that job. She was a tender — a ship that ferries people and luggage between the shore and a larger vessel anchored offshore.

She was launched in 1911 and sailed to Cherbourg to begin her work. Her partner in the harbour was a smaller tender called the Traffic, which carried mail and baggage. The Nomadic had the more glamorous role. She carried first class and second class passengers. For many wealthy travellers, her saloons were their first taste of White Star luxury before they ever saw the liner itself.

That brings us to the evening of 10 April 1912. The Titanic had left Southampton that day and stopped off Cherbourg after dark. The Nomadic came out across the harbour, her decks full of passengers in coats and hats. Among the people who boarded the Titanic at Cherbourg were the millionaire John Jacob Astor and Margaret Brown, later remembered as the unsinkable Molly Brown. Within a few days, the Titanic was lost in the North Atlantic.

Think about that as you walk her decks. The stairs, the rails, the benches around you are the last surfaces many of those passengers touched before they stepped onto the Titanic. The great liner now lies on the ocean floor. Her little tender survived, and she has come home to the city that built them both.

Her story did not end in 1912. During the First World War, she served at the French port of Brest, carrying American troops ashore as they arrived in Europe. During the Second World War, she was again caught up in the conflict and took part in the evacuation of Cherbourg as France fell in 1940. Between the wars, she returned to tender work, meeting the great ocean liners as they called at Cherbourg.

Then came a stranger chapter — and one that surprises many visitors. After her working life ended, the Nomadic was taken to Paris. For many years she was moored on the River Seine, not far from the Eiffel Tower, and served as a floating restaurant and venue. Diners ate and drank aboard, and many of them probably had no idea of the history beneath their feet.

By the early 2000s, the ship was in poor condition, and her future looked uncertain. In 2006 she came up for auction. The Northern Ireland government bought her, and she was brought back to Belfast. A long restoration followed. Craftspeople repaired her steel, rebuilt lost fittings, and returned her interiors to the look of the White Star era. She opened to visitors in 2013.

Notice where she rests now. She sits in the Hamilton Dock, a graving dock — that is, a dry dock where ships were held out of the water for repair. From here you can see the shipyard landscape that shaped her: the open slipways where the Olympic and the Titanic once stood, and the huge yellow cranes of Harland and Wolff on the skyline. She is back among the people and places that made her.

When you go aboard, pay attention to how the decks were divided. Class mattered, even on a short trip across a harbour. First class passengers had a more richly decorated saloon, with carved wood, fine tiles and comfortable seating. Second class areas were plainer. Look closely at the details in the panelling and the ironwork. They follow the same design language you would have found on the great liners.

The ship is also a place to touch and explore. Stand on the open deck and imagine the harbour at Cherbourg at night, with the lights of an enormous liner ahead. Visit the lower areas too, where you can see more of the working parts of the ship. These quieter spaces give a sense of the crew who kept her running, far from the comfort of the saloons.

Before you leave, step back onto the dockside and look at her again. A small steamer, built to carry people only a short distance, has outlived almost every ship of her age. Behind you rises the bright, angular shape of Titanic Belfast. In front of you is the real thing — steel, wood and rivets from 1911. As you walk away, listen once more to the water against the dock walls. It is the same water the Nomadic first floated on, more than a century ago.
