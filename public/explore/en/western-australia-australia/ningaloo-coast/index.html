The guide

The story

Start with the sand under your feet. It is pale, almost white, and it squeaks a little when you walk on it. Behind you, the land is red, dry and quiet. In front of you, the water turns from clear to pale green to a deep, sudden blue. That blue line is closer than you expect. It is the edge of a reef, and you could swim to it.

This is the Ningaloo Coast, on the far western edge of Australia. Here the desert runs straight into the Indian Ocean. There is no rainforest buffer and no muddy river delta. Just limestone, spinifex grass, sand dunes, and then coral. Few places on Earth put an arid landscape and a living reef this close together. That contrast is the first thing to notice, and it shapes everything else here.

Ningaloo Reef is a fringing reef. That means it grows along the shoreline itself, rather than far out to sea. The Great Barrier Reef, on the other side of the country, often lies many kilometres offshore and needs a boat to reach. Here, in many places, you can wade in from the beach and be among coral within minutes. The reef stretches for roughly two hundred and sixty kilometres along this coast. Between the reef and the beach lies a calm, shallow lagoon.

In 2011, the Ningaloo Coast was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. The listing covers more than the reef. It also includes the land behind you — the Cape Range peninsula, with its limestone ridges, gorges and caves. UNESCO recognised the way sea and land work together here as one system. The protection is a reminder that this coast is valued as a whole, not as a single snorkelling spot.

Now, a word about the people who knew this coast first. The Ningaloo region is the Country of Aboriginal peoples, including the Baiyungu, Thalanyji and Yinigudura peoples. Their connection to this land and sea goes back many thousands of years. Archaeologists have found evidence in the caves of Cape Range that people lived here and ate from the sea in the deep past. Today, traditional owners take part in the joint management of the protected areas along this coast.

Here is the first surprise. Every year, usually from around March into the middle of the year, the world's largest fish arrives off this reef. The whale shark can grow longer than a bus. Yet it eats some of the smallest food in the ocean — plankton, tiny shrimp and fish eggs. It swims slowly, with its wide mouth open, filtering the water. It is a shark, but it has no interest in people, and swimmers share the water with it on licensed tours.

Why do the whale sharks come here? Part of the answer is coral spawning. In autumn, a few nights after certain full moons, many corals on the reef release their eggs and sperm at almost the same time. The water fills with tiny pink and white bundles drifting toward the surface. This sudden burst of food feeds a whole chain of life, from small fish upward. The whale sharks follow the feast. It is one of the most remarkable timetables in nature.

The whale sharks are not alone. In the cooler months, humpback whales travel along this coast on their long migration between Antarctic waters and warmer breeding grounds. You may see a spout, a dark tail lifting, or even a full breach from the shore. Manta rays glide through the lagoon near Coral Bay for much of the year. Dugongs, dolphins and reef sharks also live here. On a calm day, simply watching the surface can reward your patience.

The second surprise comes at night, in the warmer months. Sea turtles crawl up these beaches to lay their eggs. Green turtles and loggerhead turtles both nest along the Ningaloo Coast. The female digs a pit in the sand with her flippers, lays her eggs, covers them, and slowly returns to the water. Weeks later, tiny hatchlings dig their way out and run for the sea. Bright lights confuse them, which is why visitors are asked to keep torches low and red.

Turn around and look inland for a moment. That low range of hills is Cape Range. It is made of limestone that formed under an ancient sea. Rain has carved it into gorges, such as Yardie Creek, where steep orange walls drop to still water. Black-footed rock wallabies live on these cliffs. And beneath the range, in water-filled caves, scientists have found small, blind creatures that live only underground. Even the dry land here holds hidden life.

If you are near Exmouth, at the northern end of the coast, you are close to Turquoise Bay. It is famous for drift snorkelling. You enter the water at one end of the beach, and a gentle current carries you along over the coral. You watch parrotfish, clownfish and giant clams pass beneath you. Then you swim back to shore before the gap in the reef. Further south, the small town of Coral Bay offers a quieter lagoon, easy to reach straight from the sand.

The water here is clear, and that clarity changes how you see colour. Under the surface, coral shows soft purples, browns, yellows and greens. Fish flash electric blue in the sunlight. Above the surface, the light is hard and bright for most of the day. In the early morning and late afternoon, it softens. The red cliffs glow, the sand turns gold, and the lagoon becomes almost glassy. These are the best hours to simply sit and look.

It is also worth noticing what is missing. There are no high-rise hotels along the beach. There is very little noise beyond wind, waves and birds. The coast feels remote because it is remote. Towns are small, distances are long, and the landscape is allowed to dominate. Many visitors say that this emptiness is part of the experience. It gives you space to pay attention, to the reef and to yourself.

So, before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Listen to the small waves breaking on the reef edge, that soft and constant sound. Feel the warm wind coming off the desert behind you. Look again at that line where pale water meets deep blue. Out there, a turtle may be surfacing for air, or a ray may be passing under the swell. The coast keeps going about its life, long after you walk back up the sand.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book whale shark swims only with licensed operators.
Check current conditions before drift snorkelling at Turquoise Bay.
Use red, low torchlight when watching nesting turtles.
Wear reef-safe sunscreen and a rash vest for snorkelling.
Carry plenty of water; towns and shops are far apart.
