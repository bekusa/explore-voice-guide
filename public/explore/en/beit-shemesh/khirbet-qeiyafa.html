The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves across this hilltop and through the dry grass, and somewhere below you a tractor works a field. The ground under your feet is pale, stony and uneven. Ahead of you rises a wall of huge, rough-cut blocks, grey and weathered, fitted together without mortar. Some of them are larger than a person. Lay your hand on one. It holds the warmth of the sun, and it has stood here for roughly three thousand years.

You are standing at Khirbet Qeiyafa, a small fortified town on a ridge above the Elah Valley, a short drive from Beit Shemesh. The name is Arabic. Khirbet simply means ruin, and for generations this was just that... a scatter of old stones on a hill that farmers and shepherds passed by. Few people imagined that this quiet ridge would become one of the most argued-about archaeological sites in the country.

Turn toward the valley below. This is the Valley of Elah, a broad, fertile corridor that runs from the coastal plain up toward the Judean hills. In the Hebrew Bible, this valley is the setting for the story of David and Goliath. The young shepherd faces the Philistine giant, and the armies watch from the slopes on either side. Whether that story is history, legend or something in between, this landscape is real. You are looking at the ground the storytellers had in mind.

Now look at the valley as an ancient general would. In the Iron Age, the Philistine cities lay to the west, on the coastal plain. The hill country lay to the east. This valley was one of the natural roads between them. A fortress on this ridge could watch every movement through the pass. That simple fact explains a great deal about why someone invested so much labour in these walls.

Serious excavation here began in 2007, led by the archaeologist Yosef Garfinkel of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and Saar Ganor of the Israel Antiquities Authority. The work continued for several seasons. Teams of students and volunteers from many countries came to dig under the summer sun. What they found surprised them. The main town was built, lived in and abandoned within a short span of time, in what most specialists place around the late eleventh or early tenth century before the common era.

That short life is the first surprise. Most ancient towns in this region grew layer upon layer over centuries, like a cake. Here, the main Iron Age town sits in essentially one layer. For archaeologists, this is a gift. It gives them a single, sharp snapshot of one moment, instead of a blurred mix of many periods. Radiocarbon dating of burnt olive pits helped fix that moment in time, and those dates sit at the centre of the debate.

Look along the line of the fortification. This is a casemate wall. That means two parallel walls with a row of rooms built between them. The rooms could hold stores, or serve as homes, and in a crisis they could be filled with rubble to make one solid barrier. Walk along it and you can still see the divisions between those rooms. Houses were built against the inner face, so daily life pressed right up against the defences.

Now for the second surprise. This small town had not one gate, but two. One faces west, toward the coast. The other faces south. For a town of this modest size, two gates is very unusual. The excavators linked this to a biblical place called Shaaraim, a name that means two gates in Hebrew, which appears in the story of David and Goliath. It is a tempting connection. But it remains an identification, not a proven fact, and other scholars disagree.

Then there is the inscription. In 2008, the team found a broken piece of pottery with five lines of writing in ink. Archaeologists call this kind of object an ostracon, a potsherd used as a writing surface. The letters are in an early alphabet, older than the classic Hebrew script. Scholars still argue about what language it records and what exactly it says. Some read words linked to justice and to caring for the poor. Others are more cautious. It ranks among the oldest alphabetic texts found in this region.

The digging also turned up small objects that tell you about belief. The teams found rooms that seem to have had a religious use, and small clay and stone models of shrines, carefully shaped with doorways and decoration. They also noted that pig bones were almost absent from the animal remains. Some researchers see this as a clue to the identity of the people who lived here. Others say diet alone cannot settle such questions.

And that brings us to the heart of the matter. Who built Khirbet Qeiyafa? One camp sees an early Judahite town, possibly connected to a kingdom ruled from Jerusalem in the age the Bible links to David. Another camp sees a local Canaanite community, or a town tied to a different power entirely. The debate touches a much larger question... how early did a real kingdom take shape in these hills? This ridge became a central piece of evidence, argued over at conferences and in journals.

The story does not end in the Iron Age. Centuries later, in the Hellenistic period, the era after Alexander the Great, people returned and built a fortress over parts of the old ruins. Later still, in the Byzantine era, there was activity here too. As you walk, notice how some walls look different in style and size. Not every stone you see belongs to the same century. The hill was reused whenever its view became useful again.

Before you leave, stand quietly by the western gate. Look out across the green and gold squares of the valley floor, toward the low hills on the far side. Feel the wind again, and hear how little sound there is up here. The arguments about kings and borders will continue in universities far away. But the stones under your hand are simply here. As you walk back down the slope, watch the valley open below you, the same road travellers have followed for thousands of years.
