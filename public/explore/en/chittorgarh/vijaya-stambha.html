The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the stone. It is warm, slightly gritty, and carved so densely that your fingers find a face, a hand, or a coil of drapery wherever they rest. Above you, a tower of pale red sandstone rises in stacked tiers against the sky. Pigeons shift on its ledges. The wind moves across the plateau of Chittorgarh Fort, and somewhere below, the plains of Rajasthan stretch out in a haze of dust and green.

This is the Vijaya Stambha, which means the Tower of Victory. It stands on one of the largest hill forts in India, a long rocky plateau that rises sharply above the surrounding countryside. For centuries, this fort was the seat of the kingdom of Mewar, a Rajput state in what is now southern Rajasthan. The Rajputs were warrior clans who ruled much of this region, and their idea of honour shaped nearly everything you see here.

The tower was built in the fifteenth century by Rana Kumbha, one of the most celebrated rulers of Mewar. He raised it to mark a military victory over Mahmud Khalji, the Sultan of Malwa, a neighbouring kingdom to the south-east. That battle took place in the 1440s. Construction followed over the next years, and the tower was finished around the middle of the century. So this is a monument to a single triumph... but it became much more than that.

Count the levels as your eyes climb. There are nine storeys, and the tower reaches roughly thirty-seven metres in height. Each storey has small balconies and window openings, so the outline steps in and out instead of rising as a smooth column. Near the top, the stone turns lighter, and a domed pavilion crowns the structure. From a distance, it looks almost delicate. Up close, it is solid, heavy, and covered in sculpture from base to crown.

Here is the first surprise. This is not only a war memorial. It is also a religious monument, dedicated to Vishnu, one of the principal gods of Hinduism, the preserver of the universe. The carved figures on the walls show Hindu deities, their attendants, and scenes drawn from sacred stories. Scholars have described the tower as a kind of illustrated encyclopaedia of Hindu iconography. In other words, the walls work like a picture book of gods, carved in stone for anyone to read.

Take a slow walk around the base before you look anywhere else. Notice how the figures change from panel to panel. Some hold weapons, some hold flowers or musical instruments. Some ride animals, which in Hindu art often identify which god is which. You do not need to name every figure to enjoy them. Look instead at the rhythm of the carving: the repeated arches, the bands of ornament, the way light falls into the deep cuts and leaves the raised surfaces glowing.

The tower also carries the names of the people who built it. Inscriptions record a master builder named Jaita, working with his sons. This matters, because the names of craftsmen from this period were often lost. Here, at least some of the hands behind the stone are remembered. Rana Kumbha was a great builder in general. He is also linked to the vast fortress of Kumbhalgarh, further west in the Aravalli hills, and to many temples across Mewar.

Now the second surprise, one that many visitors walk past. Look carefully at the upper levels, and visitors often point out Arabic lettering carved among the Hindu imagery. It is commonly read as the word Allah, repeated in the stone. Historians still discuss why it is there. Some see it as a gesture of tolerance, others as a statement linked to the defeated Sultan. The honest answer is that its meaning is not settled. What is certain is that it sits on a Hindu victory tower, in plain view.

Inside, a narrow staircase winds up through the storeys. The passages are tight and dim, and the stone steps are worn smooth by many feet. Access to the interior has changed over the years for safety and conservation reasons, so it may or may not be open when you visit. If it is, climb slowly. The small windows give framed views across the fort, and the interior walls also carry carving, so look closely as you pause to catch your breath.

Chittorgarh is also a place of hard memory. The fort was besieged several times, and its defenders became known for their refusal to surrender. Local tradition tells of jauhar, the self-sacrifice of women of the fort when defeat seemed certain. The most famous story involves Queen Padmini and the Sultan Alauddin Khalji in the early fourteenth century. Historians treat much of the Padmini tale as legend, shaped by later poetry. Still, it remains central to how people here remember the fort.

When you step back from the tower, look across the fort for its older companion, the Kirti Stambha, or Tower of Fame. It is smaller, slimmer, and dedicated to Adinatha, the first Jain spiritual teacher. Jainism is an ancient Indian religion that stresses non-violence. The Kirti Stambha is older than the Victory Tower, and seeing both reminds you that this plateau was shaped by more than one faith and more than one century.

In 2013, Chittorgarh Fort became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing called the Hill Forts of Rajasthan, alongside other great Rajput strongholds. The Victory Tower is the image many people carry home from it. Around you, notice the everyday life of the site: families taking photographs, school groups sitting on the steps, monkeys watching the snack bags. The tower is a sacred and historic object, but it is also simply a place where people gather.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look up once more. The sun moves across the carved faces, and the shadows shift with every passing hour. The stone that felt warm under your hand has stood through sieges, pilgrimages, and many monsoons. As you walk on across the plateau, glance back now and then. The tower keeps its place above the fort walls, and it stays in sight for a long way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full base before climbing to read the carvings.
Visit early morning or late afternoon for softer carving light.
Wear grippy shoes for the narrow, worn interior stairs.
Combine your visit with the nearby Jain Kirti Stambha.
Bring water; the open plateau offers little shade.
