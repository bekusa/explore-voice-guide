The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Below you, the River Cam moves slowly and quietly. Somewhere a punt pole knocks against wood, and a guide's voice drifts up from the water. Above that sound, a pale grey bridge spans the river in a gentle curve. Look at it closely and something feels strange. The curve seems smooth, like an arch. Yet every timber in it is perfectly straight. That small contradiction is the whole story of this bridge.

This is the Mathematical Bridge, and it belongs to Queens' College, one of the older colleges of Cambridge University. The college officially calls it simply the Wooden Bridge. It links the old college buildings on one bank with the newer buildings on the far side of the river. Students, fellows and porters cross it every day. For them it is a footpath. For everyone else on the riverbank, it is a puzzle made of oak and geometry.

First, a word about the college. Notice the apostrophe in Queens'. It comes after the letter s, not before it. That is deliberate. The college was founded in the fifteenth century by one queen, Margaret of Anjou, the wife of King Henry the Sixth. It was then refounded by a second queen, Elizabeth Woodville, wife of the rival king Edward the Fourth. Two queens, on opposite sides of the Wars of the Roses, so the college honours them both.

The bridge came much later, in the eighteenth century. The first version was built in 1749. It was designed by William Etheridge, an engineer and carpenter. A Cambridge builder named James Essex carried out the work. Etheridge was interested in a particular way of arranging timber, and he used similar ideas on a bridge across the Thames, at Walton. The Italian painter Canaletto painted that Thames bridge. So this quiet Cambridge footbridge has a cousin in eighteenth-century art.

Now look again at the structure itself, because this is where the name comes from. Each straight timber is set as a tangent to the curve. A tangent is a straight line that touches a circle at one single point. Other timbers run inward, like the spokes of a wheel. Together, these straight pieces trace an arc that your eye reads as a smooth curve. Engineers call this tangent and radial trussing. The geometry spreads the load through the frame, so the bridge carries people without a heavy stone arch.

Here is the part that surprises many visitors. Nothing on this bridge is truly bent. There is no steamed or curved timber holding up the walkway. The arch is an effect, built entirely from straight lines. Stand back and the shape looks soft and organic. Walk closer and it becomes a careful diagram, almost like a drawing in a mathematics textbook. That shift, from curve to straight lines, is why people have argued about this bridge for generations.

And people do love to tell stories about it. The most popular story says that Sir Isaac Newton built the bridge without a single nut or bolt. According to the tale, curious students later took it apart to see how it worked. Then they could not put it back together, and had to use bolts to fix it. Guides on the punts still tell this story with great enjoyment. It is a good story. It is also not true.

The dates make the problem clear. Isaac Newton died in 1727. The first bridge was built more than twenty years after his death. Newton was also a fellow of Trinity College, not Queens'. So the legend falls apart on simple arithmetic. There is a small grain of truth hidden inside it, though. The original bridge was held together with iron pins driven into the joints, rather than with visible nuts and bolts. Later rebuilding made the fixings much easier to see.

The bridge in front of you is not the one from 1749. Timber over a river does not last forever. Damp, frost and heavy use slowly wear it down. The bridge was rebuilt in the 1860s, and then again in the early twentieth century. Each time, the builders kept to the same essential design. So the idea is from the eighteenth century, even though the wood is younger. In a sense, the geometry is the real monument here, and the timber is its current copy.

Take a moment to notice the setting. On one side, the older college buildings rise in warm brick and stone, close to the water's edge. On the other, the newer buildings show a more modern style. The bridge sits between these two periods like a hinge. Below, the river is narrow, and the punts must slow down to pass underneath. Willows and lawns soften the banks. The whole scene is small and close, which makes the bridge feel more personal than grand.

If you want the classic view, walk to the road bridge on Silver Street, just downstream. From there you can see the whole span side on, with its full curve and lattice of timbers. It is the angle that appears on most postcards. Morning light tends to be softer on the wood. On busy afternoons the river below fills with punts, and you can watch them queue to slip beneath the arch. Another good view comes from a punt itself, looking up through the timbers.

A practical note. The bridge is part of a working college, so access to the grounds is controlled. At certain times visitors can enter with a ticket. At other times, especially during exams, the college may close to the public. The bridge is a route for people who live and study here, so walking across is not always possible. Even so, the best way to understand it is often from outside, where you can see the full shape of the design.

Before you move on, look once more at the bridge. Follow one straight timber with your eyes, from where it meets the bank to where it crosses its neighbour. Then let your focus soften, and watch the curve appear again. Listen to the water underneath and the knock of the punt poles. The legend may be wrong, but the puzzle is real. Straight lines, carefully placed, making a curve that has crossed this river for more than two and a half centuries.
