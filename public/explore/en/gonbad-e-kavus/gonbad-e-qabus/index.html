The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the mound and tilt your head back. The tower rises straight into the sky, one unbroken column of brick the colour of dry earth. There is no tile, no paint, no gold. In the wind of the Gorgan plain it feels almost silent. Then a bird passes the top, and you understand how far away the top really is.

This is Gonbad-e Qabus, the Tower of Qabus. It gave its name to the whole city around you, Gonbad-e Kavus, in Golestan Province in northeastern Iran. It was built just over a thousand years ago, and it is still the tallest thing for miles. Together with its mound, it stands roughly seventy metres high. Many scholars call it the tallest tower ever built entirely of fired brick.

The man behind it was Qabus ibn Wushmgir, a ruler of the Ziyarid dynasty. The Ziyarids were a local Iranian dynasty who held the lands south of the Caspian Sea in the tenth and eleventh centuries. Qabus carried the title Shams al-Ma'ali, which means roughly "Sun of the Heights". He was not only a prince. He wrote poetry and elegant letters in Arabic, and his court welcomed learned men.

Here is the first surprise. Qabus built this tower while he was still alive. It is his own tomb, planned and finished in his lifetime. The inscription on the walls dates the work to around the year 1006 or 1007, using two calendars side by side — the Islamic lunar year and the Iranian solar year. That double date is a small sign of a court that lived between Arab and Persian traditions.

Now look closely at the shape. From a distance the tower seems round. It is not. Ten sharp triangular ribs run from the base to the roof, like the points of a star. Walk slowly around the base and count them. Those ribs catch the light differently through the day. In the morning one side glows, while the next side falls into deep shadow.

The ribs are not only decoration. They stiffen the walls, a little like the folds in a sheet of paper. That stiffness helps explain how a slender brick tower has survived a millennium of earthquakes and storms on this open plain. The builders had no steel and no concrete. They had clay, fire, mortar, and very careful geometry.

At the very top sits a pointed conical roof, also made of brick. From below it looks like a sharp cap. Just under that roof, on the eastern side, there is a small opening. It is easy to miss, so look for it. Its position facing the rising sun has fed one of the tower's best-known stories.

Legend has it that Qabus was never buried in the ground. According to old accounts, his body was placed in a glass coffin and hung by chains from the roof, high inside the tower. Locals say the first light of the sun passed through that eastern opening and fell on the coffin each morning. No coffin has ever been found, so treat this as a story, not established fact. But it is a story the tower seems built to hold.

Now find the two bands of writing. One circles the tower near the base, the other near the top, just below the roof. They are carved in Kufic, an early angular style of Arabic script. Each band is split into ten panels, one between each pair of ribs. The words name Qabus, give his titles, and record when the tower was raised. The letters are made of brick, set into the wall itself.

The life of Qabus did not end peacefully. His rule was harsh, and his own army turned against him. He was deposed and died around 1012, only a few years after this tower was finished. So the monument he built for his glory became the place that held the memory of his fall. The tower outlasted the dynasty, the court, and the old city of Jorjan that once spread across this plain.

That city was once an important centre of learning. One scholar who passed through this region was al-Biruni, among the greatest scientific minds of the medieval Islamic world. He dedicated a major work on calendars and the ways different peoples measure time to Qabus. When you read the double date on the tower, you are looking at the same curiosity about time, written in brick.

Here is the second surprise, and you can test it yourself. The tower is famous for its acoustics. Many visitors stand on the platform near the entrance and clap their hands once, or speak a single word. A sharp echo comes back from the curved brick. Try it quietly, and listen for how the sound returns. In 2012 the tower was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, partly for this kind of bold engineering.

The influence of this design travelled far. Across Iran, Anatolia, and Central Asia, later rulers built tomb towers with pointed roofs and ribbed or many-sided walls. Few came close to this height, and none have its stark plainness. Here there is no glazed tile to distract you. The entire effect comes from proportion, shadow, and the rhythm of those ten ribs.

Before you leave, walk back down the mound and turn around once more. Notice the park, the families on the grass, the ordinary streets of the city pressing close. Life here has grown up around this tower for a thousand years. As you walk away, keep glancing back. The tower stays visible for a long time — still pointing at the sky, exactly as Qabus planned.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to see light rake across the ribs.
Walk a full circle around the base before climbing.
Clap once on the platform to hear the echo.
Step back into the park for a full-height photo.
Bring sun protection; the mound offers little shade.
