The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the loudest sound is often the water itself. Rain ticks on the deck, a thin wind comes off the ice, and somewhere far ahead a glacier cracks with a deep boom, like distant thunder rolling through a valley. The air is cold and clean. It smells of wet stone, salt, and moss. Before any history or any name, this is what Bernardo O'Higgins National Park is: water, ice, and rock, meeting at the far southern edge of the Americas.

You are inside the largest national park in Chile. It covers around 35,000 square kilometres, and it stretches across two regions, Aysén in the north and Magallanes in the south. Here in Capitán Prat Province, the park forms the wild western and southern edge of the map. Almost none of it can be reached by road. Most visitors arrive by boat, by small plane, or on foot from a remote trailhead. That isolation is the first thing to understand about this place. It is not a park you drive through. You come to it slowly, on its own terms.

The park takes its name from Bernardo O'Higgins, one of the leaders of Chile's struggle for independence from Spain in the early nineteenth century. Today Chileans remember him as a founding father of the nation. It is fitting that his name sits on this landscape. For a long time, these southern channels and ice fields marked the edge of what the young country could claim and explore. The park itself was created in 1969, when the Chilean state set aside this huge territory for protection.

At the centre of the park lies something few travellers ever see in person: the Southern Patagonian Ice Field. It is one of the largest bodies of ice on Earth outside Antarctica and Greenland. Think of it as a frozen plateau high in the Andes, fed by heavy snow blown in from the Pacific. From this plateau, dozens of glaciers flow outward. Some move east toward the lakes of Argentina. Many move west, down through the mountains, until they reach the sea in long fjords.

Those fjords give the park its shape. A fjord is a deep, narrow arm of the sea, carved by glaciers during past ice ages and then flooded when the ice pulled back. Here they run between steep walls of dark rock, often streaked with waterfalls after rain. The water can turn many colours. Near the glaciers it is often milky turquoise or pale grey. That colour comes from very fine rock dust, ground down by the moving ice and carried out in meltwater.

Here is the first surprise. In a world where most glaciers are shrinking, this park holds one that has been doing the opposite. The Pío Eleven glacier, also called the Brüggen glacier, is one of the largest in South America. For much of the twentieth century, scientists recorded it advancing rather than retreating. It is a rare exception, and researchers still study why it behaves so differently from its neighbours.

The contrast is close by. Not far from the village of Caleta Tortel, boats travel toward the Jorge Montt glacier, which has retreated dramatically in recent decades. Where ice once filled the fjord, there is now open water. When you see a glacier face like this, look at the bare rock on the valley walls. The paler, scraped stone often shows where the ice stood not long ago. Within one park, you can see both stories of ice side by side.

If you came through Caleta Tortel, you have already met one of the gateways to the park. The village has no ordinary streets. Instead, houses connect by wooden walkways and stairs, built from the local cypress wood, rising up the hillside above the water. The boards are often wet and dark with rain. People move goods on foot and by boat. It is a good reminder that, in this part of Patagonia, the sea and the channels have always been the real roads.

Long before the park existed, people lived in these channels. The Kawésqar, a seafaring people of the western Patagonian archipelago, travelled the cold waters by canoe for many centuries. They fished, gathered shellfish, and hunted sea mammals in a climate that most outsiders found impossible. Their language and memory survive today, though their communities are small. When you look out at the maze of islands, try to picture a canoe with a small fire kept burning inside it. Accounts of the Kawésqar describe exactly that.

Now the second surprise. This landscape, so full of ice, is also full of green. On the lower slopes, wherever there is shelter, dense temperate rainforest grows. Southern beech trees, ferns, mosses, and lichens cover every surface. The rainfall here is very high, so the forest stays damp almost all year. Look closely at a tree trunk and you may find it wrapped in so much moss that the bark disappears. Ice above, rainforest below... and the sea at the bottom of it all.

The park also protects animals that are hard to find anywhere else. The most important is the huemul, a stocky South Andean deer that appears on Chile's national coat of arms. It is endangered, shy, and rarely seen. Along the coasts, watch for sea lions on the rocks, cormorants flying low over the water, and the slow circling of condors above the ridges. In the channels, dolphins sometimes appear near boats. None of this is guaranteed. That uncertainty is part of the experience.

The weather will shape your day more than any timetable. Conditions can change within minutes. Bright sun on blue ice can turn to sideways rain and low cloud. Visibility can drop until the mountains vanish completely, and then open again just as fast. Locals here plan loosely and wait patiently. If your view is hidden, give it time. Many visitors remember the moment the cloud lifted far more than the hours of clear sky.

So take a moment where you are. Notice the colour of the water near your feet, or beneath the hull. Notice the cold on your face, and how quiet it is between the gusts of wind. Somewhere up on the ice field, snow that fell many years ago is still slowly moving toward the sea. As you leave, listen once more for that deep crack of ice. It is the sound of this park, still at work.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan access by boat or plane; roads do not enter the park.
Pack waterproof layers, even when the morning looks clear.
Build spare days into your plans for weather delays.
Book glacier boat trips ahead through operators in Caleta Tortel.
Bring binoculars for spotting condors and sea lions.
