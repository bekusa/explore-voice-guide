The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against a long black hull, and halyards tap against the masts in the breeze. Gulls call over the harbour. Then your eye climbs the side of the ship: dark iron, a white band of gunports, and three tall masts dressed in rigging. HMS Warrior looks like two ages at once. She has the masts and spars of a sailing ship and the hull of a machine. That double nature is the whole story.

First, a note on where you are. Warrior is moored at Portsmouth Historic Dockyard, on the eastern side of the harbour. From the Gosport waterfront you can see her across the water, and the harbour ferry links the two towns in a few minutes. Many visitors meet her first from the Gosport side. From there her black hull sits low against the dockyard buildings, with the masts rising above the skyline. It is one of the best views of her you will get.

To understand why she was built, picture the late 1850s. France had just launched Gloire, a wooden warship with iron armour bolted to her sides. For the British Admiralty, this was alarming. For centuries Britain had trusted wooden ships of the line, the great floating gun platforms of the age of sail. Overnight, those ships looked vulnerable. Britain needed an answer, and it needed something bolder than a copy of the French design.

The answer was Warrior. She was launched in 1860 at the Thames Ironworks on the River Thames in London, and she entered service in 1861. Unlike Gloire, her hull was built of iron, not wood. A belt of iron armour, backed by thick teak, protected her central section where the guns and engines sat. She was larger, faster and better protected than any warship then afloat. At launch, she was the most powerful warship in the world.

Here is the first surprise. Despite all that power, Warrior never fired a shot in battle. Her very existence was the point. A ship like this changed the calculations of rival navies simply by being afloat. Historians often describe her as a deterrent, a statement of industrial strength. She patrolled, she trained crews, she showed the flag. And she kept the peace, in part, by making war against Britain look like a poor idea.

Now look at the rigging. Why would a steam warship carry a full set of sails? In the 1860s, steam engines burned coal at an enormous rate, and coaling stations were far apart. Sails gave the ship range. Under sail she could cross long distances without burning fuel. Under steam she could move at speed in a battle, whatever the wind. Engineers of the time saw no contradiction. They wanted both.

That led to a clever detail most visitors miss. Warrior's propeller could be lifted out of the water. When the ship ran under sail alone, a fixed propeller would drag through the sea and slow her down. So the crew could raise the whole screw into a well inside the stern. It took many men hauling on tackle to do it. It is a small piece of engineering, but it shows how carefully the designers balanced old and new.

Step on board, and the scale of the gun deck opens around you. Long rows of cannon line both sides, their muzzles pointed at the gunports. Between them hang the mess tables, where sailors ate their meals in small groups. At night, hammocks were slung from the beams above. The men lived, ate and slept beside the guns they served. The crew numbered roughly seven hundred, so space was always tight.

Below, the ship becomes a factory. Deep in the hull sit the boilers and the great steam engine that drove the propeller shaft. Stokers shovelled coal in heat and noise that is hard to imagine on a quiet visit today. Notice the polished brass, the iron ladders and the gleam of machinery. Victorian engineering was proud of itself, and the engine spaces were kept clean and bright as a matter of discipline.

The second surprise is how quickly she grew old. Naval technology moved fast in those decades. Within about ten years, newer ships carried heavier guns in turrets and thicker armour. Warrior soon slipped from the front line. She became a reserve and training vessel, and later served as a depot ship. Her name was even taken away for a time. The ship that had frightened navies became a workhorse.

For about half a century she served as a floating oil jetty at Pembroke Dock, in south-west Wales. Her decks were covered in concrete, and ships tied up alongside her to take on fuel. It was a humble job. Yet it saved her. Because she stayed afloat and useful, she escaped the scrapyard that claimed almost every ship of her generation. Her iron hull simply waited.

In 1979 a long restoration began in Hartlepool, in the north-east of England. Craftspeople rebuilt the masts, the decks, the fittings and the figurehead, often working from old drawings and photographs. In 1987 she was towed into Portsmouth to take her place in the dockyard. Today she is the only survivor of the forty-five iron hulls built for the Royal Navy's first armoured fleet, so far as the record shows... a rare witness from the start of the modern navy.

Before you leave, walk to the bow. The figurehead is a carved warrior, painted and armed, leaning forward over the water. Then turn and follow the line of the hull back towards the stern. Notice how the iron plates meet, and how the white gunport band runs straight along her side. As the ferry crosses and the gulls circle, take a last look at those masts above the black iron. Two eras of the sea, held in one ship.
