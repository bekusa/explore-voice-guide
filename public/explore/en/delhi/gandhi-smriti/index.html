The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of central Delhi fades behind the gate, and in its place comes birdsong, the rustle of leaves, and the soft scrape of sandals on a garden path. The house ahead is white and wide, with tall windows and deep verandas. It looks like a calm family home on a quiet afternoon. And for a few months, it was exactly that... until one January evening changed the story of a nation.

You are standing at Gandhi Smriti, which means "Remembrance of Gandhi." For many years, this building had another name — Birla House. It belonged to the Birla family, one of India's great industrial dynasties. They were close supporters of Mahatma Gandhi, and they often offered him a place to stay in Delhi. Gandhi was the leader of India's long, non-violent struggle for independence from British rule. Here, in this house, he spent the final months of his life.

Gandhi arrived in September 1947, only weeks after India became independent. It should have been a time of joy. Instead, the country was in pain. The partition of British India into India and Pakistan had set off terrible violence between Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs. Millions of people were forced from their homes. Delhi itself was full of refugees and fear. Gandhi came to the capital to try to calm the city.

So picture this house in those months. It was not a quiet retreat. Visitors came and went all day — politicians, journalists, grieving families, and ordinary people seeking a word of comfort. Gandhi kept a simple routine. He rose early, he worked, he spun cotton thread, and he walked. Every evening, he held a public prayer meeting on the lawn. Anyone could attend. People from every faith were welcome, and prayers from different traditions were read aloud.

Here is something many visitors do not know. In January 1948, Gandhi began a fast in this house. He refused food to press for peace between communities in Delhi, and for fair treatment of Muslims who had stayed in India. He was in his late seventies, and the fast weakened him badly. After several days, community leaders gave him written promises of peace. Only then did he break his fast. It was his last.

Not everyone wanted that peace. Some extremists saw Gandhi's message of tolerance as betrayal. A few days after the fast ended, a bomb went off near the prayer ground during an evening meeting. No one was killed, and the prayers continued. Gandhi refused to stop holding his meetings in the open. He also refused heavy security around himself. He believed a man of peace should not hide behind guards.

Then came the evening of 30 January 1948. Gandhi left his room and walked toward the prayer ground, leaning on two young women of his household. He was running a little late. As he reached the steps near the lawn, a man named Nathuram Godse stepped forward from the crowd. He bowed, and then he fired a pistol at close range. Gandhi fell. Within minutes, he was dead.

Now look down at the path in front of you. You will see a line of footprints set into the ground. They trace the route Gandhi took on that final walk, from the house to the place where he fell. They are small and evenly spaced, the steps of an old man in no hurry. Following them is quiet and strangely personal. Many visitors walk beside the footprints in silence, rather than on them.

The footprints end at the Martyr's Column, a simple memorial that marks the spot where Gandhi was shot. There is no grand statue here, no towering monument. The modesty feels right for a man who owned so little. Many people say that Gandhi's last words were "Hey Ram," a call to God — though accounts of that moment differ. Whatever was said, the silence around this small column today is very deep.

Now return to the house and find Gandhi's room. It has been kept almost as he left it. The furnishings are plain — a low mattress on the floor, a small writing desk, a few simple objects. His personal belongings were few: round spectacles, a walking stick, sandals, a pocket watch, simple eating utensils. Seeing them up close is a small surprise. The man who moved an empire lived with almost nothing.

After Gandhi's death, the house stayed in private hands for many years. The Indian government later acquired the property and turned it into a national memorial. It opened to the public in the 1970s. Today, the rooms hold photographs, paintings, models and written records of Gandhi's life. There is also a modern multimedia exhibition, with interactive displays that tell his story in sound, light and image.

One more detail is easy to miss. The street outside used to have a colonial name. After Gandhi's death, it was renamed Tees January Marg — which simply means "Thirtieth January Road." Every time a Delhi resident writes this address, they mark the date he died. And every year on that day, people gather here for prayers, songs and quiet remembrance, much as they once gathered for his evening meetings.

Before you leave, stand for a while on the lawn. Notice the trees, the white walls, the open sky above the prayer ground. This is the same garden where crowds once sat cross-legged to hear an old man speak about patience, courage and respect. As you walk back toward the gate and the noise of the city returns, you may find yourself walking a little slower — just as he did, on that last evening.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the footprint path from house to memorial column.
Visit Gandhi's preserved room before the larger exhibition halls.
Allow quiet time on the prayer lawn at the end.
Check signage before photographing inside the exhibition rooms.
Combine with nearby Rajghat, Gandhi's cremation memorial site.
