The guide

The story

A scream rises above the rooftops. Then laughter, quick and bright. Somewhere close, metal wheels clatter on an old track. You cannot see the ride yet, but you can feel it through the walls. Cheerful music drifts over from the same direction. You are near the gate of Hanayashiki, the oldest amusement park in Japan.

The park hides in a tight block of streets behind the temple. It is small. Very small. It is squeezed between buildings on every side, with no room to spare. There are no wide open spaces here. Instead, the rides curl and stack around each other. They climb over walkways and lean close to the fences, all to fit into one crowded corner. Look up, and you may see ride tracks passing right over your head.

Hanayashiki did not begin as an amusement park. It opened in 1853 as a flower garden. The name itself means a place of flowers. People came here to walk among the plants and enjoy the changing seasons. There were no machines and no thrills back then. Just quiet paths, shaded benches, and blooms in every color. In those early days, this was a calm escape from the crowded city around it.

Over the long years, the garden slowly changed. First, animals were added for visitors to see. Then small attractions. Then mechanical rides. Bit by bit, the flower garden turned into a park of fun. The plants gave way to wheels and gears. The quiet gave way to laughter and noise.

The most famous ride here is the Roller Coaster. Its name is simple, and so is the ride itself. But it holds a record worth knowing. It was built in 1953. That makes it the oldest roller coaster in the entire country. It still runs today, carrying riders much as it did when your grandparents were young.

Do not expect a giant modern machine. This coaster is short and low to the ground. It rattles and shakes. It rushes between buildings and seems to almost brush the walls. Part of the thrill is exactly how close everything feels. You are not soaring high above an open park. You are racing through the middle of a packed neighborhood.

The rest of the park has the same old charm. The colors are bright, but worn soft by time. Some signs are hand drawn rather than printed. There is a tall tower ride that lifts you slowly up for a view over the rooftops. There are gentle rides for small children too, painted in soft pinks and blues, turning in slow circles. You can often hear the bell of a game stall and the soft hum of a carousel turning nearby.

Walk through and you can feel the layers of time. Some rides look like they come from many decades ago. The music is cheerful and a little old fashioned. Nothing here tries hard to feel new or modern. The park holds on to its past on purpose. That is the whole point of the place.

Because the park is so small, you can see most of it quickly. That is part of its character, not a fault. It does not tire you out or overwhelm you. It feels like a toy version of a much bigger park. Someone has folded a whole world of fun neatly into one single city block. Families with young children love it for exactly this reason. Nobody leaves here worn out.

Think for a moment about how rare this place is. The city around it has changed almost beyond recognition. Glass towers have risen on every side. This district has burned and been rebuilt more than once. Yet this little park has held its corner since 1853. It watched the whole modern world grow up around it, and stayed.

Stand near the coaster and watch a car go past. The riders lift their arms into the air. They shout and laugh together. The wheels grind on the old steel rails below them. It is the same simple joy that people have come to this spot to find for well over a century. The little ride is older than almost everyone watching it.

It is easy to pass this place by. It is not grand. It is not new. The rides are old and the park is tiny. But there is something gentle in a small park that has simply refused to disappear. It keeps making people laugh, year after year, in the shadow of the great temple next door.
