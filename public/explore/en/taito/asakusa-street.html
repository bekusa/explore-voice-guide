The guide

The story

A great red gate hung with an enormous paper lantern opens onto a long, narrow street lined with little stalls, leading toward the rooftops of an ancient temple. Crowds shuffle between sellers of snacks, fans, and trinkets under the lantern's shadow. This is Asakusa, the old heart of Tokyo, where the city's oldest temple draws a constant stream of visitors.

At the end of the street stands Senso-ji, the oldest temple in Tokyo, founded, by tradition, in the seventh century around a small golden image of a Buddhist goddess of mercy said to have been found in the river by two fishermen. It has been a place of pilgrimage ever since.

The approach begins at a famous outer gate, the Thunder Gate, hung with a massive red paper lantern and guarded by fierce protector statues. It is one of the most photographed spots in the city, and passing beneath the lantern marks the start of the temple route.

Between the gate and the temple runs a long shopping street, lined with small stalls that have sold snacks, souvenirs, and crafts to pilgrims for centuries. It is touristy and crowded, but it keeps an old tradition of trade tied to the temple alive.

The street is a place to eat as you walk, with stalls selling grilled rice crackers, sweet bean cakes, and other traditional snacks. Trying a few as you make your way toward the temple is part of the experience and a tasty introduction to old Tokyo flavours.

Beyond the shopping street lie the main temple hall, a five-storey pagoda, and an inner gate, all rebuilt after wartime destruction but faithful to their old forms. The smell of incense drifts from a great cauldron where visitors waft smoke over themselves for good health.

Visitors draw paper fortunes from numbered drawers, wash at a fountain, and bow before the main hall, and watching or joining these small rituals is part of the visit. The temple is busy but genuinely devotional, not just a backdrop for photographs.

Asakusa around the temple keeps more of an old, low-rise, downtown feel than much of modern Tokyo, with traditional shops, craftspeople, and old-style restaurants. Hand-pulled rickshaws wait near the gate to carry visitors around the lanes.

For a striking contrast, look across the river from near the temple to a tall modern broadcasting tower and bold modern buildings, the new city rising just beyond the old. The two skylines, ancient and modern, sit within one view.

The street and temple are busiest in the middle of the day. Early morning is calmer and cooler, and the temple grounds, lit at night after the stalls close, take on a quieter, more atmospheric mood worth seeing.

A few practical notes. The temple grounds are free and always open; the shopping street keeps daytime hours. Be mindful of others in the crush, follow any signs about eating while walking, and treat the temple as the sacred place it is.

Stand beneath the great lantern, with the stalls ahead and the temple roofs beyond, and remember that pilgrims have walked this same short street toward the same shrine for many centuries. Asakusa is where old Tokyo is easiest to feel. When you step away toward the river, the modern city returns across the water.
