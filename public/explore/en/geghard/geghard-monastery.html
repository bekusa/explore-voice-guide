The guide

The story

Listen first. Inside the rock-cut chambers of Geghard, a single footstep comes back to you a moment later, softer and rounder. The air is cool, even on a hot summer day. It smells of candle wax, damp stone and old smoke. Light falls from one round opening high above, a single pale column that drops onto the floor. Stand still for a breath. You are inside a mountain that people hollowed out by hand.

This is Geghard, a monastery in the Upper Azat Valley of central Armenia. Its full name, Geghardavank, means the Monastery of the Spear. The name comes from a relic. According to tradition, it was the lance that pierced the side of Christ at the crucifixion. Armenian tradition says the Apostle Thaddeus carried it to Armenia. For centuries the spear was kept here. Today it is held in the treasury at Etchmiadzin, the spiritual centre of the Armenian Apostolic Church.

The story of this place begins earlier than its buildings... with water. Tradition holds that in the fourth century, Saint Gregory the Illuminator founded a holy site here, beside a spring that flowed from a cave. Gregory is the figure Armenians credit with converting the kingdom to Christianity. For that reason, the monastery was first known as Ayrivank, the Monastery of the Cave. The spring still runs inside the complex today. You may hear it before you see it.

Look up at the cliffs around you. They close in on three sides, steep and pale brown. They give the monastery a quiet, sheltered feeling. This setting was a choice. Early monks wanted isolation, and the gorge offered it. The rock also offered something else — a building material that never needed to be carried in. Here, the builders did not only stack stone. They removed it.

The main church, called the Katoghike, stands in the open at the centre of the courtyard. An inscription dates it to 1215. It was built during the time of the Zakarid brothers, Zakare and Ivane. These two princes led Armenian and Georgian forces and pushed the Seljuk Turks out of much of the region. Their rule brought a period of new building across northern Armenia. Notice the church's tall drum and cone-shaped dome. This shape is typical of Armenian church design.

Now walk to the west wall of the Katoghike and enter the large hall attached to it. This is a gavit, sometimes called a zhamatun. It is an entrance hall where people gathered, where teaching took place and where important people were buried. From here, doorways lead you straight into the mountain. This is the moment many visitors do not expect. One doorway looks like an ordinary entrance. Behind it, the walls, columns and dome are all carved from a single mass of living rock.

These rock-cut churches came later in the thirteenth century. The monastery passed into the hands of the Proshyan family, a noble house of princes. They commissioned the carved chambers, and some of them used the chambers as family tombs. Think about the method for a moment. A builder who adds stone can fix a mistake. A builder who carves stone cannot. Every arch here was planned before the first strike of the chisel.

In the upper chamber, find the Proshyan family emblem carved high on the wall. It is easy to miss. Two lions face outward, joined by a chain around their necks. Between them, an eagle spreads its wings and grips a lamb in its talons. Scholars still discuss its exact meaning. It is a coat of arms, a statement of power cut into the mountain. The carving is crisp because the cave protected it from wind and rain.

Now try the second surprise. If a choir or a group of singers is here, stay and listen. Armenian sacred music is often performed in these chambers because of their acoustics. The curved rock holds a voice and lets it fade slowly. Even a single hummed note sounds fuller than it should. If the room is quiet, do not test it loudly. This is a working church. People pray here, and services still take place.

As you move through the complex, watch the walls for khachkars. A khachkar is an Armenian cross-stone, a flat stone carved with a cross at its centre. Around the cross, carvers added interlocking patterns, leaves and rosettes. At Geghard, many crosses are cut directly into the cliff face and into the walls of the chambers. Some are large and detailed. Others are small, simple and worn, perhaps left by pilgrims long ago. No two are exactly the same.

Outside again, look at the trees and bushes near the paths. You may see small strips of cloth tied to the branches. Local custom holds that a tied ribbon carries a wish or a prayer. You may also see people tossing small pebbles up toward the cliff. They try to make a stone stay in a crack or a ledge. Locals say that if it stays, the wish will come true. These customs mix faith and folk habit, and people practise them openly.

Near the gate and along the road, local vendors often sell food to visitors. Look for gata, a sweet Armenian bread, sometimes round and decorated on top. You may also find sujukh, strings of walnuts dipped in thickened grape juice and dried. It is chewy and only lightly sweet. Buying a piece is a small way to support the families who live in this valley. Geghard is often visited together with the temple at Garni, a short drive away down the same gorge.

In 2000, UNESCO added the Monastery of Geghard and the Upper Azat Valley to its World Heritage List. The listing covers more than the buildings. It also recognises the valley around them, where cave cells and small chapels are scattered across the slopes. Some are hard to reach. Many have been used by monks for centuries. As you stand in the courtyard, the whole valley feels like part of the monastery.

Before you leave, stand once more in the open courtyard. Look back at the doorways that lead into the rock. From outside, they look small and modest. You now know what is behind them. Listen for the spring, for a bird above the cliffs, for footsteps on the stone. As you walk back toward the gate, glance up at the cliff face one last time. Somewhere on a ledge, a small stone is still resting where someone threw it with a wish.
