The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment beneath the great arch. Listen. Traffic still passes through here. Cars hum over the cobbles, bicycles tick by, and footsteps echo off the pale stone vault above your head. This is not a quiet monument set apart in a park. It is a working gateway into the town of Ypres, and the living move through it every day, surrounded by the names of the dead.

Look up and around you. The walls are covered, from knee height to far above your reach, with carved names. Row after row, panel after panel. These are soldiers of the British Empire and Commonwealth who died in the fighting around Ypres during the First World War, and who have no known grave. More than 54,000 of them are recorded here. Each name is one person whose body was never found, or never identified.

To understand why this gate matters, it helps to picture this spot more than a century ago. During the war there was no grand arch here at all. There was only a gap in the old ramparts, a road leading east out of town toward the front line. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers marched through that gap on their way to the trenches. Many of them never came back along this road.

Ypres, known in Dutch as Ieper, sat inside a bulge in the front line called the Ypres Salient. German forces held higher ground on three sides. For four years the town was shelled until it was almost entirely flattened. The medieval streets, the Cloth Hall, the cathedral — most of it was reduced to broken brick and rubble. Everything you see around you today was rebuilt after the war, often carefully following the old designs.

After the fighting ended, a difficult question arose. How do you remember men who have no grave to visit? The answer was a memorial to the missing. The architect chosen was Sir Reginald Blomfield, and he designed this monument in the spirit of a Roman triumphal arch — a broad, classical gateway with a deep vaulted hall. It was unveiled in 1927, in a ceremony attended by veterans, families, and officials from across the Commonwealth.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Even this enormous structure was not large enough. When the builders counted the missing, they discovered the walls could not hold every name. So a cut-off date was chosen. The men listed here are, broadly, those lost before the middle of August 1917. Tens of thousands more who went missing after that date are commemorated a few kilometres away, on the memorial wall at Tyne Cot Cemetery.

Now look more closely at how the names are arranged. They are grouped by regiment, and then by rank. You will find panels for British regiments, and separate sections for soldiers from Canada, Australia, India, South Africa, and other parts of the old empire. New Zealand's missing are remembered elsewhere in the region. Families often come here with a regiment name written on a slip of paper, searching the walls for one particular line of letters.

Not everyone admired the memorial when it opened. The war poet Siegfried Sassoon, who had served at the front, wrote a bitter poem about the new gate. He felt that grand stone architecture could not honour the suffering of ordinary soldiers. Others found deep comfort here. That tension between public ceremony and private grief is part of the story of this place, and you may feel it yourself as you stand here.

And now the second surprise. Every single evening, traffic through the gate is stopped. Buglers step into the centre of the hall and play the Last Post, the traditional military call that marks the end of the day. This ceremony has been held here since 1928, organised by a local association of Ypres residents. The buglers have traditionally come from the town's volunteer fire brigade.

The ritual has been interrupted only once. During the Second World War, when German forces occupied Ypres, the ceremony could not be held here, and it continued in England instead. When the town was liberated in September 1944, the buglers returned and played under the arch again — on the very same day, while fighting was still going on nearby. Since then, the call has sounded every night without a break.

The ceremony itself is short and simple. A crowd gathers on both sides of the road. Sometimes school groups lay wreaths, sometimes a family places flowers for a great-grandfather whose name is carved somewhere on these walls. Then the bugle notes rise up into the vault, and the hall goes silent. Many visitors say this quiet moment, shared with strangers, is what they remember most from Ypres.

Before you leave, walk up onto the ramparts beside the gate. These earth and brick defences are much older than the war, shaped by military engineers in the seventeenth century. From the top, you can look along the moat and out toward the east, the direction the soldiers marched. The view is calm now — water, trees, rooftops — and it is hard to imagine the mud and noise that once lay beyond.

So take one last look at the names around you. Run your eye along a single panel and read a few of them slowly, one by one. Notice the small poppy wreaths tucked into the ledges, and the paper crosses with handwritten notes. As you walk back into town, listen again to the cars and the footsteps under the arch. The gate is still doing what it was built to do — keeping these names in the middle of everyday life.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive well ahead of the evening Last Post to find space.
Keep silent during the ceremony; applause is generally discouraged.
Bring a regiment name if searching for a relative.
Climb the ramparts beside the gate for views of the moat.
Combine your visit with a trip to Tyne Cot Cemetery.
