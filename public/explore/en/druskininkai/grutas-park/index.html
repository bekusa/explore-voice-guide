The guide

The story

Listen first. Pine needles crunch under your shoes, and somewhere a crow calls over the treetops. The air smells of resin and damp earth. Then, through the trunks, a shape appears. It is a man in a long coat, cast in dark metal, one arm raised toward nothing. He is not alone. All around this quiet forest, the faces of an empire that no longer exists stand among the trees.

You are in Grūtas Park, in southern Lithuania, a short drive from the spa town of Druskininkai. Locals sometimes call it Stalin's World. The name is half a joke and half a warning. This is a park built from the statues of the Soviet era. These statues once stood in town squares, outside factories and in front of government buildings across Lithuania. Here they stand among pines, wetlands and wooden walkways, far from the crowds they were made to impress.

To understand why they are here, it helps to remember what happened around 1990 and 1991. Lithuania declared its independence from the Soviet Union, and the country moved quickly to remove the symbols of Soviet rule. Monuments to Lenin and to local communist leaders came down from their pedestals. Many were simply put into storage. For years, the question was open. Should a free country destroy these figures, hide them, or keep them somewhere people could see them?

The answer came from an unexpected person. Viliumas Malinauskas was a local businessman who had made his fortune in mushrooms and food canning. He proposed to collect the statues and display them in one place. The idea was controversial from the start. Some people said a park like this would honour the occupiers. Others said it would trivialise the suffering of the Soviet years. Malinauskas went ahead, and the park opened to visitors in 2001.

Here is a detail that surprises many visitors. In 2001, Malinauskas received an Ig Nobel Prize, a satirical award given for achievements that first make people laugh and then make them think. His prize was in the category of peace. That combination fits the park well. Some things here look almost comic at first, like a giant bronze head among the ferns. But the longer you stay, the more serious the place becomes.

Walk slowly along the paths and read the faces. You will meet Lenin more than once, in different poses and different sizes. Stalin appears too, stiff and square-shouldered. You will also find Lithuanian communists who were celebrated in Soviet times, such as Vincas Mickevičius-Kapsukas, a revolutionary leader. Next to many figures, small signs explain who the person was and where the statue once stood. Those signs matter. They turn a field of metal into a record of real streets and real towns.

Notice how the sculptors worked. Socialist realism was the official art style of the Soviet Union. It aimed to show leaders and workers as heroic, confident and always moving forward. Look at the chins lifted high, the eyes fixed on a distant future, the clenched fists and the flowing coats. In a busy city square, these poses were meant to dominate the space. Here, among birch trees and moss, the same gestures look strangely lonely.

The setting is not an accident. The park lies in a wooded, marshy landscape, and the paths lead you through it in a slow loop. Some sections recall the shape of a Soviet prison camp. You may see watchtowers, barbed wire fences and a guarded entrance. These elements are a deliberate reminder. Behind the confident statues stood a system that sent many thousands of people to labour camps and into exile.

That reminder becomes sharper near the old railway wagons. Freight cars of this kind are linked in Lithuanian memory to the mass deportations of the 1940s and early 1950s. During those years, Soviet authorities forced large numbers of Lithuanian families onto trains bound for Siberia and other distant regions. Many never came home. Standing beside a wooden wagon in a quiet forest, it is easy to feel the weight of that history without any words at all.

The park also has indoor spaces. Small exhibition buildings display Soviet-era objects: paintings, posters, medals, flags, and the everyday items that filled homes and offices of the time. Look closely at the propaganda posters. Their bright colours and cheerful slogans often sit uneasily beside the objects nearby. There is also a café styled on Soviet canteens. Visitors sometimes find the nostalgic menu amusing, but many older Lithuanians remember those meals as part of ordinary life, not as a theme.

One more surprise: the park has a small zoo and a children's play area. Families come here on weekends, and you may hear children laughing near a statue of a stern party leader. Some visitors find this mix uncomfortable. Others think it is exactly the point. The statues have lost their power. They no longer command anyone. They have become objects to look at, question and, sometimes, walk past without a second glance.

It is worth asking yourself how you feel as you move through the forest. Are you curious, uneasy, amused, sad? Each reaction is honest. Grūtas Park does not give one official answer about how to remember the Soviet period. Instead, it places the evidence in front of you and lets you decide. In a country where many families still carry stories of occupation and deportation, that open question is part of the experience.

Before you leave, stop for a moment on one of the wooden walkways. Look back at the nearest figure, still pointing toward a future that never arrived. Listen again to the wind in the pines and the crunch of the path. These statues were built to stand in the centre of everything. Now they stand at the edge of a Lithuanian forest, and the trees around them keep growing.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for forest paths and wooden walkways.
Bring insect repellent on warm days near the wetlands.
Combine your visit with a trip to Druskininkai town.
Read the small signs explaining where each statue once stood.
Allow extra time for the indoor exhibition buildings.
