The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the southern edge of greater Osaka, the city noise thins out. You hear wind in the pines, a few crows, and gravel shifting underfoot. Then you look up, and a tall white shape rises above the trees. It is smooth and pale and oddly soft at the edges, almost like something modelled from clay. It does not look like a temple, a castle, or an office tower. It looks like nothing else on the skyline.

This is the Dai Heiwa Kinen Tō, the Great Peace Memorial Tower. One point matters before anything else. Many visitors know the tower as a landmark of the Sakai area, because it stands clearly against the sky from parts of southern Osaka. Strictly speaking, though, it stands in Tondabayashi, a neighbouring city a short trip southeast of Sakai. It rises from the grounds of the headquarters of a religious organisation called Perfect Liberty Kyodan, usually shortened to PL.

PL is a Japanese new religion. The term covers faiths founded in the modern era, often with a strong focus on daily life and practice. PL took its present form after the Second World War. Its central teaching is often summed up as "life is art". The idea is that every person should express their true self in what they do, much as an artist does. Keep that phrase in mind. It helps to explain why the tower looks the way it does.

The tower was completed in 1970. Its height is usually given as around 180 metres, which makes it very large for a structure that is neither a skyscraper nor a broadcast mast. The leader of PL at the time drove the project personally. The organisation describes it as a memorial to all the people who have died in wars throughout human history. The memorial is not limited to one nation, one army, or one side.

That last point surprises many visitors. Japan has many war memorials, and most of them remember particular soldiers, particular battles, or the victims of particular cities. This tower takes a deliberately wider view. It is meant to stand for the dead of every war, in every country and every century. It also serves as a prayer for peace in the future. It is a tower for strangers, in a sense — for people its builders never knew.

Now look again at the shape. The base flares outward, and the walls curve as they climb. Near the top, the form narrows and then swells slightly before it tapers again. People describe it in many ways. Some see a folded hand, some see a flame, and some see a seed or a bud. PL has not tied it to one simple image. That openness fits the idea of a memorial meant to belong to everyone.

The surface is a pale off-white, and it changes noticeably with the weather. On a clear morning the tower looks almost bright, with sharp shadows along its curves. Under grey cloud it turns flat and chalky and seems to sink into the sky. At dusk the white takes on the warm colours of the sunset. If you have time, watch it for a few minutes. The building looks different from one quarter of an hour to the next.

Here is a practical detail that many people miss. The tower stands on private religious grounds, and it is generally not a sightseeing building with a public observation deck. Most visitors see it from outside, from nearby roads, from the surrounding neighbourhood, or from a distance across the Osaka plain. Do not expect a lift to a viewing platform. The experience here is about looking up at the tower, not looking down from it.

For that reason, the best views often come from some distance away. From farther off, you see the full outline above the low houses and green hills of southern Osaka. Closer in, the tower can disappear behind trees and buildings, and then suddenly fill the view when a road opens up. Locals use it almost as a compass point. If you can see the white tower, you know roughly where you are.

The tower is also closely linked to two other things that are well known in Japan. The first is fireworks. For many years, PL held a very large summer fireworks display on these grounds. It drew huge crowds from across the Kansai region, and many people remember the white tower lit by bursts of colour. The display has not run every year, so check current news before planning a summer visit around it.

The second link is baseball. PL also ran a high school, PL Gakuen, whose baseball team became famous at the national high school tournament at Koshien Stadium. The team produced several players who later played professionally. For many Japanese sports fans, the letters P and L bring baseball to mind before they bring religion. It is an unexpected connection — a peace memorial and a school sports legend from the same few square kilometres.

As you stand here, think about the setting. Around the tower, ordinary suburban life goes on. There are commuter trains, small shops, rice fields at the edges of town, and houses with tiled roofs. The memorial does not stand in a grand plaza or in a city centre. It rises out of everyday Japan. That contrast gives it some of its quiet power. The tower asks a very large question in a very ordinary place.

Please remember that this is an active place of worship. If you walk near the grounds, keep your voice low. Follow any signs about where visitors may go, and avoid photographing people at prayer. The best way to show respect is simple. Look, listen, and give the place a moment of your attention. So before you leave, look up one more time. Notice how the white curves catch the light right now, and how the trees move at the base of the tower. As you walk away, turn around once or twice. You will probably still see the pale spire above the rooftops for a long time — a quiet reminder of everyone, everywhere, who never came home from war.
