The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the traffic of Sakai, there is a low and steady sound of wind moving through a dense wall of trees. Across a band of still, dark water, a forested hill rises from the flat city. It looks like a natural wood, but it is not. Every slope in front of you was shaped by human hands more than fifteen centuries ago. You are standing at the edge of a tomb.

This is the Mozu Kofun Group, a cluster of ancient burial mounds spread across the southern part of the Osaka plain. The word kofun simply means old mound. Japanese historians gave their name to a whole era, the Kofun period, which ran roughly from the third century to the late sixth or seventh century. In those centuries, powerful rulers were buried beneath earthworks of a size never seen in Japan before or since.

In 2019, UNESCO inscribed these mounds on the World Heritage List. They share that listing with a sister cluster, the Furuichi group, a short distance to the east in the cities of Habikino and Fujiidera. Together they are known as the Mozu-Furuichi Kofun Group. The listing recognises them as the clearest surviving record of how early Japanese rulers expressed power, and how a political centre took shape on this plain.

The largest mound here is Daisen Kofun. It is one of the largest burial mounds anywhere on Earth, measured by its footprint. From end to end it stretches close to half a kilometre. Walking all the way around its outer moat takes most visitors well over half an hour. Yet from where you stand, you see only trees and water. The full shape stays invisible at ground level.

That shape is the first surprise. Seen from the air, Daisen and many of its neighbours form a keyhole. A square front section joins a round rear section, where the main burial was placed. Japanese archaeologists call this design zenpo-koen-fun, which means square in front, round behind. The form appears only in this part of the world, and the biggest examples of it cluster right here in Sakai and in the Furuichi area.

The Imperial Household Agency manages Daisen and attributes it to Emperor Nintoku, a ruler described in Japan's earliest chronicles. Many scholars treat that attribution with caution. The chronicles were compiled centuries after these mounds were built, and no excavation has confirmed who lies inside. Because the agency treats the site as an imperial tomb, visitors cannot climb or enter it. At the front, a worship place with a simple gate faces the mound, and people still stop there quietly to bow.

Here is the second surprise. These hills did not always look green. When they were new, their slopes were covered with stones, laid like a paved skin over the earth. Rows of clay figures called haniwa stood along the terraces. Some were plain cylinders. Others took the shape of houses, warriors, horses, and shields. A new mound would have shone pale in the sun, a hard geometric form, clearly visible to anyone on the plain or approaching by sea.

That sea matters. In the Kofun period, the coastline of Osaka Bay lay much closer to this spot than it does today. Many historians believe the mounds stood where travellers arriving by ship would see them first. If so, they worked as monuments to the dead and also as a message to visitors about who held power in this land.

The name Mozu carries its own story, and it belongs to legend rather than to proven history. Mozu is the Japanese word for the bull-headed shrike, a small, sharp-beaked bird. According to a tale recorded in the old chronicles, a deer once ran out and died during work on a tomb. A shrike flew out of the deer's ear. People say the place took its name from that strange moment, and an old local name, Mimihara, means field of the ear.

The cluster was once far larger. Records and old maps show that more than a hundred mounds once rose across this area. During the twentieth century, as Sakai grew into a modern industrial city, many smaller ones were levelled for housing, roads, and factories. Dozens survive today. Some are vast, like Daisen and the mound attributed to Emperor Richu, one of the largest in Japan. Others are small round hills tucked between homes and parking lots.

That contrast is part of the experience here. You can walk down a quiet residential street, past bicycles and vending machines, and suddenly find a fenced grove that is a fifth-century tomb. Turtles sun themselves on the moat banks. Herons stand very still in the shallow water. Neighbours walk their dogs along paths that follow the outline of a dead ruler's mound. The ancient and the ordinary sit side by side.

To understand the scale you cannot see from the ground, many visitors take the lift to the observation floor of Sakai City Hall, which looks out over the city toward the mounds. Others visit the Sakai City Museum, inside Daisen Park, which displays haniwa figures and models of the tombs. Seeing a model first makes the walk around the moat feel very different. You begin to sense where the corners turn, and where the round rear section curves away.

Sakai's later history grew from this same ground. Centuries after the mound builders, the city became a busy trading port, known for skilled craftsmen and, today, for fine kitchen knives. Sen no Rikyu, the great master of the Japanese tea ceremony, was born here. The patience and precision of those later crafts feel at home beside earthworks that took enormous organised labour to raise.

Before you move on, look once more at the tree line across the water. Notice how the canopy rises gently, then levels, then rises again. That changing height follows the buried terraces underneath. Somewhere inside, a ruler from fifteen centuries ago still lies undisturbed. As you walk away, listen again for the wind in those trees, and the quiet that settles over this water, even in the middle of a modern city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Sakai City Hall's observation floor to grasp the scale.
See the tomb models at Sakai City Museum first.
Allow well over half an hour to circle Daisen's moat.
Wear comfortable shoes for long, flat walks between mounds.
Respect the worship gate; climbing the mounds is forbidden.
