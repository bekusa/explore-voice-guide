The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the stone in front of you. It is not smooth. Up close, the pale golden blocks are full of tiny holes, ridges and swirls. Some of those marks are fossils of sea creatures — shells and coral that lived in warm Caribbean water long before anyone built a city here. You are looking at a church made, quite literally, from the floor of an ancient sea.

This is the Cathedral of Havana, dedicated to the Virgin Mary of the Immaculate Conception. It fills one whole side of the Plaza de la Catedral, in the heart of Old Havana. The square around you is paved with stone, and it is surrounded by colonial mansions with heavy wooden doors and shaded arcades. In the late afternoon, the light turns the facade the colour of honey, and the shadows sink deep into its curves.

Here is the first surprise. This square was once a swamp. Low, wet ground collected rainwater here, and the early name for the area referred to that marsh. Over time, the land was drained and paved, and wealthy families moved in. Today it is one of the most elegant squares in the city. But when the rain comes hard in summer, you can still imagine the water gathering here, just as it did centuries ago.

The church did not begin as a cathedral. In the eighteenth century, the Jesuits — a Catholic religious order known for its schools and missions — started building a church on this site. Then, in 1767, the Spanish crown expelled the Jesuits from all its territories, including Cuba. Their church stood unfinished. The building was later completed by others, and in the late eighteenth century it became the cathedral of Havana's new diocese.

Now look at the shape of the facade. This is Cuban Baroque. Baroque is a European style that loves movement, curves and drama, and here it arrived in a Caribbean form. The front of the church seems to ripple. Columns step forward and step back. The upper section rises in curves rather than straight lines. Many visitors call this the finest Baroque facade in Cuba, and it is easy to see why. The whole wall feels alive in changing light.

Next, look at the two bell towers. Do they match? Look again. They do not. One tower is clearly wider than the other. Visitors often notice this only after several minutes in the square. A popular explanation says the narrower tower left space for a street beside it, so that rainwater could flow away toward the sea. Whether that is the full story or not, the uneven pair gives the cathedral its slightly lopsided, very human character.

The Cuban writer Alejo Carpentier spent much of his life thinking about Havana and its buildings. A phrase often credited to him compares this facade to music turned into stone. Whatever his exact words were, the idea fits. Stand in the middle of the square and let your eyes move from left to right. The curves rise and fall like a melody, and the columns mark a kind of rhythm.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a big one. For about a century, this cathedral held remains believed to be those of Christopher Columbus. They were brought here from Santo Domingo, in what is now the Dominican Republic, in the 1790s. When Spain lost Cuba in 1898, the remains were taken across the ocean to Seville, in Spain. Santo Domingo still claims to hold the true bones, so the debate has never fully ended.

If the doors are open, step inside. The mood changes at once. After the swirling Baroque front, the interior is calmer and more restrained. It was redecorated in the early nineteenth century in the Neoclassical style, which favours clean lines, columns and balance, inspired by ancient Greece and Rome. The air is cooler, the light is softer, and the noise of the square fades behind you. Take a few seconds to let your eyes adjust.

Inside, notice how the space is arranged. There is a central nave, the long main hall, with aisles running alongside it. Look up at the ceiling and toward the main altar at the far end. Look also at the side chapels, each with its own altar and images. This is still a working church. People come here to pray, to light candles and to attend Mass, so move quietly and keep your voice low.

Step back outside and look at the buildings around the square. These were the homes of powerful colonial families, built around inner courtyards to catch the breeze. One of them now holds a museum of colonial art, with furniture and decorative pieces from wealthy Havana homes. Another holds a restaurant with tables under its arches. Old Havana as a whole was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982, and this square is one of its centrepieces.

Listen to the square for a moment. You may hear musicians playing near the cafes, the clatter of plates, guides calling to their groups, perhaps the bells above you. Women in bright colonial-style dresses sometimes pose for photographs with visitors. Children run across the open stone. This mix of prayer, tourism and everyday life has been part of the square for a long time. The cathedral watches over all of it without changing its expression.

Before you leave, walk to one corner of the square and look back. From an angle, you can see both towers together, and the difference in their width becomes clear. Notice how the stone looks rougher near the bottom, where centuries of hands, rain and salt air have worn it down. Then, as you walk away down one of the narrow side streets, glance back once more. The cathedral will slip out of view between the old walls — a piece of the ancient sea, shaped into a song, standing quietly in the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon when sunlight warms the facade.
Dress modestly with covered shoulders to enter the church.
Stand in a far corner to frame both towers.
Keep voices low inside; services still take place.
Bring water and shade; the open square gets very hot.
