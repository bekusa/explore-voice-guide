The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. It comes straight off the Florida Straits, warm and full of salt, and it pushes against you as you climb onto the stone. Below, waves break white against the rocks. Across the narrow channel, the long curve of the Malecón runs west, and the pastel buildings of Havana catch the light. You are standing on the headland that guarded this harbour for centuries.

This is the Castillo de los Tres Reyes del Morro, the Castle of the Three Kings of the Morro. The Three Kings are the Magi of the Christian nativity story, the wise men who followed a star. The word morro is Spanish for a rocky headland. So the name tells you two things at once: a sacred dedication, and a plain description of the ground under your feet.

Why build here? In the sixteenth century, Havana became the gathering point for Spain's treasure fleets. Ships loaded with silver and goods from the Americas met in this bay before they crossed the Atlantic together. That made the harbour one of the richest targets in the world. Pirates and rival powers raided Caribbean ports again and again, and Spain needed a lock on the door.

The man chosen to design that lock was an Italian military engineer, Juan Bautista Antonelli. He worked for the Spanish crown on fortifications across the Caribbean. Construction began in the late sixteenth century and continued for many years. Look at how the walls follow the shape of the rock. Antonelli did not flatten the headland. He used it, so the cliff itself becomes part of the defence.

Now look at the stone. It is pale, rough, and full of small holes. Much of Havana's colonial building used local limestone formed from ancient coral and sea life. Run your hand over a wall and you can sometimes feel the texture of shells. It is a surprising thought — the fortress that held off navies is made from the remains of the sea it faced.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Morro did not work alone. Across the channel, on the city side, stands a smaller fort called La Punta. The two faced each other like guards at a gate. According to historical accounts, a heavy chain or boom could be stretched between them at night to block ships from entering. Between the cannons and the chain, the harbour mouth became a narrow and dangerous place for any enemy.

For a long time, the system worked. Then came 1762. During the Seven Years' War, a large British expedition arrived off Cuba. Instead of forcing the harbour mouth, the British landed troops and attacked the Morro from the land side. The siege lasted for weeks in the summer heat. Soldiers on both sides suffered badly from disease as well as gunfire.

The Spanish defence was led by a naval officer, Luis Vicente de Velasco. He held the castle far longer than many expected. In the end, British engineers dug a mine beneath one of the bastions and exploded it, opening a breach in the wall. Troops stormed through. Velasco was mortally wounded in the fighting. Spain still honours him as a hero, and the British themselves treated him with respect.

And now the second surprise. The British did not keep Havana. The following year, under the peace treaty that ended the war, Spain got the city back. In exchange, Spain gave Florida to Britain. Think about that for a moment. The loss of this headland helped redraw the map of North America. After the siege, Spain built the huge fortress of La Cabaña on the ridge just behind you, so the high ground could never be used against the city again.

Look up at the lighthouse. It is younger than the castle walls. The original tower was replaced by the current lighthouse in the 1840s, and its light has guided ships into Havana ever since. From a distance, this pale tower on its dark rock is the image that sailors, and later cruise passengers, remember first. Many people see it as the symbol of arrival in Havana.

Walk along the ramparts slowly. You will pass old cannons, narrow stairways, and thick walls cut with openings for guns. Notice how the paths turn and narrow. Fortress builders designed them that way, so attackers who broke in would face confusion and close fire. Inside, some rooms hold displays about the castle's history and about seafaring in the Caribbean. Small details here reward patience.

Today the Morro is part of a larger UNESCO World Heritage Site that includes Old Havana and its whole system of fortifications. That listing matters. It reminds us that this castle is one piece of a design that covered the entire bay. Walls, forts, and the city grew together. If you stay toward evening, you may hear the cannon shot from La Cabaña next door, a ceremony that continues an old colonial custom of marking the close of the city gates.

Before you leave, stand at the seaward edge one more time. Watch the water move in and out of the channel. Fishermen sometimes sit on the rocks across the way, and old cars roll along the Malecón like bright toys. The cannons are quiet now. The wind is the same wind the gunners felt, and the light on the stone still turns gold as the sun drops. Take a breath of that salt air, and carry the view of the city with you as you walk back.
