The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, cars hum along the coast road, and beyond them the Mediterranean breaks softly on shingle. The air carries salt, warm asphalt, and the resin of umbrella pines. Out here, between Antibes and the village of Biot, the land is flat and low. It feels like a place built for the sea, and in a way it was. You are standing beside what was, for more than half a century, the largest marine park in France.

Before we go further, an honest note about where you are. Marineland closed its gates to the public in early January 2025. The shows have stopped, and the pools no longer fill with crowds. What remains is a site in transition, and its future is still being decided. So this guide is not a tour of what lies behind the fence. It is a story about why this place existed, why it ended, and what it leaves behind.

The park opened in 1970. Its founder was Roland de La Poype, and his life reads like a novel. During the Second World War he flew as a fighter pilot with the Normandie-Niemen squadron, a French unit that fought alongside the Soviet air force on the Eastern Front. After the war he became an industrialist. He made his fortune in plastics and boating equipment, including the marine company Plastimo. The sea was already part of his working life long before he built a park beside it.

Why here, on this stretch of the Côte d'Azur? Think about the Riviera in the late 1960s. Package holidays were growing fast. Families drove south on the old coastal routes, looking for something to do between beach days. A marine park offered a new kind of spectacle. Visitors could see creatures that most people had only met in films or encyclopedias. For many children who grew up in France, this was their first meeting with a dolphin.

Over the decades the park grew into a large complex. It held orcas, bottlenose dolphins, sea lions, seals, sharks, penguins, polar bears for a time, and many species of fish. The orca shows became the main attraction. Generations of visitors remember the splash zone, the wet clothes, and the gasps when a whale several tonnes heavy rose out of the water. Here is a detail many people never knew. Several of the park's orcas were born here, in captivity, rather than taken from the wild.

That fact matters, because it shaped the debate that followed. Through the 2000s and 2010s, public opinion about keeping whales and dolphins in tanks began to change, across Europe and far beyond it. Scientists and animal welfare groups argued that orcas travel huge distances in the wild and live in complex family groups. A concrete pool, however large, could not offer that life. The park answered that its animals supported research, education, and conservation. The argument grew louder each year.

Then came a night that nobody here forgets. In October 2015, violent storms struck the Alpes-Maritimes. Torrential rain caused deadly flooding across the region, and the nearby river Brague burst its banks. Mud and floodwater poured into the park. Many animals died, and the facilities suffered heavy damage. Look around at how low and flat this ground is, close to the river mouth. Standing here, you can understand how quickly the water came.

The park rebuilt and reopened. But the flood had raised hard questions about the site, and the wider debate did not go away. In 2021, France passed a law on animal welfare that phased out shows with captive cetacean, meaning whales and dolphins, by the end of 2026. It also banned their breeding in captivity. For a park built around the orca show, this was the beginning of the end. The law did not close Marineland directly, but it removed the heart of its business.

The final years were difficult. Two of the park's orcas, a young male named Moana and an older male named Inouk, died in 2023 and 2024. Visitor numbers fell. The owners announced that the park would close, and it did so at the start of 2025. Some animals were moved to other institutions. But the fate of the remaining orcas and dolphins became a public argument of its own, involving the French government, animal welfare groups, and zoos in other countries.

The largest question concerned two orcas, a mother named Wikie and her son Keijo. Both were born in captivity, so experts agreed they could not simply be released into the open sea. Proposals included transfers to parks abroad, or to a planned sea sanctuary, a protected bay where whales could live in natural water. Each option met objections. If you are listening to this guide today, the details may have changed again. It is worth reading the latest news before you leave.

Now take a moment with the physical place. From the roadside you can still sense the scale of the complex, the long low buildings, the pale walls, the painted signs fading in the Riviera sun. Next door, other leisure parks and holiday campsites line this stretch of coast. Together they show what this corner of Antibes became in the late twentieth century — a strip of summer entertainment, built between the railway, the road, and the beach.

This ground is also part of a longer story. Antibes is one of the oldest towns on this coast, founded by Greek settlers in antiquity under the name Antipolis. Its old town and ramparts lie a few kilometres south of here. For most of its history, people came to this shore to fish, to trade, and to defend it. Only in the last century did they start coming to watch the sea perform. Marineland sits at the end of that long arc.

Whatever you think about marine parks, this site shows how quickly values can shift within one lifetime. A place that many families once saw as pure wonder became, for many others, a symbol of something to leave behind. Both memories are real. People who worked here often cared deeply for the animals. People who campaigned against it cared deeply too. The story of this park is the story of that disagreement.

Before you walk on, turn toward the water. Beyond the road, the Mediterranean is the same colour it was in 1970 — a deep, moving blue. Somewhere far out there, wild dolphins still cross the bay of Antibes, unseen from the shore. Listen once more to the waves on the pebbles. Then, as you head back toward the town, notice how quiet this corner has become.
