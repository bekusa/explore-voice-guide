The guide

The story

Listen first to the water. Out here in the Gulf of Aqaba it slaps gently against dark granite, and the wind carries a faint taste of salt and dust. Under your feet the rock is rough and warm. Around you, the mountains of Sinai rise in shades of rust and violet. This small island sits only a short boat ride from the Egyptian shore, near the town of Taba. Yet from its walls, the whole history of this narrow sea seems to open up.

The island goes by several names. In English it is Pharaoh's Island. In Arabic it is Jezirat Fara'un, which means the same thing. Crusader chroniclers knew it by a French name, often written as Île de Graye. Despite the name, the stone walls you see are not the work of the pharaohs. They are medieval. The name is a later label, the kind that people attach to old ruins in Egypt almost by habit.

So why would anyone build a fortress on such a small rock? Look at the map in your mind. The Gulf of Aqaba is a long, thin finger of the Red Sea. At its northern tip, routes met between Egypt, Syria, and Arabia. Pilgrims travelling to Mecca passed this way. So did merchants carrying goods between the Mediterranean world and the Red Sea. Whoever held this island could watch the gulf, and could tax or block the traffic moving along it.

The Crusaders understood this early. In the early twelfth century, the forces of the Kingdom of Jerusalem pushed south toward the Red Sea. Historians generally credit King Baldwin the First with establishing a Crusader presence on the island around the 1110s. For a European kingdom based far to the north, this was an extraordinary outpost. It gave the Crusaders a foothold on a sea that connected to Arabia and the Indian Ocean beyond.

That foothold did not last. In the 1170s, the island was taken by Salah ad-Din, known in the West as Saladin. He was the Kurdish-born ruler who united Egypt and Syria and later recaptured Jerusalem. Under his rule the island was fortified again, and most of the walls you see today belong to this Ayyubid period. The Ayyubids were the dynasty Saladin founded. Their castle turned the island into a guard post for the Muslim world's southern routes.

Here is a story many visitors never hear. In the early 1180s, a Crusader lord named Reynald of Châtillon launched one of the boldest raids of the age. He had ships carried overland in pieces and reassembled them on the Red Sea. His fleet attacked this island and raided along the coasts toward the holy cities of Arabia. The raid shocked the Muslim world, and Saladin's forces answered it with force. The episode deepened the hostility that ended, a few years later, at the Battle of Hattin.

Now turn your attention to the fortress itself. It is built in two main parts, one on each of the island's rocky hills, joined by walls that follow the shape of the land. The stone is local granite, cut into blocks and set with mortar. Towers rise at intervals along the walls. Inside you will find narrow passages, small rooms, and steep steps worn smooth by centuries of feet. Take your time on the stairs. They were built for defence, not comfort.

As you walk, notice how the builders worked with the rock rather than against it. Walls sit directly on the natural granite ridges. In places, it is hard to see where the mountain ends and the masonry begins. Inside the fortress, archaeologists and restorers identified features of daily life for the garrison — storage spaces, a place for baking bread, and cisterns for collecting precious fresh water. On an island with no river and almost no rain, every drop mattered.

A second surprise waits down by the water. On the western side of the island, there is a small sheltered lagoon, partly enclosed by land. Scholars have suggested it may have served as a protected anchorage, a safe place for boats in a gulf known for sudden winds. Some researchers have even linked the island with ancient harbours mentioned in very old texts. That idea remains a theory, not a settled fact. But standing here, it is easy to see why sailors in any age would value such a shelter.

In modern times, the island sat on a frontier once more. After 1967, the Sinai Peninsula was occupied by Israel, and it returned to Egypt in stages after the peace treaty between the two countries. Egyptian archaeologists later restored much of the fortress in the 1980s. Some of the cleaner, sharper stonework you notice comes from that restoration. Older sections look rougher and more weathered. Comparing the two is a quiet lesson in how monuments survive.

Climb to one of the higher towers and look around slowly. This is perhaps the most remarkable thing about the island. On a clear day you can see the shores of four countries. Egypt lies behind you. Across the water to the north and east are Israel, Jordan, and Saudi Arabia. Few places on earth gather so many borders into a single view. The gulf that once carried pilgrims and raiders now divides, and connects, modern nations.

The sea around the island is also worth your attention. The water here is very clear, and coral reefs grow close to the shore. From the walls you may see the shifting colours of the reef below — pale turquoise over sand, deep blue where the bottom drops away. Many visitors combine a visit to the fortress with snorkelling. The life on the reef is as old, in its own way, as anything built of stone.

Notice, too, how the light changes the island through the day. In the morning, the granite looks grey and cool. By late afternoon, it turns warm and golden, and the mountains of Sinai behind it glow red. Shadows gather in the arrow slits and doorways. The sounds are simple here — wind, water, gulls, perhaps the engine of a returning boat. It is a place that rewards slowness.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the walls. Feel the wind coming down the gulf, the same wind the garrison felt eight centuries ago. Look at the narrow strip of water between the island and the shore. Crusaders crossed it. Saladin's soldiers crossed it. Now you will cross it too, on your way back. As the boat pulls away, turn around once. Watch the towers grow small against the mountains, and the island become a rock in the sea again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven stone stairs.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is very limited.
Pack swimwear to snorkel the reef near the shore.
Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the granite.
Check boat departure points near Taba before setting out.
