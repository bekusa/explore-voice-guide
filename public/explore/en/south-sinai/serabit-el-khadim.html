The guide

The story

The last stretch of the climb is loose stone, pale and sharp, and every step lands with a small dry crunch. The wind up here carries almost no smell at all, only dust and heat. Then the plateau opens out in front of you, and you see them: tall slabs of stone standing upright across the summit, like the masts of ships that sank into the rock. This is Serabit el-Khadim, high in the mountains of southwest Sinai.

The name comes from Arabic, and it is usually translated as something like 'the heights of the servant' or 'the heights of the slave'. The local Bedouin gave the place that name long after its builders had gone. But the builders themselves were Egyptians, and they came here for one reason. They came for a blue-green stone that they valued very highly. They came for turquoise, and the hills around you still carry the scars of their work.

To understand this place, picture the journey the ancient miners made. Egypt sits across the Gulf of Suez, to the west. Expeditions crossed the water, then travelled inland through dry valleys to reach these mountains. They brought officials, scribes, soldiers, stonecutters and labourers. They also brought donkeys, food and water, because the plateau gives very little of its own. Each expedition was an organised state project. Many of them left a written record of their visit carved in stone.

Those records are the standing slabs you passed on your way in. They are stelae, which means inscribed stone markers. Many of them name the ruling king, the leader of the expedition, and sometimes the number of workers. Some describe the hardship of the work or the heat of the season. The stelae stand along the approach to the temple, so the path itself works like a long archive. Each slab adds another voice to the record.

The temple at the centre of the plateau belonged to Hathor, one of the great goddesses of ancient Egypt. Hathor was linked with love, music, joy and the sky. She was also the protector of distant lands, and in Sinai she carried a special title: Lady of Turquoise. It makes a kind of sense. The miners were far from home, in a hard place, digging for a stone the colour of a clear sky. They wanted her on their side.

Work on the temple began in the Middle Kingdom, the period of Egyptian history that started roughly four thousand years ago. At first it was a sacred cave cut into the rock. Over the following centuries, later rulers extended it outward. New Kingdom pharaohs added halls, courts and shrines, so the building grew in a long line toward the east. Walking through it today is like walking forward through time, from the oldest rock-cut rooms to the later stone chambers.

There is a second god here too, and many visitors miss him. Sopdu was a god of the eastern borderlands, a guardian of the desert roads and the frontier. A shrine to him sits within the temple complex beside Hathor's. Together the two gods covered what the miners needed most: protection on the dangerous route, and blessing at the mine. The temple was a place of prayer, but it was also a kind of insurance for every expedition.

Now for the surprise that made this remote plateau famous among scholars. In the early twentieth century, the archaeologist Flinders Petrie excavated here. Among the finds were short inscriptions in a strange script. It looked a little like Egyptian hieroglyphs, but it used only a small set of signs. Experts now call it Proto-Sinaitic. Many of them believe it is one of the earliest known forms of alphabetic writing, written by people who spoke a Semitic language, not Egyptian.

A scholar named Alan Gardiner studied these signs a few years later. He suggested that each picture stood for the first sound of its own word. A sign shaped like an ox head, for example, could stand for a single sound. Using this idea, he read one repeated group of signs as a name for a goddess, Baalat, often understood as a local name for Hathor. If that idea is right, the letters you use every day may have distant roots in workers who prayed on this hilltop.

One of the best-known pieces from the site is a small stone sphinx. It carries both Egyptian hieroglyphs and the early alphabetic signs. That object is now held in the British Museum in London, not here. Other finds went to museums in Cairo and elsewhere. So what you see on the plateau today is mostly what was too heavy or too fixed to move: the stelae, the walls, the pillars, and the cave shrines cut into the rock.

As you walk through the ruins, look at the tops of some of the pillars. On certain fragments you can find the face of Hathor, a calm woman's face with the ears of a cow. That was one of her classic forms in Egyptian art. Nearby, the old mine entrances open into the slopes around the plateau. They are low, dark shafts, cut by hand with stone and copper tools. Inside, the rock still shows the marks of chisels.

The Bedouin communities of this part of Sinai know this land better than anyone. Most visitors come up with a local guide, and the guide often knows the safest path, the shaded spots and the way down before the heat rises. They may point out turquoise traces in the rock, or tell local stories about the mountain. Listen to those stories as part of the place, too. They are a living layer on top of the ancient one.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the temple and turn slowly in a full circle. Around you, ridge after ridge of brown and red mountains runs to the horizon. There are no towns, no roads in sight, and almost no sound except the wind. The stelae cast long thin shadows on the stone. On your way down, look again at those upright slabs, and remember that each one began as a message from someone who stood exactly here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb early to avoid the midday heat.
Hire a local Bedouin guide for the mountain route.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose, sharp stone paths.
Carry plenty of water for the climb and descent.
Bring a hat and sun protection for the open plateau.
