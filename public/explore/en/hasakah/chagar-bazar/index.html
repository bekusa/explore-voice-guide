The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Out here on the plain it moves across the grass and the ploughed fields with almost nothing to stop it. Underfoot, the ground rises gently into a low, rounded mound. It is soft earth mixed with crumbs of pottery, pale and reddish, scattered like broken eggshell. This is Chagar Bazar, a tell in the upper Khabur region of northeastern Syria. It looks like a small hill, but nature did not build it.

A tell is a mound that people built without meaning to. Families built houses of mud brick. Over generations, those houses collapsed or were knocked down, and new homes rose on top of the old ones. Layer after layer, the ground climbed upward. So when you stand on the summit, you are standing on thousands of years of daily life. Kitchens, storerooms, courtyards and graves are stacked beneath your feet, one on top of another.

Look around at the landscape. This is the Khabur basin, a wide, fertile plain fed by the Khabur River and its tributaries. In spring the fields can turn green with wheat and barley. In summer they bake to gold and dust. Rain falls here in most years, enough to grow crops without irrigation. That simple fact explains a great deal. For thousands of years, farmers settled across this plain, and their villages left mounds like this one all across the horizon.

The oldest layers at Chagar Bazar reach back to what archaeologists call the Halaf period, in the sixth millennium before the common era. The Halaf culture is named after another site in this part of Syria. Its people made some of the finest painted pottery of the ancient Near East. The pots are thin-walled and decorated in dark paint with geometric patterns, and sometimes with bull heads. Fragments of this kind of pottery have come from the lowest levels here.

Above those early farmers, the mound kept growing through the following millennia. Later layers include material from the third millennium before the common era, a time when towns across northern Mesopotamia were growing larger and more organised. Here is the first surprise. A small, quiet mound like this one can hold almost the whole story of early village and town life in the region, from painted pottery to written records. Chagar Bazar holds both.

Now for the people who first dug here in a serious way. In the mid-1930s, the British archaeologist Max Mallowan led excavations at Chagar Bazar. He worked across the Khabur region in those years, including at the larger site of Tell Brak, not far from here. With him came his wife. She was already a very famous writer — Agatha Christie.

This is the second surprise for many visitors. The queen of the detective novel spent seasons living on this plain. She helped with the work of the dig, and she photographed and recorded finds. Later she wrote a warm, funny memoir about these expeditions, called Come, Tell Me How You Live. In it she describes the heat, the dust, the local workmen, the mice, and the small dramas of camp life. If you have read it, you are standing inside some of its pages.

What did Mallowan find? Among the most important discoveries were clay tablets written in cuneiform. Cuneiform is the wedge-shaped script that scribes pressed into wet clay with a reed. These tablets date to the early second millennium before the common era. That was the age when the ruler Shamshi-Adad the First controlled a large kingdom across northern Mesopotamia. The texts are administrative, not poetry or legend. They record practical matters such as the issue of food rations and other supplies.

That practical quality is exactly what makes them precious. Lists of rations tell us who worked here, how they were fed, and how a small centre was tied into a much larger state. Scholars have long discussed what this town was called in ancient times. Several names have been suggested, but the question is not fully settled. So this mound keeps a little mystery. We can read its paperwork, yet we cannot be completely certain of its name.

The story did not end in the 1930s. Around the turn of the twenty-first century, a new international team returned to Chagar Bazar. Archaeologists from Belgium, Britain and Syria worked together here for several seasons. They used modern methods that Mallowan did not have, such as careful environmental sampling. Their excavations added more detail about the houses, burials and objects of the second millennium before the common era. They also helped place the older discoveries in a clearer context.

That later work stopped when war came to Syria. Since then, many archaeological sites in this region have faced damage, looting or simple neglect. Access to the area can be difficult, and conditions change. If you are here, you are among very few outside visitors to see this place in recent years. Please treat the mound gently. Do not pick up sherds or dig in loose earth, because every fragment has meaning in its place.

Take a moment to read the surface of the mound with your own eyes. You may see old trenches, now softened by rain and grass. You may notice how the slopes are steeper on some sides than others. You may spot bits of mud brick, dull orange and grey, in the eroded edges. Pottery sherds show different colours and textures. Some are coarse and thick. Others are fine and smooth. Each one came from a different moment in the life of the town.

Then lift your gaze to the horizon. On a clear day, other low mounds may appear across the plain, each one another ancient settlement. These villages and towns once traded with each other and fought each other. They shared roads, rivers, rulers and gods. Chagar Bazar was one point in a dense web of communities. Standing here, you can begin to imagine that web, with farmers walking between villages and scribes sending their records onward.

Before you walk down, stand still for one more minute. Feel the wind again, and the soft give of the earth under your shoes. Somewhere below you, a scribe once pressed a reed into wet clay to count out grain. Much later, a novelist sat in the dust nearby and wrote about the people she met. Now you are here, adding one more quiet visit to the long story of this hill. As you leave, notice the shape of the mound against the sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring water, sun protection and sturdy shoes for loose earth.

Read Christie's memoir Come, Tell Me How You Live beforehand.
Leave pottery sherds exactly where you find them.
