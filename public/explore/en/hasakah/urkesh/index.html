The guide

The story

Underfoot, the ground is soft and pale. It is dust from ancient mudbrick, dried and crumbled over thousands of years. Wind moves across the open plain and carries the smell of wheat fields and warm earth. To the north, a low line of hills rises at the edge of the sky. And in front of you, a long mound stands above the flat farmland. Locals call it Tell Mozan. Archaeologists know it by an older name... Urkesh.

A tell is a hill made by people, not by nature. Each generation built new houses on top of the ruins of the last. Over centuries, the layers piled higher and higher, until a whole city became a hill. Here in northeastern Syria, close to the modern town of Amuda and the Turkish border, this mound holds one of the oldest known cities of the Hurrians. They were a people of ancient Mesopotamia whose language belongs to no living family.

For a long time, scholars knew Urkesh only from texts. Its name appeared in old inscriptions and in myths written down much later. The city was important, but nobody knew exactly where it was. Then, from 1984, a team led by Giorgio Buccellati and Marilyn Kelly-Buccellati began digging at this mound. In the 1990s, they found the answer. It was written on small pieces of clay.

Here is the first surprise. The proof did not come from a great monument. It came from seal impressions. In the ancient Near East, important people owned carved cylinder seals. They rolled them across wet clay to sign and secure goods. At this site, the team found impressions naming a king called Tupkish and a queen called Uqnitum. The king carried the title of endan, the Hurrian word for ruler. Those tiny marks identified the city.

The seals show more than names. They show a royal household in surprising detail. Some scenes show the queen with her children. Others name members of her staff, such as a nurse and a cook. For a city more than four thousand years old, this is rare. We do not only see a king on a throne. We see a family, with servants and small daily routines, pressed into clay.

Now look toward the highest part of the mound. The excavators found a large temple terrace there, with a monumental staircase leading up to a temple. The building dates to the third millennium before Christ. Many scholars connect it with Kumarbi, the father of the gods in Hurrian belief. In later versions of these myths, Kumarbi fights for power over heaven. Some of these stories passed to the Hittites and travelled far beyond this plain.

The terrace was built to be seen. On this flat land, a raised temple could be seen from far away across the fields. Imagine the staircase in use, with people climbing toward the sanctuary above the roofs of the city. Today, the stones and mudbrick sit quietly under the open sky. Even so, the shape of the climb is still clear. The building was meant to lift the eye, and the step, upward.

The second surprise lies below ground. Near the royal palace, the team found a deep stone-lined shaft. Scholars interpret it as an abi, a ritual pit used to reach the world of the dead. Hurrian and Hittite texts describe rites at such pits. Priests offered food and animals to call up spirits from below. Bones of young animals were found inside this structure. Many people think of temples as the way to reach the gods. This pit pointed the other way, down toward the dead.

The palace itself stood close by. It was built of mudbrick on stone foundations. The rooms were arranged around service areas and more formal spaces. Mudbrick is simple to make. Clay, water and straw are pressed into moulds and dried in the sun. But it is also fragile. Once a building like this is exposed, rain and wind begin to wear it away. Protecting it became one of the main tasks of the project.

So look at how the walls are protected. The excavators covered many exposed walls with light metal frames and fabric covers. From a distance, they look a little like tents laid along the ruins. They keep the rain off the ancient bricks. They also let visitors understand the shape of rooms and corridors. This approach later became a model that other sites studied. It is a practical answer to a delicate problem.

The story of the site in recent years is also a human one. When war came to Syria in the 2010s, the foreign team could no longer work in the field. But the work did not simply stop. People from the nearby villages, many of whom had worked on the excavation for years, continued to guard and maintain the site. The project also worked with local communities on exhibitions and education. For them, Urkesh became part of their own heritage.

Think about the long span of time here. The Hurrians built this city before many famous ancient capitals became powerful. Their language later travelled into the Hittite world, and their gods appeared in foreign myths. Yet for many centuries their own city lay hidden under a field of wheat. Archaeology gave Urkesh back its name. Much of the mound is still unexcavated. More of the story still lies under your feet.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind across the mound and the sounds from the farms nearby. Look at the pale earth, the covered walls and the line of the terrace against the sky. The hills to the north are the same hills the people of Urkesh saw. As you walk down from the tell, notice the soft dust on your shoes. It is the remains of a city, carried a little further by the wind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current security conditions in northeast Syria before travelling.
Arrange access through local contacts or the site guardians.
Bring sun protection; the mound has very little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for the dusty, uneven mound.
Stay on paths and never step on mudbrick walls.
