The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, the traffic of Palma moves along the seafront. In front of you, honey-coloured sandstone walls rise in a long, square-towered line. The stone is warm to the touch on a sunny afternoon, and it carries a soft golden tone. Seagulls cry somewhere above the battlements. Just beside you, the great Gothic cathedral fills half the sky. This is the Royal Palace of La Almudaina, and you are standing on one of the oldest seats of power on the island.

Start with the name, because it holds the whole story in a single word. Almudaina comes from Arabic. It is usually traced to a word meaning citadel, or little city. Long before any Christian king lived here, this hilltop above the bay held the fortress of the island's Muslim rulers. Mallorca was part of the Islamic world for centuries. The governors who ruled from this spot looked out on the same harbour you can see today.

In 1229, that world changed. King James the First of Aragon landed on Mallorca with a large fleet and took the city after a hard siege. Catalan and Aragonese settlers followed. The new rulers kept the strategic hilltop, and they kept the old fortress too. They did not tear everything down. Instead, they moved in, adapted the buildings, and slowly reshaped them to fit Christian and royal life.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. For a few generations, Mallorca was a kingdom of its own. After the death of James the First, his lands were divided. One son, James the Second, received the Kingdom of Mallorca. It included the Balearic Islands and lands across the sea on the mainland, such as Roussillon and Montpellier. For a short, bright period, this palace was the home of an independent royal court.

James the Second is the king who gave La Almudaina much of the shape you see now. In the early fourteenth century, he rebuilt the old Islamic citadel in the Gothic style. Gothic is the medieval European style of pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and tall windows. Builders worked on the palace and on the cathedral next door at roughly the same time. So when you look from one to the other, you are seeing two parts of a single royal vision.

That independent kingdom did not last. Its rulers were caught between powerful neighbours, and in the mid fourteenth century Mallorca was absorbed back into the Crown of Aragon. The last king of Mallorca died fighting to win back his throne. After that, La Almudaina was no longer the home of a resident king. But it never lost its royal status. Over the following centuries, it served governors, officials, and the military.

Now look up at the towers along the walls. They are square and solid, and they still feel like a fortress. On top of one tower stands the figure of an angel. Locals often call it the Tower of the Angel. The figure looks out over the city and the sea. Seen against a clear blue sky, the angel is one of the small details that make this skyline unmistakably Palma.

Inside, the mood changes. The rooms are cool and quiet after the brightness of the street. You walk through large halls with high ceilings and wide pointed arches. Some of these spaces once held banquets and royal audiences. Today they are hung with old tapestries and furnished with period pieces. The stone floors carry the sound of footsteps, and voices drop almost without anyone deciding to lower them.

One highlight is the Chapel of Saint Anne. It is a Gothic royal chapel, built for the court's private worship. Pay attention to its entrance. The doorway is older in style than much of the chapel, with rounded arches rather than pointed ones. That rounded style is called Romanesque, the form that came before Gothic in medieval Europe. It is a small reminder that this palace grew in layers over time, not all at once.

Here is the second surprise. La Almudaina is still a working royal palace. It is one of the official residences of the Spanish royal family, and it is cared for by the state heritage body, Patrimonio Nacional. When the royal family spends time on Mallorca, the palace is used for official receptions and ceremonies. So the halls you walk through are not only a museum. On certain days, they still host the life of the Spanish state.

The palace also keeps a military connection. Part of the complex has long been linked to the armed forces on the islands. That is one reason why some areas are closed to visitors. As you walk, you may notice doors that stay shut and courtyards you can see but not enter. This mix of public and private space has been part of La Almudaina's character for centuries.

Step back outside and walk toward the terrace or the edge of the walls. From here, the view opens to the south. Below you lies the Parc de la Mar, with its long lake that mirrors the cathedral and the palace walls. Beyond it, the bay of Palma stretches out toward the horizon. Ships and sailing boats move slowly across the water. At the foot of the palace, the formal gardens of S'Hort del Rei, the King's Garden, offer fountains, shade, and quiet paths.

Think about how this hill must have looked to sailors arriving by sea. For an Islamic governor, a Christian king, or a modern visitor on a ferry, the message is the same. This is where power sits. The palace and the cathedral stand shoulder to shoulder on the old sea wall. One represented the crown, the other the church. Together they still form the face that Palma shows to the water.

Before you leave, pause once more by the walls. Touch the sandstone and feel how it has been worn smooth by wind, salt, and centuries of hands. Look up at the angel on its tower, then across to the cathedral's tall buttresses. As you walk down toward the gardens or the old town, glance back over your shoulder. The palace changes colour with the light... pale in the morning, golden at sunset. It has watched this harbour for a very long time, and it is still watching now.
