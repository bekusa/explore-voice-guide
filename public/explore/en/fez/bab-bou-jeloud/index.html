The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Hooves click on stone. A cart rattles past, pushed by hand. Someone calls out a warning, "Balak!", which means "watch out", and a mule loaded with gas canisters squeezes through the crowd. Ahead of you rises a wall of blue. Thousands of small glazed tiles catch the light, and through three pointed arches you can already see the tops of minarets. This is Bab Bou Jeloud, the main western gateway into the old medina of Fez.

Before we go through, look at the shape of the arches. They curve inward at the bottom, like the shape of a horseshoe. This horseshoe arch is a signature of architecture across Morocco and Moorish Spain. The large central opening is framed by two smaller ones, one on each side. Around them, carved stucco and bands of geometric tilework climb toward the top of the wall. Every surface is decorated, but the effect is calm rather than crowded.

Here is the first surprise. Many visitors assume this gate is centuries old, as old as the medina itself. It is not. The gate you see was built in the early twentieth century, around 1913, during the first years of the French protectorate over Morocco. The craftsmen worked in the traditional style, and they did it so well that the gate looks timeless. But in the long story of Fez, this is one of the newer pieces.

And the city behind it is very old indeed. Fez was founded at the end of the eighth century and the start of the ninth, under the Idrisid dynasty, the first Islamic dynasty to rule this part of Morocco. The walled old town, called Fes el-Bali, which means "old Fez", grew over the following centuries into one of the great centres of learning and trade in the Islamic world. In 1981, UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site.

Now walk a few steps back and compare the two faces of the gate. On the outside, facing the newer city, the tiles are blue. Blue is often called the colour of Fez, and you will see it on pottery from the city's workshops. Walk through and turn around. On the inner side, facing the medina, the tiles are green. Green is the colour traditionally linked with Islam. One gate, two colours, two faces... one looking out at the world, one looking in toward faith and home.

Here is the second detail most people miss. Before this gate existed, entrance to the old town here passed through an older gate nearby. Like many defensive gates in Morocco, it was a bent entrance. Travellers had to turn a corner inside it, which slowed down any attacker on horseback. The new gate is straight. You can look right through it. That change says a lot about its era — this gate was built for welcome and for movement, not for defence.

The name itself has more than one explanation. One common account links it to the nearby Bou Jeloud area and its gardens. Another tradition says the name grew from an older phrase meaning something like "father of soldiers", linked to troops once stationed close by. Historians are not fully agreed, so treat both as possibilities rather than settled fact. What is certain is that every Fassi, every local, knows exactly where you mean when you say Bou Jeloud.

Look up through the central arch. Framed inside it, you can see minarets rising over the rooftops. One of them belongs to the Bou Inania Madrasa, a religious school built in the fourteenth century under the Marinid dynasty, a later royal family who made Fez their capital. The framing is so neat that it almost looks planned. Photographers wait here for the moment when no one walks through and the towers sit perfectly inside the curve.

Once you pass through, the city changes speed. Cars stop at the gate. Inside, the medina is one of the largest car-free urban areas in the world. Goods still move by donkey, by mule, and by handcart, as they have for centuries. The lanes are narrow, and sound carries differently between the high walls. You hear footsteps, voices, the tapping of metalworkers, and the call to prayer coming from several directions at once.

Just inside the gate, two main streets begin. Talaa Kebira, the "big slope", and Talaa Seghira, the "small slope", both run downhill into the heart of the old town. They are the two spines of the medina. Many first-time visitors follow one down and the other back up. Along the way, the lanes branch again and again, and it is very easy to lose your sense of direction. That is part of the experience.

The square around the gate is a meeting place. Locals arrange to see friends here. Guides wait for clients. Taxis drop people off at the edge. Small cafés line the area just inside, with plastic chairs facing the lane, and the smell of mint tea mixes with grilled meat and fresh bread. In the evening, the light softens, and families come out to walk. It feels less like a monument and more like a front door.

If you have time, the Jnan Sbil gardens, also known as the Bou Jeloud gardens, are a short walk away. After the noise of the medina, they offer shade, fountains, and quieter paths under tall trees. Locals use them to rest and to talk in the cooler part of the day. It is a good place to sit for a while before or after you go into the old town.

Before you move on, take one more look at the tiles. Come close and you will see that they are not perfectly identical. Each piece was cut and set by hand, a craft called zellige that is still taught and practised in Fez today. Small differences in colour and edge give the surface its soft shimmer. Now turn toward the arch, take a breath, and step through. The old city is waiting on the other side.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to photograph the arch without crowds.
Step aside quickly when you hear someone call "Balak".
Wear comfortable shoes for the steep medina slopes.
Rest in the nearby Jnan Sbil gardens before entering.
Agree on a guide's fee clearly before you set off.
