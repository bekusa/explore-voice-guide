The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the walls of this excavation, the modern town of Torre Annunziata carries on: scooters, voices, a shop shutter rolling up. Then you step down, below the level of the street, and the noise softens. The ground underfoot changes from asphalt to ancient paving. Painted walls rise around you in deep red, golden yellow, and soft green. You are now standing several metres below the present-day town, inside a Roman villa that lay buried for many centuries.

This place is called Oplontis. The name comes from a medieval copy of a Roman road map, known as the Peutinger Table. On that map, Oplontis appears as a stop along the coastal road near Pompeii. Archaeologists use the name today for the whole site. The great house in front of you is usually called Villa A. It is one of the largest and best-preserved Roman seaside villas ever found.

The story of its burial is the same story as Pompeii. In the year 79, Mount Vesuvius erupted. Ash, pumice, and superheated currents of gas and rock poured down its slopes. Oplontis was covered completely. Over time, the land rose, the coastline moved, and people forgot the villa was here. Today the sea is some distance away. In Roman times, this house looked directly out over the water of the Bay of Naples.

The villa was not discovered all at once. In the eighteenth century, diggers working for the Bourbon kings of Naples cut tunnels through this area, searching for treasure. They found some things, then moved on. Systematic excavation only began in the 1960s. Even now, part of the villa remains unexcavated. It continues under the modern road and buildings beside you. Look at the edges of the site, and you can see where the dig simply stops against the town.

So who lived here? This is where the story becomes interesting — and uncertain. Many scholars connect the villa with Poppaea Sabina, the second wife of the emperor Nero. The link comes partly from an inscription on an amphora, a clay storage jar, which names a freedman of Poppaea. That is a clue, not proof. You will often hear it called the Villa of Poppaea. It is best to treat that name as a strong possibility rather than a settled fact.

Here is one detail many visitors miss. When the eruption came, the villa appears to have been mostly empty. Archaeologists found signs of ongoing repair work, with building materials and unfinished areas. Perhaps the house was damaged in the earthquakes that shook this region in the years before the eruption. Perhaps the owners were simply away. Either way, no bodies were found in the main villa. The house was waiting for people who never returned.

Now look closely at the walls. Many of the paintings here belong to what scholars call the Second Style of Roman wall painting. In this style, artists painted false architecture: columns, doorways, balconies, and views into imaginary rooms. The goal was to make a flat wall look deep. Stand still in front of one of these rooms, and let your eyes adjust. The columns seem to step forward. The painted spaces behind them seem to open up.

The painters also loved small, precise details. Search the walls for birds — peacocks with spread tails, and smaller birds perched among the painted architecture. Look for theatre masks resting on ledges. In one well-known panel, there is a transparent glass bowl filled with fruit. The artist painted the glass so that you can see the fruit through it. In another, there is a basket of figs. These are still lifes, painted nearly two thousand years ago.

Walk on toward the open spaces. Roman villas like this one were built around gardens, and Oplontis had several. Excavators found the cavities left by ancient tree roots in the volcanic material. By filling those cavities, they could identify where trees once stood. Some of the gardens have been replanted based on this evidence. So the green around you is not random decoration. It follows the plan of a Roman garden that existed here before the eruption.

The second surprise is the swimming pool. On the eastern side of the villa lies a very long outdoor pool, lined with stone and bordered by gardens. It is far larger than you might expect from a private home. Sculptures were found in this area, and they once stood along the water. Imagine the scene: still water, white marble figures, trees moving in the sea breeze, and the bay beyond. This was a house built for leisure and display.

There is also a second building nearby, known as Villa B. It was very different from this luxury residence. It seems to have been a commercial site, perhaps for storing and trading goods such as wine. Many amphorae were found there. In Villa B, archaeologists did find human remains — people who sought shelter during the eruption and did not survive. It is a quiet reminder that Oplontis was a working landscape, not only a place of pleasure.

Together with Pompeii and Herculaneum, Oplontis is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 1997. Yet it receives far fewer visitors than its famous neighbours. That is part of its value. Here you can often walk through painted rooms with almost no one else around. You can hear your own footsteps on the floors. You can take your time. The town above — later known for its pasta makers — grew up without knowing what lay beneath it.

Before you leave, stand in one of the painted rooms once more. Look at a single detail: a bird, a mask, a piece of painted fruit. Someone mixed that colour, held a brush, and worked on this wall for a family they may never have met. Then climb back toward the street. As the sounds of Torre Annunziata return, remember that the old coastline, the gardens, and the long pool are still here — just below the modern town.

Practical tips

Walk from the Torre Annunziata Circumvesuviana station.
Visit after Pompeii to compare painted wall styles.
Bring water and sun protection for the garden areas.
Pause in each room to let painted depth appear.
Combine with Pompeii using a shared archaeological pass if available.
