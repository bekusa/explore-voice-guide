The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the old stones and listen. Below you, the River Monnow runs shallow and quick over its gravel bed. Above you, a heavy gate tower blocks the sky, its walls of rough red sandstone softened by moss and weather. The road surface is smooth under your feet now, but this narrow crossing has carried feet, hooves and cartwheels for more than seven hundred years. Welcome to Monnow Bridge, on the western edge of Monmouth.

Here is the first surprise. Medieval Europe once had many bridges with gate towers built directly on them. In Britain, almost all of them have gone, pulled down to widen roads or lost to floods. This is the only fortified river bridge in Britain where the gate tower still stands on the bridge itself. So the structure in front of you is not just old. It is the last survivor of a whole type of building on this island.

The stone bridge dates from the late thirteenth century. It was not the first crossing on this spot. When archaeologists studied the riverbed, they found the remains of an earlier timber bridge, dated to the twelfth century. So people have crossed the Monnow at this exact point for a very long time. The river decided the route, and the town followed. Monmouth sits where two rivers meet, the Monnow and the Wye, and bridges were always its lifelines.

Look down at the arches beneath you. There are three of them, built of the same local red sandstone as the tower. Their undersides carry stone ribs, a technique that let medieval masons build strong arches with less material. The piers that hold them up stand in the current, shaped to split the water. In flood, the Monnow rises fast and brown, and these piers have taken that force season after season, century after century.

The gate tower came a little later than the bridge. Its job was simple. Control who entered the town, and collect money from those who did. Monmouth had a castle and a market, and the town held royal rights to raise taxes on goods to pay for its walls. A gate on the bridge meant that every trader coming in from the west had to pass through here, and pay.

It was also a defence, though perhaps a more symbolic one than you might think. Historians debate how useful the tower was in a real attack. It is fairly small, and an army could cross the river elsewhere. But a gate sends a message. It says that this town is organised, protected, and in control of its own doorway. For a medieval traveller arriving on foot, that message would have been very clear.

Now look up at the face of the tower above the main archway. You will see openings near the top, where the wall projects outward. These are machicolations. In simple terms, they are holes in the floor of an overhanging gallery. Defenders could drop stones or other unpleasant things onto anyone standing at the gate below. Stand directly under them for a moment, and imagine looking up at a face looking down.

The tower did not stay a fortress. Over the centuries it found other uses. At various times it served as a lock-up for prisoners, as a watch house, and as a dwelling. The rooms above the gateway were altered, and the roofline changed. What you see today is a medieval core with later layers added on top. That mix is part of its honesty. It is a working building that people kept adapting, not a museum piece frozen in one moment.

Here is the second surprise. Notice the smaller arches cut through the tower beside the main roadway. These are not medieval. They were added in the nineteenth century to give pedestrians a safe way through, as cart and coach traffic grew heavier. For most of the twentieth century, cars and lorries squeezed through the old gate too. Only in the early 2000s did a new road bridge open nearby, and this crossing was finally given back to people on foot.

Because of that change, you can now do something earlier visitors could not. You can stop in the middle, lean on the parapet, and simply watch the river. Look upstream and downstream. The water is often clear enough to see stones on the bottom. Swans and ducks gather on the quieter stretches. In summer, the banks turn thick and green, and the light comes through the willows in moving patches.

Turn west, away from the town centre, and you are looking into Overmonnow, the old suburb on the far bank. Close by stands the small church of Saint Thomas the Martyr, one of the oldest buildings in Monmouth, with early Norman work inside. The Normans were the French-speaking rulers who took control of England in the eleventh century. Bridge and church together mark this side of the river as a very old part of the town.

Turn east, and Monnow Street climbs gently towards the heart of Monmouth. This long, wide street was the town's main approach. At the top lies Agincourt Square and the remains of Monmouth Castle, where King Henry the Fifth was born in the late fourteenth century. The bridge was his town's western door. Walking up from here follows the same line that merchants, soldiers and pilgrims took for centuries.

Before you move on, step back to the riverbank if you can and look at the bridge from the side. From there you see the full shape, the low arches, the tower sitting squarely on top, the way the whole structure seems to grow out of the water. Then walk through the gate one more time. Feel the cool shade inside the arch, hear your footsteps echo off the stone, and notice the river still running underneath, exactly as it did when the first masons set these blocks in place.

What to look for

Machicolation openings projecting above the main gate arch.
Stone ribs on the underside of the bridge arches.
Nineteenth-century pedestrian arches beside the main roadway.
Pointed cutwater piers splitting the river current.
Weathered red sandstone blocks patched with later repairs.
Before you go

Practical tips

View the full profile from the riverbank beside the bridge.
Walk up Monnow Street afterwards towards Agincourt Square.
Visit the nearby Church of Saint Thomas the Martyr.
Come early morning for quiet, still reflections on the water.
Wear grippy shoes; riverbank paths can be muddy after rain.
