The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your feet. It rises gently here, a low hill of grass and old stone above the rooftops of the town. The walls around you are warm in colour, a soft reddish sandstone worn smooth by rain and wind. Below, out of sight but close, the River Monnow runs towards its meeting with the Wye. Listen, and you may hear the town carrying on below you, quite unbothered by the ruins at its back.

This is Monmouth Castle. It is not a large ruin, and it does not try to impress you with height or size. What it offers instead is depth. On this small hill, a royal story began, a war left its mark, and a later family built something entirely new from the wreckage. Everything is close together here. In a few steps you can move across several centuries.

The story starts soon after 1066, when the Normans arrived in England. William the Conqueror needed loyal men to hold the borderlands with Wales, and one of them was William FitzOsbern. He was among the most trusted of the Conqueror's companions. FitzOsbern built a chain of castles along this frontier, and Monmouth was one of them. The site made sense. Two rivers protected it, and the hill gave a clear view over the routes into Wales.

The first castle was almost certainly made of earth and timber, as most early Norman castles were. It was quick to raise and easy to defend. Over the following century, stone slowly replaced wood. The tall ruined block in front of you is the Great Tower, built in the twelfth century. Look at its walls and you can see how thick they are. These were meant to last, and in part, they have.

Now here is the surprise that many visitors miss when they walk past these quiet stones. A King of England was born here. In the late fourteenth century, a boy named Henry was born at Monmouth Castle. Nobody expected him to wear a crown. His father, Henry Bolingbroke, was not yet king. But history turned, his father took the throne, and the boy from Monmouth grew up to become King Henry the Fifth.

Henry the Fifth is the king of Agincourt, the famous battle in France in 1415, where his outnumbered army won a victory that English and Welsh storytellers have retold ever since. Shakespeare made him the hero of a play. Inside the play, Henry is even called Harry of Monmouth. So this modest ruin on the Welsh border is tied to one of the best-known kings in the English story. Monmouth has never forgotten it.

How did a castle here come to belong to such a family? Over time, Monmouth passed into the hands of the House of Lancaster, one of the most powerful noble families in medieval England. It became one of their estates, and they added to it. The Great Hall, whose remains stand beside the tower, belonged to this later medieval phase. Picture it with a roof, a fire, long tables, and servants moving between them. Today it is open to the sky.

The castle's real decline came much later, in the seventeenth century, during the English Civil War. That was a long struggle between King Charles the First and Parliament, and it reached deep into Wales and the borders. Monmouth changed hands more than once. After Parliament's forces took the town for the final time, the castle was deliberately damaged, a practice known as slighting. The aim was simple. No army should ever be able to use it again.

Tradition in the town tells a vivid story about that moment. Locals say the Great Tower was so weakened that part of it collapsed one night, and the noise woke the people sleeping below. It is a memorable tale, so treat it as local memory rather than a firm record. Either way, the jagged edges you see today show what happened. Someone set out to break these walls, and they largely succeeded.

Now turn around, because the second surprise stands right beside the ruins. That handsome, symmetrical building is Great Castle House. It was built in the 1670s by Henry Somerset, the Marquess of Worcester, whose family later became the Dukes of Beaufort. And here is the detail worth knowing. Much of its stone is believed to have come from the ruined castle itself. The old fortress was quietly recycled into a gentleman's residence.

The contrast is striking when you stand between the two. On one side, rough medieval walls built for war. On the other, a calm seventeenth-century front with regular windows and careful proportions, built for comfort and status. Great Castle House is still in use. It is the home of the Royal Monmouthshire Royal Engineers, a regiment with very long roots, and it holds a small regimental museum. Few castle sites in Britain still have soldiers living on them.

When you leave, the town itself carries the castle's story outward. Walk down to Agincourt Square, the heart of Monmouth, and look up at the old Shire Hall. A statue of Henry the Fifth stands there, looking out over the square. Further on, by the river, you can find the Monnow Bridge with its fortified medieval gate, a rare surviving example of its kind in Britain. Together they show how this border town was shaped by defence.

Before you go, take one more look at the Great Tower. Notice how the light catches the red stone, and how grass grows right up to its base. Think of the Norman builders, the Lancastrian lords, the child who became a king, and the soldiers who broke it apart. Then listen again to the ordinary sounds of the town below. The castle is quiet now, but it is still here, keeping its place on the hill.
