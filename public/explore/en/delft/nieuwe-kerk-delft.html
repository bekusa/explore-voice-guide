The guide

The story

Stand on the Markt for a moment and listen. Bicycle bells, the scrape of café chairs on brick, and then, above it all, a run of bells falling from the sky. That sound comes from the tower in front of you. It rises from the eastern end of the square, pale stone climbing in narrowing stages until it ends in a thin spire. At the other end of the square stands the City Hall. Between them lies the heart of Delft.

This is the Nieuwe Kerk, which means the New Church. The name is a little misleading. Work began here in the late fourteenth century, and the building took roughly a hundred years to finish. It is called new only because Delft already had an older church, the Oude Kerk, a short walk away along the canals. In a city this old, new is a relative word. The church was dedicated to Saint Ursula, a martyr popular in medieval northern Europe.

The style is Gothic. In simple terms, that means pointed arches, tall windows, and a structure that pushes upward instead of spreading out. Look at the walls and you will see brick and stone working together. Stone was expensive in the Low Countries, because the land here has almost none. Builders often saved it for the places that mattered most, like window frames, buttresses and the upper tower. Brick did the rest.

Now look up at that tower again. It is one of the tallest church towers in the Netherlands, second only to the cathedral tower in Utrecht. Here is something many visitors miss. The very top is not medieval. In the late nineteenth century, lightning struck the spire. The architect Pierre Cuypers, the same man behind the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam, led the restoration. So when you look at the tip, you are looking at a Victorian-era repair on a medieval body.

The church has survived more than lightning. In 1536, a great fire swept through Delft and badly damaged the building. Then, in 1654, a gunpowder store in the city exploded. People remember that disaster as the Delft Thunderclap. Whole streets were flattened, and windows across the city were blown out, including windows here. The church you see today carries the marks of those repairs, layer upon layer, from different centuries.

Step inside, and the noise of the square drops away. The space feels light and open. Like many Dutch churches, this one became Protestant during the Reformation in the sixteenth century. The new faith removed most of the painted saints and decoration. What remains is white walls, clear lines and tall windows. Many of the stained-glass windows you see now were added in the twentieth century, so their colours belong to a modern hand.

But the real reason people come is at the far end, in the choir. There you will find a large monument in black and white marble, with bronze figures. This is the tomb of William of Orange, often called William the Silent. He led the Dutch revolt against Spanish rule in the sixteenth century. Many Dutch people still think of him as the father of their nation. The national anthem, the Wilhelmus, is named after him.

William did not choose Delft by accident. He lived here, in a former convent now known as the Prinsenhof, just across the canals. In 1584, an assassin shot him there. It was one of the first political murders of a head of state with a handgun. You can still visit the Prinsenhof today. Some visitors walk there after this church, to see the place where the story ended before coming back to where it rests.

The tomb itself was designed by the sculptor Hendrick de Keyser in the early seventeenth century, and finished by his son. Walk slowly around it. William appears more than once. He sits in armour, and he also lies at rest. At his feet, look for a small dog. According to a popular tale, a little dog once woke William and saved him from a surprise attack. Legend says the dog refused to eat after his death. It is a story, not a record, but it has followed the tomb for centuries.

Beneath the floor lies a second surprise. Below this monument is the royal crypt of the House of Orange-Nassau, the family of the Dutch kings and queens. For centuries, members of the royal family have been buried here, including queens of the twentieth century. The crypt is not open to the public. It is opened only for royal funerals. So as you stand by the tomb, you are standing above a royal burial place that almost no one ever sees.

This church holds another famous name. Near the choir, look for the memorial to Hugo Grotius, a seventeenth-century scholar from Delft. Grotius wrote some of the first serious works on international law, the idea that nations should follow shared rules even in war. Outside on the Markt, a statue of him stands in front of the church. And one more connection: the painter Johannes Vermeer was baptised in this church in 1632. He is buried, however, in the Oude Kerk.

If your legs are ready, the tower is open for climbing. The stairs are narrow and spiral, and there are several hundred steps. As you climb, you pass close to the carillon. A carillon is a set of tuned bells played from a keyboard. Part of this one was cast by the Hemony brothers, famous bell makers of the seventeenth century. From the top, Delft lays itself out: red roofs, green canals, and on a clear day, the towers of The Hague and Rotterdam.

Back on the ground, take a last look along the nave. Notice how quiet it feels compared to the square outside. Generations of Delft families have walked these stones. Many of the floor slabs are old gravestones, worn smooth by shoes. Some carved names and dates have almost disappeared. It is easy to walk over them without noticing. Look down for a moment and you will see how much of the past here is literally underfoot.

When you step back onto the Markt, turn around once more. Let your eyes travel from the church door, up the stone stages of the tower, to the thin spire. Then look across the square to the City Hall, and to Grotius on his pedestal. Church, government and scholarship, facing each other across one open space. Listen for the bells again. They have been marking time over this square for centuries, and they will mark yours as you walk away.
