The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the trees, birds call from branches heavy with leaves. Somewhere nearby, a group of pilgrims chants softly in Pali, the old language of the Buddhist scriptures. The ground underfoot is packed earth and old brick, worn smooth and warm in the sun. Low walls of thin red bricks rise just a little above the grass, like the outline of a city drawn on the land. This is Shravasti. For many centuries, it was one of the great cities of ancient India.

Around two and a half thousand years ago, Shravasti was the capital of Kosala. Kosala was one of the most powerful kingdoms of the northern Gangetic plain. Merchants travelled here on trade routes, and caravans crossed the region. Wandering teachers came to speak in the groves outside the walls. The river that ran past the city appears in old texts as the Achiravati. Today it is known as the Rapti. The modern landscape is quiet farmland, but the city around you was once crowded with people.

Shravasti matters most because of one visitor. Buddhist tradition holds that the Buddha spent more rainy seasons here than anywhere else. The rainy season was a time when wandering monks stopped travelling and stayed in one place. For the Buddha, that place was often Shravasti. Many of his recorded teachings begin with the same short phrase: he was staying at Shravasti, in the Jeta Grove. So many sermons are set here that the name repeats again and again through the scriptures.

The Jeta Grove is the site most visitors come to see. Today it is known locally as Saheth. Its story begins with a rich merchant called Sudatta. People remembered him as Anathapindika, which means "the one who feeds the helpless". He wanted to give the Buddha a quiet place to stay. He chose a park owned by Prince Jeta. The prince did not want to sell it, and he named a price as a joke.

According to the well-known tale, the prince said the price was enough gold coins to cover the whole ground. The merchant did not laugh. He brought carts of gold and began to lay coins across the earth. The prince was moved by this, and he gave the last piece of land himself. That is why the grove carries his name. Ancient carvings at other Buddhist sites show this scene, with carts and coins spread across the ground.

As you walk through Saheth, you move between the foundations of monasteries, shrines and small stupas. A stupa is a rounded mound built to hold relics or to mark a holy place. Most of the structures you see are from later centuries, not from the Buddha's own lifetime. Monks rebuilt and expanded the site again and again for more than a thousand years. Look closely and you can often see one layer of brick sitting on top of an older one.

Two spots here draw the deepest attention. One is the Gandhakuti, which means the "fragrant hut". Tradition identifies it as the place where the Buddha himself stayed. Pilgrims often cover its brick base with gold leaf, flowers and candles. Nearby is another shrine, often called the Kosambakuti. Here you may see monks in saffron, maroon or grey robes, sitting in silent meditation. Their robe colours often show the country they come from.

Now for something many visitors miss. Near the entrance stands a large, old tree, wrapped in prayer flags. This is the Ananda Bodhi tree. Tradition says that the Buddha's close disciple, Ananda, planted it from a seed or sapling of the tree in Bodh Gaya. Bodh Gaya is where the Buddha reached enlightenment. The tree you see today is not the original one. Still, pilgrims treat it as a living link to that first moment, and many sit under it to pray.

A short distance away lies Maheth, the ruins of the old walled city itself. Here the earth rises in long ramparts that once protected Shravasti. Inside, two large brick mounds stand out. They are known as Pakki Kuti and Kachchi Kuti. Local tradition links them with stupas for Angulimala and for the generous merchant Sudatta. Scholars are careful about these identifications, so treat them as tradition rather than certainty. Still, standing on top of them, the scale of the old city becomes clear.

Angulimala's story is one of the most dramatic in Buddhist literature. The texts describe him as a feared bandit in the Kosala forests. His name means "garland of fingers", from a grim necklace he was said to wear. According to the story, he chased the Buddha but could not catch him, even though the Buddha walked calmly. The Buddha's words changed him, and he became a monk. Buddhists still tell this tale as proof that anyone can change.

Shravasti is also holy to another faith. Jains believe this is the birthplace of Sambhavanath, the third of their twenty-four Tirthankaras. A Tirthankara is a spiritual teacher who shows the path to liberation. In Maheth you can find the remains of an old Jain temple, and there are newer Jain shrines nearby. Buddhist tradition also places a famous event here, called the Twin Miracle. In that story, the Buddha produced fire and water from his body at the same time.

For centuries after its decline, Shravasti was largely forgotten. Two Chinese monks, Faxian and Xuanzang, visited the region in the early centuries of the first millennium. They wrote that much of the city was already in ruins. Their travel accounts later helped scholars find the site again. In the nineteenth century, the archaeologist Alexander Cunningham linked these mounds with ancient Shravasti. Later digs uncovered inscriptions and objects that supported his identification.

Today, a new Shravasti is growing around the old one. Monasteries and temples built by Buddhist communities from Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Korea, Japan and other countries line the roads. Each one follows its own country's style, with curved roofs, golden spires or white domes. Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the old bricks. Feel the shade, hear the birds and the quiet chanting. For many centuries, people have come here simply to sit and listen. You are now part of that long line.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Saheth in the early morning for cooler air.
Remove shoes before approaching the Gandhakuti shrine.
Walk to Maheth separately to see the old city ramparts.
Carry water and a hat; there is little shade.
Keep your voice low near monks in meditation.
