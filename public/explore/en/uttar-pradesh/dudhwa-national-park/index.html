The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest reaches you through sound. A peacock calls somewhere behind the trees. Dry sal leaves crackle under a jeep tyre. Then comes the long hush of tall grass moving in the wind. The air smells of damp earth and woodsmoke from distant villages. This is Dudhwa National Park, in the far north of Uttar Pradesh, close to the border with Nepal. Here India's great plains begin to rise toward the Himalaya.

The landscape around you has a name, the terai. It is a band of low, wet land at the foot of the mountains. Rivers come down from the hills, slow down, spread out, and soak the ground. Long ago the terai stretched for hundreds of kilometres across northern India. Most of it has since become farmland, towns and sugarcane fields. Dudhwa protects one of the last large pieces that still looks the way the whole region once did.

Look at the trees nearest the track. Most of them are sal. The trunks are tall and straight, with rough grey bark. In spring the old leaves fall and new ones come in bright green. Sal timber is heavy and hard. For generations it was cut for railway sleepers and buildings. Forests like this one were managed for their wood long before anyone thought of them as wildlife habitat. Today these same trees give shade to tigers, deer and hundreds of kinds of birds.

Between the forest blocks, the land opens into grassland. Some grasses here grow taller than a person, and taller than a jeep. That height matters. A whole elephant can stand inside this grass and stay almost invisible. Rangers and researchers often say the grassland is the true heart of Dudhwa. Many of the rarest animals in the park depend on these open, flooded meadows, not on the forest itself. So when the grass looks empty, keep watching. It rarely is.

The animal most closely tied to this place is the swamp deer, known in India as the barasingha. The name roughly means twelve-tined, after the many points on a stag's antlers. Swamp deer need soft, wet ground and tall grass. As the terai shrank, so did their numbers. Protecting them was one of the early reasons this land was set aside as a sanctuary. Dudhwa was later declared a national park in 1977, and today herds still gather near marshes and lakes.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Dudhwa has one-horned rhinoceroses. Rhinos once lived across the terai, but they disappeared from this part of India long ago. In the 1980s, conservationists brought rhinos back. Animals were moved here from Assam and from Nepal and released inside a fenced, protected area. It was one of India's first attempts to return rhinos to a former home. The population has grown since then, slowly and with care.

Then there is the tiger. Dudhwa became a tiger reserve under India's Project Tiger in the late 1980s. The reserve now links this park with other protected forests nearby, including the Kishanpur and Katarniaghat sanctuaries. Tigers here move through dense grass and thick forest, so sightings are never guaranteed. Many visitors leave without seeing one. But you may still find pugmarks pressed into the soft mud of the track. Those footprints are a quiet reminder that tigers walk these same paths.

The story of this forest is closely linked to one man, Billy Arjan Singh. He was a hunter who became one of India's best-known conservationists. He lived on a farm at the edge of the forest, a place he called Tiger Haven. He raised leopards there, and also a tigress called Tara, and he tried to return them to the wild. His methods caused real debate among scientists. Still, his long campaigns helped push for the protection of Dudhwa as a national park.

This forest is not only about large animals. Birdwatchers come here for the Bengal florican, a rare bustard of the grasslands. In the breeding season, the male jumps up above the grass in a short display flight, then drops out of sight again. Look also for storks wading in the shallow lakes, and eagles on high branches. Kingfishers flash blue over the water. Some mornings the bird calls are so many and so layered that it is hard to pick out a single voice.

The people who live around the park are part of this landscape too. Many villages nearby belong to the Tharu community. The Tharu have lived in the terai for a very long time, on both the Indian and Nepali sides of the border. Their houses, their craftwork and their knowledge of the forest grew out of this wet, fertile ground. Some local guides come from these villages. If you get a chance to talk with them, ask what the forest looked like when they were children.

Life in the park follows the seasons. Winter mornings are cold and often wrapped in thick fog. The fog lifts slowly from the grass and leaves dew on every blade. As spring turns into summer, the heat builds and the grass dries. Animals then gather closer to water, which can make them easier to see. When the monsoon arrives, rivers rise and much of the ground floods. Tracks become impassable, and the forest closes itself off for a time.

Out on safari, patience is your best skill. Drivers and guides read the forest closely. They look for fresh tracks and fresh dung, and they listen hard. An alarm call from a spotted deer or a langur monkey can mean a predator is moving nearby. When the jeep stops and the engine goes quiet, stay still and listen with them. Many of the best moments in Dudhwa come from simply waiting, and letting the forest come to you.

Now, wherever you are standing, take one more look around. Notice the light coming through the sal leaves, and the colour of the grass. Notice the smell of the soil. This ground has flooded and dried, again and again, for thousands of years. Animals that vanished from much of India still live here. Some of them were brought back by human hands. As you leave, listen once more for that peacock... and for the soft sound of the grass moving behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book an early morning safari for cooler air and activity.
Carry warm layers on winter mornings when fog settles.
Bring binoculars for grassland birds and distant deer.
Wear muted greens and browns, avoiding bright clothing.
Stay quiet when the jeep stops to hear alarm calls.
