The guide

The story

Listen first to the water. The River Avon moves slowly here, brown and green, and the swans drift close to the bank in hope of bread. Across the grass rises a long wall of warm, rust-coloured brick. On a damp morning the brick looks almost red. In evening light it turns to soft orange. This is the Royal Shakespeare Theatre, and it sits so close to the river that its terraces seem to lean out over the water.

Before we look at the history, it helps to know one simple thing. This is not a museum. It is a working theatre, and it is the main home of the Royal Shakespeare Company. Actors rehearse here, sets are built here, and audiences arrive here almost every week of the year. The building you are looking at has a front of house, a backstage, and a heartbeat. It has been rebuilt and reshaped several times, and each layer tells part of the story.

That story begins in the Victorian era. In the town where Shakespeare was born, there was no permanent theatre for his plays. A local brewer, Charles Edward Flower, gave land on this riverbank and backed a campaign to change that. The first Shakespeare Memorial Theatre opened here in 1879. It was a fanciful building, with turrets and gables in a Victorian Gothic style. Old photographs show something closer to a small castle than a modern playhouse.

Then, in 1926, fire struck. The Victorian theatre burned, and much of it was lost. Here is the first detail most visitors miss. Not everything was destroyed. Part of the old structure survived the flames, and that surviving shell still stands today, joined to the newer building. If you walk around toward the far end, you can still find the older, more decorative brickwork of the nineteenth century. It sits quietly beside the plainer lines of the twentieth.

After the fire, an architectural competition was held to design a replacement. The winner was a young English architect named Elisabeth Scott. Her design opened in 1932. It is widely described as the first major public building in Britain designed by a woman. Think about that for a moment. In an age when very few women worked as architects, the national theatre of Shakespeare's hometown came from her drawing board. Many visitors walk past without ever hearing her name.

Scott's building was modern for its time. It belongs to the Art Deco period, a style of the 1920s and 1930s known for clean lines, bold shapes and geometric decoration. She chose brick instead of carved stone, and long, simple walls instead of turrets. Not everyone liked it. Locals say the building was once mocked as a jam factory, because the blocky brick reminded people of an industrial works. Today, those same walls are admired for their calm, honest strength.

Look closely at the facade, and you may notice sculpted panels set into the brickwork. These were carved by the sculptor Eric Kennington. Instead of grand statues, he cut figures and symbols directly into the brick surface. Up close, you can see the texture of individual bricks inside the shapes. It is a quiet kind of decoration. It rewards a slow walk along the front of the building, especially when low sunlight throws shadows across the carved surfaces.

In 1961, the company that performed here took a new name. It became the Royal Shakespeare Company, and the building became the Royal Shakespeare Theatre. This was more than a change of sign. The company grew into one of the best-known theatre ensembles in the world. Actors who later became famous on stage and screen trained and performed on these boards. For many of them, Stratford was where they learned to speak Shakespeare's verse to a living audience.

The second surprise is inside. Between 2007 and 2010, the theatre went through a major transformation. The old auditorium had a traditional stage behind a frame, with the audience sitting far away in rows. The new design created a thrust stage. That means the stage pushes forward into the audience, and people sit on three sides of it. This is much closer to how Shakespeare's own plays were performed in the open theatres of his time. Actors and audience now share the same space.

Remember that surviving Victorian shell from the fire? It found a new life in 1986 as the Swan Theatre. The Swan is a smaller, more intimate space, built largely in warm timber inside. It often hosts plays by Shakespeare's contemporaries, as well as newer work. From outside, you can see how the old and new parts meet. The change in brick colour and style marks the line between the centuries. It is like reading the building's history in its walls.

The transformation also added a tall brick tower. It rises above the riverbank and gives the building a new landmark. Visitors can usually go up to a viewing level near the top. From there, the whole town opens out below you. You can follow the river as it bends, pick out the old rooftops, and see the green fields of Warwickshire beyond. On a clear day, the view helps you understand how small and close-knit this market town really is.

Outside, the theatre is surrounded by Bancroft Gardens, a wide green space along the river. Families sit on the grass, and narrowboats tie up along the canal basin nearby. In the gardens stands the Gower Memorial, a statue of Shakespeare seated high above the path. Around him are figures from his plays, including Hamlet, Lady Macbeth, Falstaff and Prince Hal. Children often climb the steps to look up at them. It is a good place to pause before or after a performance.

If you have time, follow the riverside path south from the theatre. The walk takes you along the water, under trees, toward Holy Trinity Church. This is where Shakespeare was baptised, and where he is buried. So in one gentle walk, you can move from the stage where his plays are performed today to the quiet place where he lies. It is a short distance, but it connects the whole arc of his life and legacy.

Now turn back toward the theatre once more. Notice the brick, the tower, and the terraces above the water. Listen to the low voices of people waiting for the evening show, and the soft splash of oars on the river. This building has burned, been rebuilt, and been reshaped. Yet its purpose has stayed the same. Somewhere inside, right now, someone may be rehearsing a line written over four hundred years ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book performance tickets well ahead, especially for weekend shows.
Ask at the box office about access to the tower.
Walk the riverside path south to Holy Trinity Church.
Arrive early to picnic in Bancroft Gardens before a show.
Visit the terraces at sunset for warm light on brick.
