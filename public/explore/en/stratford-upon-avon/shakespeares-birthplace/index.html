The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Shoes scuff on worn flagstones. A floorboard creaks somewhere above your head. The air inside smells faintly of wood smoke and old timber. Outside, Henley Street carries on with its shoppers and coffee cups, but in here the light falls low and amber through small leaded windows. You are standing in the house where, by long tradition, William Shakespeare was born.

From the street, the building looks almost modest. Dark oak beams frame panels of pale plaster in a timber-framed pattern. The roof sits steep and the windows are small. In the sixteenth century, this was a prosperous family home on one of the busier streets of a market town. It was not a palace. It was the house of a successful tradesman, and that matters to the story.

That tradesman was John Shakespeare, William's father. He worked as a glover, cutting and stitching soft leather into gloves, and he also dealt in wool. He rose in local life, too. He served on the town council and, for a time, held the office of bailiff, which was close to the role of mayor. So the boy who grew up here was the son of a respected local man.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Nobody wrote down Shakespeare's exact birthday. What survives is a baptism record from Holy Trinity Church, dated 26 April 1564. Babies were usually baptised a few days after birth, so tradition settled on 23 April as his birthday. It is a neat date. It is also the date on which he died, fifty-two years later, in 1616.

As you move through the rooms, notice how the house is furnished. The rooms are set up as they might have looked in John Shakespeare's time, with painted cloths, simple wooden furniture, and a large hearth. One ground-floor room is laid out as a glover's workshop. Look at the tools and the leather. This was where the family money came from, and it was close enough to smell from the living rooms.

William grew up here with brothers and sisters. He likely went to the grammar school in town, where boys studied Latin for long hours. At eighteen, in 1582, he married Anne Hathaway, a woman from the nearby village of Shottery. Their first child, Susanna, was followed by twins, Hamnet and Judith. Hamnet died as a boy. Some writers link that loss to the grief in his later plays, but that is interpretation, not record.

Now climb the stairs. The upper floor holds the room traditionally called the birth room. It is a plain space with a low ceiling and a wide timber floor. Pause here a moment. For more than two centuries, this one room has drawn writers, actors, students, and pilgrims from every corner of the world. Many of them came simply to stand where you are standing now.

Some of those visitors left more than footprints. Look at the window in this part of the house. Its old glass is covered in names, scratched into the panes with diamond rings and other sharp points. It was a fashion among nineteenth-century visitors to sign the glass. Among the scratched names, it is said, is the novelist Sir Walter Scott. Today, of course, the glass is protected, and nobody adds new signatures.

The house did not always look like a shrine. After Shakespeare's death, it passed down through the family. His sister Joan lived in part of it, and her descendants kept a connection for generations. Meanwhile, part of the building became an inn. For years, people ate, drank, and slept under this roof with little ceremony. The idea of a great national poet's home took time to grow.

By the nineteenth century, that idea had grown strong. In 1847, the house came up for sale, and there were fears it could be bought and shipped away. A public campaign raised money to buy it for the nation. The novelist Charles Dickens was among the writers who supported the effort. Soon after, the house was restored, and the buildings that pressed against it were cleared away to let it stand alone.

That restoration is the second surprise. Much of what you see is genuinely old timber, but the Victorians also rebuilt and tidied parts of the house to match their picture of Shakespeare's world. So the building is two stories at once. It is a sixteenth-century home, and it is also a nineteenth-century idea of what that home should be. Look closely and you can enjoy both.

When you step out into the garden, the mood changes. Paths run between beds of flowers and herbs, many of them plants named in the plays. You may hear voices here, too. Actors often perform short scenes and speeches in the garden, and you can sometimes call out a request. Hearing a speech from Hamlet or Romeo and Juliet in the open air is a good way to feel the words land.

The house today is cared for by the Shakespeare Birthplace Trust, which also looks after other family properties in and around the town. From here, it is a short walk down to the River Avon and to Holy Trinity Church, where Shakespeare is buried. Walking that route links the start of his life with its end, in a little more than a kilometre.

Before you leave, stand on Henley Street and look back at the house. See the dark beams, the uneven windows, the roofline against the sky. A glover's son once walked out of that door into this same street. He went on to write plays that people still perform every day, in dozens of languages. As you walk on, listen to the street around you... and notice how ordinary it all is. That is where he began.

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven floors and narrow old stairs.
