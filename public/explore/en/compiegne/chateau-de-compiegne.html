The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your feet. It crunches in a slow, even rhythm, the same sound carriage wheels made on this ground centuries ago. Ahead of you, a long pale façade of cut stone runs almost the full width of your view. Its tall windows sit in strict rows, and the roofline stays low and flat against the sky. There are no towers here and no turrets. The building looks calm and orderly, and it has a quiet confidence.

This is the Château de Compiègne, a royal and imperial palace in the north of France, about an hour from Paris. French kings came here long before the present building existed. They came mainly for one reason: the forest. The vast woodland of Compiègne starts right at the edge of the town, and for centuries it was one of the great royal hunting grounds of France. The palace you see now grew out of that passion for the hunt.

The older residence on this site was irregular and cramped. In the mid eighteenth century, King Louis the Fifteenth decided to rebuild it almost entirely. He gave the work to Ange-Jacques Gabriel, his chief architect. Gabriel is the same man behind the Place de la Concorde in Paris and the Petit Trianon at Versailles. Here he worked in a restrained neoclassical style, which means clean lines and proportions inspired by ancient Greece and Rome, with very little decoration.

Now for the first detail that many visitors miss. Look at the shape of the main courtyard on the town side. The land here was awkward, and the old town walls limited the space. So Gabriel designed the palace around a roughly triangular plan. It is unusual for a royal residence. Most French palaces follow a neat rectangle, but this one had to adapt to the ground it stood on.

Gabriel did not live to see the project finished. Other architects carried his plans forward, and the work continued into the reign of Louis the Sixteenth. That king and his queen, Marie Antoinette, knew Compiègne well. Then the Revolution came. The royal family lost their power, the furniture was dispersed, and for a time the building had very little purpose. Grand rooms that were built for courtiers stood quiet and stripped.

Napoleon brought the palace back to life. In the early nineteenth century he restored it as an imperial residence and refurnished it in the fashionable Empire style. That style means mahogany and gilded bronze, eagles and laurel wreaths, and strong, deep colours. Much of what you see inside today comes from this period. Walk through the apartments and you will notice how the decoration feels heavier and more ceremonial than Gabriel's sober exterior.

Compiègne also has a love story, or at least a story about a very eager groom. In 1810 Napoleon married Marie-Louise, the daughter of the Austrian emperor. Their first meeting took place near here. Accounts from the time describe an impatient Napoleon who rode out on the road to meet her carriage early, instead of waiting for the planned ceremony. To make his young bride feel at home, he had the gardens changed to remind her of Schönbrunn, the imperial palace in Vienna.

You can still feel that gesture in the park. Behind the palace, a long covered walkway of iron trellis stretches away beneath climbing plants. It was designed so that the empress could walk in the shade, out of the sun and rain. On a summer afternoon the light through the leaves turns soft and green. Beyond the garden, a straight avenue cuts deep into the forest toward the hills. Napoleon opened that view as well.

The palace had its most sociable era later in the nineteenth century, under Napoleon the Third and Empress Eugénie. Each autumn they invited groups of guests to stay for about a week at a time. These gatherings were called the séries. Politicians, artists, scientists and aristocrats mixed together. By day they hunted in the forest. In the evenings there were games, theatre and long dinners. An invitation to Compiègne was a clear sign of status in the Second Empire.

That is why the palace today houses a museum devoted to the Second Empire, the period of Napoleon the Third's reign from the 1850s to 1870. You will find paintings, furniture and portraits that show the taste of the time. It was an era of rich fabrics, gilded frames and a love of comfort. If the earlier Empire rooms feel like a stage for power, these rooms feel more like a stage for society.

Here is the second surprise. Tucked inside this palace of kings and emperors is the national museum of carriages and early automobiles. You can see ceremonial coaches, travelling carriages and some of the first motor cars ever built in France. It makes sense in a quiet way. Compiègne has always been about arrival and departure, about horses, wheels and the road from Paris. The collection tells the story of how people moved, from horse power to the engine.

The town around you carries its own deep history. In 1430, Joan of Arc was captured during fighting outside Compiègne, an event that led to her trial and death. And in the forest nearby, in a clearing called Rethondes, the armistice that ended the First World War was signed in a railway carriage in 1918. Neither event took place inside this palace, but both belong to the same landscape that drew the kings here in the first place.

As you walk on, take a moment to look back at the long façade. Notice how the stone changes colour as the light moves across it, from cool grey in the shade to warm cream in the sun. Listen for the birds in the park and the faint sound of traffic from the town. Kings planned hunts here. An emperor waited impatiently for his bride. Guests laughed through long autumn evenings. Now the building simply holds still, and it lets you take your time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow extra time for the separate car and carriage museum.
Walk the park behind the palace after touring inside.
Combine your visit with the armistice site in the forest.
Visit in autumn to see the forest in hunting colours.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel paths and parquet floors.
