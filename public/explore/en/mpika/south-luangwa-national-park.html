The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look for anything, simply listen. There is the low grunt of a hippo somewhere downriver, the dry rattle of mopane leaves, and the high call of a fish eagle over the water. The air smells of dust and warm grass. Underfoot, the ground is pale sand, packed hard by hooves and soft where the river once ran. This is the Luangwa Valley, and it sounds like nowhere else.

You may have started your journey near Mpika, a town up on the high plateau of Zambia's Muchinga Province. From there, the land drops sharply down the Muchinga Escarpment, a long wall of hills and broken rock. Below it lies the valley floor. It is lower, hotter, and much wilder. South Luangwa National Park spreads across this floor, on the western side of the Luangwa River.

The valley is part of a very long system of rifts and depressions that cuts through eastern Africa. Over a vast span of time, the earth here sank and the river settled into the low ground. That is why the escarpment feels like the edge of a great step. Standing on the valley floor, you are in a basin. Heat collects here, and so does water, and so does life.

The river is the centre of everything. The Luangwa is one of the larger rivers in southern Africa that has not been held back by a major dam. It still floods in the rains and shrinks in the dry months. As it shrinks, it changes course. Old bends are cut off and left behind as curved lagoons. Locals and guides call these oxbow lagoons, because their shape follows the curve of an ox yoke.

Those lagoons are where many animals gather. In the dry season, water becomes scarce across the valley, and the lagoons and river pools hold what remains. Hippos crowd into them in large numbers. Crocodiles lie on the sandbanks with their mouths open to cool down. Elephants walk down the steep banks, often in family groups, with the youngest calves close to their mothers' legs. Watch how carefully they place their feet on the slope.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The giraffe you see in this valley is a special form called Thornicroft's giraffe. It lives in the Luangwa Valley and almost nowhere else on earth. Look closely at its coat. The patches are darker and more jagged than on many other giraffes, and they reach down the legs. You are looking at an animal that has stayed in this one valley, cut off by the escarpment and the river.

The zebra here are also worth a second look. The local zebra are often described as Crawshay's zebra. Their stripes are narrow and close together, and many lack the faint brown shadow stripes that you see on zebra further south. Buffalo move in herds along the river plains. Puku, a golden antelope with soft fur, graze the wet grass near the water. Impala are everywhere, and they are often the first animals you will notice.

This park has a strong reputation for leopards. Some guides and travel writers call the Luangwa the valley of the leopard. The dense riverside woodland gives these cats good cover by day. At night, they hunt. South Luangwa is one of the places in the region where night drives are allowed, with a spotlight sweeping slowly across the bush. That is when you may see leopards, along with hyenas, genets, and the bright eyes of small animals in the grass.

The second surprise is about walking. In much of Africa, visitors once watched wildlife only from a vehicle. In this valley, the walking safari became a tradition. The conservationist Norman Carr is widely credited with developing guided walks here in the middle of the twentieth century. Today, small groups still walk with an armed scout and a trained guide. On foot, you notice tracks, dung, the smell of a bush, the sudden silence of birds before something larger moves.

Look at the trees around you. The valley holds several kinds of woodland. Mopane trees have leaves shaped like butterfly wings, and they fold together in the heat of the day. Along the river, you will see tall ebony and mahogany, and huge baobabs with thick grey trunks. The sausage tree is easy to spot. Its long, heavy fruit hangs down on ropes, and elephants and baboons feed on the fallen flowers.

The park also changes completely with the seasons. In the dry months, the grass turns gold and then grey, and the animals stay close to the water. Late in the dry season, flocks of carmine bee-eaters dig nesting holes into the river banks. The birds are a deep pink-red, and a busy colony fills the bank with colour and noise. When the rains arrive, the valley turns green, roads become muddy, and many migrant birds return.

People live around this park, too. Villages and farms sit beyond its boundaries, and many local families work in the lodges, as guides, scouts, cooks, and drivers. Life on the edge of a wildlife area is not always simple. Elephants raid crops, and hippos leave the river at night to graze. Conservation here depends on the people who share the valley with these animals, not only on the fences and laws that protect it.

Now, wherever you stand, take a moment. Look at the colour of the river, brown and slow in its sandy bed. Find the line of the escarpment, blue and distant on the horizon. Notice the quiet between the bird calls. As you move on, keep watching the ground for fresh tracks. The valley will keep changing around you, one hour, one season, one bend of the river at a time.

Key facts

Park lies on the western bank of the Luangwa River.
Thornicroft's giraffe lives almost only in the Luangwa Valley.
Walking safaris here are linked to conservationist Norman Carr.
The Luangwa River has no major dam along its course.
Night drives are permitted inside the park.

What to look for

Curved oxbow lagoons left behind by the shifting river.
Dark, jagged patches on the legs of local giraffes.
Long hanging fruit of sausage trees near the water.
Bee-eater nesting holes cut into steep river banks.
Mopane leaves folded together in the midday heat.
Before you go

Practical tips

Allow a full day for travel down from the Mpika plateau.
Book guided walks ahead with an armed scout and guide.
Wear neutral colours rather than bright white on walks.
Bring binoculars for birds along the river banks.
Carry a warm layer for cool early-morning and night drives.
