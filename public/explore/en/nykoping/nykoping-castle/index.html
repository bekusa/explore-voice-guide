The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close, water is moving. The Nyköping River runs past the castle walls on its way to the Baltic. Its sound has followed this fortress for more than seven centuries. Under your feet the ground is uneven, part gravel and part old stone. Above you rises a heavy tower with small windows and thick, pale walls. This is Nyköping Castle. It is one of the places where medieval Sweden showed its darkest side.

The position of the castle explains a lot. In the Middle Ages, rivers worked as highways. Boats came up from the sea, and goods and soldiers travelled with them. A fortress at this river mouth controlled who arrived and who left. The first stronghold here was a defensive tower, most likely from the late twelfth or early thirteenth century. Over the following generations, builders added walls, houses and towers around it. Step by step, it became one of the most important royal fortresses in the kingdom.

Now for the story that every Swede knows. In December 1317, King Birger invited his two younger brothers, Duke Erik and Duke Valdemar, to celebrate Christmas here. The brothers had fought Birger for power for years. They had even taken him prisoner once. This Christmas visit was supposed to be a gesture of peace. The guests ate and drank in the castle hall and then went to bed. That night the king's men seized them and threw them into the dungeon.

The brothers never left that dungeon alive. The chronicles say they died of hunger in captivity early the following year. Swedish history calls this event the Nyköping Banquet. Then comes the detail that people remember most. Legend has it that King Birger locked the dungeon door and threw the key into the river. Nobody has ever proved this story. It is folklore, but it fits the place so well that many visitors glance at the water when they hear it.

The betrayal did not help Birger. The nobles rose against him in anger, and his own position collapsed. He fled the country, and he never ruled Sweden again. This is the first surprise of the place. The most famous feast in Swedish history ended the career of the king who gave it. Seven hundred years later, the word "gästabud", which means feast or banquet, still carries a dark echo in Sweden.

The castle you see today, however, is mostly the work of a later and more ambitious ruler. In the second half of the sixteenth century, Duke Karl of Södermanland made Nyköping his seat. He was a son of Gustav Vasa, the king who founded modern Sweden. Karl wanted a residence for a prince, not only a fortress. He rebuilt the medieval stronghold as a Renaissance castle. It had grand rooms, decorated façades and a court that could compete with Stockholm.

Duke Karl later became King Charles the Ninth. He kept a close connection to this castle, and he died here in 1611. His son grew up partly in these surroundings. That son became Gustavus Adolphus, one of the great military kings of Europe. So the walls around you are linked to two very different royal stories. One is a story of betrayal and starvation. The other is the start of Sweden's rise as a great power.

The golden age of the castle ended with fire. In 1665, a great fire swept through the buildings and destroyed most of the Renaissance palace. After that, the court did not return. Parts of the ruin stood empty, and some of the stone was taken away for other buildings. This explains the mixed feeling of the site today. You see whole walls next to broken ones, and restored towers next to open spaces where rooms once stood.

Look up at the King's Tower, in Swedish the Kungstornet. It is the strongest survivor of the old complex. Its walls are very thick, and the windows are small and deep-set. Those details tell you that defence came before comfort here. Notice how the surface of the walls changes. In some places it is smooth and rendered, and in other places you can see the rough stone and brick underneath. Each layer comes from a different age of building and repair.

Here is a second detail that many visitors miss. The castle does not sit on a hill, as many people expect from a fortress. It sits low, close to the river and the old harbour. The water itself was the protection. Moats and the river made an attack from land difficult. At the same time, the river gave fast access to the sea. Walk along the bank, and you can still feel how close the water comes to the walls.

Today, parts of the castle hold museum exhibitions about the region of Södermanland and its history. Inside, you can usually follow the story from the medieval fortress to the Renaissance court. The rooms are often cool, even in summer, because stone holds the cold. In the courtyard, the city feels far away. You hear footsteps on gravel, voices from a small group, and sometimes gulls from the direction of the harbour.

The area around the castle is worth a slow walk. A riverside path leads down toward the harbour, where old warehouses and boats line the water. In the other direction, the streets of central Nyköping begin only a few minutes away. This closeness is part of the charm. A medieval prison and a modern Swedish town sit side by side. People walk dogs, ride bicycles and eat ice cream next to walls that saw a royal murder.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the river. Look at the tower, then at the water, then at the walls between them. Somewhere under these buildings were the cells where two dukes waited for food that never came. Above them, a few generations later, a Renaissance court held music and banquets of a happier kind. As you walk away, listen once more to the river. It is the same sound that both of those worlds heard.
