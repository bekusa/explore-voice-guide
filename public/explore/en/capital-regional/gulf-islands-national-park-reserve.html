The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against sandstone. A gull cries somewhere above the tide line. The air smells of salt, warm cedar, and drying kelp. Under your feet, the rock may feel smooth and almost soft, worn into curves by wind and waves. This is the southern Gulf Islands, a scatter of land in the Salish Sea between Vancouver Island and the British Columbia mainland. Some of that land and water is protected as Gulf Islands National Park Reserve. You are standing inside it now.

This is not a park with one gate and one fence. Parks Canada protects pieces of it spread across more than a dozen islands, plus many small islets and reefs. Some parts sit on busy islands with farms, cafés, and ferry docks. Others are rocky outcrops that only seals and seabirds use. The reserve was created in 2003. That makes it one of the younger national park units in Canada, set aside while this coastline was still changing quickly around it.

Why protect these islands? The answer is in the trees and the grass. The southern Gulf Islands lie in what ecologists call the Coastal Douglas-fir zone. It is one of the smallest and rarest ecological zones in British Columbia. Much of it has been cleared for towns and farms. Here, pockets of it survive. You can find open meadows, rocky bluffs, and dry forests that look very different from the dark, wet rainforest many visitors expect on this coast.

That dryness surprises people. The islands sit in a rain shadow. Mountains to the south and west catch much of the rain before it arrives. So summers here are often bright and dry, and the grass on the bluffs turns gold. Spring is the season to remember. In the Garry oak meadows, wildflowers open across the thin soil, in blues, yellows, and whites. Garry oaks are twisted, slow-growing trees. On this part of the coast, they are increasingly rare.

Now look for a tree with red bark. That is the arbutus, also called the Pacific madrone. Its bark peels away in thin, papery curls. Underneath, the new wood is smooth and pale green, almost like skin. Arbutus trees often lean out over the water from cliff edges, holding on to very little soil. Run your hand along a trunk on a warm day. It often feels cool to the touch — one of the small pleasures most people walk past.

The shoreline has its own surprises. On several islands, the sandstone is carved into honeycomb patterns. Geologists call this tafoni. Salt water seeps into the rock, dries, and slowly breaks the grains apart. Over long periods, this creates hollows, pockets, and delicate ridges. Some shapes look like sponges. Others look like the inside of a seashell. Every one was made by salt and time, not by any human tool.

Long before the national park existed, this was home. These islands are the traditional territories of Coast Salish peoples, including the WSÁNEĆ and Hul'q'umi'num-speaking nations. For thousands of years, people fished, gathered, and travelled between these islands by canoe. The WSÁNEĆ are known for reef-net fishing, a method of catching salmon between two canoes with a net set over a reef. Along some beaches, shell middens remain. These are layers of shells left from generations of meals, and they are protected cultural sites.

Today, First Nations and Parks Canada work together on parts of the reserve's management. You may see place names in local Indigenous languages on signs. Some sites carry stories and meanings that are not always shared publicly. If you notice a shell midden, or a beach covered in crushed white shell, please treat it with respect. Do not dig, and do not take anything. These places are part of a living culture, not only a historic landscape.

The water is the other half of this park. Kelp forests sway below the surface. Harbour seals rest on rocks at low tide, and Steller sea lions pass through in some seasons. Bald eagles watch the shore from tall firs. And sometimes — not often, but sometimes — orcas move through the channels. The southern resident killer whales are endangered. If you see them, keep your distance, stay quiet, and enjoy a moment many locals wait years to see.

Each island offers a different way into the reserve. On Sidney Island, a long sandy spit reaches out into the sea. In summer, a passenger ferry crosses from the town of Sidney. You can still find traces of an old brickworks there, from the island's industrial past. On Saturna Island, East Point looks out across the water toward the United States. On Mayne Island, the lighthouse at Georgina Point watches over Active Pass, where large ferries move through a narrow channel.

The Pender Islands have forest trails, quiet coves, and viewpoints at higher ground. Many visitors arrive by kayak or small boat, moving slowly from one island to the next. That pace suits the place. There are no big tour buses here and few crowds. Much of the experience is simple: a walk through dry forest, lunch on warm rock, and an afternoon watching the tide come in. The park rewards people who slow down.

There is also a quiet tension here. These islands are close to major cities, and many people love them. Homes, boats, and visitors all bring pressure on a fragile landscape. Parks Canada and island communities try to balance access with protection. Staying on marked trails helps the meadows recover. Leaving shells, stones, and flowers where you find them helps too. Small choices matter on small islands, because there is not much room for the land to absorb damage.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the colour of the water, which shifts between green, grey, and deep blue as the clouds move. Listen for the clink of rigging at a dock, or the call of an oystercatcher on the rocks. Look at the far islands, layered one behind another until they fade into the haze. All of this is part of the same sea. As you walk away, look back once at the shoreline. The tide will already have changed it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check ferry schedules carefully before planning island hops.
Bring plenty of water for dry summer trails.
Visit tidepools at low tide for the best sightings.
Book backcountry campsites in advance during summer.
Keep a respectful distance from seals and whales.
