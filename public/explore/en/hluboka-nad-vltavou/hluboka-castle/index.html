The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere below the hill the Vltava river runs quietly through the South Bohemian plain. Then you look up. White walls rise in front of you, edged with battlements and pointed towers. In bright sun the facade is almost too clean to be real, like a castle drawn for a storybook. Most visitors take a long breath here before they say anything at all. That pause is a good place to begin.

The name of the town tells you something about the site. Hluboká means deep in Czech. The town's full name, Hluboká nad Vltavou, means Hluboká above the Vltava. The castle stands on a rocky rise over the river valley, a natural lookout over fields, forests and fishponds. People recognised the value of this high ground long before anyone thought about white towers. A castle of some kind has stood on this hill since the Middle Ages.

Here is the first surprise. The building in front of you looks medieval, but most of what you see is not. The first castle here was a Gothic fortress from the thirteenth century, built for defence, not display. Over the centuries it passed through many hands. Owners rebuilt and enlarged it again and again. In later centuries it was reshaped in the Renaissance style and then the Baroque style. The romantic white silhouette you see today came much later, in the nineteenth century.

The family behind that transformation was the House of Schwarzenberg. They were one of the most powerful noble families in Central Europe. The Schwarzenbergs acquired Hluboká in the seventeenth century. Over the next generations they turned South Bohemia into one of their main estates. Their lands included forests, farms, breweries and the many fishponds that still shape this landscape. Hluboká became one of the jewels of their holdings, and eventually a favourite residence.

In the middle of the nineteenth century, Prince Johann Adolf the Second of Schwarzenberg and his wife, Princess Eleonore, decided to rebuild the castle completely. Their model was England. The couple admired Windsor Castle, the residence of the British royal family. They wanted something in that spirit here in Bohemia. The result is a style often called Romantic Neo-Gothic, meaning a nineteenth-century revival of medieval Gothic forms. The work took around three decades, and it changed the hill completely.

This was a fashionable dream across Europe at the time. Wealthy families wanted homes that looked old and noble, but they also wanted comfort. So the battlements are mostly decoration rather than defence. Behind them were heated rooms, large windows and elegant salons for guests. Walk slowly around the outside and look at the details. You will see pointed arches, carved stone tracery and slender corner turrets. Each element imitates the Middle Ages, but it was made with the confidence and precision of the industrial age.

Inside, the castle tells a different story — one of craftsmanship. The interiors are famous for their carved wood. Panelled walls, coffered ceilings and heavy doors fill many of the rooms. Much of this woodwork was made by skilled carvers. The rich brown of oak and walnut contrasts with the white exterior. On a guided tour, notice how the light changes from room to room. Some salons feel warm and dark like the inside of a violin. Others open onto bright views of the park.

The collections add more layers. The rooms hold paintings, tapestries, porcelain, glass and weapons gathered by the family over many generations. The library is one of the highlights for many visitors. Shelves of old leather-bound books rise toward a carved ceiling. Pay attention to the small things as well — door handles, stove tiles, the patterns of the parquet floor under your feet. These everyday objects show how the family actually lived inside this showpiece.

Here is the second surprise, and it comes from the twentieth century. The Schwarzenbergs did not keep Hluboká. During the Second World War, the head of the family opposed the Nazi regime. The family's Bohemian property was confiscated. After the war, the new Czechoslovak state passed a special law that transferred the Schwarzenberg estates to state ownership. Since then, the castle has belonged to the public. That is why you can walk through rooms that were once open only to princes and their guests.

The grounds deserve your time as well. The castle is surrounded by a large park laid out in the English landscape style. That means winding paths, open lawns and groups of old trees instead of strict geometric beds. Near the castle you will also find more formal gardens with flowers and clipped hedges. Walk a little way from the walls and turn back. From a distance, the white towers rise above the treetops, and you understand why the builders chose this hill.

There is more art close by. The former riding hall of the castle complex now houses the Aleš South Bohemian Gallery, a regional art museum. The building once held horses and riding displays for the noble family. Today it shows paintings and sculpture, including medieval Gothic art from this region. If you enjoy museums, it is worth combining with the castle. It gives you a sense of the older Bohemia that the nineteenth-century builders were trying to imitate.

The castle also sits at the heart of a working landscape. South Bohemia is known for its fishponds, many of them created centuries ago. Fish farming, especially carp, is still part of local life. The forests around Hluboká were hunting grounds for the nobility. The town below the castle is small and calm. Its cafés and streets fill with visitors during the day, then grow quiet again in the evening. Bigger tour groups often arrive from nearby České Budějovice, the regional capital.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Look at the white walls and the dark windows, and the flags or weather vanes on the towers above. Remember that this hill has carried a fortress, a Renaissance palace, a Baroque residence and a prince's English dream. Each generation rebuilt it to match its own idea of beauty. As you walk back down toward the town, listen again for the gravel under your feet... and glance back once more over your shoulder.
