The guide

The story

Stand at the top of Dong Khoi Street and look toward the river. Glass towers rise behind French rooftops. Motorbikes stream past marble shopfronts. Perfume drifts from open boutique doors. This street is only about one kilometer long. Yet it has carried more of this city's story than any other road in Saigon.

Dong Khoi begins near Notre-Dame Cathedral and ends at the Saigon River. You can walk it in fifteen minutes. But walk it slowly. Almost every building here has lived several lives.

Before you set off, look around the square at the top. The Central Post Office stands to one side, with its yellow facade and iron hall, finished in 1891. Notre-Dame Cathedral rises in red brick shipped from France. These two buildings anchor the old colonial quarter, and Dong Khoi flows from them like a river from a spring.

The French cut this street through the young colonial city in the nineteenth century. They called it Rue Catinat, after a French warship that took part in the conquest. It quickly became the spine of French Saigon. Cafés, pharmacies, tailors, and perfume shops lined the pavement. Officials and traders filled the terraces. Visitors called Saigon the Pearl of the Far East, and this street was a large part of the reason.

History then changed the street's name twice. After the French left in 1954, the government of South Vietnam renamed it Tu Do, which means Freedom. After 1975, the new government renamed it Dong Khoi, which means General Uprising. Three names, three eras, one street.

Start walking from the cathedral end. On your left, after a few minutes, you reach the Hotel Continental. It opened in 1880 and is the oldest hotel in the city. The English writer Graham Greene stayed here in the 1950s. He set scenes of his novel The Quiet American in its café. So many journalists once gathered on its terrace that people joked and called it the Continental Shelf.

The Continental faces a small square, and across it stands the Municipal Theatre. The French opened it as an opera house in 1900. Its arched entrance and stone figures follow the fashion of Paris in that period. For several years after 1955, the building served as the assembly hall of South Vietnam. Today it hosts ballet, classical concerts, and circus-style shows performed with bamboo poles and baskets.

On the other side of the square rises the Caravelle Hotel. It opened in 1959. With ten floors, it was then one of the tallest buildings in Saigon. During the war, foreign correspondents lived and worked here. In the evenings they climbed to the rooftop bar and watched flashes on the horizon. The rooftop bar still serves drinks, and the view is still one of the best on the street.

Continue downhill toward the water. At the end of the street, the Hotel Majestic faces the Saigon River. A wealthy businessman of Chinese descent, Hui Bon Hoa, built it in 1925. Its curved balconies and stained glass recall the age of ocean liners and slow travel. River wind moves through its ground-floor bar, as it has for a century.

Between these landmarks, the street has turned to luxury. International fashion houses fill spaces where colonial pharmacies once stood. Shopping centers hold cool marble floors behind restored facades. Rents on Dong Khoi are among the highest in Vietnam. But older textures survive. Look into the side streets and narrow passages. You find lacquerware shops, small galleries, and cafés on upper floors. An old apartment building may hold a tailor, a barber, and a coffee roaster on three different levels. The past here does not sit behind glass. It keeps working.

Take a break as the locals do. Order a cà phê sữa đá, iced coffee with condensed milk, in a café above the street. Watch the traffic braid itself below your window. Iced coffee, slow ceiling fan, one hour gone. This is also Saigon, as much as any monument.

Come back in the evening if you can. The Opera House glows under floodlights. Couples in wedding clothes pose on its steps. Families ride past on motorbikes, three helmets shining in a row. The heat softens. The street hums at a lower pitch.

A street that was French, then American in flavor, then Vietnamese again, all within one century. Peace, war, and commerce each left a layer here, like coats of lacquer on wood. Stand at the river end at dusk. Ships move slowly on the dark water. Count the footsteps that came before yours, and add your own to the story.
