The story

Stand at the main gate of Ben Thanh Market and listen. Sellers call over one another, scooters idle, ice rattles into glasses of coffee. Above it all, a clock keeps time in a small tower, as it has since this hall opened in 1914. Every walk through old Saigon begins here, where the city has traded, argued, and eaten for over a century.

Step inside for a moment. The market is a single great shed, packed tight with stalls. Silk, dried shrimp, sandals, chili paste, souvenirs. Prices are flexible, and bargaining is expected, though it stays friendly. If the crowd feels thick, hold your bag in front of you and drift with the current. Nobody moves fast in here. The heat will not allow it.

A practical word before you go further. Crossing streets here alarms first time visitors. The trick is simple. Wait for a small gap, step off the curb, and walk slowly and steadily, with no sudden stops or sprints. The motorbikes read your path and flow around you. Locals cross while reading their phones. You will manage it with your eyes open.

Leave by the main gate and walk northeast along Le Loi street. This wide avenue was laid out by the French in the nineteenth century, when Saigon was the capital of their colony of Cochinchina. The buildings have changed many times since. The plan underneath has not. You are walking a street plan drawn before cars existed, and it still works.

Le Loi delivers you to Nguyen Hue, the walking street. It runs straight to the river, broad as a runway, paved in granite. In 2015 the city closed it to traffic and gave it to pedestrians. On weekend evenings it fills with families, street performers, and children chasing bubbles. At its head stands the old French city hall, pale yellow, trimmed like a wedding cake.

The city hall, now the People's Committee building, was completed by the French administration in the early twentieth century. You cannot go inside, but the front makes the photograph. In the small square before it stands a statue of Ho Chi Minh. Around it, luxury towers rise behind colonial cornices. The whole conversation between old and new Saigon happens in this one view.

Two blocks east sits the Municipal Theatre, the opera house of Saigon, opened in 1900. Its arched entrance echoes the Petit Palais in Paris, finished the same year. Concerts and acrobatic shows still fill it at night. By day, wedding couples pose on its steps. Notice how the building sits slightly raised, letting Dong Khoi street bow around it.

Dong Khoi street itself deserves a slow stroll. Under the French it was rue Catinat, the smartest street in colonial Asia, lined with cafes and perfume shops. Graham Greene set scenes of The Quiet American around here, and drank at the Continental Hotel, which opened in 1880 and still receives guests behind its white arcades. Writers, spies, and journalists once filled its terrace.

Follow Dong Khoi away from the river and the trees open onto a square with a red brick church. This is Notre Dame Cathedral of Saigon, completed in 1880. Its bricks were shipped from France and have never been painted. The twin bell towers rose to their full height when iron spires were added in 1895. Restoration work has wrapped parts of it in scaffolding for years.

Beside the cathedral stands the Central Post Office, opened in 1891, and still a working post office. Step into the long arched hall. Green iron ribs hold up the ceiling, painted maps of old Saigon decorate the walls, and a large portrait of Ho Chi Minh watches from the far end. You can mail a postcard from a wooden counter, the old way.

Just beside the post office runs a short lane roofed with trees, given over entirely to bookshops and reading cafes. Locals call it Book Street. It is a good pause point. Buy an iced coffee, find a bench, and let your feet recover. The distance walked so far is small. The heat makes it feel otherwise, and there is no shame in resting.

Ten minutes further west, behind iron gates and a wide lawn, sits the Reunification Palace. The current building was finished in 1966 as the Independence Palace of South Vietnam, designed by the architect Ngo Viet Thu. On April 30, 1975, a North Vietnamese tank broke through these gates, and the long war ended here. Inside, the 1960s state rooms remain frozen in place.

End your walk back near the river, or with a bowl of noodles at a plastic table wherever a crowd of locals suggests the cooking is good. District 1 rewards slow attention. Its landmarks are close together, but the real exhibit is the street itself. Traffic, trade, coffee, and talk, flowing around a century of borrowed architecture, entirely at home.
