The guide

The story

Behind an iron fence in central Saigon stands a pale palace with tall white columns. Palm trees line the path. On most mornings, couples in wedding clothes pose on the grand staircase. It looks calm. Yet this building has seen plots, coups, and escapes worthy of a film.

This is the Ho Chi Minh City Museum, at the corner of Ly Tu Trong Street, one block from the walking boulevard of Nguyen Hue. Locals still sometimes call it by its old name, Gia Long Palace.

The French completed the building in 1890. The architect was Alfred Foulhoux, who shaped several of colonial Saigon's landmarks. The plan was to open a museum of trade and industry here. But governments change their minds. The palace instead became the residence of the governor of Cochinchina, the southern third of Vietnam under French rule.

Walk toward the entrance and study the facade. Classical columns support a carved stone pediment. Two long wings stretch to either side. The style is European, with high windows set against the tropical heat and thick walls that keep the halls cool. Inside, the ceilings rise high above marble floors, and ceiling fans turn slowly even now.

The building's strangest chapter came in the 1960s. Ngo Dinh Diem, the president of South Vietnam, moved here in 1962 after rebel pilots bombed his own palace across the park. The move was meant to be temporary. Diem trusted few people by then. Under this building he ordered secret tunnels, wide enough to escape through if enemies came.

In November 1963, they came. Army generals launched a coup and surrounded the palace. Diem and his brother slipped into the tunnels and escaped to the Chinese quarter of Cholon. Their freedom lasted only hours. Both were captured and killed the next day. The tunnels remain beneath the building, closed to visitors, part of the foundation of the story.

The coup of 1963 changed the war. South Vietnam cycled through military governments for years afterward. Historians still debate the role of foreign powers in those November days. This quiet palace sat at the center of that storm, and its walls heard the last free hours of a president.

After the war ended in 1975, the palace became a museum of the revolution. In 1999 it took its current name, the Ho Chi Minh City Museum. Its subject is the city itself: its land, its people, its trades, and its wars. Entry remains inexpensive, and the halls are rarely crowded on weekday mornings.

The ground floor tells the older story. You find maps of early Saigon, when canals cut through the young port town. Display cases hold ceramics, coins, and tools of river trade. Old photographs show streets you may have walked an hour ago, filled with rickshaws instead of motorbikes. One gallery covers marriage customs. Costumes show how Vietnamese, Chinese, and Khmer families in the south dressed a bride and groom. Reds and golds dominate, and the embroidery rewards a slow look.

Upstairs, the tone changes. The galleries follow the long years of war and resistance. Weapons, flags, and photographs trace the fight against France and then the United States. Some objects are homemade and rough. They show how much of this history ran on improvisation and will. Most exhibits carry short English labels.

Step into the garden before you leave. Military hardware rests on the grass: a fighter aircraft, a tank, artillery pieces. Children circle them. Gardeners trim the hedges around them. War machines as garden furniture. Saigon has a talent for moving on.

Back at the staircase, the wedding couples are still posing. Chandeliers hang above them. Light falls through tall windows onto marble. A palace built for empire, hiding escape tunnels, now framing wedding photographs.

Before you go, stand once more between the columns and look toward the street. Motorbikes flow past the iron fence in a steady river. This building watched the city grow from colonial port to open metropolis. It kept its secrets and outlived its owners. Now it simply tells the story, one visitor at a time, one photograph at a time.
