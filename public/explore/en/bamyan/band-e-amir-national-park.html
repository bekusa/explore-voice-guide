The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes off the bare hills with almost nothing to stop it. There are no trees to rustle, no traffic, only the dry hiss of air over stone. Then you look down, and the colour is hard to believe. The water below is a deep, saturated blue, almost ink, set inside cliffs of pale cream and rust. You are standing nearly three thousand metres above sea level, in the high country of central Afghanistan.

Welcome to Band-e Amir. It is a chain of six lakes strung across the mountains of Bamyan Province, part of the wider Hindu Kush range. In 2009, this landscape became Afghanistan's first national park. That status was a statement. After decades of war, the country chose to protect a place for its beauty and its water. For many Afghans, the lakes became a symbol of what the land could still offer.

So what exactly are you looking at? The answer is chemistry, working very slowly. Water seeps through the surrounding rock and picks up dissolved minerals, mostly calcium carbonate. When that water reaches the surface and flows over the lip of a lake, the minerals settle out. Layer by layer, they build a natural wall called travertine. Over thousands of years, those walls grew tall enough to hold back the water behind them.

Here is the first surprise. The dams around you are not made by people, and they are not made by landslides. They are made by the water itself. The lakes literally build their own walls. Travertine dams of this kind exist in only a few places on Earth. Few of them sit this high, in such a dry and treeless setting. That contrast is the reason the blue looks so intense.

The name carries a story. Band means dam. Amir means commander, and here it refers to Ali, the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, deeply honoured by Muslims across the region. Local legend says that Ali created these lakes himself. According to one popular tale, he struck the rock to build the dams and tame a cruel king. This is folklore, not history — but it shapes how people see this water.

Each of the six lakes has its own name, and each name holds a small idea. The largest and deepest is Band-e Haibat, which means the Dam of Awe. There is Band-e Zulfiqar, named after the famous sword of Ali. There is Band-e Pudina, the Dam of Mint, and Band-e Panir, the Dam of Cheese. Band-e Ghulaman and Band-e Qambar complete the chain.

Band-e Haibat is the one most visitors see first. Its wall of travertine rises well above the lower ground, like a pale terrace with water spilling over its edge. Walk close and you can see the texture. The surface is rough, porous, and layered, a little like coral or old bread crust. In places, thin streams of water run down the face, still adding new mineral, still building the wall today.

Near Band-e Haibat stands a small shrine linked to Ali. It is a quiet, simple place, and many Afghan visitors stop there to pray. Locals say the lake water has healing powers. Some people bathe in it, or carry it home in bottles for sick relatives. Please treat this corner of the park with respect. For you it may be scenery. For the families around you, it is a place of faith.

The second surprise is the colour itself. Why is the water so blue? Part of the answer is clarity. Very little soil or plant matter washes into these lakes, so the water stays remarkably clean. Minerals in the water and the pale lake bed reflect the sky back up. The thin mountain air adds to the effect. On a clear day, the shades shift from turquoise at the edges to near black in the deep centre.

The people of this region are mainly Hazara, a community with deep roots in the central highlands. You may meet families from Bamyan or from Kabul on a day out. On busy days, you might see pedal boats on the main lake, some shaped like swans. It is a strange and lovely sight — bright plastic birds floating on water this ancient. Small tea stalls and simple guesthouses serve people who travel here.

The journey matters too. Band-e Amir lies a few hours by road west of the town of Bamyan. That town is known for the giant Buddha statues carved into its cliffs, which were destroyed in 2001. The road between them crosses open valleys and high passes. In winter, snow and ice make travel difficult, and parts of the lakes can freeze. Most visitors come in the warmer months, when the roads are open.

The altitude affects everything here. The sun is strong, even when the air feels cool. You may feel out of breath after a short climb, so walk slowly and drink plenty of water. Weather can change quickly in the mountains. A calm morning can become a windy afternoon. Conditions for travel in Afghanistan also change, so it is wise to check the current situation with local contacts before and during your visit.

Now, take a moment before you leave. Look at the edge of the nearest lake, where the water meets the travertine. Watch how it slips over the rim. That thin movement is the whole story of this place. It has been happening, quietly, for thousands of years. It is happening right now, while you stand here. As you walk away, listen again for the wind, and look back once at the blue.
