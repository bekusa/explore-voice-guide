The guide

The story

A short way south of the city, the streets give way to a vast freshwater lagoon ringed by rice fields and reeds, where flat-bottomed boats drift among the birds and the sun sets huge over the water. This is La Albufera, a great lagoon and wetland that is, among other things, the birthplace of the region's most famous dish.

The lagoon is the largest of its kind in the country, a shallow freshwater lake separated from the sea by a sandbar of pine forest and dunes, fed by streams and ringed by the flooded fields that have shaped local life for centuries.

Those fields are rice paddies, and it is here, in the wet land around the lagoon, that paella was born, the rice dish cooked over open fire that became the signature of the whole region and, for many, of the country. The villages around the water are full of restaurants serving it.

The classic way to experience the lagoon is by boat. Local boatmen take visitors out across the open water in flat traditional craft, especially toward sunset, when the sky and the lake turn gold and the silhouettes of other boats drift across the light.

The lagoon and its wetlands are a haven for birds, a protected natural park where herons, ducks, and many other species gather, so it draws birdwatchers as well as those who come for the sunset and the food.

The villages on the shore, old fishing and farming settlements, keep a slower, traditional way of life tied to the water and the rice, and a meal of paella in one of them, looking out over the fields, is a classic Valencian day out.

The pine-forested sandbar between the lagoon and the sea, with its dunes and beaches, adds another landscape to the park, so the area mixes lake, forest, fields, and shore within a short distance.

It is an easy trip from the city, close enough for an afternoon and evening, yet a complete change from the streets, a flat, watery, open landscape that feels far from the busy centre.

The light is the great draw. The lagoon is famous for its sunsets, when the low sun spreads across the still water and the rice fields, and a boat trip timed for that hour is the highlight for most visitors.

Beyond the boat and the meal, the park rewards a slower visit, with walking and cycling routes through the fields and forest, and quiet spots to watch the birds and the water away from the main jetties.

A few practical notes. Reach it by bus or car from the city in well under an hour; time a boat trip for late afternoon into sunset, and plan a paella meal in a lakeside village. Bring insect cover for the evening by the water.

Drift out across the lagoon as the sun drops and the water turns to gold, then sit down to the rice dish that was born on these very fields, and La Albufera gathers the region's landscape and food into one evening. When you head back to the city, the flat gold water stays in your mind behind the streets.
