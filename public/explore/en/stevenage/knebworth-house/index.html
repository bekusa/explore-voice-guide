The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel. It crunches under every step as you cross toward the house, and above you the roofline breaks into a crowd of shapes. Turrets, pinnacles, small copper domes gone green with weather, and stone creatures leaning out from the edges. It looks like a castle from a Victorian storybook. But what you are seeing is a kind of costume, worn by a much older house underneath.

Welcome to Knebworth House, on the edge of Stevenage in Hertfordshire. One family has lived here for more than five hundred years. Sir Robert Lytton acquired the estate in 1490, and his descendants are still here today. That continuity matters. This is not a house frozen as a museum. It is a family home that has changed its face again and again, and each generation left something you can still see.

The first house was Tudor, built in warm red brick around a courtyard. Over the centuries, much of that original building was taken down, and only one wing survived. Then, in the 1840s, came the great transformation. The brick was covered in render, and the Gothic towers, battlements and gargoyles went up. The style is called Gothic Revival — a Victorian fashion for imitating medieval churches and castles. So the medieval look here is younger than the brick it hides.

The man behind that dramatic makeover was Edward Bulwer-Lytton. In his day, he was one of the most popular novelists in Britain, and also a politician. Today most people do not know his name, but they know his words. He wrote the opening line "It was a dark and stormy night." He also gave the English language the phrase "the pen is mightier than the sword." Both come from his work, and both are still repeated every day by people who have never read him.

Bulwer-Lytton loved the theatrical, and his house reflects it. Inside, his study and rooms carry dark wood, heavy fabrics and a sense of mystery. He was fascinated by the occult and by strange ideas of his age. Standing in front of the house, it is easy to imagine him choosing every gargoyle. He wanted a home that looked like the setting for one of his own romantic novels... and he got one.

He also had famous friends. Charles Dickens was among them, and Dickens came to Knebworth in 1850 to stage amateur theatre with a group of fellow writers. The banqueting hall became a stage, and the novelist of Oliver Twist performed for invited guests. It is a pleasing thought — two of the best-known writers of the Victorian age, acting together under this roof, for fun.

That banqueting hall is one of the oldest parts of the interior. It keeps a carved wooden screen from the early seventeenth century, dark with age, and the room has a high, echoing feel. Look up and around as you pass through. Here the older house shows through the Victorian costume. Many visitors walk past the screen quickly, but its carving rewards a slow look.

The next generation pulled the family onto a world stage. Edward's son, Robert Lytton, became Viceroy of India — the representative of the British crown there. In 1877 he presided over the great assembly in Delhi that proclaimed Queen Victoria Empress of India. The house keeps a display about that event. It is a reminder that this quiet Hertfordshire estate was tied directly to the history of empire, with all the questions that history raises today.

Here is a story many visitors miss. Robert's daughter, Lady Constance Lytton, became a suffragette — one of the women who campaigned, often at great personal cost, for the right to vote. She was imprisoned for the cause. To prove that working-class prisoners were treated more harshly than aristocrats, she disguised herself as a seamstress called Jane Warton. Her health never fully recovered. A daughter of this grand house chose prison over silence.

Step outside again, into the gardens. Another family link shaped them. The architect Edwin Lutyens married Lady Emily Lytton, and in the early twentieth century he simplified and reorganised the formal gardens. Look for long straight lines, clipped hedges and calm green rooms divided by yew. There is also a herb garden based on a design by Gertrude Jekyll, the great English garden designer. It was planned in her lifetime but only planted many decades later.

Beyond the gardens, the land opens into parkland, with old trees and grazing deer. And this lawn has a second, louder life. Since the 1970s, the park has hosted some of the largest rock concerts in British history. The Rolling Stones played here. Led Zeppelin played here in 1979. Queen performed here in 1986, in what became Freddie Mercury's last concert with the band. In 1996, Oasis played two nights to an enormous crowd, one of the biggest outdoor audiences ever seen in Britain.

So picture this same green slope covered in people, smoke and noise, then emptied again by the next morning. The house has also appeared on screen. Its Gothic front stood in for Wayne Manor in the 1989 film Batman. Somehow it suits the place. A house built to look like a story keeps being asked to play one.

Before you leave, stand back and take in the whole front once more. The red Tudor house is under there somewhere, wrapped in Victorian imagination, framed by an Edwardian garden, sitting on a lawn that once shook with rock music. Listen again to the gravel as you walk away, and the rooks in the trees. Every layer here was added by people who lived in it — and they still do.
