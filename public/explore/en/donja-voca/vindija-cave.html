The guide

The story

Listen first. Around you is the hush of a wooded hillside in northern Croatia. Leaves move overhead, and somewhere below a stream runs through the valley. Then the limestone opens. A dark mouth in the pale rock breathes out cooler air, damp and mineral. It smells faintly of wet stone and old earth. This is Vindija Cave, and the ground in front of you held one of the most important archives of human ancestry ever found.

At first glance, nothing here announces its importance. There is no grand visitor centre and no glass pavilion. There is a cave in a hill above the village of Donja Voća, in the green, rolling country of the Croatian north. Farms and vineyards spread across the slopes around it. Forest closes in on the paths. That modesty is part of its character. The most valuable things found here were small enough to fit in your palm.

The cave formed in limestone. Over vast stretches of time, water dissolved the rock and carved out a sheltered chamber. Later, layer after layer of sediment settled on its floor. Dust blew in. Rock flaked from the ceiling. Animals dragged in bones. People lit fires and left tools behind. Each layer sealed the one beneath it. The result was a kind of vertical calendar, with the oldest pages at the bottom.

Archaeologists began to read that calendar seriously in the second half of the twentieth century. The Croatian palaeontologist Mirko Malez led long excavations here, beginning in the 1970s. His teams dug carefully through the deposits, recording what came from each layer. They found stone tools, the bones of Ice Age animals, and — scattered among them — fragments of human bone. Many of those fragments belonged to Neanderthals.

Neanderthals were a group of ancient humans who lived across Europe and western Asia for hundreds of thousands of years. They were stocky, strong, and well adapted to cold climates. They made tools, controlled fire, and cared for one another. They disappeared as a distinct population tens of thousands of years ago, around the time our own species spread across Europe. Vindija sits right in the middle of that story.

Here is the first surprise. Many of the Neanderthal fragments from this cave are not impressive to look at. They are broken pieces of bone, some no bigger than a thumb. Visitors expecting a complete skeleton are often puzzled. But small and broken turned out to matter less than one thing — how well the bone had kept its chemistry. The cool, stable conditions here protected traces of ancient genetic material.

That brings us to the twenty-first century, and to a laboratory far from these woods. The Swedish geneticist Svante Pääbo and his team at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig wanted to read the Neanderthal genome. A genome is the full set of genetic instructions inside a living thing. To read it, they needed bones with unusually good preservation. Several of the most useful samples came from Vindija Cave.

In 2010, the team published a first draft of the Neanderthal genome. Bones from this cave formed a major part of it. The result changed how we think about ourselves. It showed that Neanderthals and early modern humans had children together. Today, many people with ancestry from outside Africa carry a small share of Neanderthal DNA. In a quiet way, part of the evidence for that came from the floor of this hillside cave.

The work did not stop there. In 2017, researchers published a much more detailed genome from a single Vindija Neanderthal, often described as a female. It was read with far greater accuracy than the earlier draft. In 2022, Pääbo received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on ancient genomes and human evolution. So a cave above a small Croatian village stands on the path to a Nobel Prize.

The second surprise concerns time. For years, some Vindija Neanderthals were thought to be among the very last of their kind in Europe. Later dating, using improved methods, suggested that the bones are older than first believed. This is a normal part of science. Dates get tested, challenged, and refined. It is a good reminder that the cave is not a closed book. Researchers still return to its material with new questions.

Neanderthals were not the only ones to use this shelter. The upper layers hold traces of early modern humans from the late Ice Age. Deeper down, there are many remains of cave bears — huge animals, now extinct, that used caves like this one for winter sleep. Picture the scene across thousands of years. Bears in one era, Neanderthals in another, modern humans after them. Each left something behind in the same patch of ground.

This part of Croatia has a deep connection to that story. Not far away, near the town of Krapina, another important Neanderthal site was excavated around the turn of the twentieth century. Today it has its own museum. Together, these two places make the region one of the best in Europe for thinking about our ancient relatives. The cave itself is protected, and the original finds are kept in research collections, not on the cave floor.

Now look again at the opening in front of you. Notice how the rock curves overhead, and how the light fades only a few steps inside. Feel the temperature change at the threshold. Neanderthals stood at an entrance much like this one, looking out over a very different, colder landscape. As you walk back down through the trees, listen to the stream and the birds. The valley has changed many times. The cave has kept its records quietly through all of it.

What to look for

Views down over the Voća valley from the path.
Damp mineral streaks where water runs over the rock.
Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the forest path up the hillside.
Bring a light layer for the cool air at the entrance.
Respect barriers, since the cave is a protected site.
Combine your visit with the Neanderthal museum near Krapina.
Check local access rules before planning to go inside.
