The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath your feet, through the steel grating and the open air, there is a low and steady roar. It is not traffic. It is the sea, moving with real force through a narrow gap between two islands. The wind here smells of salt and pine, and it pushes hard against your jacket. Somewhere far below, white water is turning in slow, wide circles. You are standing above the Naruto Strait, on one of the most restless stretches of water in Japan.

The Ōnaruto Bridge crosses this strait. It links Awaji Island to the north with the island of Shikoku to the south, here at the city of Naruto in Tokushima Prefecture. It is a suspension bridge, with two tall towers and long cables that carry the road deck in a single graceful line. It opened in 1985. Its central span between the towers measures 876 metres, and the whole structure runs well over a kilometre and a half from end to end.

The bridge is one link in a much larger chain. Together with the Akashi Kaikyō Bridge on the far side of Awaji Island, it forms a road route from the city of Kobe, across Awaji, and down into Shikoku. For most of history, Shikoku was reached only by boat. Today a driver can cross from Honshu, the main island of Japan, to Shikoku in a single trip. That change reshaped daily life on this coast, for farmers, for fishermen, and for families.

But the real reason people stop here is the water. The Naruto Strait connects two very different bodies of sea. On one side lies the calm Seto Inland Sea. On the other side lies the Kii Channel, which opens to the Pacific Ocean. When the tide rises and falls, the water level on the two sides does not match. The sea rushes through the narrow strait to balance itself, and the current here ranks among the fastest in Japan.

That difference in speed is what creates the famous Naruto whirlpools. Fast water in the middle of the channel slides past slower water near the edges. The two streams twist against each other, and the surface begins to spin. On the strongest days, the largest whirlpools can reach around twenty metres across. They form, turn, and break apart in moments, then new ones appear nearby. No two visits are ever quite the same.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The whirlpools do not run all day. They follow the tides, so they are strongest at certain hours and almost absent at others. The most powerful currents come during spring tides, around the full moon and the new moon. Local tourism offices publish a daily whirlpool timetable. If you arrive at the wrong hour, you may see only a fast, rippled sea... still beautiful, but without the spinning.

Now look at the structure itself. The road deck sits on a deep steel truss, a grid of beams that gives the bridge stiffness against strong winds. And this truss holds the second surprise. The bridge was designed with a lower level for a railway. Planners hoped that a high-speed train line would one day cross here into Shikoku. The rail line was never built. For years, that space under the road simply carried wind.

Today part of that empty railway level has a new life. It holds a covered walkway called Uzu no Michi, which means the Path of the Whirlpools. The walkway runs out over the strait for several hundred metres, between the steel beams. You walk inside the skeleton of the bridge, with cars passing above your head. The sound of traffic mixes with the sound of the sea below, and the whole deck carries a faint, steady vibration.

At points along the walkway, the floor changes from solid panels to thick glass. Stand on one of these panels and look straight down. The water is roughly forty-five metres below you. On a good tide, you can watch the whirlpools form directly beneath your shoes. Some people step onto the glass at once. Others stand at the edge for a long time first. Both reactions are completely normal here.

These whirlpools held a place in Japanese art long before the bridge existed. In the nineteenth century, the woodblock artist Utagawa Hiroshige depicted the Naruto whirlpools, with curling waves and rocky shores. Woodblock prints were the popular pictures of their time, made by carving designs into wooden blocks and printing them in colour. For many people in old Japan, prints like these were the only way they ever saw the strait. Now you see the same water from above.

There is also a third way to meet the strait. Sightseeing boats leave from the Naruto side and sail out under the bridge. From the water, the towers rise high above you, and the whirlpools look much larger and much louder. The boats move close to the edges of the turning water, and the hull rocks in the current. On a windy day, expect some spray. Many visitors choose both views: the boat for the sound, the walkway for the pattern.

On the Shikoku end, the bridge lands in Naruto Park, on a green headland of pine trees and rocky paths. From the viewpoints there, you can see the full shape of the bridge against the sky, with Awaji Island beyond it. Near the start of the walkway there is a museum about the bridge and the whirlpools. Small fishing harbours line the coast nearby, and the strait is well known in Japan for its seaweed and sea bream.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the line of the cables, and then look down at the moving water. The engineers built this bridge to stand firm above a sea that never stops. The sea keeps turning anyway, twice a day, as it has for thousands of years. As you walk back toward land, listen again for that low roar. It will follow you a long way up the hill.

Practical tips
