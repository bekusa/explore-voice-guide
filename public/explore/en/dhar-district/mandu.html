The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across an open plateau, and somewhere a crow calls from a broken dome. The stone under your feet is warm, pale sandstone worn smooth by centuries of bare feet and sandals. Around you, ruined arches stand in fields of grass, and huge grey-trunked trees rise between them like old pillars. This is Mandu, a city of palaces and mosques built on a high, flat-topped hill in the Malwa region of central India.

The setting explains almost everything here. Mandu sits on a spur of the Vindhya hills, cut off from the surrounding land by deep ravines on most sides. To the south, the ground drops away towards the wide valley of the Narmada River. A plateau like this was a gift to anyone who wanted a fortress. It had height, natural defences, rainwater, and cool breezes. Long before the great palaces, rulers of this region already understood its value.

Mandu's golden age came in the fifteenth century. Around 1401, a governor named Dilawar Khan broke away from the weakening Delhi Sultanate and ruled Malwa independently. His son, Hoshang Shah, moved the capital up onto this plateau. For more than a century, Mandu was the seat of the Malwa Sultanate. Its rulers filled the hilltop with mosques, tombs, reservoirs, and pleasure palaces. Many of the buildings you see today belong to this period.

To reach the city, travellers passed through a long line of walls and gateways. The walls run along the edge of the plateau for many kilometres, following the line of the cliffs. The gates are called darwazas, and the most famous is the Delhi Darwaza, the old main entrance from the north. As you drive or walk in, notice how the road bends between gates. Those bends slowed down attackers and exposed them to defenders above.

Now to one of the quiet surprises of Mandu. Near the Jama Masjid stands the tomb of Hoshang Shah, made of white marble. It is widely described as the first marble building of its kind in India. Here is the detail most visitors miss. An inscription at the tomb records that architects from the court of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan came to study it. Shah Jahan is the emperor who built the Taj Mahal. So this modest dome may stand somewhere in the family tree of that far more famous tomb.

Beside the tomb rises the Jama Masjid, the great congregational mosque. It stands on a raised platform, reached by a broad flight of steps. Inside, a vast courtyard opens to the sky, surrounded by arcades of red sandstone arches. The domes repeat in long rows, and the effect is calm and steady rather than ornate. It is often said that the builders took inspiration from the Great Mosque of Damascus in Syria. Walk slowly under the arches, and listen to how your footsteps carry.

Further north, in the Royal Enclosure, you will find the building most people come to see. The Jahaz Mahal, or Ship Palace, is a long, narrow palace built between two artificial lakes. When the lakes are full, the building seems to float on the water, like a ship at anchor. It is linked with Sultan Ghiyas-ud-din Khalji, who ruled in the late fifteenth century. Tradition says he kept an enormous household of women here. The numbers in the stories grow with every telling, so treat them as legend.

Climb to the terrace of the Ship Palace if you can. From the roof, you look down on the water and the pavilions on its edge. Look also for the curving water channels and the bathing pool with its patterned stone. Water mattered deeply to Mandu's builders. On a plateau with no river, every drop of monsoon rain had to be caught, channelled, and stored. The lakes, tanks, and wells across the site formed a careful system for a city in the sky.

A short walk away stands the Hindola Mahal, the Swing Palace. Its walls lean outwards at a steep angle, supported by heavy sloping buttresses. Seen from the side, the building seems to sway, and that is where the name comes from. It was probably an audience hall, a place for formal gatherings. The plain, massive walls feel very different from the light pavilions of the Ship Palace. Together they show two moods of the same court.

Now look at the trees. Those wide, swollen trunks belong to baobabs, a tree native to Africa. Nobody planted them here by accident. They are generally believed to have arrived through trade links between India, Arabia, and East Africa during the sultanate period. Locals call the fruit Mandu imli, which means Mandu tamarind. In season, you may see vendors by the roadside selling the hard, velvety pods. The white pulp inside tastes sour and chalky.

At the southern edge of the plateau stands Rani Roopmati's Pavilion, high on the cliff. From its domed towers, you look out over the Narmada valley far below. Nearby lies the palace of Baz Bahadur, the last independent ruler of Malwa. Popular tradition tells a love story about these two. Roopmati was a singer, and Baz Bahadur was a musician king. Legend has it that she climbed to the pavilion each day to see the distant Narmada before she ate.

The story has a dark ending. In 1561, forces of the Mughal emperor Akbar marched on Malwa, and Baz Bahadur's army was defeated. According to the popular tale, Roopmati took poison rather than fall into the hands of the Mughal general. Historians debate how much of the romance is true. But the ballad has survived for centuries in songs and poems. After this conquest, Mandu became part of the Mughal empire. Its importance faded slowly over the following generations.

When you leave, pause for a moment at any open edge of the plateau. Feel the wind coming up from the valley, and look back at the domes against the sky. Notice how the grass, the water, and the old stone share the same space now. Kings once rode through these gates with banners and drums. Today you may hear only goats, a motorbike on the road, and children calling near a tank. Take that quiet with you as you walk on.

Look closer

What to look for

Sloping buttressed walls that give Hindola Mahal its swaying look.
Jahaz Mahal's reflection lying between its two lakes.
Rows of identical domes above the Jama Masjid arcades.
Swollen grey baobab trunks standing among the ruins.
Patterned water channels on the Ship Palace terraces.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit during or just after monsoon to see the lakes full.
Hire a car or bike to cover the spread-out monument groups.
Reach Roopmati's Pavilion late afternoon for the valley view.
Carry water and a hat for the exposed plateau.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone steps and terraces.
