Zhangjiajie National Forest Park

The mist moves first.

⏱ 6 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

The mist moves first. Before you can see the stone pillars clearly, you feel the cool, damp air settle on your skin and hear water running somewhere far below. Then the white veil lifts, just a little, and grey towers of rock rise out of the green forest. They stand close together, hundreds of them, many taller than any building in your home city. For a moment you simply stop and look.

You are standing inside Zhangjiajie National Forest Park, in Hunan province, in the center of China. This was the first national forest park the country ever created, opened in 1982. The wider region around it is called the Wulingyuan Scenic Area. In 1992, the United Nations named Wulingyuan a World Heritage Site, recognizing the rare beauty of its stone forest.

The pillars are made of quartz sandstone. Over many millions of years, rain, ice, and slow-moving streams cut the solid rock apart. The softer layers washed away grain by grain. The harder columns stayed behind and kept their height. What you see today is the patient work of weather, carved one thin crack at a time, without any human hand.

There are more than three thousand of these stone towers across the park. Some of them rise more than two hundred meters straight into the air. Many have narrow tops where pine trees have taken root, their roots gripping the bare stone. From a distance, the small trees look like green caps resting on tall stone fingers. Up close, you see how little soil they need to live.

Walk slowly here, and listen. The forest is loud in a gentle way. Insects hum in the warm pockets of air. Birds call from the high canopy. Water trickles and drips between the rocks below your feet. The sound bounces off the surrounding pillars, so it seems to arrive from every direction at once, soft and constant.

Many travelers come here because of a film. When the movie Avatar appeared in 2009, viewers noticed that its floating mountains looked much like these pillars. One tall column in the park was later renamed in honor of the film. The artists may or may not have used this exact spot, but the resemblance is real, and it now draws visitors from across the world.

The air changes as you move higher. Lower down, near the streams, it feels warm and thick. As you climb, the air turns cool and the mist returns. Clouds drift between the towers and hide their bases completely. When that happens, the tops seem to float free in the white, with nothing visible holding them to the earth below.

There are easy ways to travel through the park. Glass elevators and cable cars carry visitors up the steepest cliffs in a few quiet minutes. But some of the finest paths are simple stone steps and narrow trails. Walking them, you pass through gaps where two great pillars almost touch, and the open sky narrows to a single thin, bright line far above your head.

Wild monkeys live throughout these mountains. You may see them sitting on the railings, or moving easily through the trees beside the path. They watch people closely and are not shy at all. Keep your food closed inside your bag and hold onto small items. Then simply enjoy watching them climb and leap, at home in a place that amazes you.

The light shifts through the whole day. In the early morning, the mist is thick and the pillars look soft and grey, almost like a painting. By midday, the sun reaches down between them, and the green of the pines grows bright and sharp. In the late afternoon, the rock warms to a golden color, just before the long shadows return.

Local people have told stories about these mountains for many generations. They gave the pillars names, calling some of them soldiers standing guard, or brushes, or fingers pointing toward the sky. Standing among them, it is easy to understand the names. The shapes feel almost alive, as if the stone itself is slowly reaching upward, frozen in the middle of the motion.

Take your time choosing where to stand. The most crowded viewing platforms are popular for a reason, but a few steps along a quieter trail can give you the same view with far more peace. Let other groups pass. Wait for a gap in the voices. The mountains have been here far longer than any line of visitors, and they reward a little patience.

There is no need to hurry in this place. Find a railing, or a quiet bend in the path, and rest there for a while. Watch the mist move across the towers, hiding them and revealing them again. This rock has stood for millions of years, and it will stand long after you have gone home. For a few slow minutes, you are simply here with it, sharing the same air.

Key facts

Opened in 1982 as China's first national forest park.
Part of Wulingyuan, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1992.
More than three thousand quartz sandstone pillars fill the park.
Some stone towers rise over two hundred meters tall.
A pillar was renamed after the 2009 film Avatar.

What to look for

Pine trees rooted on the bare tops of pillars.
Clouds hiding the bases so peaks appear to float.
Monkeys sitting calmly on the trail railings.
Narrow gaps where two towers nearly touch overhead.

Practical tips

Arrive early to walk the trails before the largest crowds.
Take a cable car up, then walk the quieter paths down.
Keep food sealed in your bag near the wild monkeys.
Bring a light jacket, since the higher air stays cool.
