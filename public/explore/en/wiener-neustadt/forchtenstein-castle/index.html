The guide

The story

Feel the slope under your feet as you climb toward the gate. The road bends, the trees thin, and the castle comes into view, high on its spur of rock. The air is cooler up here, often with a breeze off the wooded hills. Listen for a moment. You may hear birds in the forest below, footsteps on stone, and a heavy wooden door somewhere inside. This is Forchtenstein, a fortress that has watched this border country for many centuries.

First, a word about where you are. Wiener Neustadt lies just across the hills in Lower Austria. Forchtenstein stands in Burgenland, on the eastern side of the Rosalia hills. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. For most of its long life, this castle was not in Austria at all. Burgenland was part of the Kingdom of Hungary until after the First World War. It joined Austria in the early 1920s. So this was a Hungarian frontier fortress, facing west toward Vienna.

The castle's story begins in the Middle Ages. A local noble family, the lords of Mattersdorf, built an early fortress on this rock around the fourteenth century. They took the name of the castle as part of their own title. From this height they controlled the land and the routes through the hills. The position made sense. Steep ground protects the site on several sides, and the view reaches far across the plain. Any army or traveller approaching could be seen long before they arrived.

Over time, the castle passed out of that family's hands. It came under the control of the Habsburgs, the dynasty that ruled Austria and, later, Hungary. In the early seventeenth century, the castle was pledged to a rising Hungarian noble family — the Esterházys. That family soon became the permanent owner. And here is a remarkable fact. The Esterházy family and its foundation still own Forchtenstein today, roughly four hundred years later. Very few castles in Europe have stayed with one family for so long.

The Esterházys turned a medieval stronghold into something larger and more modern. During the seventeenth century, the castle was rebuilt and expanded. Prince Paul Esterházy, an important figure in the family, shaped much of what you see now. Look at the thick walls and the bastions, the angled defensive platforms built for cannon. These were the new military ideas of that age. Gunpowder had changed war. Tall, thin medieval walls no longer stopped an enemy. Low, heavy, angled walls did.

Why so much effort? The answer lies to the east and south. In these centuries, the Ottoman Empire, the Turkish power centred in Istanbul, pushed deep into Central Europe. Ottoman armies besieged Vienna more than once. This border region suffered raids, fires, and fear. Forchtenstein was a place of refuge and a symbol of resistance. The castle is often described as never having been taken by force. Its walls, its height, and its preparation kept attackers outside.

As you walk the courtyards, pay attention to the well. Every castle under siege needs water, and on top of a rock that is hard to find. The well here goes very deep into the stone. Locals tell a well-known tale about it. According to the story, Turkish prisoners of war dug this shaft through the rock over many years. It is a popular legend, and it is told here often. Treat it as a story of the place rather than a documented record. Either way, stand near it and imagine the labour it took.

Now to the collections, which are the real heart of a visit. The Esterházys did not only defend this castle. They also used it as a safe place for their most valuable possessions. Over generations, the family stored weapons, armour, portraits, and treasures behind these walls. Because the castle stayed with one family, much of this material simply stayed here. That is the second surprise. Many collections in Europe were sold, scattered, or looted. A large part of this one remained together.

The armoury is one of the largest private collections of arms in Europe. You will see rows of armour, swords, pistols, and long guns. Some pieces were for war. Others were for ceremony and display, decorated with fine metalwork. There are also objects connected to the wars against the Ottomans, including items described as Turkish trophies, taken during those campaigns. Look closely at the textiles and the decoration. They show how much these conflicts shaped the family's sense of itself.

The ancestral gallery shows another side of the family. Here, painted portraits line the walls — generation after generation of Esterházys. Some show warriors in armour. Others show nobles in rich Hungarian dress, with fur, braiding, and jewels. These paintings had a purpose. They told every visitor that this family was old, powerful, and loyal. Notice how the poses repeat. A hand on a sword. A proud, steady gaze. It is a family telling its own story.

The treasury holds the most delicate pieces. It is in the spirit of a cabinet of curiosities, a type of collection that European nobles loved in the seventeenth century. Such cabinets mixed art, science, and the strange. You may see gold and silver work, clocks, jewellery, carved objects, and natural rarities. The idea was to gather the whole world into a few rooms. Move slowly here. The small details are the point, and they are easy to miss in a hurry.

When you step back outside, look out from the walls. The landscape opens toward the east, across fields and villages toward the Hungarian plain. The wooded Rosalia hills rise behind you. In summer, the castle also hosts cultural events, and the courtyards can fill with music and audiences. On a quiet day, though, the sounds are simple — wind, birds, and the distant hum of the valley. It is easy to understand why soldiers once kept watch from exactly where you stand.

Before you leave, pause in the courtyard once more. Look up at the walls, the small windows, and the rooflines against the sky. For centuries, this was a place of fear, defence, and safekeeping. Now it is a place of slow walking and close looking. As you head down the hill, turn back once at the bend in the road. Watch the castle sit on its rock above the trees, the same view travellers have had for hundreds of years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep climb and stone floors.
Check ahead whether collections need separate guided tours.
Allow at least two hours to see the main collections.
Bring a light jacket; the hilltop is often breezy.
Combine the visit with a walk in the Rosalia hills.
