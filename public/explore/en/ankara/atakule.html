The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the tower and tilt your head back. A smooth concrete shaft rises from the hilltop, plain and pale, and at the very top sits a wide glassy crown. Up here in Çankaya the air often feels a little thinner and drier than in the valley below. The traffic of the boulevard comes up the hill as a low hum. The tower stands above all of it, the one vertical line that holds the southern skyline together.

This is Atakule. The name joins two Turkish words. Ata means father or ancestor, and in Turkey it also points quietly toward Atatürk, the founder of the republic. Kule simply means tower. So the name carries a small salute to the man who made Ankara the capital. For many people in this city, the tower is less a monument than a landmark for giving directions, a reference point you can see from far across town.

To understand why a tower stands here, it helps to remember what Ankara was a century ago. In 1923 it became the capital of the new Turkish Republic. At that time it was a modest Anatolian town around an old citadel. Planners then laid out a new city of ministries, wide boulevards and green parks. Over the following decades, the government district and the embassies spread south, up toward these hills of Çankaya.

By the 1980s, Ankara had grown into a large modern capital, and it wanted a symbol to match. Atakule was the answer. The architect was Ragıp Buluç, and the tower opened to the public in 1989. It rises to roughly 125 metres. That is not tall by the standards of today's glass skyscrapers, but its position on high ground gives it far more presence than its height alone would suggest.

Look closely at the shape. The design is very simple — a single slender core, and then a broad head that holds the viewing levels and the restaurant. There is very little decoration. The form belongs to its decade, when television towers and observation towers around the world followed a similar idea. A tall stem, a crown of glass, and a view for everyone who rides to the top.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. For years, the restaurant near the top was known for rotating slowly. You could sit down for a meal and, by the end of dessert, the whole city had turned around you. Families came for birthdays and anniversaries. Couples came for first dates. For a generation of Ankara residents, a dinner at the top of Atakule was a special occasion, something remembered long afterwards.

At the base, the tower was never alone. A shopping centre grew around its foot, and for a time it was one of the city's best-known places to shop and meet friends. As Ankara added larger malls in newer districts, the old complex lost some of its crowd. Eventually the tower closed for a long renovation. The surrounding buildings were rebuilt, and the tower reopened to visitors in the late 2010s with a renewed base.

This gives Atakule an unusual double life. The shaft and crown are the original structure from the 1980s. Much of what surrounds them at ground level is newer. If you walk around slowly, you can feel the difference between the two eras — the older concrete rising out of the fresher stone, glass and paving below. It is a quiet lesson in how cities keep their landmarks while changing everything around them.

Now think about the view from the top, whether you go up today or simply imagine it. Ankara spreads in every direction across a wide bowl of hills. On a clear day you can follow the long lines of the boulevards running north toward the old centre. Far away, the citadel sits on its rocky hill. The city looks less like a single town and more like many neighbourhoods poured across the land.

The light here changes the tower all day. In the morning, the pale concrete looks almost white against the sky. In the late afternoon, the Anatolian sun turns it warmer, a soft gold. After dark, the crown is lit, and the tower becomes a bright point you can spot from many parts of the city. Ankara has long winters, and on snowy evenings the glowing head above the white rooftops is a sight locals know well.

The neighbourhood around you also tells a story. Çankaya is the district of embassies, official residences and quiet tree-lined streets. Not far from here, small parks and cafés fill the slopes. Diplomats, students and families share the same pavements. Walk a few streets in any direction and you will see the calm, residential side of the capital — very different from the busy markets near the old town in the north.

And there is one more surprise. People often expect a capital city to be defined by palaces or cathedrals. Ankara is different. Its most familiar silhouette is a modern tower from the end of the twentieth century. That choice says something about the republic itself — a state that wanted to look forward, to build new things on new ground, rather than live only among the monuments of older empires.

Before you move on, take one last look upward from where you stand. Notice how the shaft seems to narrow as your eyes climb, and how the crown catches the sky. Listen to the city below, the buses, the voices, the wind on the hill. As you walk away, turn around once or twice. You will find that the tower is still there behind you, watching over the streets of Çankaya, the way it has for decades.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride to the top on a clear day for distant views.
Return after dark to see the crown lit up.
Book ahead if you plan to dine near the top.
Walk nearby Çankaya side streets for quieter cafés.
Bring a light layer since the hilltop can be windy.
