The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Up here on the slopes of Çankaya, the traffic of Ankara drops to a low hum below you. The air is dry and carries a faint smell of pine and dust. The ground rises gently, and the city spreads out in pale stone and concrete toward the horizon. A century ago, this hill was not the centre of anything. It was a quiet place of vineyards and summer houses, far from the old town around the citadel.

That changes the way you should picture the mansion. Before it became a seat of power, it was a vineyard house. Wealthy families in Ankara kept houses like this out on the hills. They came here in summer for cleaner air and for their grapes. This particular house belonged to the Kasapyan family, an Armenian merchant family from the city. It was a modest country home, built of stone, with simple lines and a view across the valley.

In 1921, the house changed hands, and its story changed with it. Mustafa Kemal, the commander who would later be known as Atatürk, moved in that year. At that time, the Turkish War of Independence was still being fought. Ankara was a small Anatolian town, and the new movement had chosen it as its base. Atatürk needed a residence close to the town, but at a safe and quiet distance. This hill gave him both.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The building where a new state was planned did not look like a palace at all. It was a farmhouse with a few rooms and a garden. Meetings, late-night discussions, and long dinners took place inside these modest walls. When Ankara was declared the capital of the new Republic in 1923, the vineyard house was already its unofficial heart. Power had arrived here before the grand buildings did.

The early years in the house also had a personal side. Latife Hanım, Atatürk's wife, lived here with him in the mid-1920s. She was educated, spoke several languages, and took part in the social life of the residence. Their marriage was short and ended in divorce. Still, those years left a mark on the house. They show that this was a home with private routines, guests, and quarrels, not only a place of politics and state decisions.

As the Republic grew, the old house became too small. A new presidential residence was built nearby in the early 1930s. It was designed by Clemens Holzmeister, an Austrian architect. He shaped much of official Ankara in those decades, including major government buildings in the city's ministerial quarter. His style was sober and solid, with clean geometry and little decoration. The new mansion followed that approach. It was meant to look serious and modern, a clear break from Ottoman palace traditions.

That contrast is worth a moment of thought. On one side was the rough stone vineyard house of the war years. On the other side was a new building in a confident European modern style. Together they tell the story of a state that moved from improvisation to institutions in roughly a decade. The original house was not torn down. It was kept and later opened as a museum dedicated to Atatürk, often called the Atatürk Museum Mansion.

Here is the second surprise. In Turkish political language, the word Çankaya came to mean far more than a hill. For decades, newspapers wrote simply that Çankaya had decided something, or that a politician had gone up to Çankaya. Everyone understood that this meant the presidency. A neighbourhood name became shorthand for the highest office in the country. Even today, many people in Türkiye still hear the word this way, as a kind of political code.

For most of the twentieth century and into the twenty-first, Turkish presidents lived and worked here. Heads of state from other countries came up this hill for official visits. Ceremonies, receptions, and quiet negotiations took place behind the walls. Then, in 2014, the presidency moved to a much larger new complex in another part of Ankara. Çankaya stepped back from the centre of power. The buildings remain in state use, and the grounds are still guarded.

That is why your view of the mansion depends on how and when you come. Much of the compound is closed to the public, and security is visible around the perimeter. Access to the museum house has changed over the years, and visits may need advance arrangements. It is wise to check current conditions before you plan your day. From outside, you can still take in the setting, the walls, the trees, and the shape of the hill itself.

Look around at the neighbourhood while you are here. Çankaya is now the name of a whole district of Ankara, home to embassies, ministries, and quiet residential streets. Many foreign missions chose this area because it sat near the seat of government. Tall trees line the roads, and high walls hide gardens from the street. The mix of guarded gates, diplomatic flags, and ordinary apartment blocks gives the area a calm, slightly formal atmosphere, very different from the older streets near the citadel.

Think, too, about the light. Ankara sits on a high plateau, so the sky here often feels wide and bright. In the late afternoon, the sun turns the pale stone and stucco of the city a warm colour. From the slopes, you can understand why a family once chose this place for summer evenings. The same qualities that suited a vineyard house, the air, the height, and the distance from the crowds, also suited a leader who needed space to think.

The story of this hill is, in many ways, the story of the capital itself. A small town became the heart of a new state. A farmhouse became a residence, then a museum. A neighbourhood name became a word for national power, and then slowly became a place name again. Few cities show their transformation so clearly in a single location. Here, you can see the change from vineyard to republic within a short walk.

Before you leave, pause and look back up the slope. Notice the trees above the walls, the quiet of the street, and the city spreading out below. Somewhere behind those walls stands the old stone house where it all began. As you walk away, listen for the hum of Ankara rising again around you. It is the sound of the capital that grew up around this hill.
