The guide

The story

Before you go underground, notice the ground itself. It is soft, grassy and gently rounded, a low hill rising from the flat farmland of northeastern Bulgaria. Wind moves across the fields. Birds call from the nearby woods. Nothing on the surface tells you what is inside. Yet under this earth is one of the most unusual rooms of the ancient world. People built it more than two thousand years ago, and almost nobody saw it until the late twentieth century.

You are standing near the village of Sveshtari, in the region of Razgrad, not far from the small town of Isperih. This mound is one of many in the area. Archaeologists counted dozens of these burial hills across the surrounding landscape. Each one is a man-made heap of earth raised over the dead. Most are modest. This one held something extraordinary, and it changed how historians understand the people who once ruled these plains.

Those people were the Getae. They were a northern branch of the Thracians, a large family of tribes that lived across much of the Balkans in antiquity. The Thracians left no long written histories of their own. Much of what we know comes from Greek writers, who often saw them as fierce horsemen and skilled metalworkers. Tombs like this one fill the silence. They show a society with wealth, ambition, and a strong belief that death was a passage, not an end.

The discovery came in 1982. Archaeologists working in the area opened the mound and found a stone structure inside. It was built from carefully cut limestone blocks, fitted together with real precision. Only a few years later, in 1985, UNESCO added the tomb to its World Heritage List. That is a remarkably fast recognition. It shows how quickly specialists understood that this was not an ordinary grave, but a rare work of art and architecture.

The tomb dates to the early third century before Christ. This was the generation after Alexander the Great, when Greek styles spread across a huge area. You can feel that influence here. But the builders did not simply copy Greek models. They mixed outside ideas with local beliefs, and the result looks like nothing else. Keep that in mind as you walk inside. You are looking at a Thracian answer to the wider Mediterranean world.

The tomb has three rooms. First comes a corridor and an entrance chamber. A side chamber opens off to one side. Then comes the main burial chamber, the heart of the monument. The rooms are covered by stone vaults, and the air inside is cool and still. Visits are kept small and short for a good reason. Human breath and body heat change the humidity, and the painted and carved surfaces are very fragile.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the side chamber, archaeologists found the remains of horses. For the Thracians, the horse was more than an animal. It was a symbol of power, of the warrior class, and of the journey into the afterlife. Burying horses beside a ruler was a way to send him onward with honour. Stand in that small room for a moment, and the ancient world feels very close.

Now step into the main chamber. This is the room that made the tomb famous. Along the walls stand ten female figures, carved in high relief from the stone. They are caryatids, a Greek term for columns or supports shaped like women. Their arms are raised, as if they hold up the ceiling above them. But look closely at their lower bodies. Their long skirts spread out like leaves or plant forms. They are half woman, half plant.

No two of them are exactly alike. The faces, the folds of the clothing, and the position of the arms vary slightly from figure to figure. Traces of paint survive on the stone, so they were once more colourful than they look today. Scholars still discuss what these figures mean. Many see them as connected to a great goddess of nature and rebirth. That idea fits a tomb built around the hope of new life after death.

Above the figures, in a curved space at the top of the wall, there is a painting. It is faded, but you can still make out the scene. A rider on horseback approaches a female figure, who appears to hold out a wreath or crown towards him. Many researchers read this as the moment a goddess grants divine power to the ruler. If that reading is correct, the dead king was being shown as a hero, raised up among the gods.

Who was buried here? Nobody knows for certain. Close by lie the ruins of a large fortified ancient city. Some scholars connect it with Helis, a Getic capital mentioned by ancient writers. One popular theory links the tomb to the circle of a Getic king named Dromichaetes, who is remembered for capturing a Macedonian general. It is an attractive idea, but it remains a hypothesis. The tomb itself carries no inscription naming its owner.

The tomb belongs to a larger protected landscape called Sboryanovo. Around you there are more mounds, the remains of the ancient city, and forest paths through oak woods. Not far away stands Demir Baba Teke, a later shrine of a Muslim dervish community. It is still visited by people of different faiths. In one small area, then, you can trace more than two thousand years of sacred places. This land kept drawing people who wanted to honour the dead and the divine.

When you climb back out into the daylight, pause for a moment. Your eyes will need time to adjust. Look again at the mound from outside. It seems simple, almost invisible in the countryside. That was partly the point. The Thracians hid their most precious rooms under earth, for their dead and for their gods, not for us. That we can stand inside one at all is a quiet piece of luck.

As you walk back towards the car park or the trail, listen to the fields and the trees. Look at the other low hills on the horizon. Some have been studied. Others still keep their secrets. Somewhere under this landscape there may be more rooms of stone, more painted walls, more figures with raised arms. For now, carry the image of those ten women with you... still holding up their ceiling in the dark.
