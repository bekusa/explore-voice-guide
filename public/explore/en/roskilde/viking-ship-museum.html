The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gulls call over the water. Halyards tap against wooden masts. Somewhere nearby, an axe strikes timber with a dull, steady rhythm. The air smells of salt, tar and fresh-cut wood. You are standing on the shore of Roskilde Fjord, a long arm of the sea that reaches deep into the Danish island of Zealand. Almost a thousand years ago, this quiet water was a defensive line. The story of this museum begins out there, under the surface.

In the eleventh century, Roskilde was a powerful place. It was a royal centre and an important seat of the church. A town like that needed protection, and the fjord was its front door. So the people of the time made a hard decision. They took five working ships, filled them with stones, and sank them on purpose. They placed them in a narrow channel near a place called Skuldelev, about twenty kilometres north of here. The barrier blocked the main sailing route toward the town.

For centuries, local fishermen knew there was something odd in that channel. Legend has it that people believed one wreck was a ship sunk on the orders of Queen Margaret, a medieval Danish ruler. That story turned out to be wrong. The truth was older and much stranger. In 1962, archaeologists built a temporary dam around the site and pumped the water out. Then they could walk on the seabed and lift the wrecks, piece by piece, by hand.

What they found was not one ship but a small fleet of different vessels. This is the first surprise for many visitors. The Vikings did not sail only the long, narrow warships we see in films. They built boats for many jobs, the way we build trucks, vans and family cars. The ships were later numbered one to six, but you will find no number four. Researchers first thought they had two wrecks, and then realised the pieces came from the same ship.

Now step inside the main hall. The room is quiet and full of soft, grey Scandinavian light. A wall of glass faces the fjord, so the ships rest beside the same water they once sailed. Do not expect complete hulls. What you see are the surviving timbers, dark and fragile, mounted on slim steel frames. Those frames trace the missing lines of each hull. Your eye fills in the rest, and the ships seem to rise out of the air.

Look first for the ocean-going cargo ship, known as Skuldelev 1. It is broad, deep and sturdy. It was built of pine in western Norway and carried goods across open sea. Ships like this one linked Scandinavia with distant shores in the North Atlantic. It needed only a small crew. Its strength was its load, not its speed. Look at the thick planks and imagine the barrels, furs, timber and wool it once carried through rough water.

Then find the great longship, Skuldelev 2. It is the warship of the group, and it measures roughly thirty metres from end to end. Here is the second surprise. Studies of the wood showed that this ship was built near Dublin, in Ireland, from Irish oak. In the eleventh century, Dublin was a Viking town. So this ship was made far away, sailed across the North Sea, and ended its life as part of a Danish barrier.

The other three wrecks are smaller and more modest. There is a coastal trader, light and easy to handle, that worked the inland waters of Denmark. There is a small warship, a lean vessel for oars and speed. And there is a boat that seems to have been used for fishing and carrying goods. Together they show daily life on the water. They were ordinary boats for ordinary people. That is exactly what makes them precious.

You may wonder how such old wood survived. Waterlogged timber can look solid, but it collapses as it dries. Conservators treated the timbers with a waxy chemical that slowly replaced the water inside the wood. Then they dried the pieces with great care. After that came the puzzle work, fitting thousands of fragments back into the shape of a hull. The museum opened in 1969, and those timbers have rested in this hall ever since.

Now walk outside, across to the museum island. This area was built in the 1990s, and the construction work brought more surprises. Diggers uncovered the remains of nine more ships buried in the old harbour. One of them is the longest Viking warship ever found. It was a lucky accident. The museum had planned a harbour, and the ground itself added a new chapter to the story of the site.

On the island you will find the boatyard. This is where that sound of the axe comes from. Shipwrights here build full-size copies of Viking boats using the methods of the past. They split logs instead of sawing them. They shape planks with axes and overlap them, like tiles on a roof. They use iron rivets, wool and tar. Each new boat is also an experiment. It tests ideas about how the original ships were built and sailed.

The most famous of these copies is a reconstruction of the Dublin-built longship. It is called the Sea Stallion from Glendalough. In 2007, a crew sailed it across the North Sea and around Scotland to Dublin. The next year they sailed it home again. The voyage was cold, wet and exhausting. The crew learned what the original sailors faced in open water. Their notes taught historians more than any drawing could.

Before you leave, go back to the water's edge. Look out over the fjord toward the north, where the barrier once lay. The water is calm and grey-blue, and small boats slip past with their sails up. Behind you, the old timbers rest in their quiet hall. Ahead of you, new wooden hulls rock gently at the jetty. As you walk away, listen once more for that axe. The same craft is still at work here, one plank at a time.

Good to know

Key facts

Five ships were deliberately sunk to block Roskilde Fjord.
The wrecks were raised from a drained, dammed seabed in 1962.
The great longship was built near Dublin from Irish oak.
There is no Skuldelev 4; two finds were one ship.
Nine more ships turned up during 1990s harbour construction.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the ship hall before exploring the outdoor boatyard.
Visit the boatyard on weekdays to watch shipwrights at work.
Bring a windproof layer; the fjord shore gets chilly.
Ask staff about seasonal sailing trips in reconstructed boats.
Allow time for the short walk from the town centre.
