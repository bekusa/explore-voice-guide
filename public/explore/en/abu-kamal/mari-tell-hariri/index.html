The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves across the mound and carries fine, pale dust. Underfoot, the ground is soft and crumbly. It is not quite soil and not quite stone. It is mud brick, melted by thousands of years of rain and sun. To the east, a green line of fields marks the Euphrates. Everything else is the colour of dry bread. You are standing on what remains of Mari, one of the great cities of the ancient Near East.

This place has a modern name, too. Locals call the mound Tell Hariri. A tell is an artificial hill, built up as people rebuilt their homes on top of older ruins, layer after layer. Mari sits on the western bank of the Euphrates, a short distance northwest of Abu Kamal, close to the border with Iraq. In ancient times this position mattered enormously. Whoever held this stretch of river could control trade moving between Mesopotamia and the lands to the west.

The story of the excavation begins with an accident. In 1933, local people were digging on the mound, by most accounts to bury someone. Their tools struck a carved stone statue. The find was reported to the French authorities, who then governed Syria. Within months, an archaeological team arrived, led by the French scholar André Parrot. He would return to this mound for decades. After him came other French directors, and the work continued into the early twenty-first century.

Mari is very old. The city was founded around the beginning of the third millennium before the Common Era. That is nearly five thousand years ago. It was not a village that slowly grew. Archaeologists believe it was planned from the start. Excavators described a roughly round layout, with protective works against floods and links to the river for water and boats. Think of it as an early example of deliberate city design — long before most of the world had cities at all.

Here is the first surprise. The most famous thing found at Mari is not a building. It is a library of clay. In the palace, excavators found a huge royal archive, numbering in the tens of thousands of cuneiform tablets. Cuneiform is the wedge-shaped writing pressed into wet clay with a reed. Most of these tablets were written in Akkadian, the diplomatic language of the time. They are letters, accounts, legal records, and reports.

Those tablets changed how historians understand this whole region. They show kings writing to other kings. They show officials complaining about supplies, describing troop movements, and reporting on omens. There are records of food, wine, textiles, and visiting messengers. Some letters even record prophets delivering messages to the king. For scholars, the Mari archive is like opening someone's office drawers, nearly four thousand years later. Ordinary, practical worries sit beside affairs of state.

The archive belonged mostly to the city's final great period. Its last famous ruler was King Zimri-Lim. His palace was enormous, with roughly three hundred rooms arranged around courtyards. It held throne rooms, storerooms, workshops, kitchens, and living quarters. Visitors in the ancient world talked about it. One tablet records a distant ruler asking if his son could come and see the palace for himself. Even then, it seems, people travelled to look at Mari.

The palace walls were once painted. Fragments of wall paintings survived, and one of them is often called the Investiture of Zimri-Lim. It shows the king receiving symbols of royal power from the goddess Ishtar, surrounded by trees, animals, and guardian figures. The colours were reds, blues, and ochres. Today, that painting is in the Louvre in Paris. As you look at the bare brown walls here, try to picture those colours in the courtyards.

Mari also produced remarkable sculpture. Many small statues show worshippers with clasped hands and wide, inlaid eyes. They were placed in temples to pray on their owner's behalf, forever. The best known shows an official named Ebih-Il, seated, wearing a skirt of woolly tufts, with eyes of shell and blue lapis lazuli. That figure is also in the Louvre. Another famous statue, a goddess holding a vase from which water once flowed, went to the museum in Aleppo.

Then came the end. Mari was caught between powerful neighbours. For a time, Zimri-Lim was an ally of Hammurabi, the king of Babylon, famous for his law code. The alliance did not last. In the eighteenth century before the Common Era, Hammurabi's forces conquered Mari and later destroyed it. The palace burned. And here is the second surprise — the fire baked many of the clay tablets hard, helping them survive. Destruction preserved the archive.

After that, Mari never returned as a major power. People lived on parts of the mound later, but the great city was gone. Sand and silt covered the ruins. Rain slowly dissolved the exposed mud brick. Look at the low walls around you. Their rounded tops are the result of erosion. The excavators built protective coverings over parts of the palace to slow that damage. Mud brick is easy to make, but it is fragile once it is uncovered.

The recent past has been hard on this place. During the Syrian war that began in 2011, armed groups controlled this area. Satellite images and later reports showed widespread illegal digging across the site. Looters searched for objects to sell. Parts of the palace and temples were damaged. You may notice holes and disturbed earth in places where no archaeologist worked. They are part of Mari's story now, too — a modern layer on top of ancient ones.

So take a moment before you leave. Turn toward the river and feel the wind again. The same river fed this city's fields, carried its boats, and brought its messengers. Look at the walls beside you and notice the thin lines of individual bricks. Each one was shaped by hand, almost five thousand years ago. As you walk back down the mound, listen to the dust under your shoes. You are walking on the city itself.
