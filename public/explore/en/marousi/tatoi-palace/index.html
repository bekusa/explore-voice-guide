The guide

The story

Listen first. Pine needles crack underfoot, and cicadas hum somewhere above you in the branches. The air here is cooler than in the city below, and it smells of resin and dry earth. Through the trees, pale walls and steep roofs appear. They look more like a country house from northern Europe than a building in Greece. This is Tatoi, the former summer estate of the Greek royal family, on the lower slopes of Mount Parnitha.

To understand why a palace stands in this forest, start with the family who built it. In 1863, a young Danish prince became King George the First of Greece. He came from a cold, green country of farms and timber houses. The Athens summer was hard for him and his family. Within a few years, he began looking for land in the hills north of the capital, where the air was fresher and the shade was deep.

He found it here. In the 1870s, the royal family acquired the Tatoi estate, and over the following decades they shaped it into a working country retreat. This was never meant to be a grand ceremonial palace like those in Vienna or Saint Petersburg. It was a place for summers, for walking, for riding, and for raising children away from the formal life of the court in central Athens.

Look at the main house. Its style surprises many visitors. The steep roofs, the timber details and the overall shape carry the taste of northern Europe, not the classical columns that Athens is known for. A Danish king, far from home, built something that felt familiar to him. Standing here, you are looking at a small piece of Scandinavian and English country taste, set down in a Greek pine forest.

Around the palace, the royal family built more than a residence. The estate had stables, farm buildings, a dairy and vineyards. Tatoi produced its own wine and agricultural goods. In other words, this was a functioning rural estate, with workers, animals and seasonal rhythms. As you walk, notice how many of the buildings are practical rather than decorative. They tell the story of a household that lived, worked and ate from this land.

The ground under this forest is also much older than the monarchy. Nearby lies the site of ancient Dekeleia. In 413 BC, during the long war between Athens and Sparta, the Spartans fortified Dekeleia. From this hillside, they could cut Athens off from its farms and silver mines. So these quiet slopes once held an enemy army, watching the great city across the plain.

Now, a story from the palace years that sounds almost too strange to be true... but it is documented history. In 1920, King Alexander was walking in the grounds here with his dog. A pet monkey attacked the dog, and when the king tried to separate them, he was bitten. The wound became infected. Within weeks, the young king was dead. His death changed the course of Greek politics, and it all began in this forest.

Fire is another part of this estate's story. In the summer of 1916, a large fire swept through the Tatoi forest while the royal family was here. King Constantine the First escaped, but the danger was real, and the cause was debated for years. More than a century later, in 2021, wildfires again reached the estate. As you walk, you may see blackened trunks or areas of young growth — signs of how this forest keeps recovering.

In the woods, a short walk from the palace, there is a place many visitors miss: the royal cemetery. Members of the Greek royal family are buried here, among the pines, in a setting that feels more like a quiet chapel garden than a state monument. King Alexander lies here. So does the last king of Greece, Constantine the Second, who was buried at Tatoi in January 2023. If you visit the graves, please treat the area with quiet respect.

The monarchy ended after a referendum in 1974. What happened to Tatoi after that became a long and complicated question. The Greek state and the former royal family disputed ownership of the estate for many years, and the case eventually reached the European Court of Human Rights. In the end, Tatoi passed into public hands. For decades, the palace stood mostly closed, and many buildings slowly fell into decay.

Today, the estate is in a new chapter. The Greek state has been restoring the palace and its surrounding buildings, with the aim of turning Tatoi into a museum and a public park. Some areas are open, while others remain under repair. Depending on when you visit, you may see scaffolding, fresh paint, or rooms that have just reopened. You are seeing the estate in the middle of its return to public life.

As you walk the paths, pay attention to the change between open lawns and dense forest. The royal family planted and managed these grounds carefully, so the landscape itself is part of the design. Old trees line some of the roads. Benches and clearings invite you to stop. On a clear day, gaps between the trees open toward the Attica plain, and you can understand why a family tired of city heat came here every summer.

Before you leave, pause somewhere in the shade. Notice the temperature, a few degrees cooler than Athens. Listen to the wind in the pines, and the sound of gravel under other visitors' shoes. Look once more at those northern roofs among the Greek trees. Kings, workers and children once walked these same paths. Now the estate belongs to everyone, and the forest goes on growing around it as you walk away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and forest trails.
Bring water, as the grounds are large and spread out.
Check ahead which buildings are open during restoration.
Keep voices low near the royal cemetery in the woods.
Visit early to enjoy cool air under the pines.
