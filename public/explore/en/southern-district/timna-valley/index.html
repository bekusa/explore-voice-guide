The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here in the far south of Israel, the silence has weight. The ground under your feet is loose and gritty, sand mixed with crushed stone. Around you, the cliffs glow red, ochre, and purple, and the air feels dry enough to taste. This is the Timna Valley, a broad, horseshoe-shaped basin in the Arava desert, north of Eilat. It looks empty at first glance. In fact, it is one of the oldest industrial landscapes on Earth.

The reason is copper. Long before anyone wrote history here, people learned that some of these green-streaked rocks held metal. Archaeologists have traced mining and smelting in this valley back thousands of years, to the Chalcolithic period. That name simply means the Copper Stone Age, the era when people first mixed stone tools with metal ones. So when you look at these hills, you are looking at a very early workplace. People came here to dig, to burn, and to carry home something new.

As you walk, watch the ground. In many places you will see small round depressions and dark openings in the rock. These are ancient mine shafts and pits. Miners cut them by hand, often with stone and later metal tools. Some shafts are narrow, with small footholds carved into the walls so a worker could climb down. Imagine the heat at midday, the dust, the dim light below. This was hard, dangerous work, repeated over many generations.

Now look for patches of black, glassy rubble. That is slag, the waste left over after copper ore was heated in furnaces. Smelting needed very high temperatures, so workers used charcoal and bellows to push air into the fire. The slag heaps tell archaeologists where furnaces stood and how much metal was produced. Here is a detail most visitors miss. Excavators also found ancient donkey dung, preserved by the dry desert air. It has helped researchers understand how animals were fed and how the camps were supplied.

For much of the twentieth century, this valley carried a famous name: King Solomon's Mines. The American archaeologist Nelson Glueck linked the copper works to the biblical king, and the name stuck. Later excavations, led by Beno Rothenberg, found strong evidence of Egyptian activity here instead, dating to the New Kingdom, the age of the great pharaohs. For a while, the Solomon connection looked like a romantic mistake. But the story did not end there.

More recent research, using careful radiocarbon dating, has shown that major copper production also took place around the tenth century before Christ. That is roughly the period the Bible gives for Solomon. Many researchers now connect this work with the Edomites, a desert people who lived in the region, rather than with Jerusalem directly. The debate continues. What is clear is that the valley was busy, organised, and important across several different eras. Each new dig adds a layer.

Turn now toward the great sandstone cliffs known as Solomon's Pillars. These tall, rounded columns were not built by anyone. Wind and water carved them over a very long time, cutting deep vertical grooves into the rock face. In the right light, they look almost like the columns of a temple. The name comes from that old Solomon legend, not from any ancient record. Stand back from the cliff, and notice how the shadows change the shape of each pillar.

At the foot of these pillars sits one of the valley's real surprises. Excavators found the remains of a small Egyptian shrine dedicated to Hathor. She was an Egyptian goddess linked with joy, music, and mining, and was sometimes called the Lady of Turquoise. Miners far from home would leave offerings here, asking for protection. A carved image associated with the goddess was cut into the rock nearby. Think about that. In this remote desert, workers built a small sacred corner for themselves.

After the Egyptians left, the shrine seems to have had a second life. Archaeologists believe it was later used by Midianites, a people mentioned in the Bible as desert dwellers in this region. Among the finds was a small copper snake. Some scholars connect it to the biblical story of the bronze serpent, though that link is an interpretation, not a proven fact. Either way, it is a striking object to imagine here, glinting in the hand of a worshipper beside these cliffs.

The valley also holds rock drawings. On some sandstone surfaces, ancient people engraved figures of animals, hunters, and even chariots. The chariot images are especially interesting, because they show warriors or travellers with wheels and horses in a place where roads were almost nonexistent. Look closely and patiently. The engravings are faint, and they appear best in low, slanting light. These scratched lines are among the most direct human traces in the whole valley.

Timna is not only about history. It is also a geological show. Look for the Mushroom, a large rock that stands on a narrow base, shaped by wind erosion over a very long time. Elsewhere, natural arches open in the sandstone like windows. The colours come from minerals in the rock, especially iron and copper. Red, yellow, white, and green appear side by side. In the shade, the colours look soft. In full sun, they look almost painted.

The modern era left its mark too. In the twentieth century, Israel reopened copper mining in the area, using industrial methods instead of hand tools and charcoal. Today, the valley is a park, and the old landscape mixes with visitor trails, viewpoints, and an artificial lake. You may also see a full-size reconstruction of the biblical Tabernacle, the portable sanctuary described in the Book of Exodus. It is a model for visitors, not an ancient structure, but it helps people picture desert worship.

Take a final moment before you move on. Notice the stillness again, and the way the light shifts across the cliffs. Under these hills lie old shafts, cold furnaces, and the marks of people who came here for metal and stayed long enough to pray. As you walk away, look down at the stones by your shoes. A green stain, a black chip of slag... small signs that this quiet valley once worked very hard.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strongest desert heat.
Carry plenty of water, even for short walks.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose gravel and rocky paths.
Come in late afternoon for warmer light on the cliffs.
Use a car to move between the spread-out sites.
