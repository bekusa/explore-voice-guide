The guide

The story

Listen to the tyres first. On the approach, the sound changes from the rough grumble of city streets to a smooth, steady hum. Through the window, the land falls away. Below you there is grey-green water, wide mudflats, and a long low haze over the harbour. Ahead, a pale ribbon of road runs straight out to sea, held up by a long row of concrete piers. This is the Mumbai Trans Harbour Link, and for the next stretch you are travelling over the sea.

Officially, the bridge carries a longer name. It is the Atal Bihari Vajpayee Sewri–Nhava Sheva Atal Setu, named after the former Indian prime minister. Most people simply call it Atal Setu, or the MTHL. It links Sewri, on the eastern side of Mumbai's island city, with Chirle, near Nhava Sheva, on the mainland side of the harbour in the Navi Mumbai region. It opened to traffic in January 2024. On opening, it became the longest sea bridge in India.

The numbers help you picture the scale. The full link runs for roughly twenty-two kilometres. Around sixteen and a half kilometres of that crosses open water and tidal flats. The rest runs over land at each end, with ramps and interchanges. It is a six-lane road, three lanes in each direction. If you are crossing now, notice how long the sea section feels. For several minutes, there is almost nothing on either side but water and sky.

Why build it at all? For decades, Mumbai has been squeezed onto a narrow peninsula. The city's original seven islands were joined by land reclamation centuries ago, but space was always short. Navi Mumbai — the name simply means New Mumbai — was planned on the mainland in the late twentieth century to take some of that pressure. The problem was the harbour in between. Crossing it by road meant a long loop north through heavy traffic. This bridge cuts straight across instead.

People talked about a harbour crossing like this one for many decades before it was built. Plans appeared, stalled, and returned again. Construction finally began in the late 2010s, led by the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority, the planning body for the wider city area. Much of the funding came through a loan from Japan's international development agency. Engineering teams from India and abroad worked on different sections at the same time, across water that rises and falls with the tide.

Here is a detail most travellers miss. Look at the deck in some of the longer spans. Parts of the bridge use orthotropic steel decks — a kind of steel road surface stiffened underneath by a grid of ribs. This design is light and strong. It lets engineers make longer gaps between piers, which matters where ships need to pass. The bridge was among the first in India to use this method at such a scale. The steel sections were made in large pieces and lifted into place.

Now look down, especially near the Mumbai end. Those wide brown and silver mudflats near Sewri are not empty. In the cooler months, roughly from late autumn into spring, thousands of flamingos come here to feed. From the bridge, they can look like a pink smudge along the edge of the water. Their arrival is one of the quiet wonders of this city. Few people expect wild birds in such numbers so close to a container port and a city of millions.

The birds shaped the bridge. Environmental groups raised concerns about noise and light disturbing them during construction. In response, the designers added noise barriers along some sections. They also chose lighting meant to fall on the road, not spill out across the water. So the panels you may see along the edge are not only for the drivers. They are partly there for the flamingos and the other wading birds that feed below.

As you move further out, the view opens on both sides. To the south, the harbour widens towards the Arabian Sea. Somewhere in that direction lies Elephanta Island, home to ancient cave temples cut into the rock. On a clear day you may see its low green hills. Closer, you will likely see cargo ships at anchor, waiting for their turn at the docks. Mumbai's harbour has carried trade for centuries, and it is still working hard today.

Toward the mainland end, the large cranes of the port come into view. This is the Nhava Sheva area, home to one of India's busiest container ports. Huge stacks of coloured steel boxes sit in rows, red, blue, and white. Part of the purpose of this bridge was to connect the city more directly with the port, and with the new airport being developed in Navi Mumbai. So this road is not only for commuters. It is part of a larger plan to move goods and people faster.

The rules on the bridge tell you something about its purpose. It is built for fast, steady traffic. Motorbikes, auto-rickshaws, and slow farm vehicles are not allowed. Pedestrians cannot walk across. Stopping on the bridge is not permitted, not even for a quick photo. Tolls are collected electronically, so traffic does not need to wait at a booth. It is a strange feeling in Mumbai — a city of crowded lanes and constant horns — to move this smoothly, with so little noise.

The light out here changes quickly. In the early morning, the water can look silver, and the far shore fades into mist. At midday, the sun is hard and white on the concrete. Near sunset, the sky behind the city skyline turns orange and gold, and the piers cast long shadows across the water. During the monsoon season, heavy grey clouds roll in from the sea, and rain moves across the harbour like a curtain. The same crossing can feel completely different on any given day.

If you cannot stop on the bridge, where can you see it properly? The shoreline near Sewri gives a good view of the Mumbai end, especially during flamingo season. From the Navi Mumbai side, the long line of piers stretching toward the city looks almost endless. Seen from the land, the bridge is a thin pale line just above the water. It is a good reminder of how much engineering can disappear into a horizon when you look from far enough away.

So, as the road carries you on, take a last look out of the window. Watch the water slide past below, the slow ships, the line of the far shore. Listen again to the soft, even hum of the tyres. A few minutes ago, this trip would have meant a long crawl around the harbour. Now it is a quiet straight line across the sea. When you reach the other side, the noise of the city returns — but for a little while, you were somewhere in between.

Before you go

Practical tips

Sit on the south side for views toward Elephanta Island.
Keep your camera ready, since stopping is not allowed.
Cross near sunset for golden light behind the skyline.
Visit Sewri shoreline in winter to watch flamingos.
Arrange a car or taxi; motorbikes and rickshaws are banned.
