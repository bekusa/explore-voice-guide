The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the rock. It is dark basalt, cool even on a hot Mumbai afternoon, and rough like coarse sandpaper. Behind you there is traffic, auto-rickshaw horns and the hum of Andheri East. In front of you, people with iron tools once cut rooms straight into this hillside. They did not build these spaces. They carved them out of the hill, one blow at a time.

You are standing at the Mahakali Caves, also known as the Kondivite Caves. This is a group of Buddhist rock-cut monuments on a small basalt outcrop in the northern suburbs of the city. Most scholars date the earliest work here to the first century before the Common Era. Carving and use continued in stages for several centuries after that. So these rooms are far older than Mumbai as a city. They come from a time when this land was a scatter of villages, creeks and islands.

First, a small surprise about the name. Nothing here is connected to the goddess Mahakali. The caves are Buddhist. The name most likely comes from the local area and its road, which took the name of a Hindu shrine to Mahakali nearby. The older name, Kondivite, comes from a village that once stood on this ground. One place, two names, and each name tells a different part of its story.

The caves sit in two clusters. Most count about nineteen excavations in total. A smaller group faces roughly north, and a larger group runs along the other side of the rock. Many are simple. You will see a doorway, a small hall, and one or more square cells cut into the walls. These were viharas, a word that means a monastery or dwelling. Monks slept in these cells, meditated and studied here, and sheltered through the monsoon rains.

That monsoon detail matters. In early Buddhism, monks wandered for most of the year. During the rainy season, they gathered in one place and stayed. Rock-cut shelters like these gave them dry, safe rooms through months of heavy rain. Look at the cells and imagine one oil lamp, a thin mat on the stone floor, and the sound of water falling outside. It was a quiet, very simple life, lived inside the hill.

Now find the most important cave in the group. It is often numbered Cave 9, and it is the chaitya, the prayer hall. Inside it stands a stupa carved from the living rock. A stupa is a rounded, dome-shaped monument that holds sacred relics or represents the Buddha's presence. Monks walked around it in a slow circle as a form of worship. Even here, a few kilometres from a busy airport, the shape of that ritual is still clear.

Pay attention to the walls of this hall. Here the carving becomes more detailed. You will find relief panels showing seated and standing figures of the Buddha, and attendant figures beside them. These sculptures are generally thought to be later than the plain early cells. They belong to a period when Buddhist art in western India turned to rich images of the Buddha himself. In the oldest phase, artists often showed his presence only through symbols.

Here is a second detail many visitors miss. Look closely at some of the rock surfaces, and you may see faint lines of old script. Inscriptions in the ancient Brahmi script have been recorded at these caves. Brahmi is the ancestor of many writing systems used across South Asia today. Such inscriptions often record donations from monks, merchants or ordinary devotees who paid for a cell or a cistern. The stone keeps the memory of their gifts.

Water was always a problem on a rock like this. As you walk, look for small cuts and basins in the stone. Early communities at sites like this carved cisterns to collect monsoon rainwater. These held drinking water for the dry months. It is clever, practical engineering. Every rainfall became a store of water inside the same rock that gave the monks their rooms and their place of prayer.

It also helps to see this site in a wider picture. Mahakali is one of several rock-cut Buddhist sites in and around Mumbai. The best known is Kanheri, in the forest of Sanjay Gandhi National Park, a short distance to the north. Monks, pilgrims and traders moved along routes between the coast and the inland plateau. Small monasteries like this one likely served as resting points and local centres of practice on those routes.

Today, the site is protected as a national monument by the Archaeological Survey of India. But look up and around you. Apartment blocks, offices and slum settlements press close to the edge of the rock. Over the years, encroachment, weathering and neglect have damaged parts of the caves. That is the strange reality of this place. A monastery from the ancient world now sits inside one of the most crowded cities on Earth.

Because of this, the experience here is unusual. Mahakali rarely feels like a polished tourist attraction. On many days you may share it only with a few students, a family on a quiet outing, or local children playing on the steps. Some cells are dim, and some floors are uneven. Monsoon damp can leave dark streaks and patches of moss on the stone. It feels raw, and that is part of its value.

Before you leave, go back to one of the plain cells and stand inside for a moment. Notice how the thick rock softens the noise of the road. Notice the cooler air, and the low square doorway that frames a small piece of the city outside. Someone sat in this exact space many centuries ago. As you walk back toward the traffic, look once more at that dark hillside. Remember that it is hollow, and that it was once full of quiet voices.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the morning for cooler air and softer light.
Wear shoes with good grip for uneven stone steps.
Bring a small torch for the darker inner cells.
Carry water, as there are few facilities on site.
Combine with Kanheri Caves for a wider Buddhist picture.
