The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water drips from leaf to leaf above you, long after the last rain has stopped. The air is thick and warm, and it smells of wet soil, rotting wood and something faintly sweet. Light reaches the forest floor only in small, shifting coins of gold. Somewhere high in the canopy a bird calls once, then goes quiet. This is the Daintree, in the far north of Queensland, and it feels less like a park than a living room for very old plants.

Scientists often describe this rainforest as one of the oldest on Earth. The commonly quoted estimate is around 180 million years. That figure refers to the age of the forest lineage, not to any single tree. Still, the idea is worth holding. Forests like this one already existed when Australia was joined to other continents. Many plant families that grow here have changed very little across that long stretch of time.

The Daintree is part of the Wet Tropics of Queensland World Heritage Area, which was listed in 1988. Just offshore lies the Great Barrier Reef, another World Heritage site. Here the two meet, and that is rare. In a few places along this coast, rainforest grows right down to the sand, and the reef sits beyond the waves. Two of the planet's great living systems touch each other along a single strip of beach.

The name comes from the Daintree River. A nineteenth-century explorer named the river after Richard Daintree, a geologist who worked for the colonial government. But that name is very recent. For thousands of years before it, this country belonged to the Eastern Kuku Yalanji people. Their knowledge of the forest is detailed and practical. They know which plants are food, which are medicine, and which places carry stories and rules.

Here is something many visitors do not know. In 2021, ownership of the national park was formally handed back to the Eastern Kuku Yalanji people. The park is now managed jointly by Traditional Owners and the Queensland Government. So the forest around you is not only a protected area. It is also returned land. When you see rangers or guides here, some of them are speaking about their own family country.

Most visitors reach the northern section by crossing the Daintree River on a small cable ferry. It is a short crossing, but it changes the mood of the trip. The road narrows after the river. Power lines thin out. Forest closes in on both sides. Look at the river banks as you cross. Estuarine crocodiles live in these waters, and on a warm day one may be lying quietly in the mud.

The park has two main parts. To the south is Mossman Gorge, where a clear river runs over large granite boulders. To the north is the Cape Tribulation section, where the forest meets the coast. Cape Tribulation got its name in 1770 from James Cook. His ship, the Endeavour, struck the reef nearby. Cook named the headland for the trouble that began there.

Now look closely at the plants around you. Fan palms spread their leaves into wide, pleated circles, some taller than a person. Strangler figs begin life high on a host tree. Their roots slowly grow down and wrap around the trunk below. Over many years the host can die and rot away, leaving a hollow lattice of fig roots. Some of these hollow figs are large enough to look through.

The second surprise is a plant with an unkind nickname, the idiot fruit. Botanists consider it one of the most primitive flowering plants alive. For a long time it was thought to be lost. Then, in the twentieth century, it was found again in this region. Its seeds are large and poisonous to cattle. To scientists, though, it is precious. It offers a glimpse of what early flowering plants may have looked like.

The most famous animal here is the southern cassowary. It is a tall, flightless bird with glossy black feathers, a bright blue neck and a hard crest on its head. Cassowaries are endangered, and they matter to the forest. They swallow fruit whole and carry the seeds over long distances. Many rainforest trees depend on them to spread. If you see one, admire it from far away. Never approach or feed it.

Smaller lives fill the forest too. Boyd's forest dragon, a lizard with a spiky crest, often clings motionless to tree trunks at head height. It is easy to walk past without noticing it. Bright blue butterflies cross the paths in sudden flashes of colour. At night, frogs fill the creeks with sound. Even fallen leaves are busy. Fungi and insects break them down quickly in this heat and moisture.

The forest's protection was not always certain. In the early 1980s, people protested against a road being cut north of Cape Tribulation. The protests drew national attention to the area. They helped to build support for the World Heritage listing later in that decade. Today, much of the experience here is shaped by that history: boardwalks, quiet roads and careful limits on development along this coast.

Before you move on, stand still once more. Notice how many shades of green surround you, from almost black to pale yellow. Feel the humidity on your skin. Listen to the drip of water and the low hum of insects. As you walk away, look up at the canopy one last time. The forest was here long before the road, the ferry and the boardwalk, and it will keep growing quietly after you leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Never swim in rivers or creeks because of crocodiles.
Carry insect repellent and plenty of drinking water.
Keep well away from cassowaries and never feed them.
Drive slowly north of the river to protect wildlife.
Stay on boardwalks and avoid touching unknown plants.
