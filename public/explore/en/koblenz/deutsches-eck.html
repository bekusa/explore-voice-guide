The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Here, at the very tip of this stone promontory, the air moves differently. It comes up off two rivers at once. On one side, the Rhine slides past, wide and heavy. On the other, the Moselle arrives, calmer and often a shade greener. Under your feet, the paving runs out to a point, like the bow of a ship. And above you, a bronze horseman rides out over the water.

This is the Deutsches Eck, the German Corner. It sits where the Moselle ends its long journey from the hills of France and joins the Rhine. For two thousand years, people have understood that this meeting of waters matters. The Romans built a fort nearby and named the settlement after the confluence itself. Their Latin word, Confluentes, slowly wore down over the centuries into the name you know today... Koblenz.

Now, here is the first surprise. The name German Corner has nothing to do with national pride, at least not at first. In the early thirteenth century, the Teutonic Order settled on this spot. They were a religious order of knights, founded during the Crusades, and in German they were the Deutscher Orden. Their local headquarters stood right here. So locals began to call the corner after the knights. The patriotic meaning came much later.

For most of its life, this point was not a grand plaza at all. It was a working edge of the city, close to the water, close to the boats. Then, in the late nineteenth century, the land was reshaped. Engineers built up the tip and extended it into the rivers. The city was preparing a stage. And the star of that stage would be an emperor on horseback.

That emperor is Wilhelm the First. Under his reign, in 1871, the many German states were joined into a single empire. After his death, his grandson, Wilhelm the Second, wanted monuments to honour him across the country. Koblenz won one of the most dramatic sites. The sculptor Emil Hundrieser designed the rider, and the architect Bruno Schmitz designed the massive base. The monument was unveiled in 1897, with great ceremony.

Look up at the statue now. The emperor sits in military dress, and beside him walks a winged female figure. She represents the spirit of the empire, guiding the horse forward. The bronze group alone is roughly fourteen metres tall. Placed on its heavy stone pedestal, it rises far above the promenade. From a passing boat, it was meant to look like a figure stepping out of the land and over the river.

And here is the second surprise. The rider you are looking at is not the original. In March 1945, in the final weeks of the Second World War, American artillery hit the monument. The bronze statue was badly damaged and later taken down. For almost half a century, the great stone base stood empty. Visitors climbed steps to a pedestal with no emperor on top.

That empty pedestal took on a new meaning. In the 1950s, Germany was divided into East and West. The base was rededicated as a memorial to German unity, a quiet promise that the country would one day be whole again. In 1953, the West German president Theodor Heuss dedicated it in this new role. For decades, an empty plinth on a river corner carried the hopes of a divided nation.

Then history turned. The Berlin Wall fell in 1989, and Germany reunited in 1990. A local couple offered to pay for a new casting of the emperor and his guardian figure. The idea caused real debate. Some people felt a monument to an imperial ruler did not belong in modern Germany. Others saw it as part of the city's story. In the end, the replica was raised onto the pedestal in 1993.

As you walk back from the point, notice the flags along the promenade. They represent the German federal states, alongside the national flag. Close by, you will also find three concrete slabs. These are real segments of the Berlin Wall, placed here as a memorial. Their grey surfaces feel rough and ordinary. Yet they stand only steps away from a symbol of imperial power. The contrast is worth a moment of thought.

If your legs allow, climb the steps of the monument base. From the platform, the view opens in every direction. Across the Rhine, on a high rocky ridge, sits the Ehrenbreitstein Fortress. Its long walls follow the line of the cliff. Below you, the two rivers run side by side for a while before they fully mix. On a clear day, you can sometimes see the difference in their colours.

You may also notice cable cars gliding over the Rhine. The cableway was built for a national garden show held in Koblenz in 2011, and it links the riverbank with the fortress above. It became popular enough to stay. Koblenz also sits at the northern gateway of the Upper Middle Rhine Valley, a stretch of river protected as a UNESCO World Heritage landscape. Castles, vineyards, and steep slopes line the water to the south.

Before you leave, stand still at the very tip once more. Listen to the water slapping against the stone. Watch the barges push slowly upstream, low and loaded. Behind you, the bronze emperor faces outward, as he has since 1993, and in another form since 1897. Ahead of you, two rivers become one. As you walk back into the old town, keep the sound of that water with you for a few streets.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the monument steps for views over both rivers.
Ride the cable car across the Rhine to Ehrenbreitstein.
Come near sunset for softer light on the bronze.
Take a short river cruise to see the corner from water.
Wear a windproof layer; the tip is often breezy.
