The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The forest floor here is soft, almost springy, a deep carpet of needles and fallen bark. The air smells of resin and cool earth. Above you, the trunks rise in a colour that is hard to name... somewhere between cinnamon, rust, and old brick. Sunlight comes down in narrow bands, and it lands on bark so thick you could push your fingers into its fibres. You are standing among some of the largest living things on Earth.

This is Sequoia National Park, on the western slope of the Sierra Nevada mountains in California. It was established in 1890. That makes it the second-oldest national park in the United States, after Yellowstone. The park climbs from warm, dry foothills full of oak and chaparral, which is a dense scrubland of tough, low bushes, all the way to granite peaks above the treeline. In one long drive, you pass through several completely different worlds.

The giant sequoia is the reason this park exists. These trees grow naturally in only one place on the planet — a narrow belt of scattered groves on the western side of the Sierra Nevada. They are not the tallest trees in the world. That title belongs to their cousins, the coast redwoods. But by sheer volume of wood, nothing alive comes close. A single mature sequoia can be older than most written history.

The most famous of them all is the General Sherman Tree. Measured by volume, it is the largest known single-stem tree on Earth. A trail leads down to its base, and the walk itself is part of the experience. As you descend, the tree does not seem to grow bigger at first. Your eyes simply cannot judge it. Only when you stand beside the people at its foot does the scale finally land.

Here is a story that many visitors miss. Before it carried a general's name, this tree had another one. In the 1880s, a group of settlers called the Kaweah Colony lived in these mountains. They were a utopian socialist community, hoping to build a cooperative society and support it partly by logging. They named this giant the Karl Marx Tree. The colony eventually failed, the park was created, and the older name, General Sherman, is the one that stayed.

The grove around you is called the Giant Forest. The naturalist John Muir walked through these woods in the late nineteenth century and gave the area its name. Muir spent much of his life arguing that wild places like this should be protected rather than cut down. Not every grove was so lucky. Elsewhere in the Sierra, sequoias were felled in that era. Because their wood is brittle, many of them shattered when they hit the ground.

Look closely at the bark of the nearest trunk. You may see black scars, sometimes high up, sometimes hollowing out the base. These are marks of fire. For centuries, fire has been part of the sequoia's life. The thick, spongy bark protects the living wood inside. Heat helps the small cones open and release their seeds. And fire clears the ground so that young seedlings can reach bare soil and sunlight.

But there is a harder chapter too. In 2020 and 2021, unusually intense wildfires burned through parts of the sequoia range. Fires that hot behave differently from the gentler ground fires these trees evolved with. They reached into the crowns and killed a significant share of the world's large sequoias. Park scientists and crews now work to protect the remaining groves. When you see thinned undergrowth or deliberate low burns, that is often careful, planned work.

Another story from these woods deserves to be better known. In the early twentieth century, before the National Park Service existed, the United States Army patrolled the parks. Among those soldiers were the Buffalo Soldiers, African American cavalry troops. In 1903, they were led here by Captain Charles Young, one of the first Black graduates of West Point. Under his command, the soldiers helped build roads and trails that opened this forest to visitors.

If you want to see the land from above the trees, there is Moro Rock. It is a great dome of granite rising from the edge of the forest. A narrow stone staircase, cut into the rock in the 1930s, winds up its side, around three hundred and fifty steps. At the top, the ground falls away dramatically. On clear days you look out across deep canyons toward the jagged peaks of the Great Western Divide.

The park's highest ground is far beyond what most visitors see. Its eastern boundary runs along the crest of the Sierra Nevada. There stands Mount Whitney, the highest peak in the contiguous United States, meaning the forty-eight states that share a border. You cannot drive to it from this side. Reaching that wild high country takes days on foot. Below ground, the park also holds marble caves, the best known being Crystal Cave, visited only on guided tours.

As you walk, keep your senses open to the smaller life here. Steller's jays, bright blue with dark crests, call loudly from the branches. Douglas squirrels chatter and drop green sequoia cones from high above. Black bears live throughout the park, which is why you see metal food-storage lockers at trailheads and campsites. Even the fallen giants are full of life. A sequoia log can lie on the forest floor for a very long time, slowly feeding beetles, fungi, and new growth.

Before you leave, find one tree and spend a full minute with it. Put your palm flat on the bark and notice how it gives slightly, like cork. Tilt your head back and follow the trunk until the branches disappear into green. Listen to the wind moving high above, while the air down here barely stirs. And as you walk away, look back once. Notice how quickly the giants blend into the forest again... as if they had always been waiting, patient, for the next person to stop and look up.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to walk the Sherman trail with fewer crowds.
Store all food in the metal bear lockers provided.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep Moro Rock staircase.
Bring layers; temperatures drop sharply as the road climbs.
Book Crystal Cave tours in advance, as access is guided only.
