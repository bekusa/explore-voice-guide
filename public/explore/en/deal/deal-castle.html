The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the bridge over the moat. Listen. On most days you can hear the shingle on Deal beach, a slow rattle of pebbles as each wave pulls back. The castle walls in front of you are low, rounded and grey. They do not rise to meet you like a fairy-tale tower. They crouch. That squat, heavy shape is the first clue to what this place was built to do.

You are looking at one of the most complete artillery forts of the Tudor age. It was built for King Henry the Eighth around 1539 and 1540, and it was built in a hurry. Henry had broken with the Church in Rome. He had divorced Catherine of Aragon, closed the monasteries and made himself head of the Church in England. Then the two great Catholic powers of Europe, France and the Holy Roman Empire, made peace with each other. For a moment, it looked as if they might turn on England together.

Henry's answer was a chain of coastal defences that historians call the Device Forts, named after the royal order, or device, that set them in motion. Deal was one of the largest. It worked as a team with two smaller sister castles, Walmer to the south and Sandown to the north. Together they guarded a stretch of water called the Downs. This is a sheltered anchorage between the shore and the Goodwin Sands, a dangerous bank of shifting sand a few miles out to sea.

Why guard this particular water? Because ships gathered here. Fleets waited in the Downs for good winds before heading into the Channel or up the Thames. An enemy fleet could use the same calm water to anchor and land troops on this flat, open beach. The castle's job was simple. It had to make that beach a very bad place to be.

Now look at the plan of the building, if you can see it from above on a sign or in your mind. The castle is made of circles inside circles. At the centre stands a round keep. Around it sits a ring of six rounded bastions. Around those sits a larger ring of six more bastions, and around everything runs the moat. From the air, the shape looks like a flower with petals. Many visitors compare it to the Tudor rose, the emblem of Henry's royal house. Whether the designers intended that symbol is a matter of debate, but the comparison is easy to see.

The real reason for the curves is practical. Round walls deflect cannon balls better than flat ones. A shot hitting a curved surface is more likely to glance away. The low height matters too. A tall medieval tower makes a big target for ships' guns. A low, thick fort is harder to hit and harder to break. This is a building designed in the age of gunpowder, and every line of it carries that new thinking.

Here is something many visitors miss. The castle was meant to fire in almost every direction at once. The walls are pierced with gun openings on several levels, well over a hundred in total. Cannon on the upper tiers could reach ships out at sea. Smaller guns lower down could sweep the moat and the beach. An attacker who reached the ditch would find himself under fire from walls on both sides.

As you walk through the entrance, look up. Above the gate passage are holes in the ceiling, often called murder holes. Defenders could drop things or fire down through them onto anyone who forced the outer door. There was also a portcullis, a heavy grille that could slide down to block the way. It is a small reminder that this fort expected a fight at its door, not only at sea.

Now for the second surprise. Go down to the lowest level, and you will find a long, dark passage running around the outer ring of the castle. It sits at the level of the moat and is lined with small gun ports. Soldiers here could fire straight along the bottom of the ditch. The passage is cool, narrow and echoing. Your footsteps sound louder down here. It is one of the most atmospheric spaces in the building, so take your time.

For all this careful planning, the castle saw little of the invasion it was built for. The French and imperial alliance did not last, and no great Catholic fleet landed on this beach. The fort's most serious fighting came a century later, during the English Civil War. In 1648, during a Royalist uprising in Kent, the castle was held against Parliament. Parliamentary forces besieged it and took it back. Cannon fire on these walls was real, but it came from fellow Englishmen.

After that, the castle settled into a quieter life. A captain held command here, and over the centuries the officers added more comfortable living quarters facing the sea. Most of those later additions are gone. During the Second World War, the seaward residence was badly damaged by bombing, and it was not rebuilt. In a strange way, that loss brought the building closer to its original Tudor form. Its sister fort at Sandown had a harder fate. The sea gradually wore it away, and very little of it survives today.

Take a moment on the upper level, if you can, and look out to sea. Somewhere beyond the horizon line lie the Goodwin Sands. Sailors feared them for centuries, and many ships were wrecked there. The calm water closer to shore is the Downs, the reason all this stone exists. On a clear day you may see cargo ships moving along the Channel, following the same busy sea lane that worried Henry's planners.

The town around you grew up with the sea as well. Deal was a working maritime town, full of boatmen, pilots and fishermen who served the ships in the Downs. Walk along the seafront after your visit and you will see fishing boats pulled up on the shingle. The castle is not separate from that story. It was the armed guard of a town that lived from the water.

Before you leave, stand once more at the edge of the moat. Look at the curve of the bastions, the dark gun openings and the pale stone against the sky. Then turn your head toward the beach and listen again to the shingle. The threat this fort was built for never came. What remains is a careful, practical piece of engineering, still keeping watch over a quiet stretch of coast.
