The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off Lake Onega and moves through the grass of the island. Somewhere a wooden plank creaks. The air smells of water, timber and wild herbs. Then you look up, and the domes come into view. There are so many of them, grey and silver, stacked one above another like a frozen wave. You are standing on Kizhi, a long, low island in the north of Russia, in front of one of the most unusual groups of buildings in the world.

The word pogost needs a short explanation. In old northern Russia, a pogost was a parish centre. It was a cluster of churches, a bell tower and a graveyard, often inside a fence, serving villages scattered across a wide area of lakes and forest. People came here by boat in summer and across the ice in winter. So this was never a remote monastery. It was the meeting point for an entire farming community, the place for prayer, news and market days.

The tallest building in front of you is the Church of the Transfiguration. It was completed in 1714, during the reign of Peter the Great. Count the domes if you have the patience. There are twenty-two of them, rising in tiers toward a single dome at the top. The structure is built almost entirely from logs, laid horizontally and locked together at the corners. Nothing like this tower of domes was built anywhere else in wooden architecture, before or after.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. This church was never heated. It was a summer church, used only in the warm months. The builders did not need to keep heat inside, so they could make the building tall and open. Its neighbour, with fewer domes, played the opposite role. Together the two churches let the parish worship all year round, despite winters that are long and very cold on this lake.

That neighbour is the Church of the Intercession, completed in 1764. It carries nine domes, eight around one in the centre. It is lower and more compact, because it was built to hold warmth. On winter Sundays, families from the surrounding villages gathered here around a stove while snow covered the lake outside. Look at the two buildings side by side. One is reaching upward, the other is settling close to the ground. They were designed as a pair.

The third tall element is the bell tower, added in the nineteenth century. Its tent-shaped roof is a traditional northern form, a steep pyramid of wood. Around all three runs a low fence of logs and stone. This enclosure turns the buildings into one composition. From almost any angle on the island, the three silhouettes overlap and shift as you walk. Try circling the fence slowly and watch how the domes seem to rearrange themselves against the sky.

Now look closely at the surface of the domes. They are covered with thousands of small wooden shingles made of aspen. Each one has a curved, pointed end, a little like a ploughshare. When the wood is new, it is pale and golden. Over the years, sun, rain and frost turn it silver. On a bright day the domes can almost look like metal. On a grey day they blend into the clouds. Many first-time visitors assume they are tin. They are not.

You may have heard that the Church of the Transfiguration was built without a single nail. This is partly true and partly legend. The log walls are held together by carefully cut joints, not by nails. However, the shingles on the domes were fixed with nails. The real achievement is still remarkable. Carpenters shaped and fitted every log mainly with axes, using skills passed from master to apprentice for generations in this part of the north.

And there is a famous story about that. According to a popular local legend, the master builder was a carpenter named Nestor. When he finished the church, he threw his axe into the lake. He said there had never been a church like it, and there never would be again. No document confirms this story. But people on the island still tell it, and it shows how proud this community was of what their carpenters had made.

Keeping wooden buildings alive is a constant struggle. Logs rot, foundations sink, and the huge weight of the domes pushes on the walls. In the twentieth century, restorers placed a steel support frame inside the Church of the Transfiguration to stop it from deforming. Later, a long restoration began. Workers took the structure apart in sections, replaced damaged logs and put the pieces back. That work took many years, so you may still notice areas of fresh, lighter timber.

The pogost is only the heart of a larger site. In the second half of the twentieth century, the island became an open-air museum of wooden architecture. Old houses, chapels, barns and windmills were brought here from villages across Karelia, the region around you. So as you walk the paths, you see a whole northern landscape in miniature. In 1990, the Kizhi Pogost was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, recognising its value to the whole world.

Take a moment to look at the island itself. It is flat and narrow, with rocky edges and meadows of grass and wildflowers in summer. Lake Onega, around it, is one of the largest lakes in Europe. Its water changes colour constantly, from steel grey to deep blue. For the people who built these churches, the lake was both a road and a danger. The domes were also a landmark, visible to boats from far away.

Before you leave, stand back from the fence and look once more at the domes. Notice how the shingles catch the light, each one slightly different in tone. Listen to the wind and the water, the same sounds the carpenters heard three centuries ago. As you walk back toward the pier, turn around once or twice. The churches look different from every distance. And slowly, they settle back into the long, grey line of the lake.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check summer boat schedules from Petrozavodsk before planning your trip.
Bring a windproof layer; lake weather changes quickly.
Walk the full circle around the fence for shifting views.
Use insect repellent on summer days in the island meadows.
Allow extra time to explore the open-air village museum.
