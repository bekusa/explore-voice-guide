The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind pushes across the grass. Waves break against sharp grey coral rock. Somewhere close, a scooter hums along the coast road. The air is warm and salty, and it stays warm for most of the year. You are standing near the southern end of Taiwan, on the Hengchun Peninsula. This is the country where the island runs out of land and the open ocean begins on three sides.

Kenting National Park covers much of this peninsula. It was the first national park in Taiwan, created in the 1980s. That date matters. Taiwan was changing fast in those years, with factories, highways, and new towns spreading across the west coast. The park was an early decision to protect a place before it disappeared. It also covers the sea, not only the land. Much of the protected area lies under the water, around the coral reefs.

Look at the shape of the coast and you can read the geography. To the west lies the Taiwan Strait. To the south runs the Bashi Channel, the stretch of water toward the Philippines. To the east is the Pacific Ocean. Three bodies of water meet at this one tip. Currents mix here. Warm water from the south helps coral grow. Fishermen, divers, and sailors have all learned to respect these crossing currents.

Now look down at the rock. Much of it is pale, rough, and full of holes. This is raised coral limestone. It was once a living reef under the sea. Over a very long time, the land rose and lifted the reef into the air. So when you walk on these cliffs, you are often walking on ancient coral. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of the forests inland also grow on old coral, far from the water.

The living reefs are still here, just offshore. Snorkellers and divers come to see soft corals, hard corals, and bright reef fish. In spring, on certain nights after a full moon, the corals spawn. They release tiny bundles of eggs and sperm into the water at the same time. The sea fills with drifting specks. Scientists and divers watch for this event each year. It is one of the quiet wonders of this coast, and most daytime visitors never see it.

At the very southern tip stands Eluanbi Lighthouse. It is a white tower on a green headland, and it was built in the late nineteenth century. Its story begins with a shipwreck. In 1867, an American ship called the Rover was wrecked near this coast. The crew who came ashore were killed in a clash with local Paiwan people, one of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples. The event became a diplomatic crisis. Pressure from foreign powers later pushed the Qing government to build a lighthouse here.

That history adds weight to a calm place. The lighthouse was built to protect ships in dangerous water. It was also built at a time when outside powers were competing for influence in East Asia. Records describe the station as defended, with walls and gun positions, because tension with the surrounding communities continued. Today the grounds are a park. Families walk the paths, and the tower still shines out over the channel.

Along the coast road, you may notice a large rock standing alone in shallow water. This is Sail Rock. Locals say it looks like a ship under sail. Many visitors say it looks like the profile of a human face. According to one popular tale, people once compared it to a famous American president. Whether you see a sail, a face, or simply a rock is up to you. Children often enjoy arguing about it.

If you are here in autumn, look up. Every year around October, large flocks of birds of prey pass over this peninsula on their journey south. The best known is the grey-faced buzzard. For these birds, the southern tip of Taiwan is a resting point before they cross the sea. Birdwatchers gather at dawn and dusk to count them. On a good evening, many hawks circle above the trees and then drop down to rest.

In winter, the peninsula feels different. Strong seasonal winds come down from the hills toward the coast. Locals have a name for this wind, which roughly means the wind that falls down the mountain. It bends trees, dries the grass, and makes the sea rough on the eastern side. You can see its work in the low, windswept shapes of the plants along the cliffs. Even on a sunny day, hold on to your hat.

The human side of Kenting is just as clear. In the evening, the main street in Kenting town fills with food stalls, neon signs, and slow crowds. You can smell grilled squid, fruit, and sweet drinks. A short drive north, the old town of Hengchun still keeps parts of its city wall and gates from the Qing period. The town also became well known across Taiwan through a popular film from 2008 that was set here.

There is a quieter Kenting too. Away from the beaches, you can find open grasslands above the eastern cliffs. There are forest trails among coral rock and tropical trees. There are small fishing harbours where boats come in early with the morning catch. Many people visit only the beach and the night market. If you have time, take one trail inland. The sounds change quickly — from waves and engines to insects and birds.

This is a working landscape as well as a protected one. Farmers grow crops on the peninsula. Fishing families still live along the coast. Hotels, dive shops, and scooter rental stores line the roads. Every year the park must balance visitors with coral, birds, and forest. You are part of that balance. Staying on paths, keeping off the reef, and taking your rubbish with you all make a real difference here.

Before you move on, take one more moment. Feel the wind on your face, coming off the open water. Look at the rough coral rock beneath you, lifted from the sea long ago. Watch the line where the colour of the ocean changes from pale green to deep blue. As you walk away, listen for the waves behind you. They have been shaping this edge of Taiwan for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in October to watch migrating grey-faced buzzards.
Rent a scooter to reach coastal viewpoints between beaches.
Bring reef-safe sunscreen and a hat for strong sun.
Take one inland forest trail to escape the beach crowds.
Expect strong winds on the eastern coast during winter.
