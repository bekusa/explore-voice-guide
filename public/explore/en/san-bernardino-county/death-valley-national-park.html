The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, silence has a weight to it. The air is dry enough to make your lips tighten, and the light lands hard on everything. Pale salt, rust-coloured rock, mountains the colour of ash. On a still day you may hear only your own footsteps and the tick of cooling metal from a parked car. Welcome to Death Valley, a place most people expect to be empty and that turns out to be full of detail.

First, a small geography lesson that surprises many visitors. Most of this park lies in Inyo County, California. But its southern reaches extend into San Bernardino County, and a separate piece crosses into Nevada. It is the largest national park in the lower forty-eight states. You could drive for hours inside its boundaries and still not see it all. The scale is the first thing to accept here, and it shapes everything else.

The valley is a basin, sunk between two long mountain ranges. To the west rise the Panamint Mountains, and their highest summit, Telescope Peak, often holds snow into spring. To the east stand the Amargosa Range. The land between them is still dropping, slowly, as faults in the earth pull it apart. So the valley is not finished. It is a landscape that is still moving, very slowly, under your feet.

At the bottom lies Badwater Basin. It sits about eighty-six metres below sea level, the lowest point in North America. Look up at the cliffs behind the parking area and you may spot a small sign high on the rock marking where sea level would be. It is a strange feeling — standing beneath the ocean's surface, in one of the driest places on the continent. The salt flats stretch out white and cracked into hexagon-like shapes.

That salt has a story. During the ice ages, this valley held a large lake, which geologists call Lake Manly. As the climate warmed and dried, the water evaporated. The minerals stayed behind. Today, rare rains still form shallow pools on the flats, then vanish again. Each cycle redraws the patterns in the crust. What you walk on is the floor of an old lake, still drying out.

And then there is the heat. In July 1913, a weather station at Furnace Creek recorded an air temperature of about 56.7 degrees Celsius. It is often cited as the highest air temperature ever measured on Earth, though some scientists question that old reading. Either way, summer here is serious. The ground can grow far hotter than the air. The mountains trap heat, and the low basin holds it like an oven.

People have lived with this heat for a very long time. The Timbisha Shoshone have made this valley their homeland for generations. They knew where the springs were, which plants to gather, when to move up into the cooler mountains. Their community still lives near Furnace Creek today. It is worth remembering that the valley was never truly empty. It was understood, by people who knew how to live within its limits.

The name came later. In 1849, a group of travellers heading for the California gold fields took a shortcut and became stranded here for weeks. They suffered badly, though most of them survived. According to a popular story, one of them looked back as they finally climbed out and said, "Goodbye, Death Valley." Nobody can confirm those exact words. But the name stuck, and it has shaped how the world imagines this place ever since.

The valley's next chapter was about minerals. In the late nineteenth century, workers gathered borax from the salt flats. Borax was used in cleaning and in industry. To haul it out, teams of twenty mules pulled enormous wagons across the desert to the railroad, a journey of many days. The ruins of the Harmony Borax Works still stand near Furnace Creek. Those mule teams later became a famous advertising image, and they gave the valley a second, gentler kind of fame.

Tourism followed the mining. Death Valley was protected as a national monument in 1933, and it became a national park in 1994. Since then, visitors have come for the viewpoints. At Zabriskie Point, eroded hills fold like crumpled golden fabric. At Dante's View, high above the basin, you look straight down onto the white floor of Badwater. On Artists Drive, minerals stain the rock in shades of pink, green, and purple.

Here is a second surprise. Far to the north, on a dry lakebed called the Racetrack, heavy stones have left long trails across the mud. For decades nobody could explain how they moved. Then, in 2014, researchers watched it happen. Thin sheets of winter ice, pushed by light wind, slid the rocks slowly across the wet surface. A mystery that lasted generations had a quiet, simple answer.

Life here is tougher than it looks. Tiny pupfish survive in salty springs and creeks, descended from fish that lived in the old lakes. After an unusually wet winter, the desert floor can burst into wildflowers, which people call a superbloom. And at night, the sky is something else. The park is recognised as a dark sky park, and on a clear night the Milky Way stretches from one ridge to the other.

So take a moment before you move on. Notice the colours shifting on the mountains as the sun changes angle. Feel how dry the air is in your throat, and drink some water. Look at the salt, the rock, the long, empty road ahead. This valley is still sinking, still drying, still changing. And for a little while, you are standing right inside that slow story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry far more water than you think you need.
Avoid long hikes during summer daytime heat.
Fill your fuel tank before entering the park.
Visit Zabriskie Point at sunrise for soft light.
Stay after dark for clear views of stars.
