The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere in the tall grass, a bird gives a sharp, repeated call. A breeze moves through the sal leaves with a dry, papery rustle. Underfoot, the earth is soft and pale, marked with the prints of things that walked here before you this morning. Welcome to Bardiya National Park, one of the last great stretches of wild lowland forest in Nepal.

If you came down from Birendranagar, you made a real descent. The town sits in a valley among the hills of Karnali Province. Bardiya lies to the southwest, on the flat plains that Nepalis call the Terai. Here the air is warmer and heavier. The mountains drop away, and the land opens into forest, river and grass. It feels like a different country, only a few hours from the hills.

The park covers nearly a thousand square kilometres. That makes it the largest protected area in Nepal's lowlands. Yet it receives far fewer visitors than Chitwan, its better-known cousin to the east. For many travellers, that is the whole point. The roads are quieter, the lodges are smaller, and a morning in the forest can pass without the sound of another engine.

The story of protection here begins in the 1970s. A smaller area was first set aside as a royal hunting reserve, and then as a wildlife reserve. Over the following years its borders grew. By the late 1980s it had become a full national park. The word royal, once part of its name, was dropped after Nepal became a republic. The forest itself, of course, is much older than any of these labels.

Look around at the trees. Most of the tall, straight trunks belong to sal, a hardwood tree that dominates this part of South Asia. Its large leaves fall in the dry season and cover the ground in a brown carpet. Between the forests lie open grasslands. Locals call these clearings phanta. In some places the grass grows taller than a person, and an elephant can disappear into it completely.

This mix of forest and grass is what makes Bardiya so good for animals. The Bengal tiger lives here, and the park is one of the strongholds for tigers in Nepal. Here is something most visitors do not know. Tiger numbers in Nepal have risen in recent decades, which is rare good news for a big cat that has vanished from much of its old range. Seeing one is never guaranteed. But finding fresh pugmarks on a sandy track is common, and strangely thrilling.

Another surprise is the rhinoceros. The greater one-horned rhinoceros had disappeared from this area. Beginning in the 1980s, conservationists moved rhinos here from Chitwan, carrying them by truck across the country. It was a bold experiment, and its fortunes have risen and fallen over the years. Today, if you meet a rhino in the grass, you are looking at the result of that long effort.

Wild Asian elephants also move through the park, sometimes in herds. They follow old routes along the rivers and across the border region. Swamp deer graze in the open phanta. Spotted deer, with their white-dotted coats, are everywhere. Monkeys watch from the branches and shout warnings when a predator is near. Guides learn to read these alarm calls, so if your guide suddenly stops and listens, stop and listen too.

Now turn your attention to water. The western edge of the park follows the Karnali, one of the great rivers of Nepal, which flows down from the far Himalaya. Its waters are wide, cold and blue-green. In these channels live mugger crocodiles and the rare gharial, a slender crocodile with a long, thin snout made for catching fish. And, most unexpected of all, the Karnali holds the Gangetic river dolphin, a nearly blind dolphin that finds its way by sound.

The dolphins are hard to see. They surface briefly, roll, and vanish into the murky current. Many people sit by the river for hours and see nothing. But a quiet riverbank at dawn is a good place to be, dolphin or not. Mist sits on the water. Kingfishers flash past. Bardiya is also excellent for birds, with hundreds of species recorded, from hornbills to fishing eagles.

The human story here is just as important. This region is home to the Tharu, an indigenous people of the Terai. For generations they have lived alongside these forests and rivers. In the villages around the park, you can still see traditional Tharu houses with walls of mud and straw. Many are smoothed by hand and decorated with painted patterns. Tharu cooking, dance and fishing traditions are part of daily life, not a show put on for visitors.

Living beside a national park is not always easy. Elephants raid crops. Tigers sometimes take livestock. Many local families now work as guides, lodge staff and community forest guards. The idea is simple: when the forest brings income to the village, the village has a reason to protect the forest. It is an ongoing balance, and you are part of it. Hiring a local guide is one of the most direct ways to support it.

So take a moment, wherever you stand right now. Feel the warmth coming off the ground. Notice the light through the sal leaves, and the smell of dust and river. Watch the edge of the grass, where something might move. As you walk on, keep your steps soft and your eyes low on the trail. The prints in the sand will tell you who passed here before you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the forest only with a licensed local guide.
Head out at dawn when animals are most active.
Wear muted green or brown clothing on jungle walks.
Bring binoculars for birds and distant river wildlife.
Visit a Tharu village near the park entrance.
