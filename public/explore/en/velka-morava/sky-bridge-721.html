The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you step on. Up here the wind moves through the spruce trees with a low, steady rush. Under that sound there is something else: a faint hum of steel cables, and the soft clank of the deck as walkers ahead of you shift their weight. Look down through the metal grating at your feet, and you will see straight through to the treetops far below.

This is Sky Bridge 721, in the mountains above Velká Morava, in the northeast corner of the Czech Republic. The name carries a number, and the number is the point. The bridge measures 721 metres from one anchor to the other. It is a single long span of steel and cable, slung across a valley between two hillsides. It is not a road, and it was never a shortcut. It was built for exactly what you are about to do: walk across slowly and feel the air underneath you.

The bridge opened in 2022. At the time, it was promoted as the longest suspension footbridge in the world. Records like this rarely last. Engineers in other countries are always planning something longer. But standing here, the exact ranking matters very little. What matters is the long, gentle curve of the deck ahead of you. It dips toward the middle and rises again toward the far side, like a rope held loosely between two hands.

It helps to understand how a bridge like this stays up. There are no towers in the middle of the valley and no supports reaching up from the ground. The whole weight hangs from heavy steel cables. Those cables are anchored deep into the rock on each hillside. The deck is light and narrow, barely wider than a person with outstretched arms. This is why the bridge moves a little. The movement is by design. A rigid structure this long would fight the wind. A flexible one simply rides with it.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The bridge is often quieter in the middle than at the ends. Near the anchors, you hear the voices of people at the station, the gravel paths, and the machinery of the resort. Out in the centre, those sounds fall away. You are left with wind, cables, and footsteps. Many people stop talking here without meaning to. It is worth letting that happen.

Now lift your eyes from the grating. You are standing in the Králický Sněžník massif, a range of rounded, forested mountains along the border with Poland. These are old mountains, worn smooth over a very long time. They have no jagged Alpine peaks. Instead you see long ridges, one behind another, fading into softer shades of blue and grey toward the horizon. On a clear day the layers seem to go on and on.

The forests below are mostly spruce, dark green and dense. In many Central European mountains, spruce was planted widely for timber over the past few centuries. In some places you may see paler patches or open slopes. These can be clearings, ski runs, or areas where the forest is recovering. The landscape around you is not untouched wilderness. It is a working mountain landscape, shaped by forestry, farming, and, more recently, tourism.

That brings us to the village below. Velká Morava is part of the municipality of Dolní Morava, a small community in a valley at the foot of these hills. For a long time this was a quiet, remote corner of the country. Over the past few decades, it has grown into a year-round mountain resort. In winter, people come for skiing. In summer, they come for hiking, mountain biking, and attractions like the one under your feet.

Here is a second surprise. The bridge is not the first bold structure on these slopes. Not far away stands the Sky Walk, known in Czech as Stezka v oblacích, which means "the path in the clouds." It is a tall tower of wood and steel with a walkway that spirals up to a viewing platform. It opened several years before the bridge. Together, the two structures show the resort's approach to the mountain: build something that lets people experience height in a new way.

If your legs feel a little uncertain, you are in good company. The see-through deck plays a trick on the mind. Your body knows the grating is solid, but your eyes keep telling you otherwise. Some people find it easier to look ahead, toward the far side, instead of down. Others enjoy looking straight down. There is no wrong way to cross. Take the pace that feels right, and hold the railing whenever you want.

Notice how the view changes as you move along the span. Near the ends, the trees seem close, almost within reach. Toward the middle, the ground drops away, and you look down into the valley from high above. The sense of scale shifts with every few steps. Notice the light as well. Clouds move quickly across these mountains. A slope that is bright green one minute can turn dark the next, as a shadow passes over.

The weather here deserves respect. Mountain conditions can change faster than they do in the valley. A warm morning in the village can become a cold, windy afternoon on the ridge. Mist can roll in and hide the far end of the bridge completely. When that happens, the crossing feels very different. The deck simply disappears ahead of you into white, and the cables vanish into the cloud. Some visitors say this is the most memorable way to see it.

As you reach the far side, take a moment to turn around. From the anchor point, you can see the full sweep of the bridge for the first time. You see the long, thin line against the valley, and the tiny figures of people still out on the span. From the deck it felt wide and solid. From here it looks like a single thread drawn across the air. That is a good image to keep.

Before you walk on, stand still for a few breaths. Feel the solid ground under your boots again. Listen to the wind in the spruce trees, the same sound you heard at the start. Look once more across the valley at the ridges fading toward Poland. The bridge will keep humming quietly behind you as the next walkers step on. And the mountains will keep doing what they have always done, long before anyone thought of crossing them this way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the mountain forecast before going; ridge weather changes quickly.
Bring a windproof layer even on warm days in the valley.
Look toward the far end if the see-through deck feels unsettling.
Combine your visit with the nearby Sky Walk tower.
Wear closed shoes with good grip for the metal grating.
