The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look up, let the hilltop wind reach you. It carries the soft clink of hundreds of small brass bells. Many hang from wires and railings, and each one moves a little differently. Underfoot, the ground is pale and smooth, warm from the sun. Somewhere nearby, you may hear the scrape of a trowel or the hum of a tool. Then lift your eyes. A white figure sits above you, calm and enormous, against an open tropical sky.

This is the Big Buddha of Phuket. It sits on Nakkerd Hill, in the green ridge that rises between Chalong Bay and the beaches of Kata and Karon. Its formal Thai name is long and ceremonial. It is often shortened to Phra Phutta Ming Mongkol Eknakiri. Most people on the island simply call it the Big Buddha. From many points along the southern coast, you can see the white figure on the skyline. Fishermen, drivers and visitors all use it to find their bearings.

The statue is a seated Buddha, roughly forty-five metres tall. That is about the height of a fifteen-storey building, placed on top of a mountain. The figure is built from reinforced concrete. Its surface is covered with white marble that is widely described as coming from Myanmar, Thailand's neighbour to the west. In strong midday light, the marble can look almost bright enough to glare. In the late afternoon, it softens to cream and pale gold.

Look closely at the pose. The Buddha sits with legs crossed. One hand rests in the lap, and the other reaches down over the knee toward the earth. In Thai tradition, this gesture is linked to a famous moment in the Buddha's story. Before his enlightenment, he touched the ground to call the earth as his witness against Mara, a figure of temptation and illusion. It is one of the most common poses in Thai temples, and here it is shown at enormous scale.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. This is not an ancient monument. Work on the statue began in the mid-2000s, and in many ways the project is still continuing. It has been funded largely by donations from local people, Thai pilgrims and foreign visitors. That is why you may see scaffolding, stacked materials or workers on site. You are not visiting a finished relic. You are visiting a place that people are still building, gift by gift.

That idea of giving runs through the whole hilltop. Near the base, you may find white marble tiles that visitors can buy as a donation. People write their names, a wish, or a message for someone they love, and the tiles become part of the site. Small bells work in a similar way. Visitors write on them and hang them up. The ringing you heard at the start comes from thousands of these quiet offerings, moving together in the wind.

Now turn around. The view is the second reason people climb this hill. To one side, Chalong Bay opens out, wide and calm, often dotted with anchored boats. To the other side, on a clear day, you can see down toward the curve of Kata and Karon, and the open Andaman Sea beyond. The island looks very different from up here. The busy beach roads disappear, and you see mostly forest, water and long ribbons of coastline.

As you walk around, look for a second, smaller Buddha nearby. It is golden rather than white, and it is much smaller than the main figure. Seen together, the two statues make an interesting pair. One is bright metal that catches the light in sharp flashes. The other is pale stone that holds the light more softly. Many photographers try to frame both in one picture, with the sea in the distance behind them.

This is an active religious site, not only a viewpoint. You may see monks in saffron robes, families making offerings, or people kneeling quietly at a shrine. Thai Buddhists often bring flowers, incense and small candles. The smell of incense can drift across the platform, mixed with the green scent of the forest below. If someone is praying, it is kind to step around them quietly. A lower voice here is noticed and appreciated.

Visitors are asked to dress modestly. Shoulders and knees should be covered. If you arrived in beachwear, do not worry. Wraps are usually available at the entrance, so you can cover up before going further. Shoes are removed in some areas, especially close to shrines. Watch what local visitors do, and follow their lead. Pointing your feet toward a Buddha image is considered disrespectful in Thai culture, so sit with your feet tucked to the side.

The journey up is part of the experience. The road climbs through thick forest, with sharp bends and steep stretches. On the way, you may pass small viewpoints, roadside stalls and patches of jungle full of birdsong and insects. In the heat of the day, the air on top often feels a little cooler, because the wind moves freely across the ridge. Many people stay longer than they planned, simply sitting in the shade and watching the light change over the bay.

Why build such a large figure here? For many Thais, a Buddha image on a high place offers a kind of blessing over the land below. Phuket has changed quickly, with resorts, roads and new neighbourhoods spreading along the coast. This statue gives the island a fixed, quiet centre that is visible from many directions. Whatever your own beliefs, it is easy to understand the wish to put something calm and lasting above a fast-changing place.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Look at the marble surface and notice how the pieces fit together, joint by joint. Listen again to the bells. Somewhere in that sound are names written by strangers from many countries, all gathered on one hill. As you walk back down, glance over your shoulder once. The white figure will still be there, sitting quietly above the bay, as the evening light moves across the water.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a scarf to cover shoulders and knees.
Come late afternoon for softer light on the marble.
Choose a clear day to see the Andaman coastline.
Sit with your feet tucked away from Buddha images.
Ask your driver to wait, as the road is steep.
