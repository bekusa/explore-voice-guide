The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, a train of steel cars crests a hill, and the sound arrives a second later. It comes as a rising rattle, a pause, and then a long rush of screams that fades into the hills. Underfoot, the pavement is warm. The air smells of sunscreen, fried dough, and dry chaparral, the low scrub that covers these slopes. Welcome to Six Flags Magic Mountain, in the Valencia area of Santa Clarita, California.

Look around at the land itself before you look at the rides. This park does not sit on flat ground. It climbs a series of rolling hills on the northern edge of greater Los Angeles, close to Interstate 5. Paths curve up and down. Coasters dive into ravines and swing out over slopes. That terrain is the secret of the place. Designers here have always used the hills as part of the ride, so a drop can feel longer than it really is.

The park opened in 1971, simply as Magic Mountain. It was developed by the Newhall Land and Farming Company, the firm that shaped much of this valley, working with the company behind SeaWorld. In those early years it was a gentler place, with gardens, shows, a sky tower, and a handful of rides. Six Flags bought the park at the end of the 1970s, and the name you see on the gates today followed. From then on, the focus moved steadily toward thrills.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. One of the oldest rides in the park is also one of the most important coasters ever built. It opened in 1976 as the Great American Revolution, and it is still running today as The New Revolution. It was the first modern steel coaster with a full vertical loop. Before it, looping a train upside down was an old idea that had mostly been abandoned as rough and unsafe. This ride proved a loop could be smooth.

That loop changed the industry. Within a few years, parks across the world wanted looping steel coasters of their own. So when you see Revolution's green track rising above the trees, you are looking at a kind of ancestor. Almost every inverting coaster you have ever ridden owes something to it. It also had a brief film career, appearing in the 1977 disaster movie called Rollercoaster.

Hollywood is close, and the park has often stood in for somewhere else. The best-known example is the 1983 comedy National Lampoon's Vacation. In that film, the Griswold family drives across the country to reach a theme park called Walley World. The Walley World scenes were filmed right here. The big wooden coaster in the movie was Colossus, which opened in 1978 as one of the tallest and fastest wooden coasters of its time.

Colossus has its own second act. In 2015, the old wooden structure was rebuilt with a new kind of steel track, laid on top of the timber frame. The ride reopened as Twisted Colossus. It now rolls, banks, and turns upside down in ways the original never could. Its two trains also run side by side on a long looping course, so riders on one train can race and wave at riders on the other.

Now for the second surprise. For many years, this park has held the Guinness World Record for the most roller coasters in a single amusement park. The number shifts as old rides close and new ones arrive, but it sits at around twenty. That is why so many coaster enthusiasts travel here. For them, this hillside works like a living museum of ride design, from classic loops to the newest experimental machines.

That museum covers several decades of engineering. Goliath, opened in 2000, is a tall steel hyper coaster built for speed and long drops. X2 is a so-called fourth-dimension coaster: the seats rotate on their own axis as the train moves, so riders flip forward and backward at unexpected moments. Tatsu is a flying coaster, where riders lie face-down, as if they were birds swooping over the slope. Each ride shows a different answer to the same question — how do you surprise the human body?

Newer rides keep that question going. Full Throttle uses a launch instead of a slow lift hill, firing riders straight into a tall loop. West Coast Racers sends two trains racing side by side. Wonder Woman Flight of Courage is a single-rail coaster, where riders sit in a line, one behind another, on a narrow track. Gold Rusher, a mine-train coaster from the opening year, is still here too. Old and new sit only a short walk apart.

Not everything is about extreme speed. Family areas offer smaller rides for younger visitors, and there are water rides that are welcome on a hot inland afternoon. Santa Clarita is often noticeably warmer than the coast, and summer heat on these slopes can be intense. Shade, water fountains, and a slow pace make a real difference to how the day feels. The hills also mean plenty of walking, much of it uphill.

The business behind the gates has changed too. In 2024, Six Flags merged with Cedar Fair, another large operator of amusement parks, under the Six Flags name. For visitors, the daily experience here is much the same. But it is a reminder that this hillside is a living commercial place. Rides come and go. Paint colours change. A ride you remember from childhood may be gone, and something taller may stand in its place.

As the day ends, watch the light. Late in the afternoon, the sun drops toward the western hills and turns the dry slopes gold. The coaster tracks become dark lines against the sky, and every lift hill throws a long shadow across the paths. After dark, the lights come on, and the rides look like drawings made with lines of colour.

So take a last look from where you stand. Find the green loops of Revolution, the oldest pioneer still working. Listen again for that rattle, the pause, and the rush of voices fading into the hills. Some of those riders are screaming for the very first time. Others have ridden these tracks for decades. Both groups are walking the same warm pavement as you, heading for the next hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive at opening to ride headline coasters before queues build.
Wear sturdy shoes because the paths climb steep hills.
Carry a refillable water bottle on hot inland summer days.
Check rider height limits online before bringing younger children.
Stay for sunset to see the coasters against golden hills.
