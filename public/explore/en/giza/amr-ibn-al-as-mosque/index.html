The guide

The story

Slip off your shoes at the threshold, and feel the change underfoot. The stone outside holds the heat of the street. Inside, the carpet is soft and cool. Then look up. Rows of marble columns run away from you in every direction, like trees in a planted grove. Light comes in from the open courtyard and falls across them in long pale bands. The traffic of Cairo is only a few steps away, but in here it fades to a low hum.

First, a small note on where you are standing. This mosque is not in Giza itself. It sits on the east bank of the Nile, in the old district of Fustat, part of what people now call Old Cairo. Giza lies just across the river. Both are part of the same great sprawl of Greater Cairo. So if you came from the pyramids this morning, you crossed the Nile to be here, and you have also crossed into a much later chapter of Egypt's story.

That chapter begins in the seventh century. An Arab army, led by a commander named Amr ibn al-As, arrived in Egypt in the years around 640. At that time Egypt was ruled from Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire. Amr's forces besieged a Roman fortress known as Babylon, which stood very close to where you are now. After the conquest, Amr founded a new settlement beside that fortress. He built a place of prayer at its centre. This mosque grew from that place.

Here is the first fact many visitors do not know. This is widely regarded as the oldest mosque in Egypt, and the first mosque built anywhere on the African continent. But nothing you see today is from that first building. The original was very simple. Historians describe walls of mud brick, columns made from palm trunks, and a roof of palm leaves. It had no minaret and no decoration. It was a shaded space for a community of soldiers to pray together.

The name of the settlement carries a well-loved story. Fustat is often linked to an Arabic word for a tent. Legend has it that when Amr was ready to march on, he found that a dove had built a nest on his tent. He did not want to disturb the bird. So he left the tent standing, and the new town grew up around it. It is a gentle story, and locals still tell it. Historians treat it as folklore, not as recorded fact.

Over the centuries, the small mosque was rebuilt and enlarged again and again. Governors added space as the population grew. A major expansion in the early ninth century gave the building roughly the large rectangular plan you can feel around you now. Later rulers repaired walls, replaced roofs, and added minarets. So when you walk through these halls, you are moving through many layers of rebuilding, one on top of another, stretching across more than thirteen centuries.

Now look closely at the columns. Walk along one row and compare them. You will see that they do not all match. Some are slightly taller. Some are darker. The carved tops, called capitals, change in style from one column to the next. This is the second surprise. Many of these columns were taken from older buildings, including Roman and Byzantine structures in Egypt. Builders reused fine stone that was already here. Each column may have stood somewhere else long before it stood in this mosque.

The neighbourhood around Fustat also suffered great damage. In the twelfth century, during wars with Crusader armies, much of Fustat was deliberately set on fire to stop it from falling into enemy hands. The old city never fully recovered. Its people moved north, toward the newer city of Cairo. The mosque survived the loss of much of its town, and later rulers restored it. Among them was Saladin, the famous sultan who fought the Crusaders and later ruled Egypt.

Much of what you see now comes from much later work. There was a large rebuilding in the late eighteenth century, and further restoration in the twentieth century. This is why the mosque can feel both very old and quite fresh at the same time. The plan and the idea are ancient. Many of the surfaces are newer. It is worth keeping both truths in mind as you look around, because the building carries the story of constant care rather than one single moment.

The mosque also carries its own legends. Locals say that one of the columns was brought here by a miracle, carried all the way from Mecca. Another popular tale speaks of a pair of columns standing close together. According to the story, only an honest person could squeeze between them. These are folk tales, told with a smile. They show how deeply the people of Old Cairo feel connected to this place, and how stories gather around old stones over time.

This is not a museum. It is a working mosque, and it is busiest during the holy month of Ramadan. On certain nights near the end of that month, very large crowds come here to pray together. Worshippers fill the prayer hall and spill into the courtyard and beyond. Even on an ordinary weekday, you may see a man reading quietly against a column, or children running across the carpet while their parents pray. Move gently, speak softly, and give space to anyone at prayer.

Step outside the walls for a moment and think about the streets around you. A short walk away is the area known as Coptic Cairo. There you can find old churches, including the Hanging Church, built above a gate of the Roman fortress. Nearby is the Ben Ezra Synagogue, linked to Egypt's old Jewish community. Within a small area, you can visit places of worship from three faiths. Few corners of any city hold so many layers so close together.

Before you leave, stand in the open courtyard one more time. Let your eyes travel from the bright sky down to the shaded rows of columns. Listen to the quiet. Notice the mismatched stone, the worn carpet, the small sounds of people praying. As you put your shoes back on and step into the warm street, remember the simple shelter of palm trunks that once stood here. Everything around you grew from that first small idea.

Practical tips
