The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. Under your shoes is a crust of sand and limestone grit, pale and warm, crunching softly with every step. The wind comes off the desert to the west and carries fine dust that settles on your lips. Somewhere behind you, the traffic of greater Cairo hums and honks. In front of you, rising out of the plateau, are three enormous stone shapes. They are the oldest things you will probably ever stand beside.

This is the Giza Necropolis, a city of the dead on the edge of a city of the living. A necropolis is simply a burial ground, and this one served the kings of Egypt's Fourth Dynasty, about 4,500 years ago. The three great pyramids belong to three rulers: Khufu, his son Khafre, and Khafre's successor Menkaure. Around them lie smaller pyramids for queens, rows of rectangular tombs for nobles, temples, causeways, and the Great Sphinx.

Start with the largest. The Great Pyramid of Khufu was built from more than two million stone blocks. When it was finished, it stood around 146 metres tall. Today it is a few metres shorter, because its top stones are gone. For thousands of years, nothing built by human hands rose higher. It is also the only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World that still stands. The other six — the gardens, the lighthouse, the great statues — have all disappeared.

Now look closely at the surface. The stepped, rough appearance you see is not how the pyramids were meant to look. Each one was once covered in a smooth casing of fine white limestone, polished so that it shone under the sun. Over the centuries, most of that casing was stripped away and reused as building stone. Much of medieval Cairo was built, in part, from blocks taken here. What you see now is the inner core.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. Turn toward the second pyramid, Khafre's. Near its very top, you can still see a cap of the original casing stones, lighter and smoother than the rest. It looks almost like a layer of snow. That small patch shows you how the whole plateau once appeared — three bright, sharp-edged geometric forms against the blue sky. Khafre's pyramid also looks taller than Khufu's, but it is a trick. It stands on higher ground.

The third pyramid, Menkaure's, is much smaller. Walk around its base and you will find large blocks of red granite, a hard stone brought from Aswan, far to the south. Some of these blocks were never smoothed. Their faces were left rough, which suggests the work was never finished. Scholars think the king may have died before his builders could complete the casing. It is a quiet reminder that even here, plans were interrupted.

Who built all this? For a long time, a popular story claimed that armies of slaves dragged the stones under the whip. The ancient Greek historian Herodotus repeated tales like this, many centuries after the pyramids were complete. Modern archaeology tells a different story. South of the pyramids, excavators found a settlement for workers, with bakeries, breweries, and storage buildings. They also found the workers' own tombs, placed with honour close to their kings.

Those tombs matter. Skeletons from the workers' cemetery show healed fractures and signs of medical care. This suggests the builders were organised labourers, fed and looked after, rotating in teams. Many were probably farmers who worked here when the Nile flood covered their fields. Graffiti left inside the monuments even records team names, some of them boastful. So when you look at these stones, think of crews of people, proud of their work, eating bread and drinking beer at the end of the day.

Now walk down toward the Sphinx. It sits lower on the plateau, beside Khafre's valley temple. The body of a lion, the head of a king. Most Egyptologists link it to Khafre's reign. And here is the second surprise. The Sphinx was not built from blocks. It was carved directly out of the bedrock. The builders cut away the rock around it, leaving the figure behind. Look at its body and you can see horizontal bands of softer and harder stone.

Those bands explain why the Sphinx looks so worn. The softer layers erode faster in the wind, leaving deep grooves. For long stretches of history, sand buried the Sphinx up to its neck. And then there is the missing nose. Locals and guides sometimes say Napoleon's soldiers shot it off with a cannon. It is a good story, but it is not true. Drawings made before the French arrived in Egypt already show the face without its nose.

Look between the great pyramids and you will see the smaller structures that fill this landscape. Beside Khufu's pyramid are three small queens' pyramids. To the east and west stretch fields of mastabas. A mastaba is a flat-roofed rectangular tomb, built for officials and members of the royal family. In 1954, a sealed pit beside the Great Pyramid gave up a complete wooden boat, taken apart and stored in pieces. It was carefully rebuilt and has since moved to a new museum nearby.

Pause for a moment and turn in a slow circle. To one side is open desert, stretching toward the horizon. To the other, the apartment blocks of Giza press almost to the edge of the plateau. Camel handlers call out. Horses shake their bells. Tour buses idle in the heat. This mix of ancient stone and modern noise is part of the experience. The pyramids were never remote. They were always built to be seen from the river and the green valley below.

Before you leave, find a patch of shade or a low stone and simply look. Watch how the light moves across the limestone. In the morning the stone looks pale and cool. Later in the day it turns warmer, almost golden. Notice the tiny figures of other people at the base of the Great Pyramid, and how small they look. As you walk away, glance back once more. These shapes have looked back at travellers for forty-five centuries... and they will still be here long after you have gone home.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid the strongest heat and crowds.
Bring water, a hat, and sturdy closed shoes.
Entering the Great Pyramid requires a separate ticket.
Agree on any camel or horse ride terms beforehand.
Walk to the desert viewpoint for all pyramids together.
