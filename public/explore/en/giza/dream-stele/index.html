The guide

The story

Feel the grit under your shoes. Fine, pale sand, the same sand that drifts across the Giza plateau with every desert wind. Now look ahead, toward the great lion body of the Sphinx. Between its outstretched paws stands a slab of dark red granite, upright like a tall stone door. It looks small against the monument behind it. But this slab carries one of the strangest stories in Egyptian history. It is the story of a prince, a nap in the shade, and a dream.

This is the Dream Stele. A stele is simply an upright stone slab carved with text and images, a kind of public notice in stone. This one was set up by Thutmose the Fourth, a king of the Eighteenth Dynasty. He ruled during the New Kingdom, the age of Egypt's great empire, around fourteen centuries before Christ. By his time the Sphinx was already very old. More than a thousand years separated him from the people who first carved it.

Here is the story the stele tells. Before he became king, the young prince Thutmose went hunting in the desert near Giza. He rode out in his chariot, shooting at targets and chasing game. At midday, the heat became too much. He stopped to rest in the shadow of the great stone figure. And there, as the sun stood high above him, he fell asleep. What happened next is the reason this stone exists.

In his dream, the Sphinx spoke to him. The text presents the Sphinx as a form of the sun god, called Horemakhet, which means Horus in the Horizon. The god complained that sand was pressing in on his body and choking him. He made the prince an offer. Clear away the sand, and you will wear the double crown of Egypt. Thutmose woke, the story says, and kept his side of the bargain.

That sand problem was real. Desert winds have buried the Sphinx again and again across its long life. For long periods only the head and shoulders showed above the dunes. So when you hear that a prince cleared it, picture serious work. Teams of labourers, baskets of sand, and walls of mudbrick built to hold the drifts back. Traces of such enclosure walls have been found around the Sphinx, though dating each one is not always simple.

Now, a gentle note of caution. Many Egyptologists read the dream as political theatre rather than a private memory. Thutmose may not have been the obvious heir to the throne. A royal dream, with a sun god choosing him by name, was a powerful way to say that his rule was willed by heaven. Kings across the ancient world used such stories. That does not make the stele less interesting. It makes it a window into how power was sold to the people.

Look at the top of the stele, at the curved upper section called the lunette. The carving there shows the king making offerings to the Sphinx. In fact, the scene appears twice, as a mirror image. On one side the king faces a sphinx on a high pedestal. On the other side, the same scene repeats in reverse. Above them both spreads the winged sun disk, a common symbol of divine protection.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The granite slab may not have started life as a stele at all. Many scholars believe it was a reused block, possibly a door lintel taken from an older temple nearby. A lintel is the heavy beam that spans the top of a doorway. The pyramid builders of Giza used red granite from Aswan, far to the south, for their finest doorways and linings. Thutmose's craftsmen may simply have borrowed one.

And here is a second surprise. The lower part of the text is badly damaged. Flakes of stone have broken away over the centuries. In one damaged line, early researchers saw part of a royal name that looked like Khafre, the Fourth Dynasty king whose pyramid rises behind you. For a long time, that broken fragment fed the idea that Khafre built the Sphinx. Today scholars rely on much stronger evidence, but that small fragment played its part in the debate.

For centuries, the stele itself lay hidden. Sand buried it along with the Sphinx's body. It came back into view in the early nineteenth century, when the Italian explorer Giovanni Battista Caviglia dug in front of the Sphinx. His team uncovered the space between the paws and found the slab still standing there. Since then it has been studied, copied and photographed many times. The original remains in place, where Thutmose's workers set it.

Think for a moment about the layers of time here. The Sphinx was carved in the Old Kingdom. Thutmose came much later and treated it as an ancient wonder, already in need of rescue. Then came Greek and Roman visitors, who also cleared sand and left their own marks. Then came the explorers of the nineteenth century. And now, you. Every generation has stood roughly where you stand, looking up at the same weathered face.

As you take in the scene, notice the scale. The stele is taller than a grown person, yet the paws on either side dwarf it. From a distance it can look like a doorway into the chest of the lion. Notice too the colour contrast. The pinkish red granite stands out against the softer, sand coloured limestone of the Sphinx body. Two kinds of stone, from two ends of Egypt, meet in one small space.

Visitors usually cannot walk right up to the paws. You will view the stele from the raised areas near the Sphinx, close to the old valley temple of Khafre. That distance has its own value. It lets you see the stele the way it was meant to be seen. Not as an object in a glass case, but as a small, deliberate message placed before a god.

So before you move on, look once more at that dark slab between the paws. Feel the warmth of the sun, the same midday sun that once sent a tired prince looking for shade. Listen to the wind moving across the plateau. It is still carrying sand toward the Sphinx, grain by grain, as it has for thousands of years. Somewhere beneath the voices of other visitors, the old bargain continues. Keep the sand away... and the stone keeps watch.
