The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. Under your shoes is a mix of fine sand and broken limestone, pale and gritty, and it crunches with every step. The wind comes in from the Western Desert, dry and warm, and it carries a faint dust that settles on your lips. Somewhere nearby a camel groans, a horse snorts, and a vendor calls out in three languages at once. Then you lift your eyes... and the pyramids are simply there, larger and closer than any photograph has prepared you for.

You are standing on a limestone shelf at the edge of the Nile Valley. To the east, green fields once ran down to the river. To the west, the desert begins and does not stop for a very long way. The ancient Egyptians placed their dead on the western side of the Nile, where the sun sets each evening. For them, the west was the land of the dead. This plateau was chosen with care. It was high, solid, and visible from far across the valley.

The largest monument here is the Great Pyramid, built for the king Khufu during Egypt's Fourth Dynasty, around four and a half thousand years ago. When it was finished, it stood about 146 metres tall. Today it is a little shorter, close to 138 metres, because its top layers are gone. For thousands of years, it was the tallest structure made by human hands anywhere on Earth. It is also the only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World that still stands.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The pyramids did not always look like this. They were once covered in smooth casing stones of fine white limestone, polished so the faces caught the sun. From a distance, they would have shone. In medieval times, most of that casing was stripped away and reused as building stone in the growing city of Cairo. What you see now is the rough inner core — a giant stepped pile of blocks that was never meant to be seen.

Look at the second pyramid, the one that seems almost as tall as the first. It belongs to Khafre, who ruled shortly after Khufu. It sits on higher ground, so it can look taller from some angles, but it is actually a little smaller. Now look near its peak. You can see a cap of smoother stone still clinging to the top. That is a surviving patch of the original casing. It gives you a small idea of how the whole plateau once looked.

The third main pyramid, built for Menkaure, is much smaller than the other two. Around these three great tombs are smaller pyramids for royal women, and long rows of flat-topped tombs called mastabas. A mastaba is a rectangular tomb with sloping sides, built for officials and members of the royal family. Walk among them and you will see that this was never just three monuments. It was a whole city of the dead, carefully organised around its kings.

So who built all this? For a long time, a popular story claimed the pyramids were built by slaves. The evidence points somewhere else. Archaeologists working just south of the plateau found the remains of a settlement where workers lived, with bakeries, breweries, and storage areas. Nearby, they found the workers' own tombs, built with care close to their kings. These were organised crews of labourers and skilled craftsmen, fed and housed by the state, and proud enough of their work to be buried beside it.

The second surprise comes from far away, on the coast of the Red Sea. At a site called Wadi al-Jarf, archaeologists found the oldest inscribed papyri known from Egypt. Among them is a logbook kept by an official named Merer. He recorded how his team moved blocks of fine limestone by boat along the Nile and its canals, for the construction of Khufu's pyramid. It is a rare thing — a working diary from the very project in front of you.

Now turn toward the Sphinx. It lies lower down the slope, facing the rising sun. Unlike the pyramids, it was not built from stacked blocks. It was carved directly out of the living rock of the plateau. The body of a lion carries the head of a king, usually identified as Khafre. Look at its body and you can see layers in the stone, some hard and some soft, worn away at different speeds by thousands of years of wind and sand.

You will notice that the Sphinx has no nose. Locals and tour guides sometimes tell a tale that Napoleon's soldiers shot it off with a cannon. That story is not true. Drawings made long before Napoleon arrived in Egypt already show the face without its nose. The damage happened earlier, and its exact cause is still debated. For many centuries, sand also buried the Sphinx up to its neck. Much of what you see today was cleared in modern times.

One more piece of this place now lives a short distance away. In 1954, workers found a sealed pit beside the Great Pyramid. Inside lay the pieces of a large wooden boat, dismantled and stored for Khufu. It was carefully rebuilt, and it is now displayed at the Grand Egyptian Museum, close to the plateau. Wood that survived four and a half thousand years in darkness is a reminder of how much of ancient life was not made of stone.

Take a moment to look past the monuments. On one side, the modern city of Giza presses right up to the edge of the plateau. You can see apartment blocks, satellite dishes, and traffic. On the other side, the desert stretches away, empty and quiet. Few places in the world put the ancient and the everyday so close together. The call to prayer may drift up from the neighbourhood below, mixing with the voices of visitors from every continent.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Notice the colour of the stone as the light changes — pale cream at midday, warmer gold toward evening. Put your hand on one of the lower blocks if you can, and feel how rough and warm it is. Thousands of people cut, dragged, and set these stones by hand. As you walk away, turn back once more. The pyramids will seem to stay the same size for a long time... and that is the real measure of them.
