The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, water runs fast over stone. The air smells of pine resin and cold earth, with a faint trace of woodsmoke from a hearth you cannot see. Prayer flags snap in the wind, red, blue, white, green and yellow, faded by sun and rain. The ground underfoot is uneven, a mix of packed soil and loose rock. Welcome to Langtang National Park, one of the closest great Himalayan landscapes to Kathmandu, and a place where mountains, forests and old faiths meet.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not realise. Langtang was the first national park created in the Himalayan region of Nepal. It was established in the 1970s, at a time when the country was only beginning to set aside land for protection. The park stretches across several districts north of the capital, including parts of Nuwakot, Rasuwa and Sindhupalchok. Its northern edge meets the border with Tibet. In a single park, the land climbs from warm, low forest to permanent ice.

That climb is the key to understanding Langtang. At the lower levels, you walk through forests of oak, maple and pine. Higher up, rhododendron takes over. In spring, these trees flower in shades of deep red and soft pink, and whole hillsides change colour for a few weeks. Higher still, the trees thin out into juniper scrub and alpine meadow. Above that, there is only rock, snow and glacier. Every few hours of walking can feel like a change of season.

The forests are home to animals that most people never see in the wild. The red panda lives here. It is a small, rust-coloured animal with a striped tail, more like a raccoon than a giant panda. It spends much of its life in the trees, eating bamboo. The park also shelters Himalayan black bears, musk deer and, in the high and remote areas, the snow leopard. Sightings are rare. But knowing they are near changes how a quiet forest path feels.

Now consider the name itself. According to a popular local tale, a Tibetan lama once followed a lost yak up into this valley. In this story, the word Langtang comes from the Tibetan words for yak and for following. It is folklore, not documented history, but it is the story you will hear most often from people who live here. And it fits the place. Yaks still graze the upper meadows, and their bells carry far across the valley.

The people of Langtang give the park its human character. Many communities here are Tamang, an ethnic group with roots in the hills around Kathmandu and close cultural ties to Tibet. Their villages are built from stone and timber, often with carved wooden windows. You will see Buddhist prayer wheels at the edges of settlements, and long walls of carved mani stones. These are flat stones cut with sacred prayers. By custom, people walk past them on the left side, keeping the wall to their right.

That custom is worth following yourself. When a path splits around a mani wall or a stupa, take the left-hand route, so you pass in a clockwise direction. This is a sign of respect in Tibetan Buddhist tradition, and local people notice when visitors do it. The same goes for prayer wheels. Turn them clockwise, with your right hand. These small gestures cost nothing, and they are often the start of a smile or a short conversation along the trail.

Higher in the main valley stands Kyanjin Gompa, a small Buddhist monastery surrounded by peaks. The settlement around it is a common resting point for trekkers. Here is a second surprise. Kyanjin is also known for its cheese. A cheese-making tradition was introduced to the area in the twentieth century with foreign support, using milk from yak and nak herds. Nak is the word for a female yak. Tasting hard yak cheese at this altitude is one of the stranger pleasures of the park.

Above the valley rise the great peaks. Langtang Lirung is the highest, a mountain of more than seven thousand metres, with a steep face of ice and dark rock. On clear mornings it fills the head of the valley. Glaciers spill down from the high ridges, and the valley is sometimes called the valley of glaciers. The light changes quickly here. At dawn the snow turns pale gold, and by midday the same slopes look hard and white.

The park also carries a painful memory. In 2015, a powerful earthquake struck central Nepal. In Langtang, it set off a massive avalanche and landslide that buried the original Langtang village. Many residents and visitors lost their lives. The village has since been rebuilt nearby, and the trail has reopened. If you pass through, you will see memorial stones and a changed landscape of rubble. Local people have worked hard to rebuild, and many welcome visitors as part of that recovery.

To the south-east of the main valley lies another side of Langtang. The Gosainkunda lakes sit high in the mountains, cold and still, surrounded by bare rock. They are sacred to both Hindus and Buddhists. Legend has it that the god Shiva created the main lake by striking the mountain with his trident, to find water to cool his throat after he swallowed poison. During a full-moon festival in summer, large numbers of pilgrims walk up to bathe in its icy water.

There is also the Tamang Heritage Trail, a route that focuses on village life rather than high peaks. It passes through farming settlements, terraced fields and hot springs. Here, the park feels less like wilderness and more like a lived-in place. Children walk to school along the same paths you use. Women carry loads of fodder in woven baskets on their backs. Mules pass by with bells on their necks, so step to the uphill side of the trail when they come.

Before you move on, pause where you are. Look at the colour of the stones beside the path, and the moss growing on their shaded side. Listen for the river, and for bells, voices or wind in the flags. This park was protected so that these sounds and sights would last. As you walk away, notice how the mountain behind you looks a little different with every step. In Langtang, the view is never quite the same twice.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk clockwise around mani walls, stupas and prayer wheels.
Step to the uphill side when mule trains pass.
Pack warm layers; temperatures fall sharply with altitude.
Ascend slowly to reduce the risk of altitude sickness.
Visit in spring to see rhododendron forests in flower.
