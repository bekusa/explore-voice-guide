The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through tall pines, and somewhere close, water touches a shore of sand and reeds. Under your feet the forest floor is soft with needles and moss. The air smells of resin, and on a warm day, of wild thyme drying on the slopes. This is Aukštaitija National Park in the far east of Lithuania. It is a country of lakes and forest, and the wide sky above it changes colour across the day.

A little orientation helps. The park was founded in 1974, and it was the first national park in Lithuania. Most of it lies in the Ignalina District, and its edges reach into neighbouring municipalities. The name comes from Aukštaitija, the Highlands, one of the historic ethnographic regions of the country. Here, highland does not mean mountains. It means rolling hills, ridges and hollows that the last Ice Age left behind.

That ice is the key to everything you see. When the glaciers melted, they left behind blocks of ice buried in sand and gravel. As those blocks thawed, the ground above them sank, and the hollows filled with water. The result is a landscape with more than one hundred lakes. Many of them are long and narrow and joined by small rivers and channels. On a map, the park looks like broken glass scattered across a green cloth.

Here is the first surprise. Lake Tauragnas, inside the park, is the deepest lake in Lithuania. It drops to roughly sixty metres, and its water is cold and clear. Some lakes here have islands, and a few of those islands have small lakes of their own. Lake Baluošas is the best-known example of this, a lake on an island in a lake. It is a small, odd detail of geography, and many visitors drive past without hearing about it.

The best place to understand the shape of the park is Ladakalnis hill. From its open, grassy top, you can see several lakes at once, and people often count six. They lie in the hollows between forested ridges, and each one catches the light in a slightly different way. Locals say the hill is named after Lada, a goddess from old Baltic belief. Treat that as folklore rather than recorded history. The view itself needs no legend.

That old belief is not just decoration. Lithuania was the last state in Europe to accept Christianity officially, in the late fourteenth century. In remote villages and forests like these, older customs lasted much longer. Hills, springs, large stones and old trees could all carry a sacred meaning. On Lake Tauragnas stands the Taurapilis hillfort, an ancient earth mound where people once built a fortified settlement. Grass now covers the slopes, but the shape of the defences is still easy to read.

Now look at the villages. Small timber houses stand in loose rows along a lane or around a pond. Many have carved window frames, painted shutters and steep roofs. Several villages in the park are protected as ethnographic villages. That means planners try to keep their traditional layout, wooden buildings and gardens. You may see stacked firewood, a well with a long lifting pole, and apple trees leaning over a fence. People still live here, so walk quietly and respect private yards.

In the village of Palūšė, on the shore of Lake Lūšiai, stands a wooden church from the eighteenth century. It is one of the oldest surviving wooden churches in Lithuania. Its bell tower stands apart from the main building, a common pattern in the region. The timber is dark with age, and the interior is simple and warm. Linger beside it for a moment. Wood this old is rare, because fire and war destroyed so many churches like it.

The second surprise is a museum about bees. In the village of Stripeikiai, an open-air museum shows the long story of beekeeping in Lithuania. Long before wooden hive boxes, people kept wild bees in hollows cut high into living trees. Beekeepers climbed with ropes to collect the honey. The museum displays old hives carved into the shapes of figures and faces, some playful and some solemn. Honey once mattered greatly in this region, for food, drink, wax and trade.

Water also powered daily life. At Ginučiai, an old watermill stands beside a small river. For generations, mills like this ground grain for nearby farms, and they were often a place for news and gossip. Today the mill is a quiet place to stop, with the sound of water running past the building. It is a good reminder that this landscape was not wilderness. People worked it with care for centuries.

The forest itself deserves attention. Pine dominates the sandy ridges, with spruce, birch and alder in the damper ground. In late summer, families come to pick blueberries and search for mushrooms. You might hear woodpeckers, see a heron lift from the reeds, or spot the tracks of deer in soft sand. Bogs and wetlands hold their own plants, including mosses and small carnivorous sundews. The park protects all of these, so stay on marked paths in sensitive areas.

The lakes are joined by small rivers, so the best way to travel here is often by water. Canoe routes link lake to lake, and a slow paddle shows you the reeds, the lily pads and the quiet bays. In the evening the water often turns perfectly still, and mist can rise at dawn. Cycling paths and walking trails also run between the villages. Distances look short on a map, but sandy tracks and hills make the journey slower and richer.

So take a moment where you stand. Notice the light on the nearest water, and the dark line of pines behind it. Listen for the wind, a bird, perhaps an oar somewhere out of sight. The ice that shaped this land melted thousands of years ago. The villages, the mills and the old wooden church came much later. As you walk on, watch how each hill opens a new lake, and each bend in the path changes the view again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb Ladakalnis hill near sunset for the multi-lake view.
Rent a canoe to travel between linked lakes.
Bring insect repellent for summer evenings near water.
Wear sturdy shoes for sandy, uneven forest tracks.
Respect private yards when walking through ethnographic villages.
