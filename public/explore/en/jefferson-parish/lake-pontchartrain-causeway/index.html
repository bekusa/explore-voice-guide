The guide

The story

Listen first. Hear the steady thump of tyres over concrete joints, a rhythm like a slow heartbeat. Then look out at the water. It is wide, flat and grey-green, and it stretches until it meets the sky. Two long, low ribbons of road run straight across it. You are on the Lake Pontchartrain Causeway, or close to its southern end in Metairie, in Jefferson Parish. The bridge does not climb or curve. It simply goes forward, close to the water, for nearly twenty-four miles.

That distance is easier to feel than to picture. It is about thirty-eight kilometres. On the far side lies Mandeville, in Saint Tammany Parish, on the north shore of the lake. For decades people in this region took the long way around the water. They drove around its edges or crossed by ferry. The Causeway changed that. It turned a long trip into a drive of well under an hour, and it pulled the quiet north shore into the daily life of greater New Orleans.

Here is the first surprise. On a hazy day, somewhere in the middle of the crossing, you can lose sight of land completely. Behind you, the shore has dropped below the horizon. Ahead, the far shore has not yet appeared. For a few minutes there is only water, sky and the road. Drivers often describe it as a strange feeling, like being at sea in a car. Many first-time visitors do not expect that from a bridge near a major city.

The Causeway is really two bridges side by side. The first span opened in 1956. A second, parallel span followed in 1969. Today traffic in each direction has its own bridge. Between them, at intervals, there are short crossovers. These link the two spans, so emergency vehicles can turn around or switch sides. From the road, they look like small concrete ladders laid across the gap between the two decks.

Now look down at the supports, if you can see them from the shore. The bridge stands on thousands of concrete pilings driven into the lake bed. The engineers relied on prestressed concrete. This means steel strands inside the concrete are pulled tight, which makes each piece stronger. The segments were cast on land, almost like products on an assembly line. Then barges carried them out and set them in place. That repetition is why the bridge looks so even and so calm.

This method was the clever idea behind the whole project. Instead of building a long bridge piece by piece in the water, the builders made identical parts in a factory setting. Speed came from sameness. Every span looks almost like the one before it. As you drive, the view hardly changes, and that is exactly the point. The design is not about drama. It is about getting across a very wide stretch of water simply and reliably.

The lake itself deserves your attention. Lake Pontchartrain is not a true freshwater lake. It is an estuary, a place where fresh water and salt water mix. It connects to the Gulf of Mexico through narrow passes to the east. The water is also surprisingly shallow, only a few metres deep across much of its area. That shallowness helped make a low bridge on pilings possible. A deep lake would have needed a very different design.

Here is the second surprise. For many years people argued about whether this was the longest bridge in the world. A bridge in China was longer in total length. So in 2011, Guinness World Records created a clearer category. It named the Causeway the longest continuous bridge over water. In other words, no other bridge stays over water without a break for so long. Locals still mention this title with quiet pride.

Weather shapes life on this bridge. Fog can roll across the lake and cover everything in white. Strong winds push hard against high vehicles. The Causeway has its own police force, which patrols the spans day and night. In poor conditions, traffic can be slowed or guided in groups. When hurricanes threaten the coast, the bridge becomes part of the region's emergency planning. It links the city to higher, safer ground on the north shore.

The bridge also has its own small landscape of details. Near the north shore, the road rises over raised sections that let boats pass underneath. Small call boxes and pull-off areas appear at intervals, for breakdowns and emergencies only. Mile markers count the distance as you cross. Brown pelicans often glide low over the water beside the spans. Gulls rest on the railings. At dawn, fishing boats sometimes work in the shallows nearby.

Back here on the Jefferson Parish side, the bridge flows straight into Causeway Boulevard. This is one of Metairie's main roads, lined with offices, shops and steady traffic. The contrast is sharp. On one side, there is the noise and movement of a busy suburb. On the other, there is open water and a single straight line of concrete. Standing near the lakefront, you can feel the city end and the lake begin.

Think about what the Causeway means to the people who use it every day. For many commuters, it is simply the road to work. They cross twice a day, listening to the radio and watching the light change. Over the decades, the bridge has helped the north shore grow from a quiet area into busy towns. Few structures change the shape of a region so much, and they rarely look this modest.

The best way to understand the Causeway is through light. In the early morning, the water can turn silver and pink, and the far shore stays hidden in mist. At midday, the sun is harsh and the lake looks almost white. At sunset, the spans become dark lines against an orange sky. Each time of day gives the same bridge a different mood.

So take one more moment where you are. Listen again for the rhythm of tyres over the joints. Watch the two spans run out over the water until they become thin lines and disappear. Somewhere out there, past the horizon, the other shore is waiting. As you turn away, notice how quiet the lake seems, even beside so much traffic. The bridge carries thousands of journeys every day, and the water simply lies still beneath it.
