The guide

The story

The wind arrives first. It comes up from the gorge below, cold even in summer, and pulls at your jacket as you walk out onto the curved stone platform. Underfoot the paving is worn and uneven. Ahead of you, the ground simply stops. Beyond the edge there is open air, and far below, a deep green valley folds into the mountains of the Greater Caucasus.

You are standing at the Russia–Georgia Friendship Monument, a large semicircular structure high on the Georgian Military Road. It sits between the ski resort of Gudauri and the Jvari Pass, the highest point of this old mountain route. Many travellers simply call it the Gudauri viewpoint. Whatever name you use, almost everyone driving north toward Kazbegi stops here, and it is easy to see why.

Look at the curved wall behind you. It is covered in a huge mosaic made from thousands of small coloured tiles. Reds, ochres, deep blues and gold. The figures are large and bold, in the style of Soviet public art — heroic faces, mothers and children, horsemen, and scenes from the shared history of Georgia and Russia. Walk slowly along the arc. The story unfolds panel by panel, and new details appear as you move.

The monument was built in 1983. Its purpose was to mark two hundred years since the Treaty of Georgievsk, signed in 1783. That treaty is the key to understanding this place. It was an agreement between the Russian Empire and the eastern Georgian kingdom of Kartli-Kakheti, ruled at the time by King Erekle the Second. Georgia was surrounded by powerful neighbours, and the king hoped for protection.

Here is the part many visitors miss. The treaty promised Georgia protection while keeping its own royal line. But less than twenty years later, in 1801, the Russian Empire annexed the kingdom outright. The Georgian monarchy came to an end. So a monument that celebrates friendship also marks the beginning of a long period of foreign rule. Georgians often view this structure with mixed feelings, and that tension is part of its meaning.

The road you came on also connects to that history. People and armies have crossed these mountains along this valley for many centuries. In the nineteenth century, the Russian Empire rebuilt the route as a military road linking Tbilisi with the north side of the Caucasus. Merchants, soldiers, writers and exiles travelled it. Russian poets wrote about these gorges. Today it carries trucks, minibuses and tourists, but the line of the road is very old.

Now turn back toward the edge and look down. The valley below is often called the Devil's Valley by visitors and guides. In summer the slopes are bright green and soft, almost like velvet from this height. Thin streams cut pale lines through the grass. In winter the whole scene turns white, and the ridges look sharp and close. On clear days, you can follow the folds of the mountains far into the distance.

Here is a second surprise. Look up, especially on a calm day. You may see bright coloured wings drifting above the valley. This viewpoint is one of the most popular places in Georgia for tandem paragliding. Pilots launch from the slopes nearby and glide out over the gorge. From the platform, you can watch them circle slowly in the rising air, tiny figures against an enormous landscape.

The monument changes with the weather. Clouds move fast at this altitude. One minute the valley is in full sun. The next, mist rises from below and fills the gorge, and the mosaic stands alone in grey light. Then the cloud lifts again. If the view is hidden when you arrive, wait a few minutes. Patience is often rewarded here.

Listen for a moment. Under the sound of the wind you may hear car doors, voices in many languages, and the bells of cattle or sheep on the slopes. Local sellers often set up stalls near the car park. You may find woollen hats and socks, honey, dried fruit, and churchkhela — a traditional Georgian sweet made of nuts threaded on a string and dipped in thickened grape juice. Friendly dogs often wander between the visitors.

The platform itself is part of the experience. It projects out over the slope, and the low walls give a strong feeling of height. The concrete and stone show their age. You can see weathering, patches of repair, and places where the tiles of the mosaic have faded or loosened. This is a monument that lives outdoors in a harsh climate, through snow, ice and fierce sun every year.

Think about the different ways people see this place. For some, it is a piece of Soviet history, a political message set in stone and tile. For others, it is simply the best view on the drive to Kazbegi. For artists, the mosaic is a fine example of late Soviet monumental design. All of these views are true at once. The monument holds them together on one curved wall.

Before you leave, stand still at the edge one more time. Feel the wind on your face and the cold air rising from the gorge. Look at the colours of the mosaic behind you, then at the green and grey of the mountains ahead. Notice how small the road looks as it winds on toward the pass. When you walk back to the car, glance over your shoulder. The monument looks different from every angle.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer, even on summer days.
Wait a few minutes if clouds cover the valley.
Visit in the morning before tour minibuses arrive.
Walk the full mosaic arc slowly from end to end.
Book tandem paragliding with a licensed local operator.
