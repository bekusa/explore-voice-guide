The guide

The story

Start with the sound. Behind you, the Star Ferry engines churn the water, and a horn carries across Victoria Harbour. Salt hangs in the air, mixed with diesel and the faint sweetness of a nearby bakery. In front of you is a low building with wide glass windows that look straight out at the water. This is the Hong Kong Museum of Art, and its view is part of what it shows.

You are standing on the southern tip of the Kowloon peninsula, in the district called Tsim Sha Tsui. The name is often translated as "sharp sandy point". Long before the hotels and shopping arcades, this was a shoreline facing the island across the water. Today the waterfront promenade runs right past the museum. Joggers, tour groups and couples taking photographs all pass the museum doors.

Look along the promenade and you will see a tall clock tower of red brick and granite. It is the last surviving piece of the old Kowloon railway terminus. Trains once ran from here toward mainland China. The station itself was demolished, but the tower was kept as a landmark. Beside it stands the Hong Kong Cultural Centre, with its long sloping roof. Together, these buildings form the cultural heart of this waterfront.

The museum is older than the building you see. It began in the early 1960s inside City Hall, across the harbour on Hong Kong Island. For three decades it shared that space with other public functions. In 1991 it moved here, to Kowloon, as part of the new cultural complex beside the clock tower. That earlier building was known for its tiled exterior and its inward-looking galleries. Many visitors barely noticed the harbour from inside.

Then came a long pause. In 2015 the museum closed for a major renovation, and it stayed closed for several years. When it reopened in late 2019, the change was clear. The architects had added gallery space and opened up the walls. Now large windows frame the harbour and the skyline of Hong Kong Island. Here is a detail many visitors miss: some of the most memorable views in the building are not on the walls at all.

So what is inside? The collection is large and varied, but it rests on a few strong pillars. There is Chinese painting and calligraphy. There are Chinese antiquities, including ceramics and decorative objects. There are historical pictures of the region. And there is a growing collection of work by Hong Kong artists. The museum is run by the city government, and the displays change often, so no two visits are quite the same.

The Chinese painting and calligraphy galleries reward slow looking. Calligraphy may seem distant if you do not read Chinese. But try to watch the brush instead of the words. Notice where the ink is thick and wet, and where the brush ran almost dry and left white streaks. Each stroke was made once, with no chance to correct it. A strong line shows confidence. A trembling line can show age, or emotion, or a deliberate style.

One treasure of the museum is the Xubaizhai collection. It is a major gift of classical Chinese painting and calligraphy, donated by a private collector, Low Chuck-tiu. It is one of the most important groups of historical Chinese painting in Hong Kong. Works from it are shown in rotation. Paper and silk fade under light, so these pieces rest in storage between displays. If you see one today, you are seeing something that may not return to view for some time.

Now for something surprising. One of the most popular galleries holds paintings made for foreigners, not for Chinese collectors. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Western traders arrived in southern China. Local artists painted harbours, warehouses and busy waterfronts for them to take home. These are often called China Trade paintings. Look for early scenes of Hong Kong, Macau and Guangzhou, then called Canton. Some show this very harbour, with sailing ships instead of ferries.

Compare those old harbour views with the window beside you. The hills of Hong Kong Island are the same shape. Almost everything else has changed. Where the painters showed a few low buildings on the shore, you now see a wall of glass towers. That contrast is one of the quiet pleasures of this museum. The past hangs on the wall, and the present waits just beyond the glass.

The Hong Kong art galleries tell a different story. Here you meet artists who grew up in a city between East and West. Some mixed ink painting with ideas from modern Western art. Others worked with photography, installation and video. You may see work that comments on crowded streets, migration, or the changing skyline. These galleries are a good place to ask how a busy, fast-changing city sees itself.

A practical note as you walk. The museum is spread over several floors, so take the lifts or stairs to explore all levels. Some special exhibitions need a separate ticket. Find a seat near a harbour window for a short rest. The light changes through the day. In the morning, the water looks pale and silver. Late in the afternoon, the towers across the harbour turn gold, and the glass reflects the sky.

When you step back outside, pause on the promenade for a moment. Listen again to the ferries and the slap of water against the seawall. Look at the clock tower, and the island skyline across the water. You have just seen this harbour through the eyes of painters from centuries ago. Now you can see it with your own eyes... and perhaps notice something they would have painted, too.
