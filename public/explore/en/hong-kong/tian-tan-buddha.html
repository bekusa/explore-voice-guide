The guide

The story

The climb begins with stone. More than two hundred and sixty steps rise ahead of you, curving up the green hillside. At the top sits a giant figure of bronze. The air on this plateau is cooler than the city below. Often a thin mist drifts across the slopes. With each step, the seated Buddha grows larger against the sky. Other visitors pause beside you to catch their breath and look up.

This is the Tian Tan Buddha. People here also call it the Big Buddha. It sits on a hill at Ngong Ping, on Lantau Island. The figure is made of bronze and stands about thirty-four metres tall. It weighs roughly two hundred and fifty tonnes. For its size, it is one of the largest seated Buddha statues of its kind. From the foot of the steps, it already fills your view.

The statue was finished in 1993. Workers spent years casting it in many separate pieces. The bronze panels were made on the mainland and brought across to Lantau. Then they were lifted into place and joined, one by one, on top of the hill. The surface was shaped to catch the light. The result is a single calm figure, looking out over the sea and the hills.

The name comes from the base. The platform beneath the Buddha is shaped like the Altar of Heaven in Beijing. In Chinese, that altar is called Tian Tan. So the statue took the same name. The round, layered base lifts the figure high above the steps. Three levels of platform raise it toward the open sky.

Look closely at the hands. The right hand is raised, with the palm facing out. This gesture means the removal of suffering. The left hand rests on the knee, palm open and turned upward. This gesture offers blessing and giving. Together they form a quiet message of peace, held in still bronze. The fingers are larger than a person.

The face is wide and calm. The eyes are half closed. The head tilts down a little, as if the Buddha is looking gently toward the people on the steps. Unlike many large statues, this one faces north, toward the mainland. Most such figures face south. Here the direction was a deliberate choice, so the Buddha looks toward the people of China.

Around the base stand six smaller bronze figures. They are often called the Offering of the Six Devas. Each one kneels and holds a gift. The gifts are flowers, incense, a lamp, ointment, fruit, and music. In Buddhist teaching, these stand for the qualities a person needs on the path to wisdom. They seem to present their offerings to the seated Buddha above.

Inside the base there are three floors. Visitors can enter and walk through quiet halls. One hall holds a large bell, marked with images and prayers. The bell is designed to sound at set times through the day. Each ring is meant to carry a wish: that people everywhere may be released from their worry. The rooms inside are cool and dim after the bright climb.

Step back outside and take in the setting. The Buddha shares this plateau with an old monastery just across the open ground. Together they make Ngong Ping a place of prayer. Monks have lived and worked on this hill for more than one hundred years. The statue joined them near the end of the twentieth century, a young addition to an older home.

The weather here changes quickly. One moment the figure stands clear against blue sky. The next, cloud rolls in and softens its outline. Many visitors wait for the mist to part. When it does, the bronze seems to float above the hill. It is a slow, patient kind of view, and it rewards those who are willing to stand and wait.

To reach this spot, most people ride a cable car or take a winding bus up the mountain. The journey itself is part of the visit. Green peaks rise on every side. The sea opens out below. By the time you arrive, the noise of the city feels far away. The pace slows. Voices drop. People speak more softly here.

There is no charge to stand before the Buddha and look up. Many who come are not Buddhist at all. They climb the steps simply to share the quiet. Some bow. Some light incense at the monastery nearby. Others sit on the steps and rest, watching the clouds move behind the great bronze head.

Stand at the foot of the steps for a moment before you leave. Watch others climb, slow and quiet, toward the calm bronze face. The Buddha has looked out from this hill for decades now. It asks for nothing. It simply sits, steady and still, above the changing weather and the moving sea.
