The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the long covered escalator hums as it carries commuters up the hillside. In front of you, behind a modest gate on Shelley Street, the noise seems to fall away. A small courtyard, a row of pointed arches, and a quiet that feels almost out of place in the Mid-Levels. This is Jamia Mosque, and it has stood on this slope longer than almost anything around it.

This is the oldest mosque in Hong Kong. Its story begins in the middle of the nineteenth century, only a few years after the British took control of Hong Kong Island. The colonial government granted this land to the Muslim community around that time. A first, simpler mosque rose here. The building you see today replaced it in 1915, when the community rebuilt and enlarged the place to fit a growing congregation.

So who were those first worshippers? Most came from the Indian subcontinent. Some were soldiers serving with British regiments. Others were police officers, watchmen, sailors and traders. They arrived in a young port city with very few familiar comforts. A place to pray together, in their own way, mattered enormously. This mosque became the centre of that community, and for many decades it was the heart of Muslim life on the island.

Here is a detail most visitors walk straight past. Look at the street signs nearby. Just around the corner you will find Mosque Street, and a little further on, Mosque Junction. These names come from this building. The mosque was here first, and the city grew up around it. The streets simply took their names from the place everyone already knew.

Now look at the building itself. The style is often described as Indo-Islamic. That term means a blend of Islamic architecture with building traditions from the Indian subcontinent. You can see it in the shape of the arches, which rise to a gentle point. You can see it in the tall, narrow windows and the decorative trim along the roofline. It is not a grand or showy building. It is careful, balanced and calm.

Notice the scale, too. Hong Kong is a city of towers, and this mosque sits low among them. Apartment blocks rise on every side. From some angles, the mosque looks tiny, almost tucked into the hill. Yet that contrast is part of its character. It reminds you that this slope once looked very different, with low buildings, open space and views towards the harbour.

If you look closely at the arches and openings, you may spot Arabic calligraphy. In mosques, calligraphy often takes the place of images of people or animals. Islamic tradition generally avoids those in places of worship. Instead, the written word itself becomes the decoration. Even if you cannot read the script, the flowing lines carry a kind of rhythm. They are worth a slow, careful look.

This is still a working place of worship, not a museum. Five times a day, Muslims gather here to pray. Friday midday prayers draw the largest crowds, and the courtyard can fill quickly. Non-Muslim visitors usually view the mosque from the gate and the courtyard area. The prayer hall is generally reserved for worshippers. If you would like to step further in, the respectful approach is simple — ask someone at the mosque first.

A few small courtesies go a long way here. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Speak quietly. Take photographs from outside, and avoid pointing your camera at people who are praying or washing before prayer. You may see worshippers removing their shoes or performing ablutions, which is the ritual washing of the hands, face and feet before prayer. These are private moments, even in a public courtyard.

The mosque also tells a wider story about Hong Kong. People often imagine the city as purely Cantonese and British. In fact, it has long been a meeting point for many communities. South Asian families have lived here for generations. Some have roots in this city that go back well over a century. Today the Muslim community also includes people from Indonesia, Pakistan, mainland China, Africa and the Middle East.

As the community grew, one small mosque was no longer enough. Larger mosques opened elsewhere, most notably the Kowloon Mosque on Nathan Road in Tsim Sha Tsui. Yet Jamia Mosque kept its special place as the first. Hong Kong now lists it as a historic building, recognising its importance to the city's heritage. It has been repaired and cared for over the years, but its basic form remains much as it was in 1915.

Think about how you arrived here. Most visitors come on the Central to Mid-Levels escalator, which opened in 1993 and climbs the hillside in long covered sections. The escalator brought a flood of new foot traffic to this quiet corner. Bars, cafés and restaurants now crowd the streets below in SoHo. Yet just a few steps away, this courtyard keeps its own slower pace. Two very different versions of Hong Kong sit side by side.

Before you go, stand still at the gate for one more minute. Look at the arches against the towers behind them. Listen for the escalator, the traffic, perhaps a voice from inside. Then, as you walk back down the hill, glance up at the street signs once more. Mosque Street, Mosque Junction... small reminders that this quiet building on the slope was here long before the city climbed up to meet it.
