The guide

The story

Salt hangs in the air. The smell of the sea and of drying fish drifts along the narrow lanes. Wooden houses stand on tall stilts above the water. Small boats knock gently against their posts with the movement of the tide. This is Tai O, an old fishing village on the western edge of Lantau Island, far from the towers of the city.

Tai O sits where a river meets the sea, on a low spit of land and water. For generations it has been home to the Tanka people, a community of fisherfolk. They built their houses not on solid land but out over the water itself. The houses rest on tall wooden legs, lifted above the tide. At high water, the whole village seems to float.

These stilt houses are the heart of Tai O. Local people call them pang uk. They are joined together by narrow walkways and small bridges that cross the channels. Families have lived in them for many years, often passing the same house down through several generations. Laundry hangs drying from the rails. Cats doze in the cool shade beneath the boards.

Walk the lanes and you pass one open shopfront after another. Many sell dried seafood, spread out in flat baskets and trays. Fish, shrimp, and squid dry slowly in the sun until they turn hard and golden. The smell is strong and salty and fills the whole street. For many visitors, that smell is the first thing they remember long after they leave the village.

Tai O is well known for one product in particular: shrimp paste. The villagers catch tiny shrimp in the shallows, salt them, and grind them into a thick, dense paste. It is then left out to ripen in the sun for weeks. The finished paste is used in cooking across the whole region, to add a deep and salty flavour to a dish. Many small shops sell it in jars.

Water runs right through the centre of the village. Narrow channels divide the houses, and small boats move along them. For a small fee, local boatmen take visitors out on short trips. They steer slowly between the crowded stilt houses, then turn out toward the open sea, where the water suddenly widens under a big, bright sky.

On these boat trips, people watch the water for dolphins. The sea near Lantau is home to a rare kind of dolphin with pale pink and white skin. They are shy, and the boatmen never promise that one will appear. But now and then a smooth pink back curves up out of the grey water, just for a second, and everyone on the boat falls quiet.

Back on dry land, look for the temples. Tai O has old shrines dedicated to the sea goddess who is said to watch over fishermen. Inside, the air is dark and heavy with incense smoke. Offerings of fruit and flowers are laid before the altars. For a village that lives from the sea, such temples have long been a place to pray for a safe return home.

Life in Tai O moves at the slow pace of the tide. In the early morning, fishermen sort through the night's catch. Older villagers sit out on low stools and talk together. The lanes are far too narrow for cars, so everyone walks or rides a bicycle. There is time here to stop, to look around, and simply to let the long day pass.

The village has faced hard times over the years. Fires have swept through the wooden stilt houses more than once, and many of the young people have left for work in the city. Yet Tai O holds on. Families rebuild their homes. Visitors come out on weekends to see it. The old way of life bends and changes with the times, but it has not yet let go.

Climb a little way above the houses and turn to look back. The roofs spread out over the water below, silver, brown, and rusted red. Beyond them the open sea reaches out toward low, distant islands. At sunset the light turns warm and gold, and the whole village seems to glow for a short while before the dark comes in off the water.

Tai O keeps a foot in two worlds. It is a real working village, where people still fish and dry their catch as they always have. It is also a place that visitors love, drawn by the boats, the houses, and the quiet. The two sides sit side by side in the same narrow lanes, sometimes uneasily, but for now they share the same salty air.

Before you leave, stand on one of the small bridges and watch the tide move slowly under the houses. Listen to the knock of the boats and the call of seabirds overhead. Tai O has lived by the sea for a very long time, taking what it needs and giving the rest back. It keeps its own slow rhythm, far from the rush of the city across the green hills.
