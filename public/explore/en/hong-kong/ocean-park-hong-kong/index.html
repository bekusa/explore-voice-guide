The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, a cable car cabin creaks softly as it rolls over its wheels. Below, the sea moves against the rocks. And from across the park comes a long, rising scream from a roller coaster on the hill. The air here smells of salt, warm concrete, and fried snacks. This is Ocean Park, on the southern side of Hong Kong Island. It is a theme park, a zoo, and an aquarium, all pressed between steep green hills and the open water.

Ocean Park opened in 1977. At that time, Hong Kong had very few big leisure spaces for families. The government gave the land, and the Royal Hong Kong Jockey Club paid for much of the first construction. The idea was simple but ambitious. People would come to learn about the ocean and also to have fun. That mix of education and thrills is still at the heart of the park almost fifty years later.

The first thing to understand is the shape of the land. The park is not one flat site. It is split into two main zones. The lower area, near the main entrance, is called the Waterfront. The higher area sits on a headland that reaches out into the sea, and it is called the Summit. Between them rises a steep ridge. For decades, the question has been the same — how do you move thousands of people over a mountain every day?

The answer, from the very beginning, was the cable car. Small open cabins carry visitors up and over the ridge and then down toward the Summit. On a clear day, the ride gives one of the best views on the island. You see the South China Sea, scattered islands, fishing boats, and the coastline curving away. Many local people remember this ride from childhood. For them, the swaying cabin and the wind are the real memory of Ocean Park.

In the 2000s, the park added a second way to cross the hill. It is a funicular railway called the Ocean Express, and it runs through a tunnel inside the mountain. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The trip is short, but the inside of the train is designed to feel like a submarine, with screens that show a journey down into the deep sea. So while the cable car takes you up into the sky, the Ocean Express pretends to take you under the water.

That second railway was part of a large redevelopment. In 2005, Hong Kong Disneyland opened on Lantau Island. Many people expected the older local park to struggle. Instead, Ocean Park invested heavily in new attractions, new animal habitats, and a new layout. The years that followed were some of its strongest. For many Hong Kong people, this became a small point of local pride. The homegrown park held its ground against a global brand.

Now let us talk about the animals that made the park famous far beyond Hong Kong — the giant pandas. In 1999, two pandas named An An and Jia Jia arrived as a gift from the central government. They became much-loved residents. Jia Jia lived to a very old age for a panda, and so did An An. Their long lives were a quiet success story for the care teams here, and visitors followed their news like news about family members.

The next part of the panda story brought a real surprise. In 2007, two younger pandas, Ying Ying and Le Le, arrived. For many years, keepers hoped for a cub. Then, in 2024, Ying Ying gave birth to twins. They were the first giant pandas born in Hong Kong. Ying Ying was also unusually old for a first-time mother. Later that same year, two more pandas arrived from the mainland. So the park now cares for a whole small panda community.

The pandas are only one part of the animal collection. Near the Waterfront, look for the Grand Aquarium. Inside, a huge curved window and a domed viewing area surround visitors with water. Schools of fish pass overhead, and rays glide slowly across the glass. Elsewhere in the park, there are sea lions, penguins, jellyfish, and other polar and tropical species. The park also runs conservation and research work, including programmes connected to local marine life in Hong Kong waters.

Of course, many people come here for the rides. Up at the Summit, the coasters are built right on the edge of the headland. This creates a strange and exciting effect. At the top of a drop, the track seems to end in open sky, with the sea far below. Even if you never ride, it is worth standing nearby to watch. The screams carry across the water, and the steel tracks look thin against the blue horizon.

The park has kept growing in recent years. In 2021, a separate water park called Water World opened next to the main site, along the coast at Tai Shue Wan. Getting here also became much easier when the South Island Line of the MTR opened in 2016. It has a station called Ocean Park, right by the entrance. Before that, most people arrived by bus, taxi, or tour coach along the winding southern roads.

There is also a gentle side to this place that many guidebooks skip. In the quieter corners, you can find gardens, shaded paths, and benches facing the sea. Older Hong Kong families often walk here slowly with grandchildren. Couples come in the late afternoon to watch the light change over the water. In the evening, the hills turn dark green and then black, and the lights of the rides start to glow. The park feels softer then, and more local.

One more detail is worth noticing. The park's longtime mascot is a cheerful sea lion called Whiskers. You will see him on signs, on souvenirs, and sometimes in person, waving to children. He is a small reminder of the original promise from 1977. This was meant to be a place about the ocean first. Rides and shows came alongside that idea, but the sea was always the main character here.

So take a moment before you move on. Turn toward the water if you can see it. Notice the islands in the haze, the boats, the green slopes falling down to the shore. Listen again for the cable cars turning on their wheels above you. Hong Kong is a city of towers and crowds, yet here the mountain and the sea still set the shape of the day. As you walk on, keep looking up — and keep looking out.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to reach the panda habitat before the crowds.
Ride the cable car one way for sea views.
Use the Ocean Express funicular when the cable car queue is long.
Bring sunscreen and water for the exposed Summit area.
Stay into the evening to see the rides lit up.
