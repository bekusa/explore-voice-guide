The guide

The story

Start with the sound. Behind you, the low diesel hum of a Star Ferry pulls away from the pier. A gull calls somewhere over the water. Then lift your eyes. Rising above the promenade is a tower of red brick and pale granite, square and steady, with a clock on each face. It looks a little out of place among the glass and concrete around it. That is exactly the point. This tower is a survivor.

This is the Clock Tower at Tsim Sha Tsui, on the southern tip of the Kowloon Peninsula. Today it stands almost alone, but it was never meant to. It was once part of a much larger building, the terminus of the Kowloon-Canton Railway. For decades, this spot was where trains ended their journey from the Chinese border and the city of Guangzhou, once known in English as Canton.

The railway opened its British section in 1910. The tower followed a few years later, and it was completed in 1915. Imagine this waterfront then. Steam trains hissed into the station behind the tower. Passengers stepped down from the carriages, walked a few minutes, and boarded ferries across the harbour to Hong Kong Island. Rail met sea right here, and the tower marked the meeting point.

Look closely at the building itself. The style is often described as Edwardian, the British architecture of the early twentieth century. It favoured warm red brick set off by bands of light stone. You can see that contrast clearly on the tower's corners and around its windows. The design is solid rather than showy. It was built to be seen from the water, a clear landmark for anyone arriving by boat.

Here is something many visitors miss. The clock faces did not start working when the tower was finished. The clocks were only installed and set running several years later, in the early 1920s. So for a time, this was a clock tower without a working clock. Since then, it has kept time for generations of travellers, commuters and dockworkers moving along this shore.

Raise your eyes to the very top. Above the cupola, a tall lightning rod points into the sky. It adds noticeably to the tower's height, and it gives the whole structure a sharper, more upright silhouette. Altogether the tower rises roughly forty-four metres. In the early twentieth century, that made it one of the tallest structures on this side of the harbour.

Now think about what happened to the station around it. As Hong Kong grew, this cramped waterfront site became too small for a modern railway. In the 1970s, the terminus moved east to Hung Hom, where the line still ends today. The old station building was demolished later in that decade. There was a public debate about what to keep. In the end, only the tower was saved.

That decision is why you see it standing by itself. The station hall, the platforms and the tracks are all gone. The tower stayed as a memory of the railway age. In 1990 it was declared a monument, which gives it legal protection under Hong Kong's heritage laws. Of all the buildings that once stood on this stretch of shore, it is one of the very few that remain.

Turn around and look at the curved, windowless building behind you. That is the Hong Kong Cultural Centre, which opened in the late 1980s on the land where the station once stood. Its sloping tiled roof could hardly be more different from the brick tower. Some people love the contrast and some dislike it. Either way, the old and the new stand side by side, and you can compare them in a single glance.

Here is a second surprise. The railway that ended here was not just a local line. Through its connection at the border, it linked into the wider rail network of mainland China. Locals sometimes say that, in theory, a traveller could set out from this spot and continue overland by train all the way to Europe. Whether many people ever did so is another matter. But the idea shows how connected this small station once felt.

Today the tower is a meeting point. Friends wait beneath it. Tour groups gather at its base. Wedding couples come here for photographs with the harbour behind them. In the evening, the promenade fills with people walking, jogging and watching the lights come on across the water. The skyscrapers of Central and Wan Chai glow on the far shore, and the tower stands in the foreground, lit warmly against the dark.

If you have a little time, follow the waterfront east. The promenade continues past the Cultural Centre and toward the Avenue of Stars, which celebrates Hong Kong's film industry. Or walk west a few steps to the Star Ferry pier. A ride across the harbour is short, and it gives you the view the tower was built for: the brick face rising from the shoreline as you approach from the water.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Listen for the ferry engines, the wind off the harbour, the voices of people around you. Look up once more at the clock faces. They have watched trains arrive and leave, a station rise and fall, and a skyline grow across the water. When you walk away, glance back over your shoulder. The tower will still be there, keeping time by the harbour.
