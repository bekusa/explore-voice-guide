The guide

The story

Listen to the ground first. Under your shoes, the cobblestones are uneven, rounded and worn smooth by countless feet. Somewhere nearby, a horse's hooves knock against stone. The air may carry wood smoke, or the warm smell of bread from a bakery oven. The rooflines lean slightly, the timber frames are dark against pale plaster, and the windows hold small panes of old glass. Welcome to Den Gamle By, the Old Town of Aarhus, where Danish town life is laid out street by street.

Here is the first surprise. This town was never a town. Almost every building around you once stood somewhere else in Denmark. Each one was taken apart, piece by piece, then carried here and rebuilt. The beams were numbered. The bricks were counted. Then the whole puzzle was put together again on this site. What looks like a small market town that grew slowly over centuries was actually assembled with great care, one house at a time.

The idea began in the early twentieth century with a local man named Peter Holm. He saw that old Danish townhouses were disappearing as cities modernised. Industry was growing, streets were widening, and old timber houses were being torn down. Holm wanted to save not only the buildings but the everyday life inside them. The first house was moved here in 1909, and the museum opened in 1914. It is often described as the first open-air museum in the world devoted to town life, rather than country life.

That first building is worth finding. It is a large half-timbered merchant's house, known as the Mayor's House, and it came from the city of Aalborg in the north of Jutland. Half-timbering means a frame of heavy wooden beams, with the gaps filled by brick or plaster. Look at the carved details on the front, and at the way the upper floors sit slightly forward over the street. That overhang gave extra room upstairs while keeping the street below narrow.

As you walk on, notice how the older part of the museum feels. These streets show Danish town life from roughly the sixteenth century up to the middle of the nineteenth century. This was the world of craftsmen, merchants, and small shops. There are workshops for trades like baking, weaving, and metalwork. There are merchants' yards with storerooms at the back. And there are simple homes where whole families lived in a few small rooms, often around one warm stove.

Step inside wherever a door stands open. The rooms are furnished as they would have been, down to the plates on the shelves and the coats on the hooks. You may notice how low some doorways are, and how little light comes through the windows. Many visitors are surprised by the smell of these interiors... old wood, wax, and a faint trace of smoke. In winter, the rooms feel close and warm. In summer, the thick walls keep them cool.

Now listen for voices. Some of the people you meet here are dressed in clothing from the period around them. They are not only costumed guides. Many of them play the role of the people who might have lived or worked in these houses. You might find a merchant behind a counter, a maid in a kitchen, or a craftsman at a bench. Ask them questions. They often answer as their character would, which makes a simple conversation into a small history lesson.

Here is the second surprise. The museum does not stop in the distant past. Beyond the old streets, there is a neighbourhood set in 1927. Here the town has electricity, shop windows are larger, and advertising signs fill the walls. You can see how quickly daily life changed in the early twentieth century. Telephones, new kinds of food, and modern shops all arrive in this part of town.

Walk further, and the calendar jumps again, to 1974. For many older Danish visitors, this is the most moving part of the museum. They walk into apartments and shops that look exactly like the homes of their parents, or their own childhood. Look for the colours of the period: orange, brown, and bright patterned wallpaper. Kitchens, record players, and cars on the street all belong to that decade. It shows that ordinary recent life is history too.

This layering of time is the real design of the place. In a short walk, you move across several hundred years of Danish town life. You can compare a candlelit room with an electric kitchen, a hand-painted sign with a printed advertisement. The museum asks a quiet question as you go: which parts of our own daily lives will one day stand in a museum like this? It is a question that works in any language and any country.

The museum also holds smaller collections inside its buildings. Over the years, these have included displays of toys, clocks, posters, and decorative arts. Exhibitions change, so it is worth asking at the entrance what is open during your visit. Children often find the toys the most interesting part. Adults are sometimes drawn to the craftsmanship of the clocks and the silverwork, made by hand long before machines took over.

The seasons change the mood of the town completely. In summer, the gardens are green, and the streets fill with people and animals. At Christmas, the museum is especially popular with Danish families. The houses are decorated in the style of their own periods, so you can see how Christmas looked in different centuries. Snow on the rooftops, warm windows, and the smell of spiced baking make the old streets feel very much alive.

The museum sits close to the Botanical Garden of Aarhus, so green space is never far away. After you leave the streets, you can walk among trees and quiet paths. It is a good place to sit, think, and let the visit settle. The modern city of Aarhus is only a short distance away, with its university, harbour, and new architecture. The contrast makes the old town feel even more carefully kept.

Before you go, pause for a moment where you are standing. Look at the line of the roofs and the shapes of the chimneys. Notice the textures: weathered wood, rough plaster, smooth stone. Listen for the small sounds, a door closing, a cart wheel, a conversation in Danish. None of these houses began here. But together, they now tell one long story of how Danes lived in their towns. As you walk away, you may start to see your own street a little differently.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ask costumed staff questions; many answer in character.
Allow several hours to cover all three time periods.
Visit at Christmas for period-style decorations in every house.
Ask at the entrance which indoor exhibitions are currently open.
Finish with a walk through the nearby Botanical Garden.
