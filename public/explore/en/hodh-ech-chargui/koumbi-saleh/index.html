The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind is the loudest thing. It moves across the sand in long, dry breaths, and it lifts fine grit against your ankles. Under your feet the ground is uneven, scattered with flat pieces of dark stone. Some of them are not natural rubble at all. They are fallen walls. You are standing in what many historians believe was once the capital of one of the great kingdoms of West Africa.

This is Koumbi Saleh, in the Hodh Ech Chargui region of southeastern Mauritania, not far from the border with Mali. Today the place looks empty. Low mounds rise from the plain, and outlines of streets and rooms show through the sand. Many scholars connect this site with the Ghana Empire, a state its own people called Wagadu. At its height, roughly a thousand years ago, it controlled trade across a huge stretch of the western Sahel.

Here is the first surprise. This ancient Ghana has almost nothing to do with the modern country of Ghana. That nation lies far to the southeast, on the Atlantic coast. When it won independence in 1957, its leaders chose the name to honour this old empire as a symbol of African achievement. So the name travelled across centuries and hundreds of kilometres. The original Ghana sat here, on the southern edge of the desert.

Why here? The answer is trade. Camel caravans crossed the Sahara from North Africa, carrying salt, cloth, copper and other goods. To the south lay the gold fields of West Africa. Ghana stood between the two, and its kings grew wealthy by controlling the exchange. Gold went north. Salt came south. In a hot land, salt was essential for life, and it could be worth a great deal. This empty plain was once a crossroads of that commerce.

Much of what we know comes from a single written source. In 1068, a geographer in Muslim Spain named al-Bakri described the capital of Ghana. He never travelled here himself. Instead he collected reports from merchants and earlier writers. He wrote that the capital was really two towns. One was a town of Muslim merchants, with many mosques. The other was the royal town, where the king lived, some distance away.

Al-Bakri's account is full of vivid detail. He described a king who held court surrounded by gold ornaments, and dogs wearing collars of gold and silver. He wrote about sacred groves near the royal town, where local religious priests lived and where rulers were buried. Remember, these are second-hand reports. Some details may be exaggerated. But they tell us how distant observers saw this kingdom, as a place of real power and wealth.

Now look at the stones around you again. The ruins that archaeologists have studied here match the merchant town quite well. The buildings were made from flat, layered stone, a kind of slate or schist that splits into slabs. Builders stacked these slabs to make walls. The town had houses of several rooms, narrow lanes, and a large mosque near its centre. This was a solid, permanent settlement, not a temporary camp.

The second surprise comes from the history of the digging itself. French researchers began investigating the site in the early twentieth century, guided partly by local traditions that remembered a great town in this area. Later excavations continued through the middle of the century and again in the 1970s and 1980s. Archaeologists found imported goods, glass, pottery, and gravestones with Arabic inscriptions. These finds connected this remote place to a much wider world.

It is honest to say that not every scholar is fully convinced. The identification of Koumbi Saleh as the capital of Ghana is widely accepted, but it is still debated. Some researchers point out that al-Bakri described a royal town that has never been clearly located. Others suggest the capital may have moved over time. So when you stand here, you stand at a strong candidate, the best-supported answer we have, not a closed case.

Another debate concerns the end of the empire. A popular story says the Almoravids, a Berber movement from the western Sahara, conquered Ghana in the late eleventh century. Many historians now question this tale. They argue that Ghana weakened more slowly, through shifting trade routes, rival states and changing climate. Over the following centuries, power in the region moved to the empire of Mali. Eventually the town here was abandoned to the sand.

Think about the landscape that surrounds you. Today this is dry country, with sparse grass and scattered thorny trees. But the climate of the Sahel has changed over the centuries, and a large town needed reliable water. Wells were vital. Herders still move through this region with cattle, goats and camels, following old patterns of seasonal grazing. The human presence here never fully disappeared. It simply changed its shape and its scale.

As you walk, move carefully. The mounds and wall lines are fragile, and every disturbed stone erases a little of the record. Notice how the light changes the ruins. In the low sun of morning or late afternoon, shadows sharpen the edges of walls and reveal the grid of the old streets. At midday, everything flattens under white light and heat, and the town almost disappears into the plain.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the dark slabs at your feet, laid by hands that traded with merchants from across the desert. Listen to the wind again. A thousand years ago, this same air carried the sounds of camels, voices in many languages, and calls to prayer. Now it carries only sand. As you walk back, watch the ground. Each flat stone is a small piece of a vanished city.
