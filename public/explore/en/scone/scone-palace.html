The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel. It crunches under every step on the long drive, and somewhere across the lawns a peacock calls, a sharp and strange cry that carries through the trees. The air smells of cut grass and damp pine. Ahead of you rises a long house of reddish sandstone, with battlements, pointed windows and slim turrets. It looks like a medieval castle. It is not. But the ground it stands on is much older than its walls.

This is Scone Palace, just outside the city of Perth, close to the River Tay. For centuries, this patch of Perthshire was one of the most important places in Scotland. It was not a fortress or a capital in the usual sense. It was a place of ceremony. Scottish kings came here to be made kings. The palace you see today belongs to a much later chapter, but it sits on top of that older story.

Start with the small mound in front of the house, topped by a little chapel. This is Moot Hill. Here, early Scottish kings were inaugurated, seated in front of their nobles and declared rulers of the realm. The name probably comes from the old idea of a moot, a meeting or assembly. Stand on the hill for a moment and look around. This modest rise of earth was, for a long time, the political heart of a kingdom.

Locals tell a lovely story about how the hill was made. According to one popular tale, every nobleman who came to swear loyalty brought earth from his own lands in his boots. He shook it out here, so he could stand on his own soil while making his oath. Over the years, the legend says, all that earth became a hill. That is why some people still call it Boot Hill. It is folklore, not documented history, but it is a good one.

The most famous object linked to this place is a block of sandstone. It is called the Stone of Scone, or the Stone of Destiny. Kings were enthroned upon it. Legends give it grand origins, even linking it to biblical stories, but those tales cannot be proven. What is certain is what happened in 1296. The English king Edward the First carried the stone south to Westminster Abbey in London, where it sat beneath the English coronation chair for seven centuries.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The stone on Moot Hill today is a replica. The original returned to Scotland in 1996. For years it was kept at Edinburgh Castle, and more recently it moved to a new museum in Perth, only a short drive from here. So the real stone is back in this part of Scotland, close to the hill where it once stood. It still travels to London when a new British monarch is crowned.

There was also once a great abbey at Scone. Monks lived and prayed here through the medieval centuries, and the abbey was closely tied to the royal ceremonies. In 1559, during the Scottish Reformation, a religious revolution that broke with the Catholic Church, the abbey was attacked and burned. This happened shortly after the reformer John Knox preached a fierce sermon in nearby Perth. Almost nothing of the abbey survives above ground today.

One more coronation took place here after that destruction. In 1651, Charles the Second was crowned at Scone, during the turmoil of the civil wars that divided Britain. It was the last coronation held at this place. After that, the age of kings at Scone came to a close. The site passed into private hands, and slowly the ceremonial ground became the setting for a family home.

That family is the Murrays, the Earls of Mansfield, who still live here. The house in its present form dates from the early nineteenth century, when an older building was remodelled in the Gothic Revival style. That style borrowed the look of medieval churches and castles, with pointed arches and battlements, but it was built for comfort. So the turrets are mostly for effect. Walk along the front and you can see how carefully the designers wanted it to feel ancient.

Inside, the rooms hold one of the finest private collections in Scotland. You will see French furniture, delicate porcelain, carved ivories and clocks. Look out for a collection of small objects decorated with Vernis Martin, an eighteenth-century French lacquer technique. It gave wood and papier-mâché a glossy, painted finish, much like Asian lacquer. The rooms feel lived in rather than staged. Portraits of family members watch from the walls, and the long gallery stretches away with its polished wooden floor.

One of those portraits holds a remarkable story. It shows two young women together. One is Lady Elizabeth Murray. The other is Dido Elizabeth Belle, her cousin, a woman of mixed race. Dido was raised in the household of the first Earl of Mansfield, William Murray, a senior judge in eighteenth-century England. Her life inspired a feature film. The painting is unusual for its time, because it shows both women with real warmth and dignity. Pause in front of it.

Now step outside again for the second surprise. The botanist David Douglas was born in Scone and worked as a young gardener on this estate. He later travelled to North America and sent back many plants and seeds. The Douglas fir is named after him. In the grounds there is a pinetum, a collection of conifer trees, where some giant firs and pines rise high above the paths. Walking under them feels like stepping into a cathedral of bark and needles.

The gardens have lighter pleasures too. There is a maze planted in the shape of a star, linked to the family's coat of arms, and it is a happy place to get lost for ten minutes. Peacocks wander freely across the lawns, often close to the house. Near the drive you may spot an old stone cross. It once marked the market place of the old village of Scone, which was moved away from the palace in the early nineteenth century.

Before you leave, walk back to Moot Hill. Feel the soft grass under your feet and look at the small chapel, the red stone of the house and the dark trees beyond. Kings were made here, a stone was taken away and brought home, and a gardener's son grew up to name a forest. Listen once more for the peacock. Then, as you walk back down the drive, notice the gravel again — and the quiet that settles in behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb Moot Hill first to understand the site's royal role.
Visit the nearby Perth museum to see the real stone.
Leave time for the pinetum walk under the giant conifers.
Try the star-shaped maze if travelling with children.
Bring a waterproof layer; Perthshire weather changes quickly.
