The guide

The story

Listen first. The River Tay moves past this flat green terrace with a low, steady rush. The grass is soft underfoot, and the wind comes down the valley from the Highland hills to the north. There is no wall here, no gate, no ticket booth. Just a raised plateau, a few gentle ridges in the turf, and sheep or cattle grazing quietly. And yet, under your feet, archaeologists traced one of the most complete Roman military plans anywhere in the empire.

This is Inchtuthil, a short drive northeast of Scone and Perth, close to the village of Caputh. Around two thousand years ago, Rome planned to make this place the anchor of its hold on northern Britain. It was a legionary fortress, built for a whole legion of around five thousand professional soldiers. That made it far larger than the many small forts and watchtowers the army built elsewhere across Scotland.

The timing matters. In the early 80s AD, the Roman governor Agricola pushed his armies deep into what is now Scotland. The Roman historian Tacitus, who was Agricola's son-in-law, describes a great battle at a place called Mons Graupius, where Roman forces defeated the Caledonian tribes. Nobody knows exactly where that battle happened. But a fortress on this scale, here on the Tay, shows that Rome intended to stay.

The site itself was a careful choice. The plateau sits above the river, with natural slopes on several sides. The Tay gave water and a route for supply boats. And the fortress faced the mouths of the Highland glens, the valleys that lead into the mountains. From here, soldiers could watch the routes that tribes used to move between the hills and the lowlands. Look north now, and you can see that same line of hills.

The fortress covered roughly twenty hectares, a rectangle about the size of a small town. A ditch and a rampart enclosed it, and the wall later received a stone facing. Inside, the Romans laid out streets in their usual grid. There were rows of barrack blocks for the soldiers, granaries for grain, workshops, and a large hospital. Almost all of the buildings were timber. That is why nothing stands today.

Here is the first surprise. The fortress was never finished. Excavators found the barracks, the granaries and the hospital, but the commander's residence was never built. The headquarters building was smaller than normal. Even some of the officers' houses were missing. It seems the builders started with what the soldiers needed most, and they planned to add the grander buildings later. That later moment never came.

Around the mid-80s AD, only a few years after construction began, the army left. Coins found at the site help to date this departure. The usual explanation is that Rome needed troops urgently on the Danube frontier in central Europe, where wars had broken out. A legion was withdrawn from Britain, and the far north could no longer be held at the same strength. So the soldiers packed up and marched south.

They did not simply walk away. They dismantled the fortress on purpose. Timber was pulled down or burned. Useful materials were taken. And anything the army could not carry, it tried to make useless to its enemies. This was standard Roman practice when an army abandoned a base. They did not want local tribes to take weapons, tools or metal that could be turned against Rome.

That brings us to the most famous discovery at Inchtuthil. In 1960, excavators found a pit beneath the floor of a workshop. Inside were iron nails, hundreds of thousands of them, together weighing several tonnes. Before leaving, the Romans buried them deep and covered them with earth. Iron was valuable, and the nails could have been reforged into spears. The nails stayed hidden for almost nineteen centuries.

The nails range from tiny tacks to long spikes for heavy timbers. The outer layer rusted into a solid crust, which protected the nails in the middle. Many came out almost as clean as the day they were made. After the excavation, many were given to museums and universities across the world. So pieces of this quiet field in Perthshire now sit in display cases far from Scotland.

Much of what we know comes from long excavations in the 1950s and 1960s, led by the archaeologists Ian Richmond and Kenneth Saint Joseph. Because the fortress was occupied so briefly, later buildings never covered the Roman plan. Elsewhere in Britain, legionary fortresses lie under the cities of York, Chester and Caerleon. Here, the whole layout was clear in one field. Aerial photography also helped, because buried ditches show as darker lines in growing crops.

That is the second surprise. The emptiness here is the reason the site is so valuable. No medieval town grew on top, and no modern streets cut through it. Today the land is farmland, and the remains are mostly below ground. On a clear day, try to read the faint ridges and dips in the grass. In low winter sun or at evening, long shadows make the old rampart line easier to see.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look along the plateau. Picture rows of timber barracks, smoke from cooking fires, and the sound of thousands of boots on gravel streets. Then look at what is here now: grass, river, wind, and the hills to the north. As you walk away, listen again to the Tay. It ran past the soldiers then, and it runs past you now.
