The guide

The story

Stand at the railing and look down. The ground drops away below the level of the street, into a sunken rectangle of pale, weathered stone. Scooters buzz past behind you. Somewhere nearby, a vendor calls out the price of fish. And right in front of you, a handful of thick, pitted columns rise out of the earth. They have stood here, in one form or another, for more than two and a half thousand years.

This is the Temple of Apollo, on the island of Ortygia. Ortygia is the oldest part of Syracuse, a small island joined to the mainland by short bridges. When Greek settlers from Corinth arrived here in the eighth century BCE, this island was their first foothold. The temple came later, in the early sixth century BCE. That makes it one of the oldest stone temples built by the Greeks anywhere in Sicily.

The style is Doric. That is the plainest of the classical Greek orders, with sturdy columns, no decorative base, and simple cushion-shaped tops. But look closely, and you will see that this is Doric in an early, experimental form. The columns are very thick. They stand unusually close together. The builders were still learning how much weight stone could carry, and so they played it safe. The result feels heavy and solid, almost muscular.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. Many of these columns were carved from a single block of stone, rather than stacked in drums. Think about that for a moment. Quarrying a block this size, moving it across the island, and raising it upright — all without modern machinery. For the people of early Syracuse, this temple was a statement. It told every passing ship that this young colony had wealth, ambition, and skill.

Now for the second surprise. Look at the top step on the eastern side, the side facing the morning sun. Cut into the stone is an inscription in early Greek letters. It names a man called Kleomenes and dedicates the work to Apollo. It also seems to boast about the columns themselves. Scholars still debate exactly what it says. Was Kleomenes the architect, or the person who paid for the temple? Nobody is fully certain. But it is one of the earliest signatures of its kind on a Greek temple.

There is another open question. We call this the Temple of Apollo because of that inscription. But the Roman orator Cicero, writing about Syracuse, mentioned a temple of Diana on Ortygia. Diana is the Roman name for Artemis, Apollo's twin sister. Some have wondered whether the two siblings shared this sanctuary, or whether Cicero meant a different building. The stones themselves do not settle the argument.

What happened next is almost more remarkable than the building itself. This temple did not simply fall into ruin. It kept being used. Over the centuries it was turned into a Christian church in the Byzantine period. When Arab rulers took Sicily, it served as a mosque. After the Normans arrived, it became a church again. Each new owner reshaped it for their own faith.

Later still, the old temple disappeared almost completely from view. Its walls and columns were absorbed into newer structures. In the Spanish period, parts of it became military barracks. Houses pressed up against it and over it. For a long time, people walking down this street had no idea that a Greek temple was hidden inside the buildings around them. It was only in modern times that archaeologists cleared away the later layers and brought it back into the light.

That history explains what you see today. Look at the eastern end, where a few columns still stand to their full height, carrying fragments of the stone beam above them. Then look along the sides, where only stumps remain. You can also trace a low section of the inner wall. That wall once enclosed the cella — the inner chamber where the statue of the god would have stood. Every gap tells you something was reused, removed, or rebuilt.

Notice, too, the colour and texture of the stone. It is a local limestone, warm and golden in the sun but grey and rough up close. Centuries of salt air from the sea have eaten into the surface. Some column shafts look almost like sponge. Originally, the Greeks would have covered this stone with smooth plaster and bright paint. What you see now is the bare skeleton, not the finished face.

Now lift your eyes and take in the setting. The temple sits in a small square just inside the island, close to where the bridges arrive from the mainland. For many visitors, it is the very first thing they see in Ortygia. Around it, the city carries on as normal. Cafés open their shutters, delivery vans squeeze through, and a short walk away the morning market fills the narrow streets with lemons, olives, and fresh fish.

That mixture is the real character of this place. Syracuse does not keep its ancient past behind glass. It lets the past sit in the middle of daily life. Children walk past these columns on their way to school. Locals cross the square without looking twice. In a way, that is how this temple has always lived — as part of the city, used and reused, never quite set apart.

If you continue deeper into Ortygia, you will find another layer of this story. In the cathedral square, the columns of a later Greek temple, dedicated to Athena, are built directly into the walls of the cathedral. Seeing both buildings on the same walk shows you how Syracuse treated its temples. It did not destroy them. It moved in.

Before you go, take one more look down into the sunken ground. Find the eastern step, where the old letters are cut. Watch how the light falls across the uneven columns. Then turn, and walk into the streets of Ortygia — knowing that the oldest stones on this island are the first ones you passed.
