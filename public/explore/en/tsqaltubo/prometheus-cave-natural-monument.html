The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the air. It changes the moment you pass inside. The warm, green smell of the Imeretian countryside falls away, and a cool, damp stillness takes its place. Water drips somewhere ahead of you, slow and steady. The path underfoot is smooth and slightly wet. Your eyes take a few seconds to adjust. Then the first chamber opens up around you, and the rock begins to take shapes you have never seen before.

You are standing under the hills near the village of Kumistavi, a short drive from the spa town of Tsqaltubo, in western Georgia. This part of the country sits on thick layers of limestone. Over very long stretches of time, rainwater seeped into that stone and slowly dissolved it. Geologists call this kind of landscape karst. It is full of sinkholes, springs and hollow spaces. The cave you are walking through is one of the largest of those spaces, and one of the most decorated.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. For most of human history, nobody knew this cave was here. Local people knew the hills had holes and springs. But the great halls inside stayed sealed in darkness. Georgian speleologists, the scientists who explore and map caves, explored the system in the 1980s. What they found was a chain of chambers, one opening into the next, with rivers and lakes running through them.

Then there is the name. In Greek mythology, Prometheus stole fire from the gods and gave it to humankind. As punishment, he was chained to a mountain in the Caucasus, the great range that runs across the north of Georgia. Georgia has its own version of the story, told about a hero called Amirani, who was also bound to a mountain for defying higher powers. Naming the cave after Prometheus links this underground world to one of the oldest legends of the region.

Now, look closely at the formations around you. The pointed shapes that hang from the ceiling are stalactites. The ones that rise from the floor are stalagmites. Each one grows from water carrying dissolved limestone. When a drop of water lands, it leaves behind a very thin film of mineral. One drop at a time, over thousands of years, those films build up into columns. Some of the shapes in front of you started forming long before any human city existed.

Sometimes a stalactite and a stalagmite grow toward each other until they meet. The result is a single column that runs from floor to ceiling. Look for these as you walk. You will also see thin, folded sheets of stone that hang like curtains. These form when water runs along a sloping ceiling and leaves a trail of mineral behind it. When the light passes through them from behind, some of these curtains look almost see-through.

The colours you see come from two sources. Some come from the rock itself. Pure calcite, the main mineral here, is pale and creamy. Traces of iron and other minerals tint it orange, brown or rust red. Other colours come from the lighting. The walkway uses coloured lamps to pick out the formations, and the effect changes from one hall to the next. Try to notice which colours belong to the stone, and which ones belong to the lamps.

The cave is not a dry, dead space. It is still active. Listen as you walk, and you will hear water almost everywhere. Underground streams run through parts of the system, and in places they gather into still, dark pools. The surface of these pools can be so calm that it looks like a mirror. The formations above reflect in the water, and it becomes hard to tell where the rock ends and the reflection begins.

That moisture is also the reason for a simple rule — please do not touch the formations. The oils and salts on human skin stop the mineral from building up. A single touch can leave a dark mark that lasts for generations. It may also stop that spot from growing ever again. The guides here care a great deal about this. So keep your hands on the railing, and let your eyes do the exploring.

The temperature down here stays cool and steady all year. In the heat of a Georgian summer, the change can feel like walking into a cellar. In winter, the cave can feel almost mild compared with the cold outside. Most visitors are glad to bring a light jacket. The floors are often wet, so shoes with a good grip make the walk much more comfortable, especially on the stairs.

The cave was developed for visitors in the early 2010s. Engineers built a walking route with steps, bridges and railings through a series of the main halls. Only part of the full cave system is open to the public. The rest remains in darkness, used for study, or simply left alone. It is worth remembering that the halls you see are only a section of a much larger world beneath these hills.

This cave also belongs to a wider story about Tsqaltubo. During the Soviet period, the town was a famous spa resort. People came from across the Soviet Union to bathe in its mineral springs. Many of the large sanatorium buildings now stand empty, slowly covered by trees and vines. The same water that made the town famous also shaped this cave. Rain soaks into the limestone, runs underground, and rises again as springs.

As you come toward the end of the route, take one last look back. The path behind you curves away into the soft light. The drip of water continues, as it has for thousands of years, and as it will long after you leave. When you step back outside, notice the warm air and the brightness of the day. Notice the smell of grass and earth again. You have just walked through the inside of these hills.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a light jacket because the cave stays cool year-round.
Wear shoes with good grip for the wet steps.
Keep your hands on railings and never touch the formations.
Combine your visit with Tsqaltubo's old Soviet spa district.
Book ahead if you want to join the underground boat ride.
