The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the moat for a moment. The water is still and dark, and the castle walls rise straight out of it, pale against the sky. On a calm day you can see the whole building twice, once in stone and once upside down in the water. Ducks cut lines across the reflection. Behind you, the old town of Celle waits with its timbered houses. In front of you is a building that was home to dukes for more than three hundred years.

This is Celle Castle, in the north German state of Lower Saxony. Its story begins long before the elegant facade you see today. The first fortification here was a defensive site near the River Aller, built to watch over a river crossing and the trade that moved along it. Rivers were highways in medieval Germany. Whoever held the crossing held the toll, the traffic, and a good share of local power.

The castle became truly important in the late fourteenth century. In 1378, the Dukes of Brunswick-Lüneburg moved their main residence here. For a ducal family, a residence was more than a house. It was the seat of government, the stage for diplomacy, and the place where the court lived, ate, prayed, and was entertained. The small settlement around the castle grew into a residence town. Much of the Celle you walk through today exists because the court was here.

Look up at the gables and the corner towers. The castle did not stay a medieval fortress. In the sixteenth century, the dukes rebuilt it in the Renaissance style. Renaissance builders in this region loved decorative gables, rhythm, and symmetry — a fortress turned into a palace. Later, in the second half of the seventeenth century, another wave of rebuilding added Baroque touches. Baroque is the more theatrical style of that era, full of display and ceremony. So the building in front of you is layered. Each generation kept part of the old and added something new.

Here is the first surprise. Inside this castle is a working theatre, and it is very old. The castle theatre dates from the late seventeenth century, and it is considered one of the oldest Baroque theatres in Germany that still stages performances. Think about that for a moment. Audiences sat in this space when the dukes still ruled. Today, people still buy seats, the lights still go down, and actors still step onto the stage. Few court theatres anywhere in Europe have kept this kind of continuity.

The man behind much of that Baroque splendour was Duke George William. He brought a taste for French and Italian court culture to Celle. His wife, Eleonore d'Olbreuse, came from a French Protestant family, a Huguenot family. Huguenots were French Protestants, and many of them left France because of religious persecution. Through Eleonore, French manners, French language, and French refugees came to this small German court. Celle became a place where several cultures met.

The couple's daughter has one of the saddest stories connected to this castle. Sophia Dorothea of Celle was born here in the 1660s. She married her cousin, the future King George the First of Great Britain. The marriage was unhappy. After a scandal involving a Swedish count, the marriage was dissolved, and she was confined for the rest of her life at Ahlden, another house in the region. She never saw her children again. The girl who grew up in these rooms became a prisoner far from court.

When George William died in 1705, the Celle line came to an end. The territory passed to the Hanover branch of the family, and the court moved away. Celle Castle was no longer the centre of power. It became a secondary residence, used from time to time, but quieter. In a way, this quiet helped the building. Without constant rebuilding by ambitious new rulers, many older rooms survived.

The second surprise involves a queen. In the 1770s, Caroline Matilda came to live here. She was a British princess, a sister of King George the Third, and she had been Queen of Denmark. Her affair with the royal physician, Johann Friedrich Struensee, who had gained great political power at the Danish court, ended in his execution and her exile. She was sent to Celle. She died here in 1775, still a young woman. Visitors who know the story often look at these walls a little differently.

If you go inside, try to see the castle chapel. It is one of the most remarkable rooms in the building. The chapel kept its Renaissance interior, with richly painted surfaces and dense decoration. It served a Protestant court, and its images were meant to teach as well as to impress. The colours are deep, and almost every surface carries some ornament. Give your eyes time here. Details appear slowly as you stand still.

Today, part of the castle houses a residence museum. It tells the story of the dukes, their court, and the long connection between this region and the British crown. That connection is easy to forget. For more than a century, the rulers of Hanover were also kings of Great Britain. Threads from this castle run all the way to London.

When you step back outside, walk around the building if you have time. The park and the moat give you different angles on the towers and gables. From one side the castle looks severe and defensive. From another it looks almost playful. Then turn toward the old town. The streets of timbered houses, with their carved beams and painted inscriptions, grew up in the shadow of this court.

Before you leave, stop once more by the water. Look at the reflection, and then at the real walls above it. Fortress, palace, theatre, refuge, museum — this one building has played many roles. Listen for the ducks, the wind in the trees, the footsteps on the paths. The court is long gone, but the castle still stands where the dukes chose to build it, watching over the town that grew around it.
