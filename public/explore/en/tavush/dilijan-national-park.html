The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through oak and beech leaves above you. Somewhere below, water runs over stones. The air here is cool and damp, even on a summer afternoon, and it smells of moss, wet bark and fallen leaves. Under your feet, the path is soft, layered with seasons of leaf litter. This is Dilijan National Park, in Armenia's northeastern Tavush Province. For many visitors, it changes what they expect Armenia to look like.

Most people picture Armenia as dry highland. They imagine bare volcanic slopes, apricot orchards and pale stone under a hard sun. Tavush is different. The mountains here catch moisture, and the slopes are covered in thick broadleaf forest. Oak, beech and hornbeam grow close together, and their canopy shades the ground. In Soviet times, people gave this area a nickname, Armenian Switzerland. You will still hear locals use it, partly with pride and partly with a smile.

The protected status of these forests is older than the park. A state nature reserve existed here during the Soviet period. Then, in 2002, independent Armenia reorganised the area as a national park. The difference matters. A strict reserve is mostly closed to people. A national park tries to balance protection with visitors, villages and trails. So the forest you walk through today is a working compromise between wilderness and the communities that live around it.

At the heart of the park sits the town of Dilijan itself. The Aghstev River runs through the valley, and the town follows its banks. Look at the older houses near the centre. Many have wooden balconies and carved details, which is unusual in a country famous for stone. In the Soviet era, Dilijan was a place of rest. Writers, artists and composers came here for the clean air and the quiet. A composers' retreat in the hills became part of the town's identity.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The forest is not only scenery. It is also a mountain medicine cabinet. The region has mineral springs, and Dilijan mineral water is a familiar label on Armenian tables. Locals collect wild herbs, berries and mushrooms from the edges of the woods in their seasons. If you meet someone selling small jars of honey, dried thyme or forest fruit preserves by the roadside, you are seeing a very old relationship with this landscape.

Now think about what lives in these woods, mostly out of sight. Brown bears move through the deeper forest. Wild boar dig in the soil, and roe deer feed at the edges of clearings. Lynx and wolves are present too, though almost no one sees them. Birds of prey circle above the open ridges. You are unlikely to meet the large animals on a busy trail. But turned earth, scratched bark and tracks in the mud tell you they passed this way.

The park also holds something you do not expect in a forest — medieval monasteries. Armenia became one of the first Christian nations in the early fourth century, and monks often chose remote, wooded places to build. The most visited is Haghartsin Monastery. Its churches and buildings date mainly from the tenth to the thirteenth centuries. The grey stone sits in a forested gorge, and the trees press close to the walls. The setting feels quiet and enclosed, almost hidden.

A short drive in another direction brings you to Goshavank. The monastery carries the name of Mkhitar Gosh, a scholar and priest of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. He wrote one of the earliest Armenian law codes, a collection of legal and moral rules for society. Goshavank was a centre of learning in its time. Look closely at its carved stone crosses, called khachkars. Khachkars are a uniquely Armenian art form, and some here are carved with lace-like detail.

Smaller ruins are scattered through the forest too. Jukhtak Vank and Matosavank are two of them. They are older stone chapels, partly covered by moss and roots. Reaching them takes a short walk on a forest path. These sites feel very different from the restored monasteries. There are fewer visitors, the roofs are broken in places, and the forest is slowly taking back the stone. Standing there, you can see how nature and history share the same ground.

For many families, the easiest destination is Lake Parz. The name means clear lake in Armenian. It is a small lake surrounded by forest, and on calm days the trees reflect on its surface. People come here for picnics, short walks and boating. A marked trail also links the lake with Gosh village and its monastery. It is a good choice if you want forest, water and history in a single walk.

Longer walkers will find more. The Transcaucasian Trail, a long-distance hiking route across the South Caucasus, passes through this region. Sections near Dilijan follow ridges, forest tracks and village paths. On the high ground, the forest opens, and you can see waves of green mountains in every direction. In autumn, the slopes turn copper, gold and red. Many locals say this is the most beautiful season in Tavush.

The weather here changes quickly. Clouds form over the ridges, and fog can fill the valleys in an hour. A clear morning can become a rainy afternoon. This moisture is the reason the forest exists, so the rain is part of the experience. Trails can be muddy and roots get slippery. Locals often joke that Dilijan has weather, while the rest of Armenia has only sunshine.

Before you move on, stop for a moment. Look at the light coming through the leaves. Notice how many shades of green are around you. Listen again for the river, the birds, the wind in the canopy. Monks walked these slopes eight hundred years ago, and bears still cross them today. As you walk away, keep your eyes on the edges of the path. The forest often shows its small details only to people who slow down.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a rain layer, since fog and showers arrive quickly.
Wear shoes with good grip for muddy, root-covered trails.
Walk the Lake Parz to Gosh trail for forest plus monastery.
Visit in autumn for copper and gold forest colours.
Arrive early at Haghartsin to enjoy the quiet gorge.
