The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The air here is cool, even in summer. It carries the smell of damp leaves, wood smoke, and moss on old stone. Somewhere below, a stream runs through the valley. Above you, beech and oak trees close in on every side. The path softens underfoot, and then the forest opens. Grey-green stone walls appear among the trunks, with conical domes rising over the treetops. This is Haghartsin Monastery, in the wooded hills of Tavush Province, in north-eastern Armenia.

The monastery sits a short drive from the spa town of Dilijan, inside a protected landscape of mountain forest. That setting matters. Many medieval Armenian monasteries stand on bare cliffs, open to the wind and the sky. Haghartsin is different. It hides. The monks who chose this valley wanted water, timber, shelter from passing armies, and silence. Today the trees still give the place a closed, private feeling. Sound does not carry far here. Voices drop without anyone asking them to.

Start with the name, because it comes with a story. Haghartsin is often translated as something close to the game of the eagle, or the eagle's play. Legend has it that when one of the churches was consecrated, an eagle circled over the dome again and again, as if it were taking part in the ceremony. Locals still tell this tale, and guides like to repeat it. It is folklore, not documented history. But look up at the domes for a moment, and you can understand why the image stayed.

What we can say with more confidence is that this complex grew over several centuries, roughly from the tenth to the thirteenth century. That was a turbulent period for Armenia. Kingdoms rose and fell. Raiders crossed the region more than once. Monasteries like this one were not only places of prayer. They were also schools, libraries, and centres of land and power. Princely families paid for new churches here, and their gifts were often recorded in inscriptions cut into the stone.

The complex is not one building. It is a small cluster of churches, halls, and chapels, set close together on uneven ground. The oldest church is usually identified as the Church of Saint Gregory, named for Gregory the Illuminator, the saint who brought Christianity to Armenia in the early fourth century. Armenia is often described as the first kingdom to adopt Christianity as its state religion. Walk around Saint Gregory slowly. Its walls are plain and solid, with a weight that matches its age.

Next to it stands a large hall built in front of the church entrance. In Armenian architecture this kind of hall is called a gavit, a covered space where people gathered, where monks taught, and where important people were sometimes buried. Inside, the light is low. Notice how the ceiling is held up by heavy arches that cross each other. Builders across medieval Armenia used this method to cover wide spaces with stone, without timber beams that could rot or burn.

The largest church in the complex is dedicated to the Holy Mother of God, known in Armenian as Surb Astvatsatsin. It is the tallest building here, and the one most visitors photograph first. Look at its outer walls. Shallow arches and narrow decorative lines run across the stone, giving the flat surface a sense of rhythm. Look for carved figures high on the walls as well. Some of these show donors, the patrons who paid for the work, holding a model of the church in their hands.

Now for a detail most visitors walk straight past. One of the most remarkable buildings here is not a church at all. It is the refectory, the dining hall where the monks ate together. It dates from the thirteenth century and is one of the finest medieval refectories in Armenia. Step inside. The long room is divided into two halves, with stone arches crossing overhead. Imagine long tables, bread, and the sound of one monk reading aloud while the others ate in silence.

Around the churches, look carefully at the walls and the ground. You will find khachkars, which are carved stone crosses unique to Armenian tradition. A khachkar is not a simple cross. Each one is a flat slab covered in lacy patterns, rosettes, and twisting vines, with the cross growing out of them like a living tree. No two are exactly alike. Some were made to remember the dead. Others marked a gift, a vow, or the building of a church.

Here is a second surprise. The monastery you see today looks clean and complete, but for much of the twentieth century it was in poor condition. In the early 2000s, a major restoration project was funded by the ruler of Sharjah, in the United Arab Emirates. The work was finished around 2011. It is an unusual story: a Christian monastery in the Armenian forest, restored with support from a Gulf Arab leader. Look closely at the stone, and you can sometimes see where new blocks sit beside the old.

The restoration also brought practical changes. The access road was improved, and the area around the monastery became easier to visit. Some people miss the quiet of earlier years. Others are simply glad the buildings are safe. Either way, Haghartsin is a working church again. You may hear a service, smell burning candles, or see families lighting thin beeswax tapers in a tray of sand. If you enter during prayer, stand at the back and keep your voice low.

Take a moment before you leave to walk the edges of the site. Behind the churches, the forest climbs steeply. Old trees stand close to the walls. Moss grows in the joints between stones, and lichen spreads in pale circles across the carvings. In autumn, the slopes turn copper and gold. In spring, the green is almost bright enough to hurt your eyes. In any season, the place feels like it belongs to the valley, rather than sitting on top of it.

So look once more at the domes, rising above the leaves. Listen to the stream, still running somewhere below. Stone, water, and trees... the same three things that brought the first monks here. As you walk back along the path, notice how quickly the walls disappear behind the trunks. A few steps, and the monastery is hidden again. The forest closes, and the valley keeps its quiet.

What to look for

Donor figures holding church models on Surb Astvatsatsin's walls.
Crossing stone arches supporting the gavit ceiling.
Two-part vaulted hall inside the monks' refectory.
Lacy carved khachkars set into walls and ground.
Newer restored blocks sitting beside weathered original stone.
Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with nearby Dilijan and its old town.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone paths and slopes.
Bring a light layer; the forest stays cool and damp.
Stand at the back quietly if a service is underway.
Visit in autumn for copper and gold forest colours.
