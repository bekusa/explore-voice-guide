The guide

The story

Stand in the small square just beside the cathedral and listen for a moment. Behind you, the Piazza del Duomo is full of voices, pigeons and rolling suitcases. In front of you, the noise drops away. A pale, calm facade looks back at you, the colour of weak tea with milk. This is the Royal Palace of Milan, the Palazzo Reale. For centuries, the people who ruled this city lived and worked right here.

That closeness to the cathedral is the first thing worth noticing. In most European cities, the palace and the church keep a polite distance. Here they sit almost shoulder to shoulder. This spot has been the political heart of Milan since the Middle Ages, long before the white marble of the Duomo was finished. Civic power and religious power grew up side by side on the same patch of ground... and you can still feel that pairing as you stand between them.

The early story belongs to Milan's great ruling families. The Visconti, who dominated the city in the fourteenth century, held court on this site. After them came the Sforza, the dukes who turned Milan into one of the richest courts of Renaissance Italy. Over time, the old medieval complex was rebuilt, extended and patched together again and again. What you see today is not one single building from one single moment. It is the latest layer in a very long story.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the late fifteenth century, Leonardo da Vinci worked for the Sforza court. According to well-known accounts, the old palace on this site, often called the Corte Vecchia, gave him space for one of his most ambitious projects. That project was a giant bronze horse to honour the Sforza family. Leonardo made a full-size clay model. The bronze was never cast, because the metal was needed for war.

The calm, orderly face of the palace comes from a much later age. In the late eighteenth century, Milan was ruled by the Habsburgs of Austria. The Empress Maria Theresa sent her son, the Archduke Ferdinand, to govern the city. The architect Giuseppe Piermarini was asked to remake the palace for him. Piermarini worked in the Neoclassical style — a style that borrowed the clean lines and restrained decoration of ancient Greece and Rome.

Look at the facade again with that in mind. Notice how flat and measured it is. There are no wild curves and no crowded sculpture. The windows march along in neat rows. This was a deliberate choice. After the theatrical drama of the Baroque period, architects like Piermarini wanted dignity, order and reason. The building was meant to say that the government inside was steady and enlightened.

Now for a second surprise. The palace once had its own theatre, the Teatro Regio Ducale. In 1770, a fourteen-year-old Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart premiered an opera there, called Mitridate, King of Pontus. A few years later, in 1776, that theatre burned down. The city needed a replacement, and Piermarini designed it too. That new theatre opened in 1778. Today the whole world knows it as La Scala, just a short walk north of here.

Power changed hands again at the end of the eighteenth century. Napoleon's armies arrived, and Milan became the capital of a new Kingdom of Italy under French control. Napoleon's stepson, Eugène de Beauharnais, governed from here as viceroy. Later, after Italy was unified in the nineteenth century, the palace became one of the royal residences of the House of Savoy, the family that ruled the new country. The name Royal Palace dates from these centuries of crowns and courts.

The twentieth century brought the hardest chapter. In August 1943, during the Second World War, Allied bombing raids hit Milan hard. The palace was badly damaged. Its grandest room, the Hall of the Caryatids, was gutted by fire. Caryatids are sculpted female figures that stand in place of columns, holding up the structure above them. This hall had been the setting for balls and great state occasions. After the fire, it was a blackened shell.

What happened next is the most moving part of the story. The hall was made safe, but it was never fully restored. The scorched walls and the damaged figures were left visible, as a record of the war. Then, in 1953, the city did something remarkable. It hosted a major exhibition of work by Pablo Picasso, and his painting Guernica was hung in this ruined hall. Guernica is Picasso's great protest against the bombing of a Basque town. Seeing it inside a bombed room gave it a powerful new meaning.

Today the palace is no longer a home for rulers. It is one of Milan's main venues for large art exhibitions, and the programme changes through the year. Shows here have covered everything from old masters to modern design. If you go inside, pay attention to the transitions between rooms. Some spaces are bright and freshly finished. Others still carry the marks of age and damage. That contrast is part of the visit.

Before you go, look around the little square itself, the Piazzetta Reale. To one side stands the cathedral, its marble spires rising against the sky. Close by is the Arengario, a pale stone building from the twentieth century that now holds the Museo del Novecento, Milan's museum of twentieth century art. From here you can see several eras of the city in one turn of the head — medieval roots, Neoclassical order, wartime scars and modern culture.

So take one last look at that quiet facade. Behind those even rows of windows, dukes once planned wars, a teenage Mozart heard his music sung, and a painting about destruction hung in a room that had been destroyed. Then let the sound of the main square pull you back in. As you walk away, notice how quickly the palace disappears behind the cathedral... as if it is happy to let the Duomo take the attention, and keep its own stories a little closer.
