The guide

The story

Look up. The white marble rises above the square in a forest of stone spires, each one tipped with a carved saint. On a bright day the surface seems to glow, pink and gold where the sun catches it. This is the Duomo, the cathedral of Milan, and it has stood at the heart of the city for more than six hundred years.

Work began in 1386. The archbishop and the ruling Visconti family wanted a cathedral grander than any other in the region. They chose the Gothic style, with pointed arches and tall windows, and they chose marble instead of the usual brick. The marble came from quarries at Candoglia, far to the north, floated down rivers and canals to the building site.

The cathedral took almost six centuries to finish. Generation after generation of stoneworkers lived and died while the walls slowly rose. Architects came from across Europe, argued about the design, and were replaced. The people of Milan grew so used to the endless work that they made a saying out of it. When something takes forever, they still say it is long as the building of the Duomo.

The front of the cathedral, the facade, was among the last parts completed. Much of it was finished in the early nineteenth century. Napoleon, who was crowned King of Italy here in 1805, pushed the work forward and helped pay for it. If you look closely at the facade, you can see how the older Gothic carving below meets the later work above.

Step inside and the light changes. Fifty-two great columns rise into the shadows, one for each week of the year. They are so tall that the ceiling seems to disappear. Stained glass windows, some of the largest in the world, throw colored light across the stone floor. The air is cool and still, even when the square outside is hot.

Set into the floor near the main door is a thin brass line, a meridian. Astronomers from the observatory at Brera laid it down in 1786. A small hole high on the wall lets a beam of sunlight fall, and at midday the beam touches the line, which is marked with the signs of the zodiac. For many years this strip helped the city keep the correct time.

High above the altar, a small red light marks a special relic. The people of Milan believe it is a nail from the cross of Christ. Once a year, in September, the archbishop is raised up in a basket to bring it down, a ceremony said to go back to the time of Saint Charles Borromeo in the sixteenth century.

The real surprise of the Duomo is the roof. You can climb it. Stairs and an elevator carry you up onto wide marble terraces, and there you walk among the spires you saw from below. Up close, the carving is astonishing. There are more than three thousand statues on this building, and many were never meant to be seen from the ground.

At the very top stands the Madonnina, the little Madonna. The golden statue was placed there in 1774. For a long time, by tradition, no building in Milan was allowed to rise higher than her. When taller towers were finally built, the city placed a small copy of the Madonnina on each one, so that she would always remain the highest point.

From the terraces, the view opens across the rooftops of Milan. On a clear day you can see the Alps to the north, a line of white peaks above the city haze. Pigeons wheel below you. The sounds of the square drift up, softened by distance.

Beneath the cathedral lies something older. Excavations have uncovered the foundations of an early Christian baptistery and church from the fourth century. By tradition, it was here that Saint Ambrose, the patron of Milan, baptized Saint Augustine in the year 387. You can walk down and stand among these ancient stones.

The Duomo is still a working cathedral, not only a monument. Mass is said here every day. People come to pray in the side chapels while visitors move quietly past. The building belongs to the city in a deep way. It survived war, including bombing in 1943, and it was carefully repaired each time.

Stand once more in the square before you go. Watch how the light moves across the marble as clouds pass. Six centuries of hands shaped this stone, most of them names we will never know. They built something they would never see completed, and trusted others to carry it on. That patience is still here, carved into every spire.

Practical tips

Visit the fourth-century ruins beneath the cathedral floor.
