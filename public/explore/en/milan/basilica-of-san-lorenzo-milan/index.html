The guide

The story

Listen first. A tram rattles over the rails behind you, and its bell rings once. Under your feet the paving stones are smooth and worn. In front of you stands a row of tall stone columns, grey and pitted, with the grooves running up their shafts. Beyond them rises a rounded church with a broad dome. Few places in Milan let you stand this close to the Roman city that lies under the modern one.

You are at the Basilica of San Lorenzo Maggiore, one of the oldest churches in Milan. Its origins go back to the late Roman Empire, around the end of the fourth century. Many layers have been added since then. The basic shape of the building, though, still follows that early plan. Before we go inside, let us start with the columns. Most people walk past them on their way to the church door.

There are sixteen of them, and they carry Corinthian capitals. That is the classical style with carved acanthus leaves curling at the top. The columns are older than the church itself. Historians believe they came from an earlier Roman building, perhaps a temple or public baths, and were moved here when the basilica was built. Reusing stone this way was common in late antiquity. It was cheaper than cutting new columns, and old pieces carried prestige.

Look closely at the stone. The surfaces are uneven, chipped in places and darkened by centuries of weather. The columns do not match perfectly. Some details differ from one to the next, and this supports the idea that they were gathered and reassembled. Above them runs a section of stone beam. Picture the whole row as the front of a grand Roman portico, a covered walkway that once led toward the church.

Why build such a grand church here at all? For about a century, from the late third century into the early fifth, Milan was one of the capitals of the Western Roman Empire. Emperors lived here and held court here. In 313 the Edict of Milan granted tolerance to Christians across the empire. A city with that kind of power needed buildings to match, and San Lorenzo was one of them.

That history explains the bronze figure standing in the square. It shows the Emperor Constantine, who issued the edict together with his co-emperor. The statue is a modern copy of an ancient Roman original, and it was placed here in the twentieth century. Constantine stands with one arm raised. Behind him, the columns and the dome make a neat summary of Milan's Roman and Christian past in a single view.

Now turn to the church itself. Notice that it is not a long rectangle like most Italian churches. It has a central plan, built around a square core with rounded sides. A dome crowns the middle, and four towers stand at the corners. This layout was unusual and ambitious for its time. Scholars still debate who commissioned it. One theory links it to the imperial court, but the records are not certain.

The dome you see today is not the original. The building suffered fires and collapses over the centuries. In the late sixteenth century the old dome fell in, and a new one was built in the style of that period. So the outside is a mix of ages. Roman foundations sit under medieval walls, and a Renaissance dome sits on top. When you step back, try to read the building like that, in layers.

Inside, the space opens upward. The light is soft and falls from high windows into the central area. Walk slowly around the edge. The rounded walls hold galleries above, and the sound of footsteps carries in the large space. Even on a busy day, voices drop to a murmur here. Take a moment beneath the dome before you look for the side chapels.

The most precious part of the basilica is the Chapel of Sant'Aquilino, attached to one side. It is octagonal, and some historians think it began as an imperial mausoleum. Here is the surprise. Inside are mosaics from the late Roman era, among the oldest surviving in Milan. One shows Christ seated among the apostles, against a glowing golden background. The chapel usually needs a separate ticket, and it is worth it.

Ask to see what lies beneath the chapel, if access is open. Below the floor, you can see huge blocks of reused stone in the foundations. Many historians believe they came from Milan's Roman amphitheatre, which stood nearby. That arena was taken apart in late antiquity, and its stone was carried here. So you can stand above the remains of a Roman entertainment venue under a Christian chapel. It is a quiet, strange experience.

Step back outside and notice the life of the square. This part of the city sits on Corso di Porta Ticinese, a street full of small shops, cafés and bars. It leads south toward the Navigli canals. In the evenings, especially in warm weather, young Milanese gather between the columns with drinks in their hands. The ancient stones become a meeting place. Locals simply call the spot the columns.

Behind the basilica there is a green park. It connects San Lorenzo with another old church, Sant'Eustorgio, farther south. Walking through it, you get a clear view of the apse and the towers from the back. That view is less crowded and calmer. Before you leave, look once more at the columns and the dome above them. Then listen again for the tram bell, as the modern city carries on beside its Roman past.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Sant'Aquilino chapel, which usually needs a separate ticket.
Come back in the evening to see locals gathering at the columns.
Walk through the park behind for a calmer view of the apse.
Dress modestly, since the basilica is an active place of worship.
Combine the visit with a walk south to the Navigli canals.
