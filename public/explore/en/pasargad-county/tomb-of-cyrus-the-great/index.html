The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days the wind is the loudest thing out here. It moves across the open plain, catches the dry grass, and passes around one pale stone building that stands almost alone. Under your feet the ground is hard and dusty. Ahead of you, block upon block of honey-coloured limestone rises in steps toward a small house with a pointed roof. This is the Tomb of Cyrus the Great, and it has stood on this plain for about two and a half thousand years.

The plain is called Murghab, and the site around you is Pasargadae. It was the first royal capital of the Achaemenid Persian Empire. The Achaemenids were the dynasty that, at its height, ruled lands from the edges of India to Egypt and the Aegean Sea. Cyrus founded that empire in the sixth century before Christ. He died around 530 BCE, and ancient writers tell us he was laid to rest here, in the city he built himself.

Look at the shape of the monument. There is a wide stepped base made of six levels. Each step is tall, much taller than a human stair, so this was never meant to be climbed casually. On top sits a single chamber with a gabled roof, like a simple house. The whole structure is roughly eleven metres high. It is modest compared to the pyramids of Egypt — and that modesty is part of its power.

Here is the first surprise. That pointed roof is unusual for Persia. Many scholars think the design borrows ideas from Anatolia, the region that is now western Turkey. Cyrus had conquered the kingdom of Lydia there, with its famously wealthy capital at Sardis. Stone masons from conquered lands may have worked on this tomb. So the building may carry ideas from more than one corner of the empire Cyrus brought together.

Now look closely at the joints between the stones. No mortar holds them together. The masons cut each block to fit tightly against its neighbours. In the past, metal clamps joined many of the blocks. Over the centuries people dug out much of that metal to reuse it, and you can still see the small cavities where it once sat. Those scars are a quiet record of everyone who passed by later and took something useful away.

The chamber itself is small. It is only a few metres across, with a narrow, low doorway on the western side. Greek and Roman writers describe what once lay inside: a golden coffin, a couch, fine textiles, and weapons. Today the chamber is empty. Visitors cannot enter it or climb the steps, so you will view the tomb from the path around it. From here, try to imagine how dark and still that little room would have been.

The most famous visitor came about two centuries after Cyrus died. Alexander the Great conquered Persia and came to Pasargadae. According to the historian Arrian, who wrote centuries later using earlier accounts, Alexander found the tomb already robbed. He was angry, and he ordered one of his officers, a man named Aristobulus, to restore it. It is a striking picture: a foreign conqueror showing respect for the founder of the empire he had just destroyed.

Ancient writers also report an inscription on the tomb. In the versions told by Plutarch and Strabo, Cyrus asks the passer-by not to begrudge him the little earth that covers his body. It is a moving idea. But please hold it lightly. No such inscription survives on the stone today, and the exact wording comes only from those later Greek accounts. We cannot be sure what, if anything, was ever carved here.

Here is the second surprise. For many centuries, local people did not call this the tomb of Cyrus at all. In the medieval period it was known as the tomb of the mother of Solomon, the biblical king. That name probably helped protect it. In Islamic times a small mosque grew up around the monument, and a prayer niche was carved inside the chamber, facing toward Mecca. Columns taken from the old palaces were reused nearby.

The identification with Cyrus returned through the work of travellers and scholars, especially from the nineteenth century onward. They compared the building with the old Greek descriptions and saw that they matched. In 2004 Pasargadae was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list. Today, Iranians from many backgrounds come here to stand in front of a figure they often describe as the father of their nation.

Cyrus is also remembered for the way he ruled. A clay object known as the Cyrus Cylinder, now held in the British Museum in London, records his conquest of Babylon. It describes the return of displaced peoples and the restoration of their temples. Some modern voices call it an early charter of human rights. Historians are more careful. They see it as royal propaganda in an old Mesopotamian style — but a remarkable one all the same.

When you have spent time at the tomb, look out across the plain. The remains of Pasargadae are spread over a wide area. There are palace foundations, tall stone column bases, and a gatehouse where a carved figure with four wings still stands on a stone slab. Further away, a great stone terrace sits on a hill. Walking between them helps you sense how large this royal city once was.

Before you leave, turn back one more time. Notice how the stone changes colour as the light moves, from pale cream to warm gold. Listen again to the wind, the same wind that crossed this plain when Cyrus was alive. The little house on its steps is still here, plain and patient. As you walk away, let your eyes rest on that pointed roof against the sky for one last moment.
