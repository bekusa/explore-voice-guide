The guide

The story

Listen first. Cicadas buzz in the trees around you, and the air feels thick and warm, almost wet against your skin. The grass under your feet is soft and very green. Ahead, grey stone rises out of the lawn in steps and shadows. This is El Tajín, in the hills of northern Veracruz state near the town of Papantla. It lies a few hours' drive north of the port city of Veracruz. For centuries it was one of the great cities of the Gulf Coast of Mexico.

The name itself is a good place to start. In the Totonac language, still spoken in villages around here, Tajín is linked to thunder and lightning. Many scholars connect it to a storm and rain deity. On a summer afternoon, as clouds pile up over these hills, the name makes immediate sense. The rain here arrives fast and heavy. It keeps the forest dense, and it soaks the stones you are about to walk among.

The city grew over a long period, but its peak came roughly between the ninth and twelfth centuries. That was a time of change across Mesoamerica — the cultural region that stretched from central Mexico into Central America. Great powers like Teotihuacan had already fallen. New regional centres rose to fill the gap, and El Tajín became one of the strongest. Archaeologists still debate exactly who built it. The Totonac people live here today, but the original builders may have been a related or earlier population.

Now look for the building that draws everyone first — the Pyramid of the Niches. It rises in six stepped levels. Each level is lined with deep, square recesses, framed in stone. In the sun, every niche holds a block of black shadow, so the whole face of the pyramid seems to flicker between light and dark. The niches are usually counted at 365. Many people link that number to the days of the solar year, though the exact meaning is still discussed.

Stand still for a moment and study the edges of those niches. Above each opening, a stone cornice leans outward, like the brim of a hat. This flying cornice is a signature of El Tajín. It throws an extra band of shade and makes the building look lighter than it really is. Archaeologists believe the pyramid was once plastered and painted in strong colours, possibly red and blue. Picture that colour against this green landscape, and the scene changes completely.

Here is something many visitors walk straight past. El Tajín has more ball courts than almost any other known site in Mesoamerica — more than a dozen of them. The ball game was played across ancient Mexico with a heavy rubber ball, struck with the hips. It was a sport, but it was also ritual. Here it seems to have sat at the very centre of city life. The long, narrow courts lie between parallel walls throughout the site.

The South Ball Court holds the most famous carvings. Its walls carry relief panels that show ceremonies connected to the game. Figures face each other in elaborate costumes. In one scene, a player appears to be sacrificed, while a skeletal figure watches from above. The style is full of curling, interlocking scrolls. Take your time here. The carvings are shallow, and the angle of the sun decides how much detail you can actually see.

That scroll design is everywhere once you notice it. You will also see the stepped fret, a zigzag pattern that climbs and turns like a staircase folding back on itself. It appears on walls, panels, and building faces across the city. Some people read it as a symbol of lightning or of moving water. Whatever its meaning, it gives the stone a sense of motion. Follow one line with your eyes, and it leads you around a corner.

Now the story of how the outside world found this place. In 1785, a Spanish official named Diego Ruiz came into these hills. He was not searching for ruins at all. According to the usual account, he was looking for hidden tobacco fields, grown in secret to avoid the royal monopoly. Instead he found a pyramid covered in forest. His report brought El Tajín to wider attention. Serious excavation came much later, in the twentieth century.

Walk uphill toward the area called Tajín Chico, which means Little Tajín. This raised section seems to have been for the elite. The buildings here are more private, set around terraces above the main plazas. Higher still stands the Building of the Columns. Its carved column drums show processions and figures, and many researchers link them to a powerful ruler known as Thirteen Rabbit. The view back down over the city from this level is worth the climb.

Before you leave, go back toward the entrance. There you may see a tall wooden pole and a small group of men in red and white. These are the Voladores, the flyers of Papantla. Four men tie ropes to their waists and drop backward from the top. They circle slowly toward the ground while a fifth man plays a flute and drum on the tiny platform above. UNESCO lists this ritual as intangible cultural heritage of humanity.

The ceremony is a prayer for rain and fertility, and it belongs to living Totonac tradition. It is not a reconstruction. The surrounding region also carries another living tradition — vanilla. The vanilla orchid is native to these forests, and Papantla has long been known for growing it. In town and near the site, you may smell it in the markets, sold as dark pods or sweet extract. El Tajín itself has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1992.

So stand once more in the main plaza. Feel the heat rise from the stone. Listen to the insects, and maybe to the flute from the pole near the gate. The niches on the pyramid are still filling with shadow, one by one, as the sun moves. People have watched that same play of light here for more than a thousand years. As you walk away, look back once — and count a few of those dark squares.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Bring a hat, water, and insect repellent.
Watch the Voladores ceremony near the site entrance.
Visit the site museum to see carvings up close.
Climb to Tajín Chico for a view over the plazas.
