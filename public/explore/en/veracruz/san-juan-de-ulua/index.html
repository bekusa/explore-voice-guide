The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the walls you can hear the low thrum of the port: cranes groaning, a ship's horn, the slap of grey-green water against stone. Then put your hand on the wall beside you. It feels rough and full of tiny holes, like a dried sponge. That texture is the first secret of this place. You are touching coral, cut from the reefs of the Gulf of Mexico and stacked into a fortress.

This is San Juan de Ulúa, the stone guardian of the harbour of Veracruz. Today it sits among container cranes and industrial docks. For most of its life, though, it stood alone on a low island of reef and sand, facing open water. For nearly three centuries, it was the sea gate between Spain and its richest colony. Almost everything that came to New Spain by sea, and much of what left, passed under these walls.

The story begins in 1518. A Spanish expedition led by Juan de Grijalva reached this stretch of coast and landed on the small island. The name San Juan honours the saint, and possibly Grijalva himself. The word Ulúa is less certain. One popular explanation says the Spaniards heard local people say the name of a place called Culúa, a word linked to the people of central Mexico. Historians still discuss it, so treat that as a likely story rather than settled fact.

A year later, Hernán Cortés arrived on this same coast with his ships. From here, his expedition moved inland toward the Aztec capital, Tenochtitlan. That march changed the history of two continents. It helps to remember this as you stand here. This small island was one of the first footholds of Spanish power on the American mainland, long before any stone fortress existed.

The fortress itself grew slowly, across generations. Work began in the sixteenth century and continued, in stages, into the eighteenth. Builders had a problem: there was no quarry on a reef. So they used the reef itself. Workers cut blocks of coral stone from the surrounding shallows and bound them with lime mortar. Many of those workers were enslaved Africans and Indigenous labourers, and their effort is in every block you see. Look closely and you can sometimes spot the fossil patterns of coral branches inside the stone.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past. Along one stretch of the old wall, you can find large iron rings set into the stone. Before proper docks existed, ships tied up directly to the fortress. The wall was a quay as well as a defence. The northerly winds here, called nortes, can blow with real force, and a ship without shelter could be driven onto the reef. These rings held the treasure fleets steady.

And what treasure it was. Silver from the mines of central Mexico travelled down to Veracruz on mule trains. Here it was loaded onto galleons bound for Spain. In the other direction came wine, cloth, olive oil, iron tools, and people. That flow of wealth made the harbour a target. Pirates and rival navies watched it closely, and the fortress grew thicker and stronger in answer.

One of the most famous fights came in 1568. An English fleet under John Hawkins, with a young Francis Drake aboard, sheltered here after storm damage. The Spanish fleet arrived, a truce was agreed, and then it broke down in a surprise attack. Most of the English ships were lost. Drake escaped. English writers later described the attack as betrayal, and Drake spent years raiding Spanish ships and ports afterwards. It is a strange thought: a battle at this quiet spot helped shape the career of one of England's best-known sailors.

Now the second surprise. When Mexico won its independence in 1821, the Spanish army did not leave this island. A Spanish garrison held San Juan de Ulúa for years after the rest of the country was free. From these walls, they could fire on the city of Veracruz across the water. Only in 1825, after a blockade by the young Mexican navy, did the last Spanish soldiers surrender. So this fortress was among the first places Spain claimed in Mexico, and the very last place it gave up.

The nineteenth century brought more cannon fire. In 1838, French warships bombarded the fortress during a dispute that Mexicans remember as the Pastry War. The odd name comes from the claim of a French pastry cook whose shop in Mexico had been damaged. In 1914, United States forces occupied Veracruz and its harbour. Each time, this old coral wall stood in the line of fire, facing the sea as it was built to do.

As you walk deeper inside, the mood changes. For long periods, San Juan de Ulúa served as a prison, and it gained a dark reputation. Some cells were small, damp, and very hot. Locals called certain cells tinajas, a word for large clay water jars, because they felt like being sealed inside one. Benito Juárez, later one of Mexico's most respected presidents, was held here in the 1850s before being sent into exile. Folk stories also connect the prison to Chucho el Roto, a bandit who became a popular hero in Mexican legend, though details of his time here are hard to confirm.

Take a moment in one of these dark rooms. Notice how the air feels thick and warm, even when the sea breeze is blowing outside. Notice the salt stains and the dark streaks of moisture on the ceiling. Water slowly moves through porous coral stone. You can feel, without any record or text, why prisoners feared this place. The same stone that protected the harbour made life inside it very hard.

When you step back into the light, look toward the city. The cranes, the warehouses, and the long pier show that Veracruz is still a working port. The ships are larger now, but they follow routes that the galleons once sailed. As you leave, touch the coral wall once more. Feel the tiny holes under your fingers... reef turned into fortress, and fortress turned into memory. Then listen again to the harbour. It has not stopped working for five hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring water and a hat for the exposed open courtyards.
Come early to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Check the weather for strong northerly winds before visiting.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone floors and ramps.
Allow time to walk the outer walls for harbour views.
