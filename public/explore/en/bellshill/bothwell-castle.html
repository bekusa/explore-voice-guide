The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, through the trees, the River Clyde is moving over stone. The air down here smells of wet leaves and old sandstone. And then the castle appears: a great wall of red-brown stone, warm even on a grey day, rising from a green bank above the water. You are a short drive from Bellshill, on the edge of the village of Bothwell in South Lanarkshire. Factories, motorways and housing estates are close by. Yet the castle stands in its own pocket of woodland, above a deep bend in the river. That position is the first thing to understand about this place. The builders chose a high bank, with the Clyde guarding one side.

Bothwell Castle was begun in the late thirteenth century by the Moray family, also written Murray. They were powerful lords in Scotland at that time. They wanted more than a defended house. They planned a large stone castle of the newest kind, with a huge round tower at its heart. Historians often call it one of the finest thirteenth-century castles in Scotland. When you walk around it, the scale explains why.

That round tower is called the donjon. The word is French, and it means the great tower, the last place of defence. It is not a dungeon in the modern sense, although the English word comes from it. Look at it now. It is a broad drum of carefully cut stone, with its own moat cut into the rock on the inner side. Even inside the castle, the lord could close himself off from everyone else.

Here is the first surprise. The donjon is cut in half. Walk around to the side above the river, and you will see that part of the tower is simply gone. This was not caused by time or weather. People took it down on purpose. During the Wars of Independence, the long struggle between Scotland and England, the Scots sometimes broke their own castles. A ruined castle could not be held by the enemy.

Those wars arrived here very soon after the castle was built. King Edward the First of England invaded Scotland in the 1290s, and Bothwell became a prize worth fighting for. In 1301, Edward came in person with a large army. The castle changed hands more than once over these years, between Scottish and English forces. Each time, the stones you are looking at carried the weight of a new siege.

The second surprise comes from the siege of 1301. English records describe a great wooden siege tower, a kind of moving fortress on wheels. It was built in Glasgow and carried in sections to Bothwell. A road was prepared for it across the land. Soldiers then pushed it against the walls so men could climb across. The plan worked. After a siege of some weeks, the castle fell to Edward's army.

Later, after the Scottish victory in the wars, the castle was slighted again. That word means deliberately damaged so it could not be used. For some years Bothwell stood broken. Then, in the late fourteenth century, it passed by marriage to Archibald Douglas, known as Archibald the Grim. He married the Moray heiress, Joanna. The Douglas family were among the most powerful nobles in Scotland, and they rebuilt the castle in their own way.

As you walk through the courtyard, you can see two periods side by side. The older Moray work is around the donjon, with very fine, smooth stonework. The Douglas rebuilding is larger and plainer. Look toward the east side for the remains of the great hall. Tall window openings still frame the sky. Here the lord would have eaten with his household, and guests would have warmed themselves by a fire.

There is a quieter detail that many visitors miss. The castle you see today is smaller than the one the Morays seem to have planned. Their first design may have been even more ambitious, with a larger enclosure and a grand gatehouse. War interrupted the work, and the full plan appears never to have been finished. So these walls show two stories at once: what was built, and what was only imagined.

Now look down from the walls toward the river. On the far bank, among the trees, are the remains of Blantyre Priory, a small medieval religious house. A short distance away is Blantyre itself, where the explorer David Livingstone was born. This stretch of the Clyde valley held monks, lords, mill workers and miners over the centuries. The castle is only one layer of a long human landscape.

Today the castle is cared for by Historic Environment Scotland, the public body that looks after many of the country's monuments. Paths lead around the walls and through the woods. Families picnic on the grass. Dog walkers pass along the river path. The castle no longer guards anything. It has become a place for slow afternoons, for children climbing steps, and for people who simply want to stand somewhere old.

If you have time, walk the full circuit outside the walls. From the field side, the castle looks heavy and closed, as its builders intended. From the river side, the broken donjon opens like a cut stone fruit. Inside you can see the thickness of the walls and the shape of rooms that once stood one above another. Notice how the colour of the sandstone changes as the light moves.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the donjon. Put your hand on the stone if you can. It is cool, slightly rough, and marked by tools from more than seven hundred years ago. Behind you, the Clyde is still running over its stones, as it did when the siege tower rolled in. Take that sound with you as you walk back under the trees.

What to look for

The split donjon, cut open on the river side.
Rock-cut moat separating the donjon from the courtyard.
Tall window openings of the Douglas great hall.
Fine, smooth Moray stonework beside rougher later masonry.
Blantyre Priory ruins among trees across the Clyde.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the outer circuit to see both faces of the castle.
Wear sturdy shoes for muddy woodland paths after rain.
Take the riverside path for views toward Blantyre Priory.
Combine your visit with the nearby David Livingstone Centre.
Visit in late afternoon when the sandstone glows warmest.
