The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Pine needles shift underfoot, and a light wind moves through the tall trees. Somewhere beyond the forest, the Baltic Sea is only a short walk away. The air here carries resin and salt. It is a quiet place now, and that quiet is part of what you came to understand. This guide will walk with you slowly. There is no need to hurry, and there is no wrong way to feel as you move through these grounds.

You are standing at the site of Stutthof, the German name for the village of Sztutowo. The camp sits on the Vistula Spit, a strip of land between the Baltic Sea and a wide lagoon, east of Gdańsk. The Nazi German authorities chose this remote, marshy, forested place on purpose. It was hard to reach and hard to escape. The same trees that give the site its stillness today once hid what happened inside the wire.

Here is a fact many visitors do not know. Stutthof opened on 2 September 1939, the day after Germany invaded Poland. It was the first camp of its kind that Nazi Germany built outside its pre-war borders. The first prisoners were Poles from Gdańsk and the surrounding region of Pomerania. They were teachers, priests, officials, community leaders, and activists. The aim was to remove the people who might hold Polish society together.

In its early years, Stutthof was run as a civilian internment camp. In 1942, it became an official concentration camp within the wider German system. That change brought more prisoners and more brutality. Over the years, people arrived from across Europe. There were Poles, Jews, Soviet prisoners of war, Lithuanians, Latvians, Norwegians, Danes, and many others. Historians estimate that around 110,000 people passed through the camp, and more than sixty thousand of them died.

Now look ahead, toward the gate. Prisoners called it the Death Gate. It belongs to the Old Camp, the original section that still stands. Its plain, low shape is almost ordinary, and that is unsettling in itself. Thousands walked through this opening and never walked back. Take a moment here before you pass through. Many visitors stop at this spot without being told to.

Inside, you will see wooden barracks, watchtowers, and lines of fencing. The barracks housed far more people than they were built for. Prisoners slept in crowded tiers, with little heat during the long Baltic winters. Hunger, forced labour, beatings, and disease killed many of them. Some exhibitions inside the buildings show the objects prisoners left behind. These small, everyday items often stay with visitors longer than any number.

Beyond the Old Camp lies the area known as the New Camp. It was built later, as the number of prisoners grew. Most of its buildings are gone today. What remains are rows of foundations in the grass. Walk along them and you can sense the scale. The open ground makes the size of the camp easier to read than walls ever could. Here, absence tells you as much as any surviving structure.

In 1944, the camp changed again. Very large transports of Jewish prisoners arrived, many of them women. They came from ghettos and camps in the Baltic states, and from Auschwitz, including many Hungarian Jews. Conditions collapsed. That year, a gas chamber was used to murder prisoners, and the crematorium nearby burned the dead. Over the winter, a typhus epidemic spread through the overcrowded barracks. Thousands more died from it.

A second fact many visitors miss concerns the end of the war. In January 1945, as the Soviet army advanced, the SS began to evacuate the camp. Thousands of prisoners were forced to march west in deep snow and freezing cold. These became known as death marches. Many people died on the roads. Near the Baltic coast, at a place called Palmnicken, a large group of Jewish prisoners was murdered on the shore. The camp itself was among the very last to be liberated, in May 1945.

After the war, justice came slowly and only in part. In 1946, a trial in Gdańsk tried members of the camp staff, and several were executed. Decades later, the courts were still at work. In 2022, a German court convicted a woman who had worked as a secretary in the camp office as a teenager. She was in her late nineties at the time. The case showed that even clerical work kept this system running.

The museum here was created in 1962 to keep this memory in place. Near the crematorium and the gas chamber stands a large stone memorial, known as the Monument of Struggle and Martyrdom. It was unveiled in the late 1960s. Its heavy, rough forms are meant to feel weighty rather than beautiful. Look closely at the surfaces and the figures carved into them. Many visitors leave flowers or small candles at its base.

As you walk, notice how people move through this place. Voices drop. Groups of students from Poland, Germany, and elsewhere walk together with their teachers. Some people pray. Others simply stand still. The museum also works as an education centre, and survivors' testimonies are part of how the story is told. This is a place of study as much as a place of mourning, and both happen side by side.

When you are ready to leave, turn back for a last look. Notice the line of the fence against the pines, and the low roofs of the barracks. Notice the open field where the New Camp once stood. As you walk out, listen again to the wind in the trees, and to the distant sound of the road. The forest is still here. So are the names and the stories. Carry a few of them with you as you go.
