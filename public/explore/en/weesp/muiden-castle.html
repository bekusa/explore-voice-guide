The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against old brick. Somewhere a gull calls over the river, and a wooden bridge creaks under footsteps. In front of you stands a compact red-brick castle, square and solid, with round towers at its corners and a moat running close around its walls. The brick is warm in colour, a deep orange-red, and on a grey Dutch day it seems to hold the little light there is.

This is Muiden Castle, known in Dutch as the Muiderslot. It stands in the small fortified town of Muiden, a short trip from Weesp, at the point where the river Vecht meets the open water that leads toward the old Zuiderzee. That position explains almost everything about this building. Whoever held this river mouth could watch, and tax, the boats moving inland toward Utrecht and beyond.

The first castle here goes back to the late thirteenth century. It was built under Floris the Fifth, Count of Holland, a powerful and ambitious ruler. Floris wanted control of this trade route, and a stone stronghold at the river mouth gave it to him. Castles in this flat, wet landscape were rare and costly, so a building like this was a clear statement of power.

Now comes the darkest chapter in the castle's story. In 1296, a group of discontented nobles turned against Count Floris. They captured him and held him at this castle. When people tried to free him, the conspirators panicked, and the Count was killed nearby. His death shocked the County of Holland. Names linked to the plot, such as Gerard van Velzen and Gijsbrecht van Amstel, became part of Dutch history and literature for centuries afterwards.

The original castle did not survive those troubled years. It was torn down, and the walls you see today come mostly from a rebuilding in the late fourteenth century, under Albrecht of Bavaria, who was then Count of Holland. So the castle in front of you is not the place where Floris was held — it stands on that ground, and it follows the same idea, but it is a later building. Many visitors miss this point.

Look at the shape again. The plan is simple: a square of walls around a central courtyard, a tower at each corner, and water on every side. Builders in this region worked with brick rather than stone, because good building stone is scarce in the Low Countries. Clay was everywhere. The result is a castle that looks less like a fairy tale and more like a practical machine for defence.

The castle's second life began in the early seventeenth century, and it was a very different kind of life. In 1609, the poet and historian Pieter Corneliszoon Hooft became the bailiff of Muiden, which meant he was the local governor and judge. He lived here in the summers. Instead of soldiers, the rooms filled with conversation, music, and poetry. Hooft is still remembered as one of the great writers of the Dutch Golden Age, a time of trade, art, and learning in the Netherlands.

Hooft's guests became known as the Muiden Circle. Historians use this name for the writers, scholars, and musicians who visited him here. The circle is often linked with figures such as the poet Joost van den Vondel, the diplomat and poet Constantijn Huygens, and the gifted Maria Tesselschade Visscher, who was admired for her singing and her verse. How formal this circle really was is still debated. The picture of friends meeting by the river, however, has become part of Dutch cultural memory.

Here is a second surprise. In the early nineteenth century, the castle was in such poor condition that it was nearly sold off for demolition. The building had lost its purpose. Its brick was worth more as material than the castle was as a monument. Royal intervention helped to stop the sale. Later in that century, interest in national history grew, and the castle was restored and opened to the public as a museum.

The castle never fully lost its military role. Muiden later became part of the Defence Line of Amsterdam, a ring of forts, dikes, and floodable land built around the capital in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The plan was to flood the countryside to stop an enemy army. Today this defence line belongs to the Dutch Water Defence Lines, which are listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Inside, the rooms are furnished to suggest the time of Hooft. You may see dark wooden furniture, painted ceilings, tiled floors, and tables laid out as if guests might arrive soon. Elsewhere, the castle shows its medieval side, with displays of weapons and armour and the steep, narrow stairs of the towers. Climbing those stairs gives you a strong sense of how tight and dark life inside a defensive wall could be.

Do not skip the outdoor spaces. Around the castle there are gardens laid out in an older style, with neat beds, clipped hedges, and fruit trees. There is also a herb and vegetable garden that recalls how a household like Hooft's would have fed itself. From the walls and the edge of the moat, you can look across the harbour of Muiden, with its masts and moored boats, and out toward the water beyond.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the bridge. Look at the brick close up — you can see different shades, patches of repair, and lines where newer work meets older work. Seven centuries of use are written into these walls in that simple way. Then turn toward the harbour. Listen again to the water against the walls, the halyards tapping on the masts, the gulls overhead. It is the same river mouth that Floris the Fifth wanted to control, and the same light that Hooft's guests saw from these windows.

Practical tips

Combine your visit with a walk around Muiden's small harbour.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, narrow tower stairs.
Allow extra time for the gardens outside the walls.
Check the castle website for guided tours and seasonal events.
