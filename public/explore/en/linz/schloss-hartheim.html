The guide

The story

Gravel shifts under your feet. Around you the farmland of the Danube plain lies flat and quiet, and a light wind moves through the fields. Ahead stands a pale Renaissance castle with towers at its corners and a taller tower in the middle. From a distance it looks calm, almost ordinary. That ordinary look is the first thing to hold in mind here. This place is a memorial, and it asks for a slower, quieter kind of attention.

You are in the village of Alkoven, roughly twenty kilometres west of Linz, in Upper Austria. Schloss Hartheim dates to around 1600. Its builders shaped it in the Renaissance style, which looked back to the balance and symmetry of classical Rome. Four wings enclose a central courtyard. Arcades, rows of open arches, run along the inner walls on several levels. For centuries the castle passed between noble families. It was a country seat, a place of estates, harvests and rents.

The building's story changed at the end of the nineteenth century. The princely Starhemberg family gave the castle to a charitable association in Upper Austria. The castle became a home for people with mental and physical disabilities. Religious sisters and lay staff cared for the residents. For several decades the arcades and rooms were part of daily care... meals, routines, visitors from families in the surrounding villages. It is important to remember this chapter. The castle was first a place of shelter.

Then came 1938, and Austria's annexation into Nazi Germany. The Nazi state followed an ideology of so-called racial hygiene. It labelled sick and disabled people as lives without value. In 1939 the regime began a secret programme to murder them. After the war it became known as Aktion T4, named after the Berlin address of its headquarters, Tiergartenstrasse 4. Six killing centres were set up across the Reich. Hartheim was one of them.

In 1940 the residents were moved out, and the castle was rebuilt for killing. A gas chamber and a crematorium were installed on the ground floor. Grey buses arrived from psychiatric hospitals and care homes across Austria and southern Germany. Their windows were covered. People stepped down from the buses believing they had come for a medical examination or a bath. Most of them were dead within hours of their arrival.

Smoke from the crematorium chimney carried over the fields. People in Alkoven saw it, and they smelled it. Many villagers understood what was happening. The secret was never complete. Historians have collected accounts from local people who saw the buses pass and who noticed that no one ever left. This detail surprises many visitors. The killing did not happen in a remote wilderness. It happened beside farms, a road and a village church.

In 1941, after public protest from some church leaders, the regime officially stopped the T4 gassings. At Hartheim, the killing did not stop. Under a second programme, known by the code 14f13, the castle received prisoners from concentration camps. Many came from Mauthausen and its subcamp Gusen, not far from here. Others came from Dachau and elsewhere. They were selected because they were too sick or too weak to work.

In total, around 30,000 people were murdered at Hartheim between 1940 and 1944. They were children and old people, farmers and teachers, patients and prisoners. Each had a name and a family. Late in 1944, as the war turned against Germany, the staff took apart the gas chamber. They tried to remove the traces and restore the rooms to look harmless. The effort failed. Testimony and documents survived, and they later became evidence.

Here is a second fact that many visitors do not know. Several men trained at Hartheim later ran the death camps in occupied Poland. Franz Stangl, an Austrian police officer, worked in the castle's office. He later became commandant of Sobibor and then Treblinka. The methods and the staff of this quiet castle helped prepare the murder of Europe's Jews. Hartheim stands at an early point on that path.

After the war the castle held apartments for many years. People lived in rooms where others had been killed. Remembrance grew slowly, and families and initiatives pushed for it. In the early 2000s, archaeologists excavated the grounds. They found human ashes and personal objects, small belongings left behind by the victims. In 2003 the castle opened as a Place of Learning and Remembrance. A permanent exhibition called The Value of Life now occupies the upper floors.

The memorial sections on the ground floor are bare and quiet. You walk the route the victims walked, from the arrival area through the rooms where they were undressed and killed. There is very little decoration. Light falls on plaster walls and stone floors. In one room, names of the victims are displayed. Many visitors stop here for a long time. Take your time. There is no right speed for this walk.

Upstairs, the exhibition widens the question. It asks how societies have judged the worth of human lives, before the Nazi era and after it. It looks at disability, medicine and the idea of usefulness. The goal is not only to remember the past. It also asks you to think about the present, about care, dignity and who is included. School groups come here often, and the rooms are designed for reflection and discussion.

When you step back into the courtyard, look up at the arcades. They are the same arches that residents once walked under on ordinary days. Listen to the birds, the distant tractors, the wind across the plain. This calm was here then too. As you walk back along the gravel toward the fields, carry one name with you from the memorial room. That is how this place asks to be remembered.
