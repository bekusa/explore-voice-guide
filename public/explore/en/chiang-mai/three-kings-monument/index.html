The guide

The story

Stand in the open square and look up. Three figures rise above you, cast in dark bronze. They are kings, standing close together, their robes flowing in frozen metal. The air around their feet smells of incense and fresh flowers. People come and go quietly. Some pause, lower their heads, and leave small offerings on the stone below. This is one of the calm hearts of the old city.

These three figures are the founders of Chiang Mai. Their names are Phaya Mangrai, Pho Khun Ramkhamhaeng, and Phaya Ngam Mueang. Each ruled his own land in his own time. Mangrai ruled the northern kingdom of Lanna. Ramkhamhaeng was the king of Sukhothai, far to the south. Ngam Mueang ruled the smaller state of Phayao, which lay nearby. Together they shaped the early history of this region.

The old accounts tell of a deep friendship between them. The three kings trusted one another. They met, they spoke as equals, and they agreed to help build a new city together. According to the chronicles, Mangrai even sought the counsel of his two friends on where and how the city should be laid out. Alliances like this were rare among rulers, who more often fought one another for land and tribute. That shared trust is the reason all three stand here today, side by side, none raised above the others.

They chose the site with great care. The land lay beside the Ping River. Wide, fertile plains spread out in every direction. To the west rose the mountain of Doi Suthep, green and steep, a natural wall against attack. In 1296, the new city was founded on this ground. They named it Chiang Mai, which means new city in the northern Thai tongue.

The monument itself is far younger than the story it tells. It was designed and cast by a Thai national artist named Kaimook Chuto. The work was completed in 1983, and the official opening ceremony followed in early 1984. The figures were raised here to mark the founding of the city and to honor the men who began it. Over the years since, the bronze has darkened, polished in places by the touch of countless hands and the smoke of daily offerings.

Behind the three figures stands a low and graceful building. It was once the provincial hall, the seat of local government in this part of the country. Today it holds the Chiang Mai City Arts and Cultural Centre. Inside, calm exhibits trace the long story of the city, the Lanna kingdom, and the people who have lived on this plain.

Watch the people who gather here. Many are not travelers but local residents. They kneel on the stone. They place marigolds and lotus buds at the kings' feet. They light slim sticks of incense and press their palms together in respect. For them, these three rulers are more than figures from a book. They are seen as guardians who still watch over the city.

The square is also a place of gathering and celebration. Festivals fill it through the year. During Songkran, the Thai new year in April, water and laughter spill across the open ground. On ordinary days it is quieter. People cross it on their way elsewhere, or sit in the shade at its edges to rest from the warm afternoon.

Look closely at how the three kings are arranged. Not one of them stands higher than the others. The sculptor placed them as partners, never as rivals. The meaning is simple and clear. This city was born from cooperation, from three leaders who chose to share a vision rather than to compete for power and glory.

The monument sits near the very center of the old walled town. From this point, straight roads lead outward to the ancient gates and the worn brick remains of the city wall. Temples stand within a short walk in almost every direction, their golden roofs catching the light above the trees and rooftops of the quarter.

Try to come in the early morning, or near sunset. The light is soft and golden then. The heat of the day eases. The incense smoke drifts slowly upward into the still air, and the square grows peaceful in the spaces between the comings and goings of those who come to pay their respects.

Look once more at the three faces in bronze. They have stood over this ground for more than forty years. The city they honor has endured for over seven centuries, through war and peace, through kingdoms that rose and fell around it. Friendship and trust laid its first stones. That is the quiet thing this square gently asks each visitor to pause over, and to carry away and remember.
