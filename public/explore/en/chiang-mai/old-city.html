The guide

The story

Stand at any corner inside the square moat and you will feel it — the air is cooler here, filtered through temple courtyards and old tamarind trees, slower than the traffic rushing past the outer ring road. This is Chiang Mai's Old City, a compact grid barely a mile across, ringed by water and fragments of brick wall that have stood since the late thirteenth century.

King Mengrai founded this city in 1296 as the capital of the Lanna Kingdom, a northern Thai realm that stretched from the mountains down to the Mekong. The moat and ramparts formed a perfect square, aligned to the cardinal directions, following ancient principles of city planning borrowed from Indian cosmology. Four main gates punctured the walls — one at each compass point — and the design was meant to channel good fortune inward while keeping danger at bay.

Today only a few stretches of the original wall remain, most notably along the north and east sides, where red laterite blocks rise in weathered tiers above the moat. The stone came from quarries in the surrounding hills, hauled by elephant and laid without mortar. Centuries of monsoon rains have softened the edges, and roots thread through the cracks, but the mass still holds. Walk along the base and you will see repair layers from different eras — darker patches where later builders filled breaches, lighter sections rebuilt after wars with Burma.

The gates themselves were rebuilt in the twentieth century, but they follow the old footprints. Tha Phae Gate on the east side is the most photographed, a broad archway flanked by crenellated towers, perpetually crowded with pigeons and tourists feeding them fistfuls of seed. The pigeons are a modern fixture — locals say they arrived in numbers only in the nineteen eighties — but the ritual of feeding them has become its own kind of tradition now, a noisy daily ceremony at the threshold of the old town.

Inside the square, the street grid is a warren of narrow lanes threading between temples, guesthouses, cafes, and shopfronts selling hill-tribe textiles. There are more than thirty temples within the walls, some dating back to the Lanna period, others rebuilt multiple times after earthquakes and invasions. Wat Chedi Luang at the centre is the most imposing — its chedi, a massive stepped pyramid, once rose to eighty metres before a sixteenth-century earthquake brought the top third down. The ruined spire still dominates the skyline, its brick core exposed where the stucco has fallen away, elephants carved in relief around the lower tiers.

The temples here share a distinct northern style, different from the slender chedis of Bangkok. The roofs sweep low and layered, carved wooden gables stacked like wings, often gilded or painted in deep reds and ochres. Inside, the principal Buddha images tend to be cast in the Lanna manner — round faces, high ushnishas, a serene inward gaze. Many of the murals are recent, painted over older layers that flaked away in the humidity, but a few temples preserve fragments of original Lanna frescoes showing court scenes, celestial beings, and everyday life from centuries past.

Walk the Old City early in the morning and you will see monks in saffron robes moving quietly from house to house on their alms rounds, accepting rice and fruit into their bowls. The ritual happens every day just after dawn, a thread of continuity linking the present to the city's founding. By mid-morning the streets fill with scooters, tuk-tuks, and tourists navigating the one-way system, but in those first hours the place still feels like a provincial temple town.

The moat itself is murky green, thick with lotus and water hyacinth, home to fat carp that surface near the bridges. Locals fish here in the evenings, and on Loy Krathong — the festival of lights in November — the water fills with thousands of floating krathongs, small rafts made of banana leaves and flowers, each carrying a candle. The sight from any of the bridges is otherworldly, the moat transformed into a river of flickering lights.

Outside the walls, the city sprawls in every direction, but the Old City remains the symbolic heart, the place where Chiang Mai's identity is most concentrated. The Lanna Kingdom fell under Burmese control in the sixteenth century and did not fully return to Thai rule until the late eighteenth century, and those centuries of occupation left their mark — in architectural details, in dialect, in the way people here still refer to themselves as khon mueang, people of the principality, distinct from central Thais.

On Sunday evenings, Ratchadamnoen Road, the main east-west artery through the Old City, closes to traffic and becomes a walking street market. Vendors spread blankets on the pavement selling handmade crafts, paintings, silver jewellery, and street food. The crowd is thick, a slow shuffle past stalls, the air heavy with grilled pork, coconut pancakes, and incense drifting from temple courtyards. It is one of the best times to experience the Old City's density and energy, the way the historic core still functions as a living neighbourhood rather than a museum.

As you leave, notice how the light changes near the walls at dusk — the laterite turns a deeper red, almost rust-coloured, and the moat reflects the sky in bands of pink and violet. The Old City is small enough to cross on foot in twenty minutes, but most visitors find themselves circling back, drawn by a temple they missed, a side lane they want to explore, the sense that there is always another courtyard, another carved lintel, another quiet corner where the past sits undisturbed.

Stand still for a moment before you go. Listen past the traffic — temple bells, the rustle of bodhi leaves, the low chant of evening prayers. This square mile has been the centre of something for more than seven centuries, and it still is.
