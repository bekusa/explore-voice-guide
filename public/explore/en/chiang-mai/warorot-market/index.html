The story

Step in from the street and your senses fill at once. The smell of dried spices, ripe fruit, and frying garlic hangs in the air. Color is everywhere, in stacked oranges, red chilies, and yellow sweets. Voices call out prices. Trolleys rattle past your legs. This is the great market of Chiang Mai, and it has fed the city for generations.

Local people call it Kad Luang. In the northern tongue, the words mean the great market, or the royal market. It is the largest and best known market in the north of Thailand. Outsiders know it by another name, Warorot, written on signs above the doors. Both names point to the same wide, busy halls beside the river.

The market was established in 1910. The work was led by Princess Dara Rasmi, a royal figure of high standing who had close ties to both Chiang Mai and the court in distant Bangkok. Under her hand, an open ground was cleared and shaped into a proper market of stalls and orderly lanes. More than a century later, it is still trading, day after day, much as she first intended.

The ground beneath your feet once held a different purpose. Before the market was ever built here, this was an open field used for the funeral rites of the rulers of Chiang Mai. It was a place of solemn ceremony and farewell. In time, the city turned the ground toward daily life, and the quiet field of the past slowly became a place of trade and plenty.

The name Warorot honors a person. It remembers Prince Inthawarorot Suriyawong, who was the eighth ruler of Chiang Mai. In the years when the market took shape, the city was still governed by its own line of princes, under the wider Thai kingdom. The name on the gates keeps the memory of that old ruling house alive.

The river shaped the market's early life. The Ping River runs close by, and in earlier days boats and ships tied up near these halls. Goods came and went along the water, carried in long wooden craft. Trade grew strong between Chiang Mai and the distant capital at Bangkok, far to the south. What arrived by river was sold here, and what the north produced was loaded up and carried away.

Begin on the ground floor, where the food is. Tables overflow with fresh produce, mounds of green vegetables, herbs, and fruit picked in the nearby countryside. Dried goods fill deep baskets, from rice and beans to salted fish and dried chilies. Sellers weigh, wrap, and call out to passing shoppers. Move slowly, and you will see the everyday foods of northern Thailand laid out in honest abundance.

Seek out the local specialties. There is sai ua, the spiced sausage of the north, coiled and fragrant with herbs and lemongrass. There is nam phrik, the chili relishes eaten with rice and fresh vegetables. There are khaep mu, the crisp fried strips of pork skin sold in paper bags. Sweets wrapped in banana leaf sit in careful rows along the counters. Much of it is made fresh to take away and eat as you walk.

Climb to the upper floors for other goods. Here you will find cloth and clothing, lengths of bright fabric, and ready-made shirts and dresses. There are household wares, baskets, bags, and goods prepared for festivals and gifts. The deeper aisles hold dried foods and packaged treats that travelers carry home to distant kitchens. Each floor has its own character and its own steady trade.

Step outside toward the river and you meet the flowers. A flower market sits close to the halls, heavy with the scent of jasmine and marigold. Garlands are strung by hand for temple offerings and ceremonies. Petals gather on the ground. The color and the perfume make this corner one of the most pleasant in the whole quarter.

A few habits will serve you well. Come in the morning, when the food is freshest and the air is cooler. Carry small cash, since few stalls take cards. Ask before you photograph a seller, and smile. Taste what is offered, and buy a little of what you like to carry through your day.

Before you leave, pause at the edge of the crowd. Watch the trolleys, the bundles, the easy flow of hands and money. For more than a hundred years, this market has done the same quiet work, feeding a city and binding it together. You came to shop, and you also touched something steady at the city's center.

Key facts

Established in 1910 by Princess Dara Rasmi.
Locally called Kad Luang, meaning the great market.
Named for Prince Inthawarorot, eighth ruler of Chiang Mai.
Site once held royal funeral grounds before 1910.
Largest traditional market in northern Thailand, beside the Ping.

What to look for

Stacked oranges, red chilies, and yellow sweets.
Banana-leaf parcels tied in careful rows.
The flower market scented with jasmine and marigold.
Upper floors stacked with cloth and clothing.

Practical tips

Come in the morning for the freshest food.
Carry small cash, since most stalls avoid cards.
Ask before photographing any seller, and smile warmly.
Try sai ua sausage and crisp khaep mu.
