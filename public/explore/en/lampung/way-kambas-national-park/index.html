The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest arrives as sound. Cicadas rise in a long metallic wave and fall again. Somewhere above you, a bird calls twice and stops. The air is warm and heavy, and it smells of wet leaves, red earth and something faintly sweet. Under your feet, the track is soft. This is lowland Sumatra, and you are standing at the edge of Way Kambas National Park.

The park lies on the eastern side of Lampung province, at the southern tip of the island of Sumatra. The land here is flat, low and close to the sea. Rivers wander slowly through it toward the Java Sea. In the local Lampung language, the word way means river or water, so the name itself points to the rivers that shape this landscape. Water decides almost everything here: where the forest grows, where the grass spreads, and where the animals go to drink.

Way Kambas is not one single forest. It is a patchwork. There are freshwater swamp forests, where the ground stays flooded for months. There are mangroves near the coast, with roots that stand above the mud like the legs of a table. There are drier stands of lowland rainforest. And there are wide open grasslands of tall, sharp alang-alang grass. Each habitat holds different life, and the edges between them are often the best places to watch for animals.

The protection of this land began long ago. A reserve was first set aside here during the Dutch colonial period, in the 1930s. Later, Indonesia gave the area national park status in the late twentieth century. But protection on paper did not always mean protection on the ground. Logging in the middle of the twentieth century removed many of the large old trees. Much of what you see today is forest that is still growing back. That is worth remembering as you look around — this is a landscape in recovery.

For most visitors, the elephants are the reason to come. Way Kambas is one of the best-known homes of the Sumatran elephant. This is a subspecies of the Asian elephant. It is smaller than its relatives on the mainland, with a rounder body and relatively large ears. Today it is classified as critically endangered. Across Sumatra, its forests have shrunk under farms and plantations, and herds have lost the routes they once walked.

Inside the park is an elephant conservation centre, set up in the 1980s. Its first purpose was difficult and practical. Wild elephants were raiding crops in villages around the forest, and people and elephants were both getting hurt. The centre took in elephants caught up in that conflict. Over the years, its role has changed. Today the focus is on care, education and protecting the wild herds that still live in the park.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of the trained elephants at Way Kambas do real work. Teams of rangers, called Elephant Response Units, ride with them along the park boundary. When a wild herd moves toward village fields, these teams help guide it back into the forest. In other words, elephants help protect elephants. They also help protect the farmers whose harvests sit right at the forest edge.

Now for the park's quietest and rarest resident. Deep inside Way Kambas is the Sumatran Rhino Sanctuary. The Sumatran rhinoceros is the smallest living rhino. It has two horns and, unusually, a coat of coarse reddish hair, especially when young. It is one of the most endangered large mammals on Earth. Only a small number survive in the wild, scattered across shrinking pockets of forest.

The sanctuary opened in the 1990s as a place for these animals to live in large, natural forest enclosures and, with care, to breed. Then came a moment of real hope. In 2012, a calf named Andatu was born here. He was the first Sumatran rhino born in captivity in Indonesia in more than a century. His father, Andalas, had himself been born at Cincinnati Zoo in the United States and was later brought home to Sumatra. More births have followed since. Visits to the sanctuary are very limited, so the chance to see a rhino is never guaranteed.

The park holds far more than elephants and rhinos. Sumatran tigers live here, though they are almost never seen. Malayan tapirs move through the swamp forest at night — black at the front and back, with a pale band around the middle. Sun bears climb the trees in search of honey. In the mornings, you may hear siamang gibbons. Their calls start as a low boom and rise into long, echoing whoops that carry across the canopy.

Birdwatchers know Way Kambas well. Its wetlands draw storks, hornbills and kingfishers. One special visitor is the white-winged duck, a rare forest duck that depends on quiet, shaded swamps. Along the rivers, look for flashes of blue and orange as kingfishers dive. If you take a boat trip on the Way Kanan river, keep your eyes on the overhanging branches. Snakes, monitor lizards and long-tailed macaques often rest just above the waterline.

The human story here matters too. Villages press right up against the park boundary. Families grow rice, maize and cassava on land that elephants once walked freely. Living beside a forest like this is not romantic for the people who farm here. It means nights spent guarding fields. Many local people now work in the park as rangers, guides and keepers. Their knowledge of the forest is often the best guide you will find.

A few practical thoughts. The heat builds quickly, so the early morning is usually the most comfortable time, and also the most active time for wildlife. Move slowly. Keep your voice low. Animals here are wild, and the forest rewards patience far more than speed. Bring water, light long sleeves and insect repellent. And give every elephant, trained or wild, plenty of space.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Look at the line where the grass meets the trees. Notice how the light changes between the open ground and the shade. Listen again for the cicadas, the birds, perhaps the far call of a gibbon. Somewhere in that green, a rhino may be feeding, or a herd may be walking toward the river. You will not see most of the life around you. But it is there — and this forest is one of the places keeping it alive.
