The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Below you, the Yenisei moves north, wide and steady, toward the Arctic Ocean. Wind comes up the slope off the water. It carries the sound of trains on the nearby line and the low hum of a modern city. Under your feet is loose, pale earth. This slope looks ordinary. Yet it holds one of the oldest human stories in all of Siberia.

This is Afontova Gora, a high bank on the left side of the river in Krasnoyarsk. The name means, roughly, Afonty's Hill. Locals say it comes from a man who once owned land here. That is a popular explanation, not a documented one. What is documented lies deeper, in the layers of soil inside this hillside. Archaeologists have studied those layers for well over a century.

The story of discovery begins in 1884. A local teacher and amateur archaeologist named Ivan Savenkov began to notice stone tools and old bones in the eroding bank. He collected them with care and recorded where they came from. His work showed that people lived here during the last Ice Age. At the time, this was a remarkable claim for Siberia. Many scholars then thought of the region as a frozen edge of the human world.

So who were these people? They were hunters of the Late Paleolithic, the final part of the Old Stone Age. They lived here many thousands of years ago, roughly fifteen to twenty thousand years before our time. The climate was colder and drier than today. Grassland and open steppe covered much of the land. Glaciers still covered parts of the northern world.

Now imagine the animals that walked across this view. The bones found here include reindeer, which these hunters followed and ate. Researchers also found remains of mammoth, bison, horse, and Arctic fox. The fox is a surprising detail for many visitors. It tells us how cold the Ice Age winters were, here in what is now a city of apartment blocks and bridges.

The tools tell their own story. Archaeologists found stone blades, scrapers, and points, shaped by careful strikes. They also found tools made of bone and antler, such as needles and points. A bone needle is a quiet but important object. It means people sewed fitted clothing from animal skins. Without that skill, it would be very hard to survive a Siberian winter in the Ice Age.

Afontova Gora is not a single spot. It is a group of sites along this bank, and researchers number them separately. Over the decades, different teams dug here at different times. Each layer of soil is like a page in a book. The deeper the page, the older the moment. Scientists use these layers, together with radiocarbon dating, to build a timeline of human life on the river.

Here is the second surprise, and perhaps the biggest one. Among the finds were a few human bones from one of these sites. In the 2010s, geneticists studied ancient DNA from these remains. They found a close link to the Mal'ta boy, an Ice Age child buried near Lake Baikal, far to the east. Both belonged to a population that scientists now call the Ancient North Eurasians.

Why does that matter to you, standing here today? Because that ancient population left traces in people living far away. Genetic studies show that part of the ancestry of Native Americans goes back to this group. Some of it also reached Europe, and many Europeans today carry a small share. So a few fragments of bone from this riverbank help connect Siberia, the Americas, and Europe in one long family story.

You may notice that there is little to see on the surface. There is no grand museum building on the slope, and no open excavation pit. Over the years, erosion, the railway, and city construction changed much of this bank. Many finds now rest in museum collections and scientific institutes. This is normal for Ice Age sites. The value of a place like this is in its soil, its position, and its view, not in ruins.

So look at that position. Why would hunters choose this high bank? From here, people could watch the valley below. They could see herds moving along the river and across the open land. The river gave water and a natural route through the landscape. A high, dry slope was a good place to rest, to work stone, and to keep watch. In a sense, you are using this hill exactly as they did.

The view itself has changed beyond recognition. Across the Yenisei, the right bank rises with buildings, roads, and green hills. Bridges cross the water where Ice Age hunters could only wade or wait for winter ice. The city of Krasnoyarsk grew from a Russian fort founded in the seventeenth century. Compared with the people of Afontova Gora, that founding happened only a moment ago.

If you want to see what came out of this ground, visit the city's regional museum of local history. Collections like these hold Paleolithic finds from the Yenisei valley. Seeing a stone blade in a glass case means more after you have stood on the slope where such tools were made. The object and the place complete each other.

Before you leave, take one last look down at the river. The water you see is moving north, as it did during the Ice Age. The wind on your face comes over the same valley. A train passes, and the city goes on with its day. Underneath it all, the soil of this hill keeps its layers in order... one quiet page above another, waiting for the next careful reader.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes because the slope is loose and uneven.
Pair your visit with the regional local history museum.
Bring a windproof layer for gusts coming off the river.
Stay clear of the railway tracks near the bank.
Visit in clear weather for the widest valley view.
