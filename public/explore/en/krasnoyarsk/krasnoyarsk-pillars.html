The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The wind moves through the tops of Siberian pines, a low and steady sound, like distant surf. Under your boots the path is a mix of packed earth, roots, and coarse pink grit. Put your hand on the nearest boulder. It feels rough, almost like sandpaper, and on a sunny day it holds a surprising warmth. That grit and that warmth belong to the same rock. You are standing among the Krasnoyarsk Pillars, which locals simply call Stolby, meaning the pillars.

The pillars rise out of the taiga on the edge of Krasnoyarsk, on the right bank of the Yenisei River. They sit on the northwestern foothills of the Eastern Sayan mountains, where the great Siberian forest meets the city. That closeness is part of the strangeness of this place. A short ride from apartment blocks and tram lines, you can find yourself among towers of stone with only birds for company. For many people in Krasnoyarsk, this forest is not a distant attraction. It is the weekend, the family tradition, the place they grew up climbing.

The rock here is mostly syenite. That is an igneous rock, related to granite, which formed from molten material deep underground. Over an immense span of time, the softer rock around it wore away. Rain, frost, and wind did the slow work. Water entered the cracks, froze, expanded, and split the stone along its joints. What remained are these rounded towers, stacked slabs, and blocks that look as if a giant placed them carefully one on top of another. The pinkish and grey colours come from the minerals inside the syenite.

Look closely at the surfaces nearby. You will see that the rock does not break into sharp edges like slate. Instead, it weathers into curves, soft shoulders, and deep vertical grooves. This is why so many of the pillars resemble figures. Over generations, people gave them names. There is Grandfather, with a profile that many visitors read as an old man's face. There are Grandmother and Granddaughter nearby. There are Feathers, Lion's Gate, and a sequence of numbered pillars. Part of the pleasure here is to look and decide for yourself whether the names fit.

The area became a protected nature reserve in 1925. In Russia, a reserve of this type is called a zapovednik. It is the strictest kind of protected land, set aside mainly for nature and for scientific study. That is why the reserve has two very different parts. A tourist and excursion zone lets visitors walk the trails and see the famous pillars. The much larger remaining area is kept closed, so that the forest and its animals can live with as little human pressure as possible.

Now for something most visitors do not expect. Krasnoyarsk has its own climbing culture, and it was born right here. It is called stolbizm, from the word for the pillars. From the late nineteenth century onward, local people began climbing these rocks without ropes. They developed their own routes, their own names for holds and passages, and their own codes of behaviour. The climbers, called stolbisty, formed small groups that often built huts in the forest. Some of these communities lasted for decades.

The traditional stolbist look is memorable. Climbers often wore a red sash tied around the waist, and on their feet they wore simple rubber galoshes. That may sound odd, but on this grippy syenite, soft rubber holds well. Several well-known Russian mountaineers began their climbing lives on these rocks. If you see people moving across a rock face without ropes today, please do not copy them. The local climbers know the routes intimately. Falls here are serious, and the reserve has its own rescue service for a reason.

Here is a second surprise, written on the stone itself. On the pillar called Feathers, you may see a large word painted high on the rock: Svoboda, which means freedom. According to the common account, young revolutionaries painted it in the early twentieth century, during the political unrest that swept the Russian Empire at that time. The inscription has been renewed over the years. It turns a natural formation into a piece of political history, visible from the trail below.

Let your attention move from the rock to the forest. The trees around you are typical of the Siberian taiga. You will see Siberian pine, which Russians often call cedar, with long, soft needles in bundles. There is fir, with its dark, flat needles and resin smell. There is larch, a conifer that turns golden and drops its needles every autumn. Birch trunks add flashes of white. On a warm day, the air carries the scent of resin and dry needles, especially on sunny slopes.

The animals here are often closer than you think. The most common sight is the Siberian chipmunk, small and striped, which can be quite bold around picnic spots. Squirrels and many forest birds are easy to notice too. Deeper in the reserve live larger animals, including sable, deer, and occasionally brown bears. Most of them avoid the busy trails. Please do not feed any wildlife. Food from people changes animal behaviour, and in a strict reserve, the goal is to leave the forest as wild as possible.

The pillars change a great deal with the seasons. In summer, the forest is green and full of the sound of insects and birds. In autumn, the larches turn yellow and the light becomes low and clear. In winter, snow sits on the flat tops of the rocks and the trails become quiet and white. Spring and early summer bring one important caution. This is tick season in Siberia, and some ticks can carry disease. Long sleeves, tucked trousers, and a careful check after your walk are sensible habits.

It is worth thinking about what this place means to the city. Many families in Krasnoyarsk have photographs of grandparents posing on the same rocks you see today. Schoolchildren come on excursions. Older stolbisty still return to familiar routes. In that sense, the pillars are both a natural monument and a shared memory. The rock has not changed much in a human lifetime, but each generation adds its own stories. Some of those stories are documented, and some are simply told around the fire.

Before you leave, choose one pillar and look at it for a full minute. Notice the cracks running down its face, the lichen in pale green and grey patches, the way the light falls across its curves. Then look up at the line where the stone meets the sky and the tops of the pines. As you walk back toward the city, listen again for the wind in the trees. It is the same sound that greeted you, and the same sound these rocks have heard for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes with good grip for rocky trails.
Use tick protection in spring and early summer.
Carry water and snacks for the uphill walk.
Do not climb rocks without an experienced local guide.
Start early to enjoy quieter trails and softer light.
