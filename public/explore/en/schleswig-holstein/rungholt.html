The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Out on the mudflats, the sound is soft and wet: a faint crackle as countless tiny creatures shift in the silt, the call of oystercatchers, the slow suck of water draining away. The ground under your feet is grey, cool, and ribbed by the last tide. Somewhere out here, beneath this shining mud, lie the traces of a town that the sea swallowed more than six centuries ago.

That town was Rungholt. In the Middle Ages it was a trading settlement on the North Frisian coast, in what is now Schleswig-Holstein, in the far north of Germany. Today there is nothing to see standing above the surface. No tower, no wall, no ruin to photograph. And that absence is exactly what makes this place so powerful. You are not visiting a monument. You are visiting a memory held in the tidal flats.

To understand why Rungholt vanished, you need to picture this coastline in the fourteenth century. It looked very different. Where you now see open mudflats and scattered small islands, there was a wide, low marshland. Farmers lived on it. They drained it with ditches, protected it with earthen dykes, and controlled the water with sluice gates. It was fertile land, but it lay barely above the level of the sea. People lived here by an uneasy agreement with the water.

In January 1362, that agreement broke. A violent storm surge struck the North Sea coast. It is remembered in the region as the Grote Mandrenke, a Low German name that means, roughly, the great drowning of men. Huge areas of marshland flooded. Dykes failed, whole parishes were lost, and the coastline was torn into new shapes. Rungholt was one of the settlements that disappeared beneath the water and never returned.

How many people died is not known with certainty. Estimates from that time vary widely, and medieval numbers are often unreliable. What is clear is that the flood changed this coast for good. Much of the land that was lost was never reclaimed. The sea simply stayed. Over the following centuries, tides and currents covered the old fields, ditches, and house sites with layers of silt and sand.

And here is the first surprise. For a long time, many people believed Rungholt was only a legend. Without stone ruins or clear written records, its exact location was a mystery. Then, in the early twentieth century, the mudflats began to give up evidence. A local amateur researcher named Andreas Busch spent years walking these flats. He recorded remains that appeared when the sand shifted: wooden sluice structures, the lines of old dykes, and traces of farmsteads.

Those finds showed that a real cultivated landscape lay beneath the water. Since then, researchers have continued the work with modern tools. Geophysical surveys can map buried features without digging. In 2023, a research team from Kiel University and partners reported that they had located the remains of a medieval church in the tidal flats near the small island of Südfall. They connected it with the lost settlement. Slowly, a drowned map is coming back into view.

Now for the legend, because Rungholt has one, and locals still tell it. According to the popular tale, the people of Rungholt grew rich and proud. They mocked a priest and treated sacred things with contempt. As punishment, so the story goes, the storm came and the town was drowned. It is a moral story, the kind told across many coastal cultures. It is folklore, not history. But it shows how people tried to make sense of a disaster that seemed far too large to be random.

Another part of the legend is gentler, and stranger. Some say that on calm nights, you can hear the church bells of Rungholt ringing beneath the waves. Of course, there are no bells down there to hear. Yet standing here at dusk, with the water moving quietly and the light fading over the flats, it is easy to understand why people imagined that sound.

The legend also lives on in German literature. The poet Detlev von Liliencron wrote a famous ballad about Rungholt in the late nineteenth century, called Trutz, Blanke Hans. The title is hard to translate. Blanke Hans is a North Frisian nickname for the stormy North Sea itself, treated almost like a living giant. Many German schoolchildren learned the poem, and it helped turn this drowned town into a lasting story.

Today the area around Rungholt belongs to the Wadden Sea, a vast system of tidal flats that stretches along the coasts of the Netherlands, Germany, and Denmark. Here in Schleswig-Holstein it is protected as a national park, and the Wadden Sea is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. So this place carries two stories at once. One is about human loss. The other is about a living landscape of mud, worms, shellfish, and migrating birds.

That leads to the second surprise. The same tides that buried Rungholt also keep moving its remains. Storms and currents can uncover old traces for a short time, then cover them again. Researchers and guides know that the flats are never quite the same from one season to the next. The drowned town is not frozen. It is still being shaped, very slowly, by the sea that took it.

If you want to walk toward these places, go only with an experienced mudflat guide. The tide here returns faster than many visitors expect, and channels can fill quickly. Guides read the water, the weather, and the ground in ways that take years to learn. Ashore, regional museums on the coast, including in Husum, show finds and models that help you picture the lost marshland.

Before you leave, look out across the flats once more. Notice how flat the horizon is, how thin the line between land and sea. Feel how soft the ground is under your feet. Somewhere out there, beneath the shine of the water, people once ploughed fields, kept animals, and rang church bells. As you walk back, listen to the tide coming in. It is the same sea, still moving, still deciding where the coast will be.
