The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. It comes up the canyon of the Pinturas River, dry and steady, and it carries the smell of dust and low shrubs. Under your boots the path is pale gravel, loose in places. Then the rock wall turns, the overhang opens above you, and you see them. Hands. Hundreds of them, in red and ochre and white and black, pressed onto the stone as if someone had just pulled them away.

This is the Cueva de las Manos, the Cave of the Hands. It sits in the far south of Argentina, in Santa Cruz province, in the open steppe of Patagonia. From the town of Perito Moreno, most visitors travel a long stretch south on gravel and highway to reach it. The emptiness on the way matters. When you arrive, you understand that people came to this canyon on purpose, again and again, for thousands of years.

The word cave is a little misleading. Most of the art is not hidden deep underground. It covers the walls of rock shelters and overhangs along the canyon cliff, where the stone leans out and gives some protection from rain and sun. There is a true cave here as well, a shallow opening in the rock. But the real gallery is the long, sheltered wall in front of you. It runs along the cliff like a painted frieze.

Now, the dates. Archaeologists believe the oldest paintings here are roughly 9,000 years old. People continued to add images over a very long span, until roughly a thousand years ago. Think about that for a moment. The newest hands on this wall are older than most cathedrals in Europe. The oldest belong to a time when the last ice age was still fading from the southern Andes.

The people who made them were hunter-gatherers. They moved across this steppe following game, especially the guanaco — a wild relative of the llama, tawny and long-necked, which you may still see on the drive here. Scholars often connect these early painters with the ancestors of later Patagonian peoples, but the exact links across so many millennia are uncertain. What is clear is that this canyon held meaning for them, generation after generation.

Look closely at how the hands were made. Most of them are not painted hands but stencils. A person placed a hand flat against the rock. Then they sprayed pigment around it, probably by blowing it through a tube or straight from the mouth. When the hand lifted away, its outline stayed behind, a pale shape in a cloud of colour. Archaeologists have suggested that hollow bones may have served as the spraying tubes.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The large majority of the stencils are left hands. The simplest explanation is practical. If you hold the spray tube in your right hand, you press your left hand to the wall. So in a quiet way, these outlines also tell you how the painters stood. You can almost picture each one leaning into the rock, cheeks full, right hand at the mouth.

The colours came from the land itself. Iron-rich earths gave the reds, purples and ochres. White came from pale clay minerals, and black from manganese or charcoal. These pigments were ground and mixed, possibly with water or other binders. The dry climate of the steppe and the shelter of the overhang have helped them survive. That is why some of these hands still look so fresh after thousands of years.

Hands are only part of the story. Between and around them, you will find small painted figures. Guanacos appear again and again, alone and in herds. In some scenes, groups of hunters surround the animals, and researchers read these as hunting strategies drawn on the rock. There are also geometric shapes — circles, zigzag lines, dots — and marks that resemble the three-toed tracks of the rhea, the large flightless bird of the Patagonian plains.

The site became widely known to science in the twentieth century. The Argentine archaeologist Carlos Gradin led detailed research here, beginning in the 1960s, and his work helped define the different phases of painting. In 1999, UNESCO listed the Cueva de las Manos as a World Heritage Site. That recognition brought protection, and it brought rules. Today you visit with a guide, and the walkways keep everyone at a careful distance from the wall.

That distance can feel frustrating at first. You want to lean in. But the rock is fragile, and the oils on human skin can damage pigment that has lasted for millennia. So use your eyes instead. Some of the hands are small — very likely the hands of children. Some overlap so densely that the wall looks like a crowd. A few hands are positive prints, where a painted palm was pressed flat against the stone.

Step back now and look out from the shelter. Below you, the Pinturas River runs through the bottom of the canyon, a thin green line in a world of brown and gold. Willows and grasses grow along the water. The cliff walls show layers of volcanic rock in rust, cream and grey. This view is part of the site. The painters looked at this same river, and for them, water and shelter here meant survival.

Before you leave, choose one hand on the wall. Just one. Notice its size, the spread of its fingers, the colour around it. Someone stood exactly where you are standing and made that mark. We do not know their name or their language. But the gesture is simple, and it is human: I was here. As you walk back along the path, the wind will still be blowing up the canyon, just as it did for them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan a guided visit; access to the wall requires one.
Bring warm layers against strong Patagonian canyon winds.
Carry water and snacks for the long remote drive.
Bring binoculars to study small figures from the walkway.
Never touch the rock; skin oils damage ancient pigment.
