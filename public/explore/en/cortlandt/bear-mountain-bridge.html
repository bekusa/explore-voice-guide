The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Hudson River moves slowly between steep wooded hills. Above you, steel cables hum faintly in the wind. Trucks rumble across the deck, and the whole span answers with a soft, steady vibration you can feel through your shoes. On a still morning, mist lies low on the water, and the far shore appears one ridge at a time.

This is the Bear Mountain Bridge. It links the town of Cortlandt, on the eastern bank, with Bear Mountain on the western side. Two roads cross it together, US Route 6 and US Route 202. For most drivers, it is simply a way over the river. For anyone who stops and walks, it is one of the best viewpoints in the whole Hudson Valley.

The setting matters as much as the steel. This stretch of river is called the Hudson Highlands. Here the river pushes through a gap in an old mountain range, and the hills rise straight from the water. Geologists describe this part of the Hudson as a fjord, which means a deep valley cut by ice and later filled by the sea. Tides from the Atlantic still reach this far upstream. The water under you rises and falls twice a day, even though the ocean is many kilometres away.

When the bridge opened in 1924, it held a remarkable title. Its main span was the longest of any suspension bridge in the world. A suspension bridge hangs its road from giant cables, and those cables rest on tall towers and anchor deep into rock at each end. The central span here stretches nearly five hundred metres between the two towers. That record did not last long, because bigger bridges soon followed. But for a short moment, this quiet crossing in the Highlands led the world.

Here is the first surprise. The bridge was not built by the state. It was a private venture, financed largely by the Harriman family, the railroad fortune behind much of Bear Mountain State Park. Before the bridge, people crossed here by ferry, and traffic to the new park was growing fast. The private company paid for construction and collected tolls to recover the money. Years later, New York State bought the bridge, and today a state bridge authority runs it.

The builders also worked quickly. Construction took less than two years, which was fast for a project of this size in the 1920s. Crews had to anchor the cables into the hard rock of the mountains on both banks. Look at the dark granite slopes around you. That same stone holds the weight of the entire road.

Now look toward the eastern end, on the Cortlandt side. The steep, rounded mountain there is Anthony's Nose. Nobody agrees on the origin of the name. According to one popular tale, made famous by the writer Washington Irving, a trumpeter named Anthony had a very large, very red nose. Legend has it that sunlight bounced off that nose and struck a fish in the river below. It is a joke, not history, but the name has stayed for centuries.

The second surprise is underfoot, if you are walking. The Appalachian Trail, a hiking path that runs from Georgia to Maine, crosses the Hudson on this bridge. Hikers walk the same narrow sidewalk you may be standing on. Just west of the bridge, near the Trailside Museums and Zoo, the trail reaches its lowest point along its whole route. A path famous for mountains drops almost to sea level right here.

The river also carries a darker story. During the American Revolution, the Hudson was a key route between New England and the other colonies. Two forts, Fort Montgomery and Fort Clinton, stood on the western bank just south of the bridge. In 1777, British forces captured both forts after hard fighting. Today, the remains of Fort Montgomery are a state historic site, and you can see the river edge where the battle took place.

Take a moment to study the bridge itself. The two towers are tall steel frames, painted a muted green that blends with the hills. The main cables curve down from the tower tops, almost touching the deck at mid-river, then rise again. Thin vertical cables hang from the main cables and carry the road. The design is plain and practical. Its beauty comes from proportion, not decoration.

The view changes with the seasons. In autumn, the slopes turn red, orange, and gold, and the river reflects the colour. In winter, ice sometimes gathers along the banks, and the bare trees show the rock beneath. In summer, boats pass slowly underneath, and you can hear their engines echo off the mountainsides. Trains run along both shores too, close to the waterline, and their horns carry far across the valley.

The bridge was later listed on the National Register of Historic Places, a federal list of buildings and structures worth preserving. That listing recognises both its engineering and its role in opening the Highlands to visitors. Generations of families have crossed here on the way to picnics, hikes, and swimming at Bear Mountain. For many people in the region, this crossing marks the start of a day outdoors.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the wind, which is always stronger out over the water. Notice how small the cars look against the hills, and how the cables frame the river like the lines of a drawing. As you walk back toward solid ground, look south. The river bends out of sight between the mountains, and the forts, the tides, and the old trail all continue just beyond the turn.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the pedestrian sidewalk rather than viewing from your car.
Come in autumn for the brightest colour on the slopes.
Bring a windproof layer; gusts are strong mid-span.
Combine your visit with the Fort Montgomery historic site.
Park legally at the ends before walking onto the deck.
