The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic on the road behind you fades quickly here. In its place there is the low sound of water in the moat, the call of a bird somewhere in the grass, and the crunch of gravel under your shoes. Ahead of you is a long, low mass of grey concrete. It does not rise up to meet you. It seems to sit inside the earth. That is your first clue to what this place was built to be.

Fort Breendonk began as a Belgian military fort in the early twentieth century. Construction started around 1906. It formed part of a ring of forts that protected Antwerp, which Belgium treated as its last line of defence, a national stronghold. The engineers designed the fort to be hard to see and hard to hit. They covered the concrete with a thick layer of soil and surrounded it with a wide moat. From a distance, an enemy gunner would see little more than a grassy mound.

The fort saw action when German forces advanced on Antwerp in 1914. Like the other forts in the ring, it could not hold for long against modern heavy artillery. Between the wars it lost much of its military value. Then, in May 1940, Belgium was invaded again. For a short time the Belgian army used Breendonk as a headquarters. Within weeks, the country had surrendered. And the fort began the chapter that brings most visitors here today.

In September 1940, the German SS took over Breendonk. They called it an Auffanglager, which means a reception camp. That name sounds almost administrative. The reality was different. Breendonk became a prison camp for people the occupiers considered a threat. Over four years, around 3,600 people passed through these gates. They included resistance fighters, political prisoners, hostages, and, in the early years, Jewish men arrested in Belgium.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. When the prisoners arrived, the fort was still buried under its protective earth. The SS decided to remove it. They gave the work to the prisoners. Day after day, men dug out the soil by hand and moved it in wheelbarrows and carts, often at a run, under shouting and blows. Much of the bare concrete you see now was uncovered by that forced labour. The building itself carries the marks of what was done here.

As you walk inside, notice how the air changes. It is cooler, damper, and the light drops quickly. The corridors are narrow and long, with curved ceilings of concrete. Sound behaves strangely here. Footsteps carry. Voices from another room arrive faintly, as if from far away. Prisoners lived in these conditions through cold Belgian winters, with very little food and almost no heat.

The camp was small compared to the large concentration camps further east. But it gained a terrible reputation. Prisoners were held in crowded rooms and in tiny solitary cells. Hunger was constant. Beatings were routine. The camp was run by SS officers, and the guards included Flemish volunteers who had joined the SS. The commandant for most of the war was Philipp Schmitt. After the war, he was tried in Belgium and sentenced to death.

One room inside the fort was used for interrogation and torture. Among the people tortured there was a young Austrian-born Jewish resistance member who later wrote under the name Jean Améry. Decades afterwards, he wrote a well-known essay about torture, drawing on what happened to him in this fort. He described how it changed his trust in the world for the rest of his life. His words are one reason Breendonk is known far beyond Belgium.

For many prisoners, Breendonk was not the end of the journey. It was a stop on the way. Groups of prisoners were deported from here to camps in Germany and further east, places such as Buchenwald and Mauthausen. Many did not return. In 1942, a separate camp in the nearby town of Mechelen became the main departure point for Jews deported from Belgium. Breendonk continued as a camp for political prisoners and resistance members.

Outside, at the back of the fort, there is an execution ground. You will see wooden posts and a gallows. Prisoners and hostages were shot or hanged at this site, often in reprisal for attacks by the resistance. The space is quiet and open to the sky. Many people stop speaking here without being asked. Take as much time as you need. There is no right way to stand in a place like this.

The camp closed in 1944, shortly before Belgium was liberated. In the years after the war, Breendonk was declared a national memorial. The decision was to keep it, not to tear it down. That choice matters. Instead of a monument built later, visitors walk through the real rooms, the real corridors, the real yard. The exhibitions add names, faces, photographs, and personal stories to the concrete.

It is worth looking at the fort as a building as well as a memorial. The same features that made it a good military fort made it an effective prison. The moat isolated it. The thick walls blocked sound. The low profile hid it from the surrounding countryside. Engineers designed it to keep an enemy out. The SS used the same design to keep people in, and to keep the outside world from seeing what happened.

When you leave, pause on the bridge over the moat and look back. You will see the grey concrete, the grass on the remaining banks, and the calm water reflecting the sky. It looks peaceful now, and in a sense it is. But you know what the bare walls mean, and who uncovered them. As you walk back toward the road and the ordinary sounds of Willebroek, carry a few of those names with you.
