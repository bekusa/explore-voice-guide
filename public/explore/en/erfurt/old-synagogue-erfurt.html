The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The narrow lane around you is quiet, tucked behind the shops of Michaelisstraße. In front of you rises a tall, plain wall of rough stone, grey and ochre, patched and uneven. It does not look like much at first. No dome, no tower, no grand doorway. Yet this modest building is one of the oldest surviving synagogue buildings in Europe. Its oldest walls go back to around the year 1100.

That date is worth holding onto. When the first stones of this building were laid, Erfurt was already a busy trading town in the heart of what is now Germany. Merchants passed through on the long roads crossing the region. A Jewish community settled here and became part of the town's economic life. Families lived close together in this quarter, near the river Gera and the old market streets. This synagogue was the centre of their religious life.

Look closely at the stonework, and you will see that the building did not appear all at once. It grew in phases across about two centuries. The earliest walls were extended and raised in the thirteenth century. Further work followed in the early fourteenth century. On the west front, look for the tall, narrow pointed windows and the round rose window above them. These are Gothic forms, the style of pointed arches that spread across medieval Europe.

Then came 1349. That year, the Black Death was moving across Europe. In town after town, Jewish communities were falsely blamed for the plague. In Erfurt, a violent pogrom destroyed the community. Many people were murdered, and survivors fled. It is one of the darkest moments in the city's history. And it is the reason this building survived in such an unusual way.

After the massacre, the city took possession of the synagogue. Instead of tearing it down, the new owners turned it into a warehouse. Floors were inserted. The interior was divided up for storage. For centuries, goods were stacked where prayers had once been said. Over time, other buildings grew up around it, hiding the old walls from the street. The synagogue simply disappeared from view... while its walls remained standing.

That later life continued for a very long time. In the nineteenth century, the building was used for an inn and a restaurant, and a dance hall was set up inside. Imagine music and laughter in a room whose original purpose had been almost forgotten. Here is one of the surprises of this place: those later uses are not erased. Inside, the museum keeps traces of the warehouse and the dance hall alongside the medieval stone.

Because the building was so well hidden, it was not recognised as a synagogue during the Nazi period. Elsewhere in Germany, synagogues were burned and demolished. This one was disguised by its own ordinary history. Only in the late twentieth century did researchers fully understand what was standing here. The city acquired the building in the late 1990s. After careful restoration, it opened as a museum in 2009.

Now comes the second surprise, and it is waiting for you below ground. In 1998, during excavation work just a short walk from here, archaeologists found a buried hoard. It contained thousands of silver coins, silver bars, and hundreds of pieces of jewellery and tableware. Experts believe the treasure was hidden around the time of the 1349 pogrom. Whoever buried it never came back to collect it.

This find is known as the Erfurt Treasure, and it is displayed in the vaulted cellar of the synagogue. The rooms are dim and cool. The pieces glow under soft light: brooches, belt fittings, cups, rings. The most famous object is a Jewish wedding ring made of gold. On top sits a tiny building with a pointed roof, a symbol often linked to the Temple in Jerusalem or to the new home of a married couple. It is a small object, but it carries a very personal story.

On the upper floor, the museum turns to another kind of treasure: books. The medieval Jewish community of Erfurt owned important Hebrew manuscripts, including Bibles and religious texts. The originals are now kept in Berlin, but the exhibition helps visitors understand what they contained. These pages show a community of scholars and readers. They were not only merchants, as they are sometimes remembered, but people of learning and faith.

The synagogue is not the only survivor of this world. Near the Krämerbrücke, the old merchants' bridge lined with houses, archaeologists uncovered a medieval mikveh. A mikveh is a ritual bath used in Jewish religious practice, fed by natural water. Together with this synagogue and a medieval stone house nearby, these places form a single group. In 2023, they were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list as the Jewish-Medieval Heritage of Erfurt.

It is worth thinking about why that recognition matters. Very few buildings of medieval Jewish life in Europe still stand with their walls intact. Most were destroyed, rebuilt, or lost to later centuries. Here, by an accident of history, the structure survived because it was used for something else. The result is a building that carries every layer of its past: worship, violence, storage, celebration, rediscovery.

Before you go, step back and look once more at the outer walls. Notice how the stones change in size and colour from one section to another. Each change is a moment in the building's long life. Then walk out toward the lanes leading to the river. As you pass the shops and cafés, remember that a whole community once lived on these streets. This quiet stone wall is where their story is still kept.
