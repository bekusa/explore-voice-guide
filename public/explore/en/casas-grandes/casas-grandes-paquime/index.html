The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the high desert of northern Chihuahua, and it carries fine dust that settles on your shoes. Around you rise walls of pale, sun-baked earth. Their edges are softened and rounded, like sandcastles after a tide. Some stand taller than a person. Others are low lines in the dry grass. This is Paquimé, the ancient city the Spanish called Casas Grandes, meaning 'the big houses'.

That name tells you what the first Europeans saw when they reached this valley in the sixteenth century. The city was already in ruins, but the ruins were large. Thick walls of earth still stood several stories high. The travellers had seen nothing like it in the northern deserts. The name stayed, and today it belongs to the river, the town, and the whole municipality that grew up around these walls.

The people who built Paquimé were at their height roughly between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. Archaeologists call this the Medio period. In those years, this valley held one of the largest and most complex settlements anywhere in the region between central Mexico and the American Southwest. In 1998, UNESCO added the site to its World Heritage list. The listing recognised the site as a key link between those two cultural worlds.

Look closely at the walls. They are not made of bricks. The builders used poured adobe, a method sometimes called rammed earth. They packed wet mud into wooden forms, let it dry in the sun, then raised the forms and poured the next layer. If you stand close, you can sometimes see faint horizontal lines. Each line marks one pour. These are the working rhythms of builders who lived here more than five centuries ago.

Now find a doorway. Many openings at Paquimé are shaped like a capital letter T. They are wide at shoulder height and narrow toward the floor. The same shape appears at sites far to the north, in the canyons of the American Southwest. Nobody knows its exact meaning. Some researchers link it to defence. Others link it to ritual, or to the simple practice of carrying loads on the shoulders. It is a small shape, but it connects this valley to a much wider world.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. Scattered through the compounds are small square boxes made of adobe, each with a round opening. These were nesting pens for scarlet macaws. Excavators found the remains of many birds, along with eggshell fragments and perches. The people of Paquimé were not only trading these tropical birds. They were breeding them, far from any rainforest. The bright red feathers were valuable trade goods across the northern deserts.

The macaws are a clue to the city's role. Paquimé sat at the centre of long trade routes. Excavations turned up enormous quantities of marine shell, much of it from the Gulf of California, far to the west. Workers found copper bells, turquoise, and worked stone. The city seems to have gathered these goods and sent them onward. Shell and feathers moved north. Other materials arrived from many directions.

Water was everything in this dry valley, and the builders treated it with great care. They built a system of channels to bring water from springs in the hills. They also built drains to carry used water away from the rooms. One of the most striking features is a deep walk-in well, with steps leading down toward the water table. In a land of thin rain, this was serious engineering. As you walk, look for the low stone-lined channels at your feet.

Beyond the residential compounds, you will see open spaces and mounds. Paquimé had several ballcourts, long flat fields shaped like a capital letter I. Ballgames like these were played across ancient Mexico, often with deep ritual meaning. There are also earthen platforms shaped like symbolic figures. One is often described as a bird. Another is laid out in the form of a cross. From the higher ground, you can trace these shapes against the grass and gravel.

Much of what we know comes from a large joint expedition in the late 1950s and early 1960s. The project was led by the American archaeologist Charles Di Peso, working with Mexican colleagues. His team excavated widely and published many volumes of findings. Later researchers have revised some of his dates and ideas. That is normal. Archaeology is a long conversation, and Paquimé still has questions left to answer.

One of those questions is how the city ended. By the fifteenth century, the great compounds were largely abandoned. Excavators found signs of burning in parts of the site. Some early accounts imagined a single violent attack. Other scholars point to a slower decline, with drought, shifting trade, or political change. The honest answer is that the full story is still debated. When the Spanish arrived, they found only the walls you see now.

Before you leave, visit the site museum, the Museum of the Cultures of the North. Its low, earth-coloured building sits quietly into the landscape. Inside, you can see the pottery that made Paquimé famous. The pots are cream-coloured, painted in red and black with tight geometric patterns. Some are shaped like human figures, birds, or serpents. Seeing them up close helps you picture the rooms outside as homes full of colour and daily life.

That pottery tradition did not stay in the past. In the village of Mata Ortiz, a short drive south, a self-taught potter named Juan Quezada began studying ancient sherds in the second half of the twentieth century. He worked out how to make similar vessels with local clay and natural pigments. Today, many families in the village make fine pottery. Their work is sold far beyond Mexico. A craft born here centuries ago is alive again nearby.

Now stand still for a moment among the walls. Notice how the afternoon light turns the adobe from pale beige to warm gold. Notice the mountains on the horizon, and the quiet of the valley. People once raised macaws here, carried water through these channels, and traded shell from a distant sea. As you walk back toward the museum, look down at the dusty ground. It is the same earth those walls were made from.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strongest desert heat.
Bring a hat, sunscreen, and plenty of drinking water.
Tour the site museum before walking the ruins.
Stay on marked paths to protect the fragile adobe walls.
Plan a side trip to Mata Ortiz to see potters.
