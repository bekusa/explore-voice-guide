The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the pale cobbles of Place Royale. Trams hum and rattle around the square, and the air moves freely across this high ground above the old city. In front of you rises a calm neoclassical facade, cream-coloured and orderly, with tall windows in neat rows. Nothing about it looks strange. That is the joke, and you are about to step inside it. Behind this polite exterior hangs the largest collection anywhere of work by René Magritte, the Belgian painter who made ordinary things feel quietly impossible.

The Magritte Museum opened in 2009. It belongs to the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium, the group of national galleries that cluster around this square. Inside, you will find more than two hundred works. There are oil paintings and gouaches, drawings and sculptures. There are also photographs, home films, posters and letters. Together they follow one life from a small industrial town to the centre of modern art. It is a portrait of a painter, but also of a very particular way of looking at the world.

Magritte was born in 1898 in Lessines, in the province of Hainaut, a region of quarries and canals. His childhood carried a dark mark. In 1912 his mother died in the river Sambre. A popular story says the boy saw her body pulled from the water, her nightgown wrapped around her face. Historians are careful with this tale, and Magritte himself rarely spoke about it. Still, many visitors think of it when they see the cloth-covered faces in some of his paintings.

As a young man, Magritte came to Brussels to study at the Academy of Fine Arts. To earn a living, he designed wallpaper, posters and advertisements. Keep that detail in mind as you walk through the rooms. His paintings often look like clear, clean commercial images — a pipe, an apple, a bowler hat, a blue sky. He used the language of the shop window and the magazine page. Then he placed one wrong element inside it, and the whole picture tilted.

In the late 1920s, Magritte and his wife Georgette moved to the outskirts of Paris. There he met the French Surrealists, the circle of artists and writers who explored dreams and the unconscious mind. The relationship was never fully comfortable. Magritte was not interested in automatic drawing or wild, dripping forms. He wanted precision. His surrealism was cool and logical, almost like a riddle. After a few years, the couple returned to Brussels, and he stayed in Belgium for the rest of his life.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Magritte did not live like a bohemian. For years he lived in a modest house in the Brussels district of Jette. He often painted in a corner of the main living space, dressed neatly, like an office clerk. He kept regular hours. He walked his dog. The man who painted rain made of men in overcoats looked, on the street, exactly like one of them. That house is a separate museum, by the way, so do not confuse the two.

As you move through the galleries, watch how the mood changes over the decades. The early rooms show a young painter testing ideas, borrowing from Cubism and Futurism. Then the familiar Magritte appears — the sharp edges, the smooth skies, the objects that refuse to behave. Words and images begin to argue with each other. A label names one thing while the picture shows another. He asks a simple question again and again. How do we know that what we see is real?

The second surprise comes from the war years. During the German occupation of Belgium, Magritte suddenly changed his style. He painted in soft, bright, feathery strokes, close to the French Impressionist Renoir. Scholars often call this his sunlit period. He said he wanted to answer a grim time with pleasure and colour. Critics did not love it, and many visitors walk past these canvases in confusion. Pause in front of them anyway. They show an artist willing to risk his own reputation.

Shortly after the war came another provocation. Magritte produced a group of rough, garish, deliberately clumsy paintings for an exhibition in Paris. This episode is known as his vache period, from the French word for cow. It was a kind of insult to the Paris art world, loud and comic. Then, just as suddenly, he returned to his calm, exact manner. The museum lets you follow these turns closely. You see that his famous style was a choice, not a habit.

One of the works people come here to find is a version of The Empire of Light. Magritte painted this idea many times. A quiet house stands in the dark, its street lamp glowing, while above it spreads a bright daytime sky full of white clouds. Night and day share one canvas, and the picture feels peaceful at first. Stay with it for a minute. The longer you look, the more unsettling it becomes, because your eyes keep searching for the moment where the two times meet.

Beyond the paintings, look closely at the photographs and personal documents. Magritte and his friends staged playful photos and short films at home, wearing hats and posing like characters in a puzzle. Letters and sketches show how carefully he planned his images. Some of his commercial designs also appear, and they make a good comparison with the canvases nearby. The same steady hand drew both. This mix of the private and the public gives the collection its warmth.

As you leave the final rooms, think about how often you have already met Magritte without knowing it. His floating apples, bowler hats and impossible skies appear on album covers, in films and in advertising all over the world. The painter borrowed from commercial images, and commercial images borrowed him right back. It is a neat circle. Few artists have shaped everyday visual culture so quietly, and from such an ordinary life in Brussels.

When you step back out onto Place Royale, give your eyes a moment to adjust. Look up at the sky above the square. Watch the clouds move behind the statue at the centre and the tall facades around you. After an hour with Magritte, the ordinary city can feel a little less certain. A man in a dark coat crosses the cobbles. A window reflects a piece of blue. Nothing has changed... and yet you may find yourself looking twice.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least ninety minutes to follow the chronology properly.
Pair your visit with the neighbouring Old Masters collection.
Visit Magritte's former home in Jette on another day.
Arrive early to see the popular paintings with fewer crowds.
Pause longer at the wartime canvases most visitors skip.
