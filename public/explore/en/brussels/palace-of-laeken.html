The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, on the northern edge of Brussels, the traffic thins out and the sound of the city changes. Gravel crunches under your feet, crows call from tall old trees, and the wind moves across open lawns. In front of you, behind iron railings, a long pale façade stretches across the grass. Its columns are calm, its windows are tall, and its lines are even. This is the Palace of Laeken, home of the Belgian royal family.

The first thing to understand is that there are two royal palaces in Brussels. The Royal Palace in the city centre, near the Park of Brussels, is the working palace. It holds offices, receptions, and state ceremonies. Laeken is different. It is the residence. It is where the royal family lives its private life, away from the monuments and crowds of the old town. So the gates stay closed, and that is part of the character of this place.

The story begins in the late eighteenth century, before Belgium existed as a country. At that time, these lands belonged to the Habsburgs and were known as the Austrian Netherlands. Their governors were Archduchess Maria Christina, a daughter of the Empress Maria Theresa, and her husband, Duke Albert of Saxe-Teschen. In the 1780s, they built a country residence here, outside the city walls, on gently rising ground with views across the countryside.

The style they chose is neoclassical. In simple terms, that means architecture inspired by ancient Greece and Rome — columns, triangular pediments, balance, and restraint. After the rich curves of earlier palaces, this was the fashion of the age of reason. Look at the central section of the façade. The columns and the strong horizontal lines are meant to feel calm and orderly. It is a building that wants to look rational and composed rather than showy.

The governors did not enjoy their palace for long. At the end of the eighteenth century, revolutionary France swept into these provinces, and the Habsburg court fled. The palace passed through several hands. Then came Napoleon. He acquired Laeken and stayed here during his visits to the region. According to a popular story, he worked on plans for his invasion of Russia in these rooms. Historians treat that tale with caution, but it is told often.

After Napoleon's defeat in 1815, these lands became part of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, and King William the First used the palace. Then, in 1830, the Belgian Revolution created a new country. Its first king, Leopold the First, made Laeken his home. He lived here for decades, and he died here in 1865. From that moment, the palace was firmly tied to the new Belgian monarchy, and it still is today.

Now for something many visitors never learn. Much of what you see is not the original building. In 1890, a serious fire swept through the palace and destroyed large parts of it. The rebuilding took place under King Leopold the Second. He was an ambitious builder, and he did not simply restore the old house. He enlarged it. The palace you see now is wider and grander than the country residence of the Habsburg governors.

Leopold the Second worked with the architect Alphonse Balat, and later the French architect Charles Girault helped shape the long façade in the early twentieth century. The result keeps the neoclassical spirit of the original, but on a much larger scale. When you look at the building from end to end, try to picture a smaller house at its heart. The extensions grew outward from it, making a residence fit for the image Leopold wanted for his kingdom.

That image has a dark side, and an honest guide should mention it. Leopold the Second ruled the Congo Free State as his personal possession, and the brutal exploitation of its people paid for much of his building in Belgium. Today, many Belgians debate how to remember this period. As you stand before these elegant walls and gardens, it is worth holding both stories in mind — the beauty of the architecture and the human cost behind it.

Here is the second surprise. Behind the palace, inside the royal grounds, stand the Royal Greenhouses of Laeken. Balat designed them for Leopold the Second, using iron and glass to create a chain of domes and galleries. They hold palms, camellias, and other plants from warm climates, some of them very old. For most of the year, the greenhouses are closed. But for a few weeks each year, usually in spring, the public can walk through them.

Now turn around, away from the palace, and look across the park. On the hill opposite, you can see a tall stone spire. This is the monument to Leopold the First, built in the Gothic Revival style — a nineteenth-century return to the pointed arches and spires of medieval cathedrals. Inside the spire sits a statue of the first king. It looks back toward the house where he lived and died, across lawns that are open to everyone.

The wider neighbourhood is full of royal traces. Nearby stands the Church of Our Lady of Laeken, with its dark spires. Beneath it lies the royal crypt, where Belgian kings and queens are buried. A little further away, you will find the Japanese Tower and the Chinese Pavilion, both commissioned by Leopold the Second after he saw similar buildings at a Paris exhibition. And not far away, the silver spheres of the Atomium rise above the trees.

So you cannot walk inside these walls. But you can do something perhaps more interesting. You can see how a royal family lives at the edge of a modern capital. Joggers pass the railings, dogs run across the park, and families sit on the grass in front of a palace that is still in use. Before you leave, look once more at the windows. Somewhere behind them, ordinary daily life goes on — and the gravel under your feet keeps its quiet rhythm as you walk away.

Bring comfortable shoes for the large surrounding park.
