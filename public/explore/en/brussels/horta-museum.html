The story

The staircase curves upward like a ribbon of iron, its banister flowing in a single unbroken line from the ground floor to the skylight three stories above. Stand at the base and look up — the metalwork branches into tendrils that grip the handrail, each one shaped by hand, no two quite identical. This is the home Victor Horta built for himself in the eighteen-nineties, when Art Nouveau was still a radical idea and Brussels was the wealthiest city in Europe.

Horta was thirty-three when he designed this house, already known for breaking the rules of Belgian architecture. He hated the heavy stone facades that lined every Brussels boulevard — all those columns and cornices pretending to be Greek temples. He wanted buildings that moved, that breathed, that let light pour through rooms the way it pours through a forest canopy. Iron was his material. Not hidden behind plaster like a shameful secret, but exposed, celebrated, bent into shapes that mimicked vines and stems and the curve of a breaking wave.

The house sits on Rue Américaine in the Saint-Gilles neighborhood, a narrow plot hemmed in by taller buildings on both sides. Horta turned the constraint into an advantage. He pushed the staircase to the center of the plan and wrapped the rooms around it, so every space borrows light from the central well. Walk from room to room and notice how the floors shift half a level at a time — you are always climbing or descending a few steps, never quite on the same plane. It keeps you moving, aware of your body in space.

The walls are pale, the woodwork honey-colored, the ironwork painted cream and ochre. Horta chose these tones deliberately — he wanted the house to feel warm even on a gray winter afternoon, which in Brussels is most afternoons. Look closely at the mosaic floors. Each room has a different pattern, abstract swirls in soft greens and browns, and the tiles continue seamlessly from the floor up onto the baseboard, erasing the line where wall meets ground. It is a small detail, easy to miss, but it changes the way the room feels — less like a box, more like a shell.

Horta lived here for thirty years, from eighteen-ninety-eight until nineteen-twenty-eight, when he moved to a larger house nearby. He used the ground floor as his studio, the upper floors as his residence. After he died in nineteen-forty-seven, the house sat empty for years, slowly decaying. Developers wanted to demolish it and build apartments. A group of Horta's former students fought to save it, and in nineteen-sixty-nine it opened as a museum, preserved almost exactly as Horta left it — his furniture, his drawings, his books still on the shelves.

The dining room is the heart of the house. A long table sits beneath a skylight, and the walls are lined with built-in cabinets, their glass doors etched with more of those sinuous plant forms. Horta designed everything in this room — the chairs, the light fixtures, the door handles, even the heating grates. He believed a house should be a total work of art, every element in conversation with every other element. Stand in the center and turn slowly. There is not a single straight line in sight.

Upstairs, the bedroom and study are smaller, more intimate. The furniture is simpler here, the ironwork less exuberant. Horta was fifty when he moved in, no longer the young firebrand. He wanted comfort, quiet, a place to think. The study overlooks a small winter garden at the back of the house, a glass-roofed courtyard filled with ferns and palms. It is the only green space on the property, and Horta positioned his desk so he could see it while he worked.

The museum also holds Horta's archive — thousands of drawings, photographs, letters, furniture pieces from his other projects. Some of the chairs and tables came from the Hôtel Tassel, the house that made Horta famous in eighteen-ninety-three. Others came from buildings that no longer exist, demolished in the nineteen-sixties when Brussels was tearing down its Art Nouveau heritage to make way for modernist office blocks. The museum is a record of what was lost, and what survived.

Art Nouveau burned bright and brief — by nineteen-ten it was already out of fashion, dismissed as too decorative, too expensive, too tied to the Belle Époque optimism that died in the First World War. Horta himself moved away from it, designing stark geometric buildings in the nineteen-twenties and thirties. But this house on Rue Américaine remains his masterpiece, the purest expression of his belief that architecture should be organic, fluid, alive.

Before you leave, walk back to the staircase one more time. Press your hand against the iron banister. It is cool to the touch, smooth from a century of hands sliding along it. Look up again at the skylight, at the way the afternoon sun catches the curves of the metalwork and throws shadows across the white walls. Horta wanted you to feel this — the weight of the iron, the warmth of the light, the sense that you are standing inside a living thing.
