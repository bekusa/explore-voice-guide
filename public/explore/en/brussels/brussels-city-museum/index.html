The guide

The story

Stand on the Grand Place and look across the wide stone square. On one side rises the tall thin tower of the Town Hall. On the other side stands a building of dark stone, covered in arches, small statues, and pointed peaks. This is the Brussels City Museum. People who speak French call it the Maison du Roi. People who speak Dutch call it the Broodhuis. Both names carry a story.

The Dutch name means the bread house. Long ago, this was the place where bakers came to sell bread. A simple wooden hall stood on this spot in the thirteenth century. Later it was rebuilt in stone. The French name means the king's house, yet no king ever slept inside. The building belonged to the ruler of the land, and over time the grand name stayed.

The building you see today is not the old one. By the nineteenth century, the medieval hall had grown weak and worn. The city chose to rebuild it from the ground up. An architect named Pierre-Victor Jamaer led the work. He shaped it in the Gothic style, with slim towers and long rows of arches. The new building was finished in 1896.

Step inside, and the sound of the square slips away. The museum tells the long story of Brussels. You move through rooms filled with paintings, old maps, and fine woven cloth. Large tapestries hang on the walls, made by hand many centuries ago. Pieces of carved stone rest in quiet corners, saved from older buildings that no longer stand in the city.

One small statue draws a steady crowd. It is the first Manneken Pis. You may know him as the little boy at a street fountain close by, who pours water without rest. The figure standing at the fountain now is a copy. The true old statue lives here, kept safe inside these walls, far from the weather and from thieves.

Near the boy you find his clothes. Over the years, the little statue has been given hundreds of costumes. Cities, clubs, and travelers from far away have sent outfits as gifts. He has worn the dress of soldiers, kings, farmers, and saints. A whole room holds this wardrobe, and the bright colors fill the air around you.

Climb to the upper floor, and the museum offers something more. Look out through the window toward the Town Hall across the square. From this height, you see the carved stone of the Grand Place in fresh detail. The open space below was once a busy market. Carts, sellers, and thick crowds filled it from morning to night.

The Grand Place around you is protected as a site of world value. Its ring of guild houses, each with gold trim and a proud front, was raised by the trade groups of the city. Bakers, brewers, and boatmen each built their own house here. Together they form one of the most complete old squares left in Europe today.

The museum sits in the center of all this, holding the memory of the square. Through its windows and its rooms, you can trace how Brussels grew from a small river town into the heart of a nation. Models of the old city show narrow streets that have since changed their shape or vanished from the map.

As you walk, notice how the building itself feels like part of the story. The pointed windows, the narrow stairs, the worn stone under your feet. It was built to look old even when it was new, so that it would match the square it faces. The past and a careful copy of the past stand side by side in this one place.

There is a gentle lesson in the bread house name. This corner of the square has always been about daily life. First bread, then trade, then memory. Ordinary people passed through here for hundreds of years. The museum simply gathers their traces and sets them where you can see them now.

When you step back outside, the square opens around you once more. The light moves slowly across the gold trim of the houses. The thin tower of the Town Hall points up to the sky. And somewhere inside the dark stone building behind you, a small bronze boy stands quietly among his many coats.

Take one slow look before you go. The Grand Place has watched markets, fires, parades, and plain quiet mornings like this one. The Brussels City Museum keeps all of those moments, so the square is never only what you see today. Stand still for a breath, and let the old square hold you a little longer.
