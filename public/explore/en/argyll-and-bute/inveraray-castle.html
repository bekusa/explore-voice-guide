The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water laps against the stones of Loch Fyne. Gravel shifts under your feet. The air carries a mix of salt, cut grass and damp woodland. In front of you rises a building that looks as if it came out of a storybook: four round corner towers, each topped with a pointed slate cap, and walls of an unusual greenish-grey stone. This is Inveraray Castle, and it is a lived-in family home as well as a visitor attraction.

For centuries this place has been tied to one family, the Campbells. The castle is the seat of the Duke of Argyll, and the Duke is also chief of Clan Campbell. That is one of the most powerful names in Scottish history. The Campbells rose through politics, law and alliances with the Crown. They often sided with the government while other Highland clans rebelled. As a result, many Scots admired them and many others resented them, and both feelings still exist today.

Here is the first surprise. The castle you see is not medieval at all. Its towers and battlements suggest a fortress from the age of sword and siege. But building work began in the 1740s, under the third Duke of Argyll. By then, Highland warfare was coming to an end. The battlements are decoration, not defence. The design is an early example of Gothic Revival, a style that borrowed the look of the Middle Ages for buildings of a more peaceful era.

The main architect was Roger Morris, an English architect. William Adam, one of the leading Scottish builders of his day, worked on the project too. Later in the eighteenth century, the architect Robert Mylne shaped much of the interior for the fifth Duke. So several generations of skill went into this building. Look at the stone again. It is a local stone with a soft green tint. On a wet day it darkens, and the whole castle takes on a cooler, deeper colour.

Now the second surprise, and it is a big one. To build this castle, the Duke moved an entire town. The old settlement of Inveraray stood close to the earlier castle. The Duke wanted space and a clear view. So a new town was planned and built along the shore of the loch. Those neat white houses you passed on your way in belong to that planned town. It is one of the best surviving examples of a Georgian planned town in Scotland, and the castle and town were designed to be seen together.

Look up at those pointed roofs on the towers. They were not part of the original design. In 1877, a serious fire damaged the castle. During the repairs that followed, a further storey was added and the towers received their conical caps. Many visitors assume these roofs are the oldest part of the building. In fact, they are among the newest. The fairy-tale outline that fills so many photographs comes from the Victorian rebuilding.

Step inside, and the scale changes. The Armoury Hall is the room most people remember. Its walls rise very high, and they are covered with weapons arranged in bold patterns: muskets, broadswords, axes and pikes fan out across the surfaces in circles and stars. These are not just decoration. Many of these weapons connect to the troubled years of the eighteenth century, when the Campbells supported the government against the Jacobite risings. The Jacobites wanted to return the exiled Stuart kings to the throne.

The mood is very different in the State Dining Room and the Tapestry Drawing Room. Here the walls carry painted decoration in soft colours, with fine plasterwork and gilded detail. The tapestries came from the French workshops at Beauvais. This French influence shows that the Dukes of Argyll moved in the highest circles of European taste. Take a moment to compare these elegant rooms with the Armoury Hall. Both sides of the family story sit under one roof: the warrior clan and the polished aristocrats.

Among the objects on display, look for items linked to Rob Roy MacGregor. He was an outlaw and cattle dealer who became a folk hero, partly through the novels of Sir Walter Scott. The relationship between the MacGregors and the Campbells was often hostile. So it is a quiet irony that pieces connected to Rob Roy now sit inside the Campbell family home. The Clan Room upstairs also traces the wider Campbell family tree, which spread across Scotland and far beyond it.

Every old castle seems to have a ghost, and Inveraray is no exception. According to local legend, a young harpist haunts the building. Some versions of the tale say he was killed in the seventeenth century, during the long conflicts between the clans. Locals say that faint harp music sometimes drifts through the rooms. Treat it as a story, not as history. Still, it shows how the people of this area remember those violent years through music and memory.

The castle has also had a life on screen. In 2012, it appeared in a Christmas special of the television drama Downton Abbey. In that episode it played Duneagle Castle, the Scottish home of the Crawley family's relatives. Because of this, some visitors arrive knowing the building from television before they know its history. If that is your story too, you are in good company. The real history, however, is older and more complicated than any script.

When you go back outside, give the grounds some time. The gardens open out in lawns and long paths, with mature trees and views back to the castle. On the wooded hill above stands a small stone watchtower known as Dun na Cuaiche. A steep path climbs through the trees to reach it. From the top, the castle, the town and the long arm of Loch Fyne lie spread out below. The view shows clearly how the whole landscape was planned around this one house.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look at the castle once more. Notice how the green stone changes as clouds pass over the loch. Notice the white town, the dark hills and the grey water, all set out in careful order. This building is less a fortress than a statement about power, taste and family identity, made in stone. As you walk away, listen again for the water against the shore... and take that quiet sound with you.
