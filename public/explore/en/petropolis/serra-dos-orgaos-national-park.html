The guide

The story

The air changes first. As the road climbs out of Petrópolis, the heavy warmth of the coast falls away. A cooler, damp breath comes down from the forest. It smells of wet soil, moss, and leaves that never quite dry. Somewhere above you, water drips from a branch onto a broad leaf. Then a bird calls once and goes quiet. This is Serra dos Órgãos National Park, and you have just stepped onto one of its edges.

The name means the Organ Mountains. The tradition says that early Portuguese sailors gave it to them. From the waters of Guanabara Bay, they looked north at a line of tall granite spires. To them, the rock looked like the pipes of a church organ standing in a row. The story may be part history and part legend. Look at the ridgeline on a clear day and the comparison still makes sense.

The park was created in 1939. That makes it one of the oldest national parks in Brazil, from the first years when the country began to protect land on a national scale. It does not belong to one town. Its territory crosses several municipalities, including Petrópolis, Teresópolis, Guapimirim, and Magé. Each town has its own doorway into the same mountains. Here on the Petrópolis side, the entry feels quieter and more intimate than the busier gate at Teresópolis.

Around you is the Atlantic Forest, called Mata Atlântica in Portuguese. This forest once ran along much of Brazil's coast. Today only a small part of it survives. That makes stretches like this one very important for conservation. The forest here climbs in layers. Low down, the trees are tall and crowded with climbing plants. Higher up, the trunks grow shorter and twisted. Near the summits, the forest opens into rocky grasslands, wind, and sky.

Take a moment to look closely at the tree bark. Many branches carry passengers. Bromeliads sit in the forks like small green cups, and they often hold water. Orchids, ferns, and mosses cover almost every surface. These plants do not take food from the tree. They simply use it as a place to live, high up where there is more light. In a single tree, you may count dozens of different plants without really trying.

Now for something many visitors miss. Some of the most remarkable animals in this park are very small. The mountains of this region are home to tiny frogs, some no bigger than a fingernail. Many live among the fallen leaves and inside the water of bromeliads. You will probably hear frogs before you ever see one. After rain, the forest floor can fill with clicks and whistles. Much of that sound comes from creatures you could hold on a coin.

The park's most famous shape stands on the Teresópolis side. It is a single, narrow tower of rock called Dedo de Deus, which means God's Finger. It points straight up, with a slight bend near the top, like a hand raised toward the sky. The peak appears on the coat of arms of Rio de Janeiro state. For many Brazilians, it is the image of these mountains. On a clear day, you can see it from roads and viewpoints far away.

God's Finger also holds a place in sporting history. In 1912, a group of local men from Teresópolis reached its summit. They had no modern climbing equipment. Many Brazilian climbers see that ascent as the beginning of mountaineering in their country. Today, the park is still a training ground for climbers. On some walls, you may notice small figures with ropes and bright helmets, moving slowly across the grey granite.

From Petrópolis begins one of Brazil's best-known mountain walks, the traverse from Petrópolis to Teresópolis. It crosses the high ridges of the park, from one town to the other. Most hikers take about three days. They sleep in mountain shelters or in tents at the camping areas. The route is serious. There are steep climbs, exposed rock, and sudden changes in weather. Fog can cover the trail in minutes. Many people choose to walk it with an experienced guide.

Along the way, hikers pass some of the park's strangest landscapes. Near the high area called Açu, wind and water have shaped the granite into rounded towers and stacked blocks. People call them the Castles of Açu, because from a distance they look like ruined forts. The highest point in the park is Pedra do Sino, the Bell Rock, at more than two thousand metres. From these heights, people on a clear morning say they can see as far as Guanabara Bay.

It helps to remember the city you came from. Petrópolis grew in the nineteenth century as a summer retreat for the Brazilian imperial family. The emperor and the court came up here to escape the heat and disease of Rio de Janeiro in summer. The same cool mountain air you feel now drew them here. The forest that surrounds the old palaces and the forest of the park belong to the same green mountain range.

You do not need to cross the whole range to feel this place. Shorter trails near the entrance follow streams and pass small waterfalls and natural pools. The water runs clear and very cold, straight from the mountains. Stand still beside a stream for a minute. Listen to the water moving over the stones. Watch how light comes through the leaves in thin, moving patches. In the afternoon, clouds often gather on the peaks, and the forest grows darker and softer.

Before you leave, look up once more at the ridgeline. Those rock towers were here long before the sailors named them, and long before the emperor built his summer city. Notice the smell of the damp earth, the cool air on your skin, and the many shades of green around you. As you walk back toward the road, listen for one more bird call, or one more small frog in the leaves... the forest is still talking quietly behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start early, because afternoon clouds often cover the high peaks.
Bring a rain jacket even when the morning looks clear.
Wear shoes with good grip for wet rock and roots.
Hire an experienced guide for the full mountain traverse.
Book mountain shelters in advance for overnight hikes.
