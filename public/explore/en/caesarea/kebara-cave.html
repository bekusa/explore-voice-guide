The guide

The story

Stop for a moment at the mouth of the cave. The air changes here. Outside, the Mediterranean sun warms the pale limestone and the scrub smells of sage and dry earth. Step into the shade and the temperature drops, the light turns grey, and the sound of the wind fades. Your footsteps land on soft, dusty ground. This quiet hollow on the western slope of Mount Carmel has sheltered people for tens of thousands of years.

Welcome to Kebara Cave. It sits a short drive north of Caesarea, above the flat coastal plain that runs down to the sea. In Arabic it is known as Mugharat el-Kebara. To a casual walker, it looks like many other caves in this limestone ridge. A wide opening, a rocky floor, a ceiling darkened by time. But for scientists who study human origins, this place is one of the most important sites in the region.

Why does it matter so much? The answer lies in the soil under your feet. Over a very long period, people came here, lit fires, ate, slept, and left. Each visit added a thin layer of ash, bone, and stone tools. Those layers piled up, one on top of another, like pages in a closed book. Archaeologists have spent decades reading those pages, centimetre by centimetre, from the most recent visitors down to the oldest.

The first major excavation took place in the early 1930s. The British archaeologist Francis Turville-Petre dug here and found material from the Natufians. The Natufians were hunter-gatherers who lived in this region at the end of the last Ice Age. Many researchers see them as the people on the edge of farming. They began to settle in one place for longer periods, and some of their habits pointed toward the first villages. Kebara added important evidence to that story.

But the deeper layers held something older. Below the Natufian material lay the remains of people who used a very different style of stone tool. Archaeologists call this style Mousterian. It is a tool-making tradition linked mainly with Neanderthals, the close relatives of modern humans who lived across Europe and western Asia. Here, on this ridge above the sea, Neanderthals came back again and again. They left behind flakes of flint, animal bones, and many hearths.

Now for the discovery that made Kebara known in laboratories around the world. In 1983, a team led by Ofer Bar-Yosef and Bernard Vandermeersch uncovered a Neanderthal burial. The skeleton belonged to an adult man. Researchers gave him the formal name Kebara 2, and a friendly nickname too — Moshe. He lived roughly sixty thousand years ago. His body had been laid in a pit dug into the floor of this cave.

Moshe's skeleton is unusual. His skull is missing, and so are most of his legs. Yet his ribs, spine, and pelvis survived in remarkable condition. It is one of the most complete Neanderthal upper bodies ever found. Here is the detail that surprises most visitors. Some researchers think the skull was removed after the body had decayed — perhaps by other people, for reasons we can only guess. The evidence is debated, but the absence is real.

The second surprise is very small. Among Moshe's bones was a hyoid — a delicate, horseshoe-shaped bone in the throat that supports the tongue. Hyoid bones rarely survive. This was the first Neanderthal hyoid ever found, and it looks very similar to our own. That single bone fed a long scientific argument. Could Neanderthals speak? The hyoid alone cannot answer the question. But it showed that their throat anatomy was not as different from ours as some had assumed.

Moshe was not the only Neanderthal found here. An earlier excavation had turned up the remains of a Neanderthal infant. Together, these finds make it clear that this cave was more than a passing shelter. It was a place where people lived as groups, cared for their young, and dealt with their dead. Look at the floor near the entrance and try to picture a small band gathered around a fire, the smoke drifting out toward the plain.

Those fires left a deep record. Archaeologists found many hearths stacked through the layers, along with burnt bones of animals such as gazelle and deer. Charred plant remains suggest that the people here also collected seeds and wild legumes. This matters because it challenges an old image of Neanderthals as hunters who ate only meat. At Kebara, the diet looks broader and more flexible, shaped by what this landscape offered season by season.

Now turn around and look out from the cave mouth. Imagine the view without the fields, roads, and fishponds. During much of the Ice Age, sea levels were lower, and the coastline lay further west than it does today. A wide plain stretched toward the water. Herds moved across it. From this height, people could watch the animals, read the weather, and stay above the damp ground. The location was a practical choice, not an accident.

Kebara also belongs to a wider story. Mount Carmel holds several caves with deep prehistoric layers. Across this ridge, researchers have found traces of both Neanderthals and early modern humans. The two groups may have used this region at different times, perhaps over many thousands of years. Kebara is one piece of that puzzle. It helps scientists ask a big question. How did different kinds of humans share, or take turns in, the same Mediterranean landscape?

Before you leave, take one more slow look around. The excavation trenches are quiet now. The walls still carry dark patches of old soot. Somewhere beneath this floor, layers remain that no one has dug yet, kept for future scientists with better tools. As you walk back into the sunlight and the smell of the scrub, remember that you are following a path people used long before there were towns, or farms, or even a Caesarea.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the rocky slope up to the cave.
Bring water and sun protection for the exposed approach trail.
Carry a small torch to see the darker back walls.
Check current trail access before driving up from Caesarea.
Visit in early morning for cooler air on the climb.
