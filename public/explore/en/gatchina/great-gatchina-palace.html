The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes on the wide parade ground. Then look up at the walls. They are not painted plaster, like so many Russian palaces. They are pale, porous stone, grey with a hint of honey. The surface is pitted and rough, almost like a sponge. On a damp day the colour darkens and the whole building seems to change its mood. This is the Great Gatchina Palace. It stands roughly forty-five kilometres south of Saint Petersburg, and it feels very different from the gold and turquoise palaces closer to the city.

That stone is the first surprise. It is called Pudost limestone, after a village not far from here. It was soft enough to cut easily when freshly quarried, and it hardened as it dried. Builders liked it for that reason. Visitors often expect a fairy-tale facade in the Russian imperial style. Instead, they find something closer to an English country house or a fortified castle. Keep that impression in mind. It matters to the story of the people who lived here.

The palace began as a gift of love and power. In the 1760s, Empress Catherine the Second, known as Catherine the Great, gave the Gatchina estate to Count Grigory Orlov. Orlov was her favourite, and he had helped bring her to the throne. He commissioned the Italian architect Antonio Rinaldi to design a residence here. Construction ran through the 1770s and into the early 1780s. Rinaldi gave the building its restrained, almost severe outline, with towers at each end.

Look at those towers. They give the palace a slightly military look, as if it guards the land around it. Orlov was a soldier and a hunter, and the estate suited him. The surrounding forest was full of game. The building sits on a low rise above the water, with long views across the park. It was designed less for grand court ceremony and more as a private retreat. That private character never fully left this place.

Orlov died in 1783. Catherine then bought the estate back and gave it to her son, Grand Duke Paul. This is where the palace changes its personality. Paul and his mother had a famously cold relationship. He spent years waiting for the throne, far from her court. Gatchina became his own small world. Here he could drill soldiers, design uniforms, and run things exactly as he wished.

Under Paul, the architect Vincenzo Brenna reworked and enlarged the palace in the 1790s. He added and rebuilt the side wings and the courtyards. The result is the long, horseshoe-like shape you see today. The palace and its wings embrace the parade ground. When you stand here, you are standing where Paul watched his troops march. Many people remember him as rigid and suspicious. At Gatchina, you can see that character written in stone and gravel.

In 1796, Catherine died and Paul became emperor. His reign was short. He was killed in a palace plot in Saint Petersburg in 1801. But Gatchina stayed linked to his name. In front of the palace stands a bronze statue of Paul the First, added in the mid nineteenth century. He stands in uniform, facing the parade ground, as if he is still inspecting the ranks. Take a moment to look at his posture. It is stiff, formal, and quite deliberate.

Here is the second surprise. Behind the formal rooms, the palace hides a secret route. An underground passage runs from inside the building down to the shore of the Silver Lake. It ends at a grotto near the water. Guides often call it the Echo Grotto, because a voice carries strangely there. Popular stories say it was an escape route for a nervous ruler. That is more legend than certainty. It may simply have been a garden feature, built for pleasure and surprise.

Nearly a century after Paul, the palace found another resident who valued privacy and security. Emperor Alexander the Third came to the throne in 1881, after his father was assassinated in Saint Petersburg. Alexander chose Gatchina as his main home. The thick stone walls and the distance from the capital made sense to him. He lived simply here, in modest rooms. His children grew up playing in this park and rowing on these lakes.

The twentieth century was brutal to the palace. After the revolution of 1917, it became a museum. Then, during the Second World War, German forces occupied Gatchina from 1941 to early 1944. Before the occupation, museum staff evacuated part of the collection. Much else was lost. When the occupying forces retreated, the palace was set on fire. The interiors burned, and for decades the building stood as a damaged shell.

Restoration has been slow and careful. For many years after the war, the building served other purposes, and full repair was postponed. Work on the museum began again in the later decades of the twentieth century. Some halls have now been restored, with their parquet floors, painted ceilings, and silk walls brought back from photographs and drawings. Other parts are still under repair. As you walk through, notice the difference. The restored rooms show what was lost, and the plain rooms show how much work remains.

Outside, the park is half of the experience. It is laid out in the English landscape style. That means winding paths, open lawns, and water that looks natural rather than geometric. The Silver Lake and the White Lake reflect the palace and the trees. The water in the Silver Lake is famously clear and cold, fed by springs. On a still morning you can see weed and stones on the bottom. Small bridges, pavilions, and islands appear as you walk.

The palace and park belong to the UNESCO World Heritage site covering the historic centre of Saint Petersburg and its related monuments. But Gatchina often feels quieter than the bigger palaces near the city. Fewer tour buses arrive here. You may find yourself almost alone on a path by the lake. That quiet is part of the place. It suits a palace built for a hunter, a lonely heir, and a cautious emperor.

Before you leave, turn back toward the facade once more. Notice the grey stone again, and how the light sits on its rough surface. Listen for the birds over the water, and the crunch of gravel as other visitors cross the parade ground. Three very different rulers looked out from those windows. Each of them wanted something from this building... a refuge, a stage, a fortress. Take a slow walk along the lake, and let the palace grow smaller behind you.

Practical tips

Walk the park after the palace to see both sides.
Visit on a still morning for clear views into Silver Lake.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel paths and park trails.
Check which restored halls are open before planning your route.
Allow extra time for the underground passage to the lake.
