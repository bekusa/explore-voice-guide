The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the Rhine here at Rees and listen for a moment. Barges pass with a low, steady hum. Water slaps against the stones of the embankment. The air smells of river mud and wet grass, and the land on the far side stretches out flat and green toward a wide sky. It is a calm scene. Yet for several centuries, this broad, slow river was one of the most heavily watched borders in the Roman world.

That border has a name today: the Lower Germanic Limes. The word limes, pronounced lee-mess, is Latin. Here it means the frontier line of the Roman Empire. Along this northern stretch, Rome did not build a great continuous wall like Hadrian's Wall in Britain. Instead, the river itself became the wall. Forts, watchtowers, roads and harbours lined the riverbank, and soldiers watched the water and the land beyond it.

In 2021, UNESCO added the Lower Germanic Limes to its World Heritage List. It is a shared listing between Germany and the Netherlands. The protected sites follow the Rhine for hundreds of kilometres, from the hills south of Bonn all the way to the North Sea coast in the Netherlands. Rees sits roughly in the middle of this long chain. So the river in front of you connects you to a frontier that once ran from the hills to the sea.

Here is the first surprise. If you came looking for tall Roman ruins, you will not find them here. Most of this World Heritage Site lies underground. It survives as foundations, ditches, post holes, coins and broken pottery under fields, meadows and town streets. Archaeologists found many of these sites through excavation, through aerial photographs, and through marks that appear in crops during dry summers. The heritage is real, but it is mostly invisible to the eye.

So how did this frontier work? Rome reached the Rhine under Julius Caesar and then under the first emperor, Augustus, in the late first century before Christ. Roman generals tried to push further east, deep into the lands of the Germanic tribes. That ambition suffered a terrible blow in the year 9, when three legions were destroyed in a forest battle far to the east. After that, the Rhine became the long-term edge of Roman power in this region.

Over the following decades, the frontier took shape. Large legionary fortresses held thousands of soldiers. Smaller forts held auxiliary units, which were troops recruited from across the empire. Between them stood watchtowers. A road ran along the riverbank and linked everything together. On the water, the Roman fleet patrolled with ships. Messages, supplies and soldiers moved up and down this line, and the river was both a barrier and a highway.

The nearest major Roman centre lies a short distance upstream, at Xanten. There, the Romans kept a legionary fortress called Vetera, and later they founded a city on the site. Today Xanten has an archaeological park where you can see reconstructed buildings. Downstream lie other Roman sites in the region of Kalkar and across the Dutch border. Rees sits between these places, in a landscape that soldiers once crossed on foot, on horseback and by boat.

Now for the second surprise. The Rhine you see today does not flow exactly where the Roman Rhine flowed. Over nearly two thousand years, the river has shifted its course again and again. It cut new bends and abandoned old ones. Some Roman sites that once stood on the bank now lie far from the water. Others have been washed away completely. Look at the curves and old channels in the flat land around Rees. They show how restless this river has always been.

It helps to think about the two sides of the water. On one side was the Roman province, with its roads, coins, wine, bathhouses and Latin inscriptions. On the other side lived Germanic peoples with their own farms, villages and traditions. Yet the border was never completely closed. People traded across it. Germanic men joined the Roman army. Goods, ideas and families moved back and forth. This frontier was a line of control, but it was also a meeting place.

Life here was not easy for the soldiers. The climate was damp and cold compared to the Mediterranean. Floods could damage forts and roads. Wood rotted, so many early structures had to be rebuilt, often later in stone. Archaeologists have found the everyday traces of these men: shoe nails, buckles, writing tablets, animal bones from their meals. These small objects show how ordinary people lived on the edge of an empire.

The frontier did not last forever. During the third century, the empire faced crises, and raids across the Rhine became more frequent. The Romans rebuilt and strengthened their defences. But by the fifth century, Roman control along the Lower Rhine had faded away. Frankish groups and other peoples settled in the region. The forts fell out of use, and the river slowly covered, buried and reshaped what remained.

The town of Rees grew up much later, in the Middle Ages. Walk along the riverside promenade and you can see parts of the old town walls and fortifications from that later period. So in one view, you have layers of defence from very different centuries. Medieval walls stand above the water, and an older Roman frontier lies hidden in the wider landscape. The same river shaped both, because the Rhine has always been a line people wanted to guard.

If you want to see Roman objects up close, the museums in this region are the best place. Xanten, in particular, displays finds from the Lower Rhine frontier and helps you picture the forts and ships. But do not underestimate the value of simply standing here. The UNESCO listing protects not only objects, but a whole frontier landscape. The flat land, the wide sky, the river and its old channels are all part of the story.

Before you walk on, look once more at the water. Watch a barge move slowly past, carrying cargo just as Roman ships once carried grain and soldiers. Notice the far bank, low and green. Feel how open this landscape is, and how far you can see. Roman sentries looked across this same kind of view, watching for movement. Today the river is calm, and the border is only a memory. But the line is still here, running quietly beneath your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the Rees riverside promenade for wide views of the Rhine.
Visit Xanten's archaeological park to see reconstructed Roman buildings.
Bring a windproof jacket; the open riverbank can feel chilly.
Come in the early morning for a quiet, misty river atmosphere.
Combine Rees with a cycling route along the Lower Rhine.
