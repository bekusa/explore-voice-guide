The story

Take off your shoes here and you will notice the sand immediately. It is coarse. The grains are large and pale, closer to fine gravel than to powder, and they make a soft crunching sound as you walk. Brush your hand across your leg and the sand falls straight off. Almost none of it sticks. The surface is warm on top and cooler a few centimetres down. Small waves arrive at a slow, regular pace, then draw back through the loose grains with a hiss.

Look east along the shore. The beach runs in a long shallow curve and ends against a wall of grey rock — the headland that carries the old fortress. That silhouette will follow you all afternoon. Turn the other way and the coastline continues west, backed by apartment blocks, palms and a promenade. Behind everything, the Taurus Mountains rise close to the shore. Very few beaches put open sea, a fortress and a mountain range into the same view.

The sand deserves a moment of attention. Coarse grains drain quickly, so this beach does not turn into hot dust in the wind. It also means the shoreline shelves more sharply in places than the fine-sand beaches further west. There is a persistent local story that the sand was shipped here from Egypt as a gift. Treat that as folklore. Beaches with coarse grains occur naturally where the coast is backed by mountains and short, fast rivers.

Now the name. Tradition says Cleopatra swam at this beach, and that Mark Antony gave her this coast as a present. The romance is folklore. The politics underneath it is not. Antony did hand Cleopatra control of territories along this southern Turkish coastline. The reason was practical rather than sentimental. These mountains held excellent shipbuilding timber, and Egypt had almost none. Whoever controlled these forests could build a fleet. That is a less charming story, and a more likely one.

The water is the main event. On a calm morning it is clear enough to see your own feet and the ripples in the sand beneath them. The colour moves from pale green in the shallows to a deeper blue further out, with darker patches where the seabed changes. The Mediterranean here stays warm well into autumn. Many regular visitors say the best swimming months are not the peak of summer at all, but the weeks on either side.

This beach is organised, and that suits some travellers and irritates others. There are rows of loungers and umbrellas for rent, showers, changing cabins and lifeguards through the season. It has held Blue Flag status, an international award given for water quality, safety and facilities. If you want an empty stretch of sand, this is not that place. If you want to swim, then sit down and have something cold brought to you, it works very well.

At the eastern end of the beach, close to the rock, there is a cave worth an hour of your time. It was found in the 1940s during quarrying work, when blasting opened a chamber full of stalactites and stalagmites. The air inside is humid and cool, with a high level of carbon dioxide and very little dust. People with asthma have come here for decades to sit inside and breathe. Whatever the medical view, the temperature change alone is a relief.

Alanya was a working town before it was a resort. Look inland from the beach and you can still see traces of it — greenhouses, banana plantations on the lower slopes, citrus groves. Bananas grown on this coast are shorter and sweeter than imported ones, and you will see them sold at roadside stalls with the fruit still on the stem. The town also has a long history of small boatbuilding, carried on in yards near the harbour on the other side of the rock.

Pay attention to the wind pattern. Mornings here are often glassy and still. By early afternoon a breeze usually comes in off the sea, the surface roughens, and small waves build along the shore. It is not dangerous on ordinary days, but it changes the swimming completely. If you want flat water and clear visibility, get in before noon. If you like being knocked about by waves, the afternoon will oblige you.

Because the beach faces roughly west, it gets the sunset. The light drops toward the water, the mountains behind go from grey to violet, and the fortress rock turns into a black outline. People gather along the promenade for this without any organisation. Swimmers stay in the water later than they meant to. The sand holds its heat for a while after the sun goes, which makes sitting on it in the evening surprisingly comfortable.

After dark the beach and the promenade change character rather than emptying. Families walk. Vendors sell roasted corn and ice cream. Fishermen set up along the rocks at the eastern end with long rods and small lamps. The mood is calm rather than loud. In Turkish coastal towns the evening walk is a social event with its own rhythm, and joining it costs nothing except the price of a tea somewhere along the way.

Before you go, walk to the water's edge one last time and stand where the waves reach your ankles. Look east at the rock, then out at the horizon, then down at the coarse pale sand around your feet. Two thousand years of ships, harbours and quarrels happened along this shoreline, and none of it changed the sound the water makes on this exact stretch. Stand there for one full minute. Then pick up your shoes and go.
