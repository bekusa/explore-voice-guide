The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, water runs over stone, a steady rush that never quite stops. Above it, if you wait long enough, comes another sound: a low rumble, then the squeal of steel wheels on a curve. A red train slides out of the forest, crosses a line of pale stone arches high above the river, and disappears straight into the face of a cliff. That is the Landwasser Viaduct, and you have come to the right place to watch it.

A quick note on where you are. The viaduct does not stand in Arosa itself. Arosa sits at the end of its own valley, with its own railway line down to Chur. The Landwasser Viaduct lies in a neighbouring part of the canton of Graubünden, close to the village of Filisur, on the Albula line. Many visitors base themselves in Arosa and make the trip by train, changing in Chur. It is a fair journey, and this view makes it worth it.

Now look at the shape of the bridge. It is not straight. The viaduct bends in a smooth curve as it crosses the gorge, so the train leans gently as it travels. The bridge carries a single track on six arches, built from local limestone. The stone is grey and cream, with patches of darker weathering where water has run down the piers for more than a century. Nothing about it is decorative. Every block is there to carry weight.

The tallest piers rise roughly sixty-five metres above the river. That is about the height of a twenty-storey building, made of stacked stone. And here is the detail that surprises most people. At the far end, the viaduct does not land on solid ground. It runs directly into a tunnel cut into a sheer rock wall. The bridge and the mountain meet with no gap at all, as if the stone arches simply continue inside the cliff.

The Albula line was built at the very start of the twentieth century. Work on the viaduct took place around 1901 and 1902, and the line opened in stages from 1903. The goal was practical. The Engadine valley, with resorts like Saint Moritz, was hard to reach in winter. A railway through the mountains would bring guests, goods, and mail all year. But the route had to climb steeply and twist through narrow gorges like this one.

The builders faced a real problem here. Raising wooden scaffolding sixty metres high in a river gorge was slow, costly, and risky. So for the tallest piers, they did something clever. They put up iron towers and then built the stone piers around them, layer by layer. Cranes on top of the towers lifted each block into place. In other words, the steel helped build the pier from the inside, and the masonry then closed around it.

This is a metre-gauge railway, which means the rails sit one metre apart. That is narrower than most main lines in Europe. A narrow gauge allows tighter curves and lighter structures, which matters in mountains. It is also why the trains look a little compact. The Rhaetian Railway, the regional operator, still runs regular services across this bridge every day. This is not a museum piece. Commuters, school children, and freight all cross the same arches as the panoramic tourist trains.

In 2008, the Albula and Bernina lines were listed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. UNESCO is the United Nations body that protects places of global cultural value. The listing recognises the whole railway landscape: the viaducts, the spiral tunnels, and the way the line fits into the valleys. The Landwasser Viaduct became the picture most people use to represent that landscape. You have probably seen it on a poster or a postcard before you ever arrived.

The river below gives the bridge its name. The Landwasser flows down from the Davos area and cuts this deep, narrow channel through the rock. Look down at the water. In early summer, after the snow melts, it can run fast and cloudy. Later in the year it turns clearer and quieter. The forest on the slopes is mostly conifer, dark green against the pale stone of the piers. In autumn, the larch trees turn gold.

If you want to see a train cross, be patient. Services on the line are frequent, but not constant. Many people wait at the viewpoint and listen. You often hear the train before you see it, as the sound carries through the gorge. Then it appears, crosses in well under a minute, and the tunnel swallows it. For a moment the gorge goes quiet again, and only the river is left.

There are a few ways to experience the viaduct. One is to walk the marked trail from Filisur, which leads down through the woods to viewing points with a clear side view. Another is to ride across it yourself. From the train, you see very little of the bridge, of course. What you feel is the curve, the drop opening up beside the window, and then the sudden darkness of the tunnel.

The viaduct has needed care over its long life. Water, frost, and the weight of trains all wear on stone. Engineers have restored it in modern times, repairing joints and protecting the structure, while keeping its original form. That is part of what makes it interesting. It is more than a hundred years old, built with cranes, iron, and hand-set stone, and it still does the same job every day.

So take one more look before you go. Follow the curve of the arches from the forest to the cliff. Notice where the newer repairs show slightly lighter than the old stone. Listen for the river under everything. And as you walk away, keep an ear open. Somewhere up the valley, another red train may already be on its way toward this bridge.
