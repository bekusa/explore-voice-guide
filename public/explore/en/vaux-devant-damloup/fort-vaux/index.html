The guide

The story

Underfoot, the ground around Fort Vaux is not flat. It rolls in soft, grassy hollows, one after another, like a sea that froze mid-swell. Each hollow is a shell crater. A century of grass and moss has softened their edges, but the shape remains. The air here is cool and often damp, and the wind moves through the beech and pine trees on the ridge. Stand still for a moment. This quiet hilltop was once one of the most violent places on earth.

You are on the heights east of Verdun, in north-eastern France. Fort Vaux was part of a ring of forts built to guard the city after France lost the war of 1870 against Prussia. The engineer behind this defensive system was General Séré de Rivières, and his forts followed a similar pattern. They were low, buried structures of stone and earth, designed to hold artillery and shelter a garrison. In the early twentieth century, many were strengthened with thick layers of concrete as artillery grew more powerful.

Look at the fort itself. It does not rise up like a castle. Instead, it sits low in the hill, almost hidden. That was the point. A tall wall would be an easy target for heavy guns. So the builders dug down and piled earth on top. What you see now is grey concrete, weathered and stained, with dark openings and scars where shells struck. Compared with its larger neighbour, Fort Douaumont, this is a small fort. Its fame comes from what happened inside.

In February 1916, Germany launched a massive offensive against Verdun. The battle would last for most of that year and become a symbol of the whole war — a long, grinding struggle where hundreds of thousands of men died on both sides. Fort Douaumont, a few kilometres from here, fell to the Germans within days. Fort Vaux became the next obstacle on the eastern side of the line. Early in the battle, heavy German shells pounded the fort and destroyed its gun turret.

By the start of June 1916, the fort was commanded by Major Sylvain Eugène Raynal. He was an officer who had been badly wounded earlier in the war and walked with a cane. He could have stayed away from the front. Instead, he took command of this battered fort. Inside, conditions were already terrible. Along with the regular garrison, the corridors filled with wounded men, stragglers, and soldiers who had sheltered there from the shelling outside.

On 2 June 1916, German troops reached the top of the fort. What followed was one of the strangest battles of the war. The fighting moved underground, into the narrow concrete tunnels. Soldiers built barricades of sandbags in the corridors and fought at very close range, sometimes only a few metres apart. The Germans used grenades, gas, and flamethrowers. The French defenders held their barricades in darkness, smoke, and dust, for day after day.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The worst enemy inside the fort was not the German army. It was thirst. The fort depended on a water cistern, and the supply ran out far faster than the defenders expected. Accounts of the siege describe men licking moisture from the damp walls. In the heat of early summer, with smoke and gas filling the corridors, the lack of water became unbearable. Soldiers could fight, but they could not survive without something to drink.

The fort was cut off. Telephone lines were gone, and runners could not get through the shelling. So Raynal used the last option he had — carrier pigeons. The final bird was released on 4 June, carrying an urgent message asking for help and describing the gas attacks. That pigeon, known as Le Vaillant, which means "the brave one", reached its loft at Verdun. It died shortly after arriving, apparently poisoned by the gas. France later honoured the bird officially, and a plaque at the fort remembers it.

Relief attempts did come. French troops tried several times to break through to the fort, but the German fire was too strong. On 7 June, after about a week of underground fighting, Raynal surrendered. He did it because his men had no water left. The German side recognised the courage of the defence. Crown Prince Wilhelm, who commanded the German army at Verdun, received Raynal and, according to the well-known account, gave him a sword as a mark of respect.

The story did not end there. Through the summer and autumn of 1916, the French slowly pushed back. In late October, they recaptured Fort Douaumont. Soon after, in early November, the Germans left Fort Vaux, and French troops moved back in. After all those months, and all those lives, the front line returned to almost where it had started. That is perhaps the hardest truth of Verdun. So much was lost to move so little.

When you go inside, notice how the temperature drops. The corridors are cool, close, and quiet. The concrete walls carry marks of the fighting, and the passages feel very narrow, even when they are empty. Try to picture them full of men, smoke, and noise, lit only by lamps. In some places you can see where the barricades stood. The spaces are small enough that you begin to understand how a few metres of corridor could be defended for days.

Outside, take time to read the plaques on the walls of the fort. They remember the defenders, the pigeon, and the units that fought here. Then walk a little way across the surrounding ground. The woods around Vaux grew back after the war, but the landscape still shows the battle. The craters, the broken concrete, and the trenches in the forest are reminders of how many shells fell on these few square kilometres. Much of this area was left as forest because farming was no longer possible.

As you leave, listen again to the wind in the trees. Notice the birdsong — and remember that one small bird once carried the last hope of the men inside these walls. Look back at the low grey shape of the fort, sitting quietly in its hill. It was built to be hard to see. Today it is easy to walk past. Take one more moment here before you go, on this soft, uneven ground.
