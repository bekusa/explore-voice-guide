The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The forest here is never silent. There is the dry rattle of bamboo in the wind, the sharp call of a bird you cannot see, and somewhere far off, a branch snapping under real weight. The air smells of damp leaves and warm earth. Light falls through the canopy in narrow shafts. Welcome to Anamalai Tiger Reserve, high in the Western Ghats of southern India.

The name gives you the first clue to this place. In Tamil, "aanai" means elephant and "malai" means hill. So Anamalai is, quite simply, the Elephant Hills. It is an honest name. Wild Asian elephants still move through these slopes in family herds. They follow old routes between water and grazing ground. If you see fresh dung on the road, or a broken branch at shoulder height, an elephant has likely passed not long ago.

The reserve sits on the border between two Indian states. On this side lies Tamil Nadu, in Coimbatore and Tiruppur districts. Across the ridge lies Kerala. There, the forest continues into Parambikulam Tiger Reserve and other protected land. Animals do not know these lines on the map. Together, these forests form one of the larger connected wild landscapes in the Western Ghats. This mountain chain runs down India's western side and is recognised for its unusual number of species found nowhere else.

For much of the twentieth century, this area carried a different name. It was known as the Indira Gandhi Wildlife Sanctuary and National Park. In 2007 it was declared a tiger reserve. That status brought stricter protection and closer monitoring under India's national tiger programme. Tigers live here, but they are rarely seen. They are shy, and the forest is dense. Most visitors leave without a sighting... and that is completely normal.

Most visitors arrive through Topslip, the main visitor hub of the reserve. It sits on a ridge above the plains near the town of Pollachi. The name sounds oddly English, and there is a reason. The story goes that during the colonial period, cut teak logs were slid down the hillside from the top. So the place became known as Topslip. Whether every detail of that tale is exact, nobody can say for certain. But the link to timber is real.

That timber history is the first surprise for many visitors. In the nineteenth century, British administrators valued these forests mainly for teak. Teak is a heavy, oily hardwood that resists rot and insects. It was ideal for ships, railway sleepers, and buildings. Large areas were logged, and teak plantations were later planted in neat rows. Look closely as you travel, and you may notice stands of trees that grow in straight lines. That order is human, not natural.

Today, the reserve holds many kinds of forest. Low down, you find dry deciduous woods that drop their leaves in the hot season. Higher up, the forest turns evergreen, wet, and thick with moss. At the very top, on places like the Grass Hills, rolling grasslands mix with small, dark patches of forest. Locally these patches are called sholas. The change from one forest type to the next can happen within a short drive.

The animal life here is wide. Gaur, the large wild cattle of India, graze at forest edges. They are dark brown, with pale lower legs that look like white socks. Spotted deer and sambar move through the undergrowth. Leopards and dholes, the wild dogs of Asia, also hunt here. In the trees, you may hear the loud, laughing call of the great hornbill. Its huge yellow and black bill is unmistakable once you see it.

Here is the second surprise. One of the rarest monkeys in India lives in these hills. It is the lion-tailed macaque. It has black fur, a silver-grey mane around its face, and a tufted tail like a small lion's. It depends on the evergreen rainforest. Some groups live in forest fragments near the Valparai plateau, between tea and coffee estates. There, people sometimes see them crossing the road. Please watch them quietly, and never feed them.

The forest is not only a home for animals. Several Indigenous communities have lived in these hills for generations. They include the Kadar, the Malasar, the Muduvar, and others. Their knowledge of plants, tracks, and seasons is deep. Many work with the forest department today as trackers, guides, and elephant handlers. When a local guide stops suddenly and points at the ground, trust them. They often read signs that visitors would walk straight past.

Near Topslip is the Kozhikamuthi elephant camp. Here, trained elephants are cared for by mahouts, the men who handle and live alongside them. Many of these mahouts come from the Malasar community. If your visit allows, you may see the elephants being bathed or fed. Watch how the mahouts speak to their animals. The bond is built over many years. It is one of the quieter, more human scenes in the reserve.

Visiting here is different from visiting a city park. Movement inside the core forest is controlled by the forest department. Safaris and treks usually run with official vehicles and guides. Some areas may close during heavy monsoon rain or fire season. Wear muted colours, carry water, and keep your voice low. The quieter you are, the more the forest shows itself. A single patient minute often brings more than an hour of noise.

Below the hills, the landscape opens out. On the drive up from Pollachi, you pass coconut groves, small villages, and the Aliyar reservoir. Its flat water reflects the steep, forested slopes behind it. Then the road climbs. The air cools. The trees close in. Within a short distance, the farmland of the plains gives way to wild forest. That sharp change tells you how much this protected land matters.

Before you leave, pause once more. Look at the canopy above you, layer upon layer of green. Notice the sounds again — the birds, the insects, the wind in the bamboo. You may not have seen a tiger today. But a tiger may well have seen you. As you walk back toward the road, watch the soft ground for prints. The Elephant Hills keep their own time, and they were here long before any of us arrived.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start your visit at Topslip, the main visitor hub.
Book forest department safaris in advance where possible.
Wear muted greens or browns to avoid startling wildlife.
Carry drinking water and keep plastic waste with you.
Check monsoon and fire-season closures before travelling.
