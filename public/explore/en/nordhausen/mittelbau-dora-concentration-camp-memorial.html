The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the beech and pine trees on the slope above you. Gravel shifts under your shoes. Somewhere a bird calls, and then the quiet returns. It is a soft, green, almost peaceful landscape on the edge of Nordhausen, in the foothills of the Harz mountains. And that calm is the first hard thing to understand about this place. What happened here happened among these same trees, under this same sky.

You are standing at the memorial site of the Mittelbau-Dora concentration camp. The hill above you is called the Kohnstein. Inside it runs a system of tunnels. During the Second World War, those tunnels became an underground weapons factory. Prisoners from across Europe were forced to dig it, to expand it, and then to build rockets inside it. Many thousands of them never left.

The story begins in the late summer of 1943. Allied bombers had struck the rocket testing site at Peenemünde, on the Baltic coast. The Nazi leadership decided to move production deep underground, where bombs could not reach it. The tunnels in the Kohnstein already existed, first dug as a fuel and chemical depot. In August 1943, the SS sent the first prisoners here from Buchenwald. At first, Dora was simply a subcamp of Buchenwald, with a code name taken from the alphabet.

Those first months are the darkest chapter of this site. There were no barracks yet. The prisoners lived inside the tunnels themselves. They slept in tiered bunks in the rock galleries, close to the blasting and the drilling. There was almost no daylight, little clean water, and dust everywhere. Disease spread quickly. Survivors later described the noise, the cold damp air, and the constant exhaustion. The death rate in that first winter was terrible.

Only in the spring of 1944 did the prisoners move into a barracks camp outside the hill — the open ground in front of you. Conditions improved a little, but the work did not stop. In the autumn of 1944, Dora became an independent concentration camp. It was now the centre of its own network, called Mittelbau, with dozens of subcamps across the region. Prisoners in those satellite camps dug new tunnels and worked on other underground projects.

What were they building? Mainly the V-2, a long-range ballistic missile. The Nazi regime called it a "vengeance weapon". It was launched against cities such as London and Antwerp. Here is a fact that many visitors find hard to absorb. Historians estimate that more people died building the V-2 than were killed by it in attacks. The weapon killed many civilians. But its production, here and in the subcamps, cost even more lives.

The factory inside the hill was called the Mittelwerk. It was run by a company, with engineers, managers and civilian staff working alongside the SS. The rocket programme involved scientists who later became famous. Wernher von Braun, the leading engineer of the V-2, visited the Mittelwerk. After the war, he and many colleagues went to the United States and helped build the American space programme. Their knowledge of conditions here remains a subject of serious historical debate.

The prisoners came from many countries. A large share were from the Soviet Union, Poland and France. There were also Germans, Czechs, Italians, Belgians, Dutch and many others. Some were political prisoners, some were resistance fighters, some were prisoners of war. Overall, around sixty thousand people were deported to Mittelbau-Dora and its subcamps. Around twenty thousand of them died — from hunger, disease, exhaustion, violence, and execution.

Some prisoners resisted, in small and dangerous ways. Workers on the assembly lines could slow their pace, or quietly damage parts. The SS treated any suspected sabotage with extreme brutality. Prisoners were executed, sometimes publicly, as a warning to everyone else. Take a moment as you walk the grounds. The open square of the former roll call area was the place where prisoners stood, often for hours, in every kind of weather.

As you move across the site, notice what remains and what is gone. Most of the wooden barracks no longer stand. Their positions are marked on the ground, so you can read the layout of the camp with your eyes. One building that does survive is the crematorium. It stands on the slope, a plain and solid structure. Inside, the ovens remain. Many visitors pause here in silence. There is no need to say anything.

The tunnels themselves are still inside the hill. Much of the system collapsed or was blown up after the war, when the area was under Soviet control. A section was later reopened and made accessible. Visitors can enter part of it on guided tours. Inside, the air is cool and damp, even in summer. You may see rusted rocket parts and machinery left on the tunnel floor. Standing in that half-light, the testimony of survivors becomes very concrete.

The final weeks were chaotic and deadly. In early April 1945, as American forces approached, the SS sent thousands of prisoners on death marches and evacuation transports. Many died on the roads and in the trains. In Nordhausen itself, a former barracks complex used for sick and dying prisoners was hit in bombing raids. On April 11, 1945, American troops reached the Dora camp. They found sick and dying prisoners, and the dead.

Today the site is a memorial and a place of learning. A modern museum building near the entrance holds a permanent exhibition. It tells the story through documents, photographs, objects and the words of survivors. Spend time with those names and faces. They turn a large, abstract number back into individual people — a student from Lyon, a farmer from Ukraine, a teacher from Warsaw, each with a family waiting somewhere.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look up at the Kohnstein. From the outside, it is just a wooded hill, like many others in this region. The trees have grown back. The birds have returned. That ordinary appearance is exactly why memory matters here. As you walk back down the path, listen again to the gravel under your feet. Thousands of people once walked this ground without any choice. You can carry their story with you.
