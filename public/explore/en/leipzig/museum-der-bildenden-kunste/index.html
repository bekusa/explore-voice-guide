The guide

The story

Before you reach the doors, look up. A great glass box rises from the old trading streets of central Leipzig, plain and pale against the brick and stucco around it. The light inside looks cool and even, almost like light on a snowy afternoon. Your footsteps change on the hard stone floor as you walk in. This is the Museum der bildenden Künste. The name simply means the Museum of Fine Arts, and it holds one of the oldest civic art collections in Germany.

The word civic matters here. This museum did not grow from a royal palace or a prince's treasure room. It grew from the ambition of Leipzig's citizens. In the 1830s, local merchants, publishers and art lovers formed an art society, the Leipziger Kunstverein. Their goal was to give a city of trade fairs and books a public art collection of its own. Over the following decades, wealthy families gave paintings, sculptures and money. The collection belonged, in a real sense, to the town.

That civic spirit gave the city its first real museum building in the nineteenth century, on the large square now called Augustusplatz. For a generation of Leipzigers, it was the place to see art. Then came the Second World War. Allied bombing in 1943 destroyed that building. Much of the collection had been moved to safety beforehand, so many works survived. But the museum itself, the walls and the rooms, was gone.

What followed is a surprise for many visitors. For roughly half a century, the collection had no proper home of its own. After the war, the paintings were shown in the former Imperial Court building, the grand old Reichsgericht in the south of the city. It was a courthouse full of marble and heavy doors, and the art lived there as a guest. Through the years of the German Democratic Republic, which locals call the GDR, and beyond reunification, the museum waited.

The answer came in 2004, when this building opened on Sachsenplatz. A team of Berlin architects designed it as a large cube of glass and concrete. Around it, smaller buildings line the edges of the square, so the cube sits inside a ring of streets. The design asked a bold question. What would a museum look like if it were built for the light of today, not for the ceremony of the past? Many people in Leipzig argued about it, and some still do.

Inside, notice how the building works. Tall halls open up suddenly, then narrow into calmer, more closed rooms. Stairs and ramps take you up through the levels, and the ceilings rise and fall as you go. At several corners, open terraces cut into the cube. From them you can look out across the rooftops and church towers of the old town. These breaks are worth looking for. They give your eyes a rest between galleries.

Now to the art. The collection runs from the late Middle Ages to today, so you can move through several centuries in one afternoon. Among the older works, look for German paintings from the age of Lucas Cranach, the sixteenth-century painter who worked for the Protestant princes of Saxony. You will also find Dutch and Flemish painting from the seventeenth century, when merchant cities filled their homes with portraits, still lifes and quiet interiors. In a merchant city like Leipzig, those Dutch rooms feel at home.

The nineteenth century is where many visitors slow down. Here you may find Caspar David Friedrich's painting known as The Stages of Life. Friedrich was the great painter of German Romanticism, a movement that looked for deep feeling in nature and in solitude. In this picture, figures of different ages gather on a shore while ships sail toward them in the evening light. Many people read the ships as a quiet image of life coming to an end. Stand still with it for a minute.

Not far away hangs another picture about the edge between life and death. It is a version of Arnold Böcklin's Isle of the Dead. Böcklin was a Swiss painter who made several versions of this scene, and Leipzig owns one of them. A small boat carries a white figure toward a rocky island with dark cypress trees. The painting became so popular around 1900 that prints of it hung in homes across Europe. Look closely at the water. It is almost completely still.

Then comes the moment that most visitors remember. In the collection stands Max Klinger's Beethoven, a large seated figure of the composer. Klinger was born in Leipzig, and he spent years on this sculpture. He built it from different materials, including coloured marble, bronze and other precious elements. The composer sits half bare on a throne, his body leaning forward, his fists closed. It is strange, theatrical and very serious. Walk all the way around it. The back is as worked as the front.

The museum is also the best place to understand Leipzig's own painters of the twentieth century. During the GDR years, the city's art academy produced a group of artists often called the Leipzig School. Names like Werner Tübke, Wolfgang Mattheuer and Bernhard Heisig belong to that story. Their work is figurative, detailed and often full of history and tension. After reunification, younger painters such as Neo Rauch carried that tradition into a new world, and the term New Leipzig School followed them abroad.

There is also a strong collection of works by Max Beckmann, the German painter whose hard, bold figures carry the shocks of the early twentieth century. Seeing Beckmann close to the Leipzig painters is useful. You can see how ideas about the human figure moved through German art across war, division and change. The museum also shows temporary exhibitions, often of contemporary art, so the cube rarely feels the same twice. Check what is on before you plan your route.

Outside the museum, the old town continues around you. The streets nearby still follow lines laid out when Leipzig was a city of merchants and fairs. Shopping arcades, cafés and old trading houses sit only a few steps from the glass walls. In a way, the building returns the collection to the commercial heart of the city, close to the families who first paid for it.

As you leave, turn around once more and look back at the cube. Notice how the glass picks up the colour of the sky. In the morning it can look grey and serious. Near sunset it can turn soft and warm. Inside are centuries of pictures that Leipzig's citizens chose to keep. Outside, the town goes on with its ordinary business, its trams and its footsteps. Take a moment in between, before you walk on.
