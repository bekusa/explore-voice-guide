The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere out in the grey, a foghorn sounds, low and long, and the wind pulls at your jacket. Under your feet, the deck hums faintly as traffic rolls past. Look up, and the tower disappears into a moving ceiling of fog. On a clear day, the whole thing glows orange against blue water. On most mornings, it shows itself slowly, one cable at a time. Welcome to the Golden Gate Bridge.

Here is the first surprise. The name does not come from the bridge, and it does not come from the Gold Rush. The Golden Gate is the strait itself — the narrow channel where the Pacific Ocean pushes into San Francisco Bay. The explorer John C. Frémont gave it that name in the 1840s. He was thinking of the Golden Horn, the famous harbour of Istanbul. The bridge simply borrowed the name of the water it crosses.

For generations, the only way across this strait was by ferry. Engineers had long said a bridge here was impossible. The currents are fierce. The winds come straight off the ocean. Fog rolls in almost daily in summer. And the water is deep, with a rocky floor. Many people also feared a bridge would ruin the view of the bay. Yet in the early twentieth century, the growing towns to the north wanted a road link, and the idea refused to die.

The man who pushed it hardest was the engineer Joseph Strauss. He spent years promoting the project and fighting for approval. But the story is more complicated than one name on a plaque. Much of the difficult mathematics was done by another engineer, Charles Ellis. Ellis was pushed off the project before it was finished, and for decades his role was nearly forgotten. Only in the twenty-first century did the bridge district officially recognise his contribution.

Now look at the colour. It is called International Orange, and it was not the original plan. The steel arrived coated in a reddish-orange primer. The consulting architect, Irving Morrow, liked how it looked against the hills and the sea. He argued to keep a warm orange tone. One popular story says the United States Navy suggested black and yellow stripes for visibility. Morrow's choice won, and today it is hard to imagine anything else.

Morrow also shaped the style you see around you. Look at the towers. They narrow as they rise, in stepped layers. The vertical ribs and recessed panels follow the Art Deco fashion of the 1930s — a style of clean lines and bold geometry. Even the streetlights along the deck carry that same simple, angular design. Engineers built the structure. But it was an architect who decided how it would feel to stand beside it.

Construction began in 1933, in the depths of the Great Depression. Workers built the south tower foundation in open ocean water, battling waves and strong tides. High above, men walked steel beams in the wind with almost no protection. Strauss insisted on safety measures that were unusual for the time. Workers wore hard hats. And beneath the deck, crews hung a large safety net, stretching out over the water.

That net saved lives. Nineteen men fell into it and survived. They called themselves the Halfway to Hell Club. But the story has a dark chapter too. In February 1937, a section of scaffolding broke loose and tore straight through the net. Ten men died that day. In total, eleven workers lost their lives building the bridge. As you walk, it is worth remembering the people who stood where you stand, long before there was a railing.

The bridge opened in May 1937. On the first day, it belonged only to people on foot. Tens of thousands walked, ran, and even roller-skated across. Cars came the next day. When it opened, the main span between the two towers measured about 1,280 metres. That made it the longest suspension span in the world. It held that record for more than twenty-five years.

So how does it actually stand? Look at the two great cables draped over the towers. Each cable is not one solid rope. It is made of tens of thousands of thin steel wires, bundled tightly together. The vertical cables hanging down from them hold up the road. The towers carry the weight down into the seabed. The ends of the main cables are anchored deep in huge concrete blocks on each shore.

Here is the second surprise. This bridge is designed to move. In strong winds, the deck can sway sideways. On hot days, the steel expands and the road sags lower. In 1987, for the fiftieth anniversary, hundreds of thousands of people crowded onto the deck at once. Photos showed the gentle upward curve of the roadway flattening under their weight. The bridge held, exactly as the engineers intended.

The work never really ends. Salt air and fog attack the steel every day. Painters and ironworkers maintain the bridge constantly, touching up the orange and replacing worn rivets. The bridge has also been strengthened over the years to better survive a major earthquake. This is, after all, a city that sits near the San Andreas Fault. What looks permanent is really the result of steady, daily care.

One more detail is easy to miss. Below the southern end, near the water, sits Fort Point, a brick fortress from the nineteenth century. The original plans would have destroyed it. Instead, the designers added a steel arch over the fort so it could survive. If you walk down later, you can stand under the bridge and look straight up at that arch. Few visitors make the short walk.

Now pause where you are. Feel the wind on your face. Watch the fog slide through the cables, or the light change on the water below. Ships pass underneath, heading out to the open Pacific. Behind you, the city rises on its hills. As you walk on, listen again for the foghorn. It has been calling across this strait for longer than the bridge has stood.
