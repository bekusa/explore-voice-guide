The guide

The story

Pause for a moment at the foot of the steps. Feel the granite under your shoes, cool even on a sunny afternoon. Behind you, the gold-trimmed dome of City Hall catches the light across Civic Center Plaza. In front of you rises a grey stone facade with tall arched windows and a sober, symmetrical face. It looks like a library... and for most of the twentieth century, that is exactly what it was.

This is the Asian Art Museum of San Francisco. Inside are objects from across Asia, from China, Japan and Korea to India, the Himalayas, Southeast Asia, Iran and beyond. The collection is one of the largest of its kind in the Western world. It numbers in the many thousands of works, and some objects are several thousand years old. But before we go inside, it is worth looking closely at the building itself, because it has two lives layered into one.

The structure first opened in 1917 as the city's Main Public Library. It belonged to the Civic Center that San Francisco built after the earthquake and fires of 1906 destroyed so much of the old downtown. The style is Beaux-Arts, a grand classical manner taught at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris. It favours symmetry, carved stone, and formal staircases. Look at how this facade lines up with City Hall and the other civic buildings around the plaza. They were planned as one ensemble, a statement that the city had rebuilt itself in stone.

For decades, San Franciscans came here to borrow books and study under high ceilings. Then, in the 1990s, the library moved to a new building just across the street. You can see it nearby, a more modern block with a lighter, contemporary face. The old library stood empty, and the city needed a new use for it. At the same time, the Asian Art Museum needed a bigger home. The two needs met here.

Here is the first surprise. The museum did not begin in this neighbourhood at all. It opened in 1966 in Golden Gate Park, in a wing beside another city museum. Its foundation was the vast personal collection of Avery Brundage, a Chicago businessman. He was also a long-serving president of the International Olympic Committee. Brundage gave his collection to the city, and it remains the heart of the museum today. His role in Olympic history is still debated, and the museum itself has openly examined his legacy in recent years.

The move to Civic Center came in 2003. To turn a library into a museum, the city hired the Italian architect Gae Aulenti. She was already known for converting a Paris railway station into the Musée d'Orsay, so she had experience giving old buildings new purpose. Her approach here kept the historic shell and the grand staircase, while opening up the interior for galleries. The building was also strengthened against earthquakes, a serious concern in this city. So the stone you touched outside now sits on a much safer base than it did a century ago.

Now step inside, and notice the change in sound. The noise of Larkin Street and the plaza falls away. Footsteps echo softly on stone. The light shifts too, from open sky to a calmer, filtered glow. Many visitors start on the upper floors and walk down through the collection. This lets you move through different regions of Asia almost like chapters, one gallery opening into the next.

The galleries are organised mostly by region and tradition. In one room you may meet Buddhist sculpture from South Asia, with calm faces carved in stone. In another, Chinese bronzes, jades and ceramics. Further on, Japanese screens, lacquer and ink painting, then Korean works and art from the Himalayan world. Take your time with the labels. They often explain not only what an object is, but how it was used, in a temple, a household, or a royal court.

Here is the second surprise, and it is easy to walk past. Among the Chinese bronzes, look for a small ritual vessel shaped like a rhinoceros. It is very old, made more than three thousand years ago in ancient China. Its body is round and heavy, its expression almost friendly. It is one of the best-loved pieces in the museum. Many visitors expect grand statues to be the stars, but this compact, quietly humorous animal often draws the longest gaze.

As you move between rooms, pay attention to materials. Bronze turned green with age. Jade, smooth and cool, carved with patience that is hard to imagine today. Gold leaf on wooden figures, worn thin where hands once touched them. Silk and paper, kept in low light to protect their colours. These choices in lighting are deliberate. Some works can only be shown for short periods before they must rest in storage again, so the displays change over time.

The museum is not only about the past. In 2020 it opened a new pavilion for special exhibitions and contemporary art. Look for the newer additions on the building's east side, where clean modern lines meet the old stone. The museum also uses its walls and terraces for work by living artists. That mix is intentional. It treats Asian art as a continuing story, not a closed chapter, and it reflects the many Asian communities who live in the Bay Area today.

That local link matters. San Francisco has one of the oldest Chinatowns in North America, and deep Japanese, Filipino, Korean, Vietnamese and South Asian communities. For many families here, these galleries hold objects from the places their grandparents knew. You may hear several languages spoken in a single room. You may also notice school groups, artists sketching, and older visitors returning to a favourite piece. The museum works as a gathering place as much as a treasury.

When you leave, walk slowly down the steps and turn around once more. You are looking at a library that became a museum, in a civic centre born from disaster. The building held books for the city for most of a century. Now it holds sculpture, painting and ceramics from across a whole continent. Across the plaza, City Hall's dome still shines, and the traffic goes on. Carry that small rhinoceros with you as you go... a quiet face from a distant past, now at home in this city.
