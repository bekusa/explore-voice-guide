The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here the city noise fades into wind moving through pine and camphor trees. Under your feet the gravel crunches, pale and dry. Around you, walls of fitted grey stone curve upward like the sides of a great ship. You are standing on Katsuyama, the hill at the centre of Matsuyama. On its summit sits one of Japan's few remaining original castle keeps. It is a place that has looked down on this city for more than four centuries.

The story begins at the start of the seventeenth century. Japan had just come through a long age of civil war. The warlord Kato Yoshiaki fought on the winning side at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600. He was rewarded with land here on the island of Shikoku. Soon after, around 1602, he began building a new castle on this hill. The town that grew at its feet took the name Matsuyama, which means pine mountain.

Kato never saw his castle fully finished. Construction took decades, and he was moved to a different domain in northern Japan before the work was complete. Another lord held the castle briefly. Then, in the 1630s, it passed to a branch of the Matsudaira family. They were closely related to the ruling Tokugawa shoguns. That family governed Matsuyama for the rest of the Edo period, until the samurai age ended in the late nineteenth century.

Here is the first surprise. The keep you see today is not the one Kato built. In the late eighteenth century, lightning struck the main tower, and it burned. Rebuilding took a long time. The new keep was finished in the 1850s, only a few years before Japan opened to the outside world. So this tower is among the youngest of the surviving original keeps... yet it is still genuine Edo-period construction, built with traditional wood and plaster.

That word, original, matters a great deal in Japan. Many castles you can visit are concrete reconstructions from the twentieth century. They look right from a distance, but inside they hold lifts and modern museum displays. Only twelve castle keeps in the whole country survive from before the modern era. Matsuyama is one of them. When you climb its wooden stairs, you walk on the same structure that samurai guards walked on.

Now look at the stone walls as you climb. They are called ishigaki, which simply means stone walls. Notice how the base flares outward, then rises in a gentle curve toward the top. This shape spread weight and made the walls harder to scale. Look closely at the corners. There the builders laid long, rectangular stones in alternating directions, locking the edge together. No mortar holds them. Gravity and careful fitting have kept them standing for centuries.

Here is a second detail that many visitors walk past. On parts of this hill, the stone walls do not just surround the summit. They run down the slope itself, climbing the hillside to connect the upper castle with the lower grounds. These are known as climbing stone walls. They are rare in Japan. They were meant to stop attackers from moving sideways across the hill and reaching the gates by another route.

The main keep does not stand alone. It is joined to smaller towers by covered corridors and walled courtyards. This design creates a tight, linked fortress at the very top of the hill. Enemies who broke through one gate would find themselves in an enclosed yard, watched from every side. As you pass through the gates, notice how often the path turns. Straight lines helped attackers. Sharp corners slowed them down.

Look up at the roof tiles. On the round ends of many tiles you can see a family crest. It shows leaves in a circular pattern. This is a version of the hollyhock crest, a plant symbol linked to the Tokugawa family and its relatives. It reminds you who held power here for most of the castle's life. The dark wooden walls, white plaster, and grey tiles together give the keep its calm, solid look.

Inside, the keep is cool and dim. The floors are polished by countless socks, because visitors take off their shoes at the entrance. The staircases are steep, almost like ladders. Hold the rail and take your time. On the way up, you pass displays of armour, swords, and old documents. Notice the small openings in the walls. Defenders could shoot arrows or guns through them, or drop stones onto anyone below.

At the top floor, the view opens in every direction. To one side lies the modern city, with its trams and rooftops. Beyond them, on a clear day, you can see the Seto Inland Sea and its scattered islands. In the other direction, green mountains roll toward the centre of Shikoku. From here, the lords of Matsuyama could watch roads, harbours, and fields at once. Today the view simply shows how the city grew around this one hill.

Matsuyama has a strong literary side, and the castle is part of it. The novelist Natsume Soseki taught here as a young man. He later set his famous comic novel, Botchan, in a town based on Matsuyama. The haiku poet Masaoka Shiki was born in this city. Haiku is a very short form of Japanese poetry. Shiki wrote about the castle and the town, and haiku boxes for visitors' poems can still be found in the city.

In spring, the castle grounds fill with cherry blossoms, and families spread blue sheets under the trees. In autumn, the maples around the slopes turn red and gold. In every season, local people walk or jog up this hill in the early morning. For them it is not only a monument. It is a daily path, a place to breathe fresh air, and a quiet point above a busy city.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look at the keep against the sky. Notice the layered roofs, the white walls, the dark wood. Feel the stone under your hand if you can. These walls were placed here by people who never imagined trams or tourists. As you walk back down, listen again for the wind in the pines... the same sound this hill has carried since the castle first rose.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the ropeway or chairlift up to save your legs.
Wear shoes that slip off easily for the keep interior.
Hold the rail on the steep, ladder-like wooden stairs.
Come early morning for quiet paths and clear sea views.
Visit during cherry blossom season for picnics under the trees.
