The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums along Columbiadamm, and somewhere behind the wall a bird calls from the old trees. Then you pass through the gate, and the city noise drops away. Gravel shifts under your shoes. In front of you, a pale dome rises over a quiet cemetery, with two slender minarets standing like pencils against the Berlin sky. This is the Şehitlik Mosque, in the district of Neukölln, and the ground you are standing on has a story much older than the building.

Start with the name. Şehitlik is a Turkish word. It roughly means a place of martyrs, and it is often used for a burial ground of honoured dead. That fits, because the cemetery came first. The mosque arrived only at the beginning of this century. So when you look around, you are seeing two layers of time at once. One layer reaches back more than two hundred years. The other is still young, and you can see how fresh the stone is.

Here is the first surprise. This cemetery is widely described as the oldest Muslim burial ground in Germany. Its roots go back to the end of the eighteenth century, when an Ottoman envoy died in Berlin. The Prussian king granted land so that he could be buried according to Islamic custom. That first cemetery lay elsewhere in the city. In the 1860s, the graves were moved to this site on Columbiadamm, and the burial ground has stayed here ever since.

Think about what that means. In the late eighteenth century, Prussia and the Ottoman Empire were trading partners and diplomatic allies. Turkish diplomats, soldiers and students passed through Berlin. Some of them never went home. This small patch of land became their resting place. Long before the large Turkish communities of modern Berlin, there was already a quiet Muslim presence here, marked by headstones carved in Ottoman script.

Take a slow walk among the graves before you go inside. Look at the older stones. Some carry inscriptions in Ottoman Turkish, written in Arabic letters, which Turkey replaced with the Latin alphabet in the twentieth century. Other stones are newer, with Latin script, photographs, or fresh flowers. You can read the history of migration on these stones. Diplomats lie near factory workers, and grandparents lie near people who were born in Berlin.

That second wave of history began in the 1960s. West Germany signed an agreement with Turkey to recruit workers for its growing industry. Hundreds of thousands came. Many expected to stay a few years, then return. Instead, they built lives here. Neukölln and neighbouring Kreuzberg became the heart of Turkish Berlin. For decades, many people prayed in converted rooms, in old shops and backyard factories. The community wanted a real mosque, one that looked like a mosque.

This building is the answer to that wish. Construction began in the late 1990s, and the mosque opened in the mid-2000s. It is run by DITIB, a large Turkish-Islamic organisation in Germany. The architects chose the classical Ottoman style, which grew up in Istanbul in the sixteenth century. That style uses one large central dome, smaller half domes around it, and tall, thin minarets. If you have seen the great mosques of Istanbul, the shape will look familiar.

Now look up at the exterior. The walls are a light, almost creamy stone. The main dome sits on a ring of smaller domes and windows. Notice how the building steps up in stages, from the low porch to the half domes to the central crown. This layering is deliberate. In Ottoman design, the eye travels upward step by step, and the dome always feels like the final point of the composition.

Here is the second surprise. Many visitors do not expect such a classical mosque to sit right next to one of Berlin's most modern public spaces. Just beyond the cemetery lies the former Tempelhof airport, now a huge open park. On windy afternoons you can see kites and cyclists across the field. An Ottoman dome on one side, a vast former runway on the other. Few places in Berlin put two such different histories so close together.

Before you enter, there are a few simple customs. Remove your shoes at the entrance, and place them on the shelves provided. Women are usually asked to cover their hair, and loose scarves are often available. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Speak softly. If people are praying, stay behind them and do not walk across their line of prayer. The community welcomes visitors, and guided tours are often offered for groups.

Step inside, and the light changes. Soft carpet replaces the gravel. The space opens up under the dome, wide and calm. Look for the tile work and painted decoration, often in blues and turquoise, and for the bands of Arabic calligraphy. Find the mihrab, the niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca. Beside it stands the minbar, a tall stepped pulpit used for the Friday sermon. A large chandelier usually hangs low over the prayer hall.

Notice too what is missing. There are no statues and no pictures of people. Islamic religious art traditionally avoids images of living beings in places of prayer. Instead, the decoration uses geometry, plant patterns and written words. The calligraphy is not just ornament. It usually quotes the Quran or names of God, so for believers the walls carry meaning as well as beauty. Spend a moment with a single pattern, and follow how it repeats.

Over the years, the mosque has also become a meeting place for the whole neighbourhood. It hosts open days, interfaith visits and school groups. On religious holidays, families crowd the courtyard, and the smell of tea and sweets drifts between the graves. On an ordinary weekday, it may feel almost empty. Both moods are true. The building is a place of worship first, but also a doorway between communities that share these streets.

As you leave, pause again at the gate. Turn around and look at the dome above the old trees and the leaning headstones. Listen for the traffic returning, the buses on Columbiadamm, the voices from the park. You have just walked through more than two centuries of Berlin's connection to the Muslim world, from a lone envoy's grave to a full Ottoman dome. Carry that quiet with you as you step back onto the street.
