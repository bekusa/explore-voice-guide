The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere beyond the curtain wall, you may hear the slow creak of timber turning on timber, and the soft thud of hooves on wooden boards. The wind comes up over the ramparts here, carrying the smell of cut grass and old stone. Below you, the village of Carisbrooke spreads out among the green folds of the Isle of Wight. This hilltop has watched over the centre of the island for close to a thousand years.

Welcome to Carisbrooke Castle, just outside Newport. The island is small, and Newport sits close to its middle, where the River Medina reaches deep inland. That location made this ridge valuable. Whoever held this hill could watch the routes across the island and gather people behind strong walls when raiders came from the sea. The first fortifications here are older than the stone you see now. The Normans saw the strategic value of the site soon after their conquest of England in 1066.

Look up at the high mound inside the walls. This is the motte. That word describes an earth mound that the Normans raised and crowned with a fortified tower. At first, the defences were timber. Later, builders replaced them with stone and created the shell keep at the top. A shell keep is a ring of stone wall around the summit, not a solid tower. Climb the steep steps if you can. The view across the island is the best reason to make the effort.

For much of the medieval period, powerful families held the castle as Lords of the Isle of Wight. One of the most remarkable was a woman, Isabella de Fortibus. She ruled the island in the late thirteenth century as a wealthy and formidable landowner. In 1293, on her deathbed, she sold the island to King Edward the First. From that moment, the castle belonged to the Crown. Many visitors pass through the gatehouse without hearing her name. She is one of the key figures in the castle's story.

The island's position made it a target. In 1377, French forces landed and besieged the castle. The walls held. Local tradition adds a hero to this episode. Legend has it that an archer named Peter de Heynoe shot the French commander through a narrow opening in the wall. Locals still point out a gap they call Peter's Hole. The siege itself is history, but the archer's story is best treated as folklore.

Now turn toward the great gatehouse. Its two rounded towers rise above the entrance, and the upper parts belong to the later medieval period. Notice the narrow slits and openings in the stone. Defenders used them to watch the approach and fire at attackers. Today, the opening frames a peaceful view of grass and sky. In the past, anyone who reached this point would have faced heavy doors, a portcullis, and defenders on the walls above.

As you walk the outer defences, you will see angular earthworks around the medieval walls. These are much later than the keep. At the end of the sixteenth century, England feared another Spanish invasion after the Armada. An Italian engineer, Federigo Giambelli, designed new artillery fortifications here. His sharp bastions were made for the age of cannon. They protect the castle like a wide stone and earth collar. Medieval ideas of defence and later military engineering sit side by side here.

The castle's most famous chapter came during the English Civil War. In late 1647, King Charles the First fled to the Isle of Wight. He hoped for sympathy, but he soon became a prisoner here. He stayed in the castle for many months. He walked, read, and played bowls on a green that was prepared for him. All the time, he was planning his escape.

His first attempt is one of the castle's best stories. The king tried to squeeze through the bars of a window. He became stuck and had to give up. Later plans also failed. In 1648, he was taken from the island, and early the next year he was tried and executed in London. His daughter, Princess Elizabeth, was later held at Carisbrooke too. She died here in 1650, still a teenager. Her story is quieter, but it is just as moving.

Now, back to that creaking sound. Inside the well house, a great wooden treadwheel turns over a deep well. In the past, people and later donkeys walked inside the wheel to raise water from deep below the castle. The castle still keeps donkeys, and they often demonstrate the wheel for visitors. This sight surprises many first-time visitors. The animals walk at their own calm pace, and the wheel turns because of them.

After the wars ended, the castle took on a gentler role. In the late nineteenth century, Princess Beatrice, the youngest daughter of Queen Victoria, became Governor of the Isle of Wight. She spent time here and supported the castle museum, which opened in 1898. The museum collects objects from the island's past, including items linked to King Charles. The Chapel of Saint Nicholas was rebuilt on old foundations in the early twentieth century. It serves as a memorial to the imprisoned king.

Before you leave, visit the Princess Beatrice Garden. It is an enclosed space of flowers and carefully planned borders inside the walls. It was redesigned in the twenty-first century and inspired by the princess's era. After the hard grey defences, the garden feels soft and calm. Bees move between the flowers. Many visitors sit here for a few minutes, away from the wind on the ramparts, and simply enjoy the stillness.

As you walk back toward the gatehouse, look again at the layers around you. You can see the Norman mound, the medieval walls, the Elizabethan bastions, and the Victorian museum. Each period added something new to this hill. Run your hand along the stone if you can reach it. Listen once more for the wheel in the well house. Then look out across the green island and notice how close the sea feels from up here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ask staff when the donkey wheel demonstration happens.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep steps up to the keep.
Walk the full wall circuit for views across the island.
Bring a windproof layer, because the ramparts feel exposed.
Save time for the museum's Charles the First collection.
