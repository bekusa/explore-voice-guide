The guide

The story

Listen to the water first. Here the Danube is not the slow, wide river most travellers know. It is pressed between walls of grey limestone, and the current pulls dark and heavy past the cliffs. The air feels cooler in the shadow of the rock. Somewhere above the waterline, cut into the stone of the Serbian bank, a Roman emperor left his signature almost two thousand years ago.

This is Tabula Traiana, which means Trajan's Tablet. It is a large inscribed plaque carved straight into the cliff face of the Iron Gates gorge. The gorge is known in Serbian as Đerdap, and it forms the border between Serbia and Romania. The tablet stands in the narrowest stretch of the gorge, called the Kazan. The name means cauldron, and on a windy day you can see why. The water swirls and churns here.

The tablet honours the Roman emperor Trajan. He ruled at the end of the first century and the start of the second. The inscription is in Latin, and it opens with his full titles. It calls him Caesar, son of the divine Nerva, and father of the fatherland. Then it reaches the point. In plain terms, it says that he cut through mountains and built this road. Historians date the inscription to around the year 100.

So what road could anyone build here? Look at the cliffs. There is almost no shore at all. Roman engineers solved the problem in a bold way. They cut a narrow ledge into the rock. In some places they drilled rows of square holes into the cliff. They pushed wooden beams into those holes and laid a walkway on top. Soldiers and supplies could then move along the gorge, hanging out over the river.

This road had a clear purpose. Across the river lay Dacia, a powerful kingdom in what is now Romania. Trajan fought two wars against the Dacians in the early second century. His legions needed to move quickly along the Danube frontier. The road through the gorge, together with work to make the river easier for boats, gave Rome that speed. The tablet is a kind of progress report, carved in stone for everyone on the river to read.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. The tablet you see today is not in its original position. In the 1960s, Serbia and Romania built a large hydroelectric dam downstream, called Đerdap One. When the dam was finished, the river behind it rose far higher than before. The original site of the tablet would have disappeared under the water. So engineers cut the entire inscription out of the cliff and lifted it higher up the rock face.

The Roman road itself was not so lucky. The ledges and beam holes that once ran along the gorge now lie mostly underwater. Older travellers wrote about seeing them clearly from passing boats. Today, the tablet is one of the very few visible witnesses to that whole engineering project. When you look at it, you are looking at the survivor of a much longer story, most of which now sits beneath the surface.

Take a moment to study the plaque itself. It is a rectangular panel with the Latin text set in neat lines. Above it, the stone is carved to suggest a small roof or canopy. Around the edges, there are decorative carvings, which scholars describe as dolphins and winged figures. Wind, rain and river spray have worn much of this down. Some letters are now faint, and the final lines are partly a matter of careful reconstruction.

The second surprise sits just downstream, near the town of Kladovo. Trajan did not stop at the road. A few years later, his architect Apollodorus of Damascus built a great bridge across the Danube. For a long time it was among the longest bridges in the Roman world. Only the stumps of its pillars survive today, near the riverbank on both sides. The tablet and the bridge belong to the same campaign, and the same ambition.

Today the tablet is protected as one of Serbia's cultural monuments of exceptional importance. The gorge around it lies inside Đerdap National Park. The park covers forests, cliffs and old settlements along the Serbian bank. If you travel on, you will also find the Ottoman-era Fetislam fortress at Kladovo. Layer by layer, this short stretch of river holds Roman, medieval and Ottoman traces close together.

Most people see the tablet from the water. Boat trips run through the gorge in the warmer months, and they slow down so passengers can look. From the river, you get the angle the Romans themselves intended. Sailors and soldiers would have read these words while passing beneath them. On the Romanian bank nearby, you may also notice a huge carved face in the rock. That is the modern sculpture of Decebalus, the Dacian king. It was made in recent decades, not in Roman times.

That pairing is worth a pause. On one bank, a Roman emperor records his road. On the other, a modern carving honours the king he defeated. The river runs between them, as it has for all these centuries. The gorge has always been a frontier. It was a line between empires, then a line between states. Today it is a border that boats cross freely, carrying visitors instead of legions.

So look once more at the tablet. Notice how small it seems against the size of the cliff. Notice the colour of the stone, and the dark water moving below it. Listen to the engine of your boat, or the wind along the road above. A team of Roman workers once stood on a ledge here and cut these letters by hand. As you move on through the gorge, watch the cliffs. Somewhere under that water, their road is still there.

Before you go

Practical tips

Join a Danube boat trip for the best view of the tablet.
Bring binoculars to read the worn Latin lettering.
Sit on the Serbian side of the boat when entering the Kazan.
Combine the visit with Trajan's Bridge ruins near Kladovo.
Bring a light jacket, since the gorge stays cool and windy.
