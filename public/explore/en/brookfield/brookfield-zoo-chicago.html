The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere beyond the trees, a peacock calls. It is a sharp, almost human cry, and it carries over wide lawns, gravel paths, and the soft splash of fountains. Under your feet the ground is level and generous. The air smells of cut grass, warm pavement, and, now and then, the earthy smell of animals close by. You are in the western suburbs of Chicago, at Brookfield Zoo Chicago, a park designed to feel like a long walk through open country.

The zoo opened in the summer of 1934, during the Great Depression. That timing matters. Money was scarce, and the project had been delayed for years. The land itself came from a gift. Edith Rockefeller McCormick, a Chicago philanthropist, donated a large parcel here in 1919. The Chicago Zoological Society took on the work of turning it into a modern zoo, and the society still operates it today, together with the Forest Preserves of Cook County.

Here is the first surprise. When this zoo opened, many visitors had never seen a large animal without iron bars in front of it. Brookfield was among the first American zoos to use moats, ditches, and rockwork instead of cages. The idea came from Germany, from the animal dealer Carl Hagenbeck, who built panoramas where animals seemed to stand in open landscapes. At Brookfield, the designers brought that idea across the Atlantic, and it changed what people expected a zoo to look like.

As you walk, notice the layout. The main paths are broad and straight, lined with trees, with formal buildings set along them. Many of the older structures follow a classical style from the 1930s, with symmetry, columns, and careful brickwork. Between them, the newer habitats bend and curve into the landscape. The result is a park with two personalities. One is ordered and civic, like a city boulevard. The other is wilder and more hidden, closer to the animals themselves.

The size of the place surprises many first-time visitors. The grounds cover more than two hundred acres, so it is easy to underestimate how far you will walk. Distances that look short on the map can take a while once you stop at every habitat. Many families plan a loop instead of trying to see everything. That approach is wise. Choose a few areas you care about most, and let the rest of the day happen on its own.

In 1937, this zoo became the home of Su-Lin, a young giant panda. Su-Lin was one of the very first giant pandas ever seen outside China. The animal had been brought to the United States by the explorer Ruth Harkness, and crowds came to Brookfield in enormous numbers to see it. At that time, few people in the West had any clear idea what a panda even looked like. For a few years, this suburban zoo held one of the most talked-about animals in the world.

The zoo kept experimenting. In 1961 it opened Seven Seas, an indoor dolphin facility that was among the earliest of its kind at an inland American zoo. Think about that for a moment. Chicago sits beside a freshwater lake, more than a thousand kilometres from any ocean. Yet here, families from the Midwest could watch dolphins up close. The zoo has rebuilt and updated its marine areas over the decades, but the idea of bringing the sea to the prairie remains part of its identity.

Then, in 1984, came Tropic World. This large indoor building was designed as a walk-through rainforest, with habitats representing Africa, Asia, and South America under one roof. It was among the first exhibits of its kind anywhere. Inside, the air is warm and humid. Artificial trees rise high above the paths, and primates move through the branches overhead. If it is cold outside, and Chicago winters are very cold, stepping inside feels like stepping into another climate.

Tropic World is also the setting for a story that travelled around the world. In 1996, a three-year-old boy fell over a railing into the gorilla habitat and lay hurt on the floor below. A female western lowland gorilla named Binti Jua picked him up, held him, and carried him toward a door where zookeepers could reach him. She was carrying her own infant on her back at the time. The boy recovered. Binti Jua became famous, and her gentle response changed how many people thought about gorillas.

Today, the zoo puts strong weight on conservation and science. The Chicago Zoological Society runs research programmes and supports field projects far beyond Illinois. One long-running project studies wild bottlenose dolphins along the coast of Florida, and it is among the longest studies of a wild dolphin population anywhere. You will not see that work directly as you walk. But much of what happens here connects to fieldwork in rivers, coasts, and forests on several continents.

In 2024, the zoo took a new name. For generations, people simply called it the Brookfield Zoo. Now the official name is Brookfield Zoo Chicago. The change links the park more clearly to the city that many visitors come from. The village of Brookfield is still the home address, though. Outside the gates, you will find quiet residential streets, small shops, and commuter rail lines that bring people out from downtown Chicago.

For families, there is a lot to do beyond looking. The Hamill Family Play Zoo is built for younger children, with hands-on spaces and gardens where kids can touch, build, and explore. Elsewhere, Great Bear Wilderness offers wide views of bears, bison, and other North American animals in a setting of rocks, water, and grass. These newer areas show how the zoo has continued the idea from the 1930s. The aim is to remove barriers between people and the animal world wherever it is safe to do so.

As the day goes on, pay attention to the light. In the morning, many animals are more active, and the paths are calmer. By midafternoon, the main walkways fill with strollers, school groups, and the smell of popcorn. In late afternoon, the sun drops lower across the lawns, and the formal buildings glow warm. The sounds change too. Children grow quieter, the birds become louder, and the fountains seem easier to hear.

So before you move on, stand still for a moment wherever you are. Look at the path under your feet, the trees above you, and the open space between you and the animals. Almost a century ago, people came here to see wild creatures without bars for the first time. That same open view is still in front of you now. As you walk toward the exit, listen once more for the peacock. It is often the last voice you hear as you leave.
