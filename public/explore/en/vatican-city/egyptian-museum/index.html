The guide

The story

The air here is cool and still, and the light falls softly on polished stone — basalt, granite, limestone — surfaces that once stood under the desert sun of the Nile valley. You are in one of the Vatican's quieter corners, a suite of rooms that Pope Gregory the Sixteenth opened in 1839 to house the papacy's collection of ancient Egyptian artifacts. The collection is smaller than the great national museums of Cairo or Turin, but what it lacks in scale it gains in focus and intimacy. Every object here passed through the hands of nineteenth-century collectors and scholars who were racing to decode hieroglyphs and reconstruct the religion, politics, and daily life of pharaonic Egypt.

Gregory was not the first pope to admire Egyptian art — Rome itself is dotted with obelisks brought from Egypt by emperors — but he was the first to create a dedicated museum space for it within the Vatican. The collection grew from papal acquisitions, gifts from European travelers, and archaeological missions in Egypt during the eighteen-twenties and thirties, a period when European powers were competing to claim the cultural heritage of the ancient world. The rooms themselves were designed by the architect Giuseppe Fabris, and the painted ceilings and wall decorations echo the Egyptian motifs below — lotus blossoms, scarabs, winged solar disks — creating a kind of nineteenth-century Egyptomania that feels both scholarly and theatrical.

The centerpiece of the first hall is a colossal seated statue of the goddess Sekhmet, carved from black granite. Sekhmet was the lion-headed goddess of war and healing, and the Egyptians made hundreds of statues of her during the reign of Amenhotep the Third in the fourteenth century before Christ. This one shows her seated, hands resting on her knees, her face serene and unreadable. The stone is so dark it seems to absorb light, and if you step close you can see the fine grain of the granite and the traces of the sculptor's tools along the base. The Egyptians believed statues were not just representations but vessels — the goddess could inhabit this stone body during temple rituals.

Nearby, a row of canopic jars stands in a vitrine. These were the vessels used to store the internal organs of the deceased during mummification — the liver, lungs, stomach, and intestines were each placed in a separate jar, sealed with a lid shaped like the head of one of the four sons of Horus. The stoppers here are carved with exquisite detail: a human head, a baboon, a jackal, a falcon. The jars themselves are made of limestone or alabaster, smooth and pale, and they were placed in the tomb alongside the mummy so the organs could be reunited with the body in the afterlife. The Egyptians removed the organs to preserve the body, but they kept them close — death was not an ending but a transformation.

In the next room, a collection of bronze statuettes catches the eye. These are votive figures — small sculptures of gods and sacred animals that worshippers dedicated at temple shrines. There are falcons representing Horus, ibises for Thoth, cats for Bastet, and tiny figures of Osiris wrapped in a shroud. The bronzes are dark green with age, and many still show traces of gilding on the details — a crown, a collar, the edge of a wing. These were mass-produced in temple workshops during the Late Period and the Ptolemaic era, but each one was made with care, cast in a mold and finished by hand. Pilgrims bought them as offerings, hoping the god would hear their prayers.

The museum also holds a remarkable collection of funerary stelae — stone slabs carved with hieroglyphic inscriptions and relief images of the deceased making offerings to the gods. One stela shows a man named Iry standing before a table piled with bread, beer, meat, and vegetables, the offerings that would sustain him in the afterlife. Above him, a line of hieroglyphs records his name, his titles, and a prayer asking the gods to grant him eternal sustenance. The carving is shallow but precise, and the stone has a warm ochre tone. These stelae were placed in tombs or temple chapels, and they served as both memorials and magical contracts — the image and the words together ensured the offerings would continue forever.

One of the museum's surprises is a wooden sarcophagus lid, painted with the face and body of a woman. The wood is sycamore, and the paint — blues, reds, yellows, blacks — is still vivid after more than two thousand years. The woman's face is idealized, with large almond eyes outlined in black, a serene expression, and a heavy wig painted in bands of blue and gold. Her body is covered with hieroglyphic spells from the Book of the Dead, prayers to guide her through the dangers of the underworld. The lid would have rested over her mummified body, and the painted face was meant to help her soul recognize and re-enter the body after death. The craftsmanship is intimate — you can see the brushstrokes, the layering of pigment, the care taken to get the proportions right.

The museum also includes a small but fascinating collection of ushabti figures — small mummiform statuettes placed in tombs to serve as laborers in the afterlife. The Egyptians believed the dead would be called upon to work in the fields of the gods, so they buried hundreds of ushabtis with the body, each inscribed with a spell that would animate it when needed. The ushabtis here are made of faience, a glazed ceramic that ranges in color from bright turquoise to deep blue. They are only a few inches tall, but the details are precise — the mummy wrappings, the tools held in their hands, the hieroglyphic text running down the front. Some tombs contained more than four hundred ushabtis, a small army of servants for eternity.

In one corner, a display case holds a collection of amulets and jewelry — tiny carved scarabs, eye-of-Horus pendants, gold rings, beaded necklaces. The scarab beetle was a symbol of rebirth because the Egyptians observed beetles rolling balls of dung and saw in that behavior a metaphor for the sun god rolling the sun across the sky. Scarabs were worn as protective charms, and many were inscribed on the flat underside with the name of a pharaoh or a blessing. The faience beads in the necklaces are strung on modern thread, but the beads themselves are ancient, each one pierced and glazed by hand. The colors — turquoise, lapis blue, carnelian red — were chosen for their symbolic meanings, and wearing them was believed to bring health, protection, and favor from the gods.

The final rooms contain a collection of Roman-era artifacts from Egypt, including a few mummy portraits — painted panels that were placed over the face of the deceased during the first and second centuries after Christ. These portraits are startlingly lifelike, with realistic eyes, shading, and expressions that seem to look directly at you. They were painted in encaustic, a technique using pigment mixed with hot wax, and the colors have survived remarkably well. The subjects are young men and women, dressed in Roman fashion, but buried according to Egyptian custom. The portraits remind you that Egypt was a crossroads, a place where Greek, Roman, and pharaonic traditions blended and evolved.

The Gregorian Egyptian Museum is not a grand spectacle, but it rewards slow looking. The objects here are fragments of a civilization that lasted three thousand years, and each one carries a story about belief, craft, and the human need to mark the passage from life to death. As you leave, you might notice the faint smell of old stone and the quiet hum of the ventilation system, small reminders that these ancient things now rest in a modern building, far from the desert that made them.

Outside, the Vatican gardens stretch green and still, and the noise of Rome feels distant. The museum's windows overlook a courtyard where a Roman obelisk stands, carved with hieroglyphs that once honored a pharaoh and now honor a pope. The past here is layered, and the stones speak in more than one language.
