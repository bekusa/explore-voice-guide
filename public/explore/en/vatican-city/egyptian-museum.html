The guide

The story

You enter, and the air changes. It is cool and dry, kept that way for the sake of very old things. The light is low. Around you stand figures carved from dark stone. Some have the faces of people. Some have the heads of animals. They have waited here a long time. This is the Egyptian Museum of Turin.

This museum opened in 1824. That makes it the oldest museum in the world devoted only to the culture of ancient Egypt. It sits inside a large old building in the center of the city, far from the Nile. Yet for those who study Egypt, this is one of the most important places anywhere on Earth.

The collection began with one man's passion. Bernardino Drovetti was an Italian who worked in Egypt in the early nineteenth century. He gathered statues, papyri, and objects by the hundreds. The king of Sardinia bought his collection and brought it to Turin. Later journeys added thousands more pieces. The museum grew into the treasure it is now.

Many great scholars came here to learn. Jean-Francois Champollion, the man who first read Egyptian writing, studied these objects closely. He is said to have remarked that the road to Memphis and Thebes runs through Turin. He meant that no one could truly understand Egypt without first seeing what this city held.

Begin on the upper floors. Here you find papyri, sheets of ancient paper made from reeds. On them are spells, accounts, and letters. One long sheet lists the kings of Egypt in order. It has helped historians fix the dates of an entire civilization. Read the small labels slowly. Each mark was made by a careful hand.

Lower down, you reach the tomb objects. The most moving belong to a man named Kha and his wife Merit. Kha was a builder who served the pharaohs. His tomb was found whole, untouched by robbers. Inside were his chairs, his bed, his food, his wife's makeup, even loaves of bread. These things were meant for a life after death.

Stand near these objects and think. They are simple. A folding stool. A jar. A comb. They were used by real people more than three thousand years ago. Their owners hoped to carry them into another world. Instead, the objects carried the owners' memory forward to you, here, in this room, today.

The heart of the museum is the great hall of statues. The room is dark. The figures are lit from below and doubled in mirrors. The hall was arranged like a stage set, with deep shadow and pools of light. Kings and gods rise above you in black and grey stone. Some stand taller than three people. Others show gods with the heads of falcons, rams, and lions. Among them sits a well-known figure of Ramesses II, calm and still. The scale of these works is meant to make a person feel small.

Look closely at the carving. The Egyptians worked very hard stone with simple tools. The edges are clean. The faces are smooth. Each pose follows strict rules that lasted for centuries. A king must stand a certain way. A god must hold certain signs. Once you learn the rules, you can read the figures like words.

One whole room holds an ancient temple. It was cut from solid rock in Nubia, far up the Nile. In the 1960s a new dam threatened to drown many such monuments. Egypt gave this small temple, called Ellesiya, to Italy in thanks for help with the rescue. Stone by stone it was carried north and rebuilt. Now it stands indoors, its carved gods watching over a quiet northern room.

The museum was rebuilt and redesigned in 2015. The new layout guides you through time, from the oldest pieces to the latest. Glass and soft light replaced the crowded old cases. The aim was to let each object breathe and to tell a clear story. Take your time. The path is long but well marked.

Remember as you walk that all of this traveled far. Every statue and scroll was carried by ship and cart from Egypt to this northern city. For two hundred years people here have cared for them, studied them, and kept them safe. The desert sun is far away, yet its world survives in these quiet rooms.

When you step back outside, the street noise returns. Cars, voices, the ordinary day. But something stays with you. You have looked into the faces of people who lived before almost all of recorded history. They wanted to be remembered. In this cool, careful building, far from home, they still are.
