The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Cars hiss past on a wide road lined with grass verges and tall trees. The houses here sit back from the traffic, each one tidy, each one ordinary. And this one, a cream-and-brick semi-detached house with a small front garden, looks almost exactly like its neighbours. That ordinariness is the whole point. You are standing outside 251 Menlove Avenue, in the Woolton area of south Liverpool. This is the house where John Lennon spent most of his childhood and teenage years.

The house has a name, as many British homes of its era do. It is called Mendips, after the Mendip Hills in the southwest of England. It was built in the 1930s, during a wave of suburban building that spread across Britain between the wars. These houses promised fresh air, gardens, and respectability. Look at the bay windows, the pebbledash, the leaded glass. This was a comfortable, middle-class home — not the working-class terrace that many people imagine when they think of the Beatles.

John came to live here as a small boy, in the mid-1940s. His parents' marriage had broken down. His father was away at sea, and his mother, Julia, was not able to raise him at that point. So he was taken in by Julia's older sister, Mimi Smith, and her husband George. They had no children of their own. Mimi was strict, careful, and fiercely protective. George was gentler, and he is often remembered as the one who encouraged John's love of words and pictures.

Inside, this was a house of books. John read constantly as a boy, and he drew cartoons and wrote comic stories. That playful, absurd humour later appeared in his books and in many of his lyrics. It is easy to picture a small boy upstairs in the front bedroom, filling notebooks with nonsense verse. Then, in 1955, George Smith died suddenly. John was still a young teenager. It was the first of several losses that shaped him.

Now look at the front of the house, at the little glass porch by the door. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Mimi did not like loud music in the house. So the teenage John was often sent out to the porch to practise his guitar. The glass and the hard surfaces gave the sound a bright, ringing echo. Lennon himself spoke fondly of that porch. One of the most important musical careers of the twentieth century was partly rehearsed in a space the size of a cupboard.

Mimi's view of music is part of the legend of this house. A line often attributed to her says that the guitar is all very well, but you will never make a living from it. Whether she said it in exactly those words, nobody can be fully sure. But the story fits. She wanted John to have a safe, respectable future. He wanted something else entirely. That quiet tug of war, between a careful aunt and a restless boy, played out in these rooms.

During these years, John grew close to his mother again. Julia lived not far away, and she was musical and free-spirited. She taught him banjo chords and encouraged him to play. Then, in July 1958, came the hardest moment of all. After visiting Mimi here at Mendips, Julia was struck by a car on Menlove Avenue and killed. John was seventeen. As you look at this road, with its steady traffic, it is worth holding that moment quietly in mind.

The surrounding neighbourhood is full of places from John's early life. A short walk away is Saint Peter's Church in Woolton. At a church garden fete there in July 1957, John's skiffle group, the Quarrymen, played a set. That same day, a teenage Paul McCartney was introduced to him. A little further on is Strawberry Field, the gates of a former children's home where John played as a child. Years later, it gave its name to one of the Beatles' best-known songs.

John lived at Mendips until the early 1960s, as the Beatles began to rise. Mimi stayed on for a few more years and later moved away to the south coast of England. The house then passed through other owners and became a private family home again. For a long time, fans came simply to stand at the gate, just as you may be doing now. They took photographs and left quietly. The house itself kept its secrets behind its net curtains.

In the early 2000s, Yoko Ono, John's widow, bought the house. She gave it to the National Trust, the British charity that cares for historic homes and landscapes. It was an unusual gift. The National Trust is better known for grand country estates than for ordinary suburban semis. But that is exactly why it matters. The house shows how a modest family home, and an ordinary childhood, can sit at the start of an extraordinary story.

The Trust restored the interior to look as it might have done in the 1950s. Visitors on the official tours see a careful recreation of the rooms John knew — the kitchen, the sitting room, the small bedroom upstairs. Much of the furniture is chosen to match the period rather than being original. A custodian lives in the house, which helps it feel lived-in rather than frozen. Look also for the blue plaque on the front wall, marking John's time here.

If you want to go inside, you need to book a guided tour in advance through the National Trust. You cannot simply knock on the door. These tours usually combine Mendips with Paul McCartney's childhood home on Forthlin Road, in another part of south Liverpool. Seeing both houses on the same day is a quiet surprise in itself. They are very different homes, and they show how different the two young songwriters' family lives really were.

In the meantime, take one more look from the pavement. Notice the porch where a teenager once practised chords. Notice the bedroom window above it. Notice the trees along the road, and the sound of traffic, steady and unremarkable. Many people walk past this house every day without a second glance. As you walk on, perhaps toward Woolton or Strawberry Field, you might find yourself listening to the street a little differently.
