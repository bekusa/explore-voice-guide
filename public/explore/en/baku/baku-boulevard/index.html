The guide

The story

Listen first. A steady wind comes off the water, pulling at your sleeves and carrying a faint salt edge. Somewhere nearby, the wheels of a child's scooter rattle over stone paving. Gulls cry above the waterline. The air here moves almost all the time, and it smells of sea, warm stone, and, now and then, roasted chestnuts or sweet tea from a kiosk. You are standing on Baku Boulevard, the long seaside promenade that curves along the bay of the Caspian Sea.

That sea is worth a moment of thought. The Caspian is the largest enclosed body of water on Earth. It has no natural outlet to any ocean. Geographers argue over whether to call it a sea or a lake, and the answer depends on who you ask. Look out at the horizon. Far out there, beyond sight, sit oil platforms. For more than a century, the wealth under this water has shaped everything you see on shore.

The boulevard began in the early twentieth century, in 1909. At that time Baku was in the middle of an oil boom. Fortunes rose fast, and wealthy oil families wanted the growing city to have a proper seafront park. Here is the first surprise. This shoreline was once dry and bare, with poor, salty ground. Locals say that soil for the first gardens was brought in from elsewhere, and that greenery had to be coaxed into this harsh, windy place with real effort.

That wind has a name — two names, in fact. Baku is often called the City of Winds. The cold, strong northern wind is called the Khazri. The warmer, gentler southern wind is called the Gilavar. Residents talk about them almost like old neighbours with different moods. On a Khazri day, the boulevard feels raw and wild. On a Gilavar day, it feels soft, and families stay out late. Notice which one is blowing as you walk.

Now turn your back to the water and look inland. The road beside the park is Neftchilar Avenue. The name means Oil Workers' Avenue, and it is a fair summary of the city's story. Along it stand grand stone buildings from the oil boom years. Their facades are carved from pale, honey-coloured limestone. Balconies, columns, and decorative window frames show how much money once flowed through Baku, and how much it wanted to look like a European capital.

Higher up, on the hill behind the city, you can see three tall towers shaped like curving flames. These are the Flame Towers, built in the twenty-first century. Their shape refers to fire, an old symbol of Azerbaijan. The country is sometimes called the Land of Fire, because natural gas seeps from the ground in some places and burns on its own. After dark, the towers' glass surfaces become screens of moving light. From the boulevard, they seem to burn over the whole bay.

The promenade itself has changed a great deal. In the Soviet decades it was a well-loved city park. Since the early 2000s, it has been rebuilt and greatly extended along the shoreline. Today it runs for several kilometres. The newer sections have wide walkways, fountains, cafes, lawns, and long rows of benches facing the sea. Walk for a while and you will see the design shift — older, shaded gardens in some places, open modern plazas in others.

One feature surprises many visitors. Along the boulevard there is a small area of canals called Little Venice. Narrow waterways wind between little islands and footbridges. You can ride a small boat through them in just a few minutes. It is a playful, slightly unexpected idea in a city on the edge of a dry, windswept region. Children love it, and couples often use it as a quiet pause in a long walk.

Near the water you may also notice a tall, slender metal tower from the Soviet period. It was originally a parachute tower, built so that people could practise jumps. It no longer serves that purpose. Today it carries a large digital display, often showing the time and other information to people walking below. It is a small reminder that this park has served many generations, each one with different ideas of how to spend a free afternoon.

Further along, a much newer landmark rises: a large observation wheel, known locally as the Baku Eye. From the top, riders see the full curve of the bay, the old walled city, and the towers on the hill. Nearby stands a striking building designed to look like a rolled carpet. It houses the Azerbaijan Carpet Museum. Carpet weaving is one of the oldest crafts in this region, and the building's shape is a quiet tribute to that tradition.

The boulevard is also very close to the Old City, called Icherisheher, with its stone walls and narrow lanes. From several points on the promenade you can see the Maiden Tower, a thick, rounded stone tower inside the old walls. Its age and original purpose are still debated by historians. Many legends surround it, and locals will happily tell you different versions. The contrast is the point — ancient stone on one side, glass and steel on the other.

But the best part of the boulevard is not any single monument. It is the people. In the evening, families stroll slowly with grandparents and toddlers. Teenagers gather by the fountains. Older men sit on benches and play backgammon, called nard here, slapping the pieces down with sharp clicks. Vendors sell balloons, corn, and cotton candy. Couples lean on the railing and watch the light change on the water.

As the sun sets, the sea turns from grey-blue to silver, then to a deep, dark colour. Lights come on along the promenade, one stretch at a time. The Flame Towers begin to glow on the hill. The wind usually continues, but it feels different at night — cooler, more steady. This is when the boulevard feels most like the living room of the city, a place where Baku comes to breathe.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. Notice the stone under your feet, the curve of the railing, the long line of the bay. Feel which wind is blowing today, Khazri or Gilavar. Over a century ago, this was bare and salty ground. Now it is a garden by the sea, shaped by oil money, Soviet planners, and modern builders. And as you walk away, listen... the waves and the voices will follow you for a while.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come at sunset to watch the Flame Towers light up.
Bring a windproof layer, even on warm sunny days.
Combine your walk with a visit to the Old City.
Ride the Baku Eye for a full view of the bay.
Take a short boat ride through the Little Venice canals.
