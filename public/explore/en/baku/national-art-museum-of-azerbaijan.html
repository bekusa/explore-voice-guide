The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Traffic hums somewhere behind you, and a breeze carries the faint salt smell of the Caspian Sea up from the shore. Under your feet, the pavement is warm stone. In front of you, pale walls and tall windows rise in the calm, ordered style of the late nineteenth century. You are standing at the National Art Museum of Azerbaijan, in Baku's Sabail district. This is the part of the city that holds the old walled town, the seafront boulevard, and many of the grand houses built during the oil boom.

The oil boom explains a great deal about this place. In the second half of the nineteenth century, Baku became one of the most important oil centres in the world. Money arrived quickly. Engineers, merchants and industrialists from many countries came to the city. They built mansions in European styles, often with stone facades, carved details and generous windows. The museum lives inside this world. Its collection is housed in historic buildings from that period, and one of them was originally a private residence. So before you see a single painting, the architecture is already telling you about the city that built it.

Take a closer look at the facade. The local stone here is a soft, honey-coloured limestone, quarried around the Absheron Peninsula, the strip of land that Baku sits on. It is easy to carve, so builders used it for cornices, window frames and decorative panels. Notice how the light changes on it. In the morning it can look almost cream. Late in the afternoon it turns warmer, closer to gold. Those small shifts are part of what makes walking through Sabail so pleasant.

Now for some history. The museum took shape in the 1930s, during the Soviet period. It began as an art department and later became an independent institution. Over the following decades, its collection grew steadily through purchases, donations and transfers from other museums. Today it holds many thousands of objects, commonly given as more than fifteen thousand. That makes it the largest collection of fine and decorative art in the country. Only part of it can be shown at any one time.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The museum carries the name of Rustam Mustafayev. He was not a painter of easel pictures in the usual sense. He was a stage designer, an artist who created sets and visual worlds for the theatre. He also played an important role in the early life of this museum. Naming a national art collection after a theatre designer is unusual. It is a quiet reminder that, in Azerbaijan, theatre and visual art grew up very close together.

Inside, the rooms are generally arranged by region and by period. One large part of the collection is Azerbaijani art, from older decorative traditions to painting of the twentieth century. Another part brings together art from Europe, Russia and parts of Asia. Moving between them feels a little like crossing borders. One room may hold oil portraits in heavy gilt frames. The next may hold delicate metalwork, ceramics, or textiles with patterns repeated in careful rhythm.

Look especially for the carpets and textiles. Azerbaijan has one of the strongest carpet-weaving traditions in the region. Different areas developed their own patterns, colours and knots. Some designs are bold and geometric, with medallions, hooks and stars. Others use flowing plant forms. Stand close and you can see the individual knots, each one tied by hand. Then step back, and the whole pattern comes together at once. That change in scale is one of the quiet pleasures of these galleries.

The manuscript and miniature tradition is another highlight. Miniatures are small, detailed paintings, usually made to illustrate books of poetry or history. In this part of the world, they were often linked to the great centres of Persian-language culture, such as Tabriz, a city that today lies in northern Iran. The figures are flat, the colours are bright, and every corner of the page is filled. Look for tiny details: a bird in a tree, the pattern on a robe, a gold line around a horizon.

Then the story moves into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Azerbaijani artists began working more in European forms, especially oil painting on canvas. You will find portraits, landscapes and scenes of daily life. Some painters recorded villages, markets and the mountains of the Caucasus. Others looked closely at Baku itself, with its oil derricks, its sea and its old streets. Names such as Azim Azimzade, known for his satirical drawings, and Sattar Bahlulzade, known for his glowing landscapes, are important here. Watch for their labels as you walk.

The European galleries add a different texture. Here you may see works from Western European schools, alongside Russian painting and decorative objects. Some arrived through collectors connected to Baku's wealthy families. Others came through Soviet-era redistribution of art between museums. It is worth asking yourself, as you walk, how each object travelled here. A painting in a Baku museum has often had a longer journey than the visitor looking at it.

Notice the rooms as well as the art. Because these buildings were not designed as modern galleries, the spaces have their own character. There are tall ceilings, old doorways and staircases, and windows that let daylight fall across the floor. Sometimes a room feels more like a drawing room in a family house than a museum hall. That domestic scale makes the experience more intimate. You are close to the works, and there is rarely a crowd between you and the canvas.

A small practical thought. Give yourself time, and do not try to see everything. Choose one tradition that interests you, perhaps the carpets, the miniatures, or the modern painters, and slow down there. Read a few labels in full. Return to a work you passed quickly. Museums like this one reward patience more than speed. Many visitors leave remembering one piece very clearly, and that is a good result.

When you step back outside, pause on the pavement again. Look at the stone facade one more time, now that you have seen what it protects. Listen to the city around you: the cars, the voices, perhaps a gull from the direction of the sea. A few streets away, the old walls of the inner city still stand. Between those walls, this museum, and the modern towers on the hills, Baku shows many centuries at once. Take that thought with you as you walk on.
