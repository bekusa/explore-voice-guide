The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the stone. It is cool, slightly rough, and the colour of pale sand. Above you, the wall curves away in a smooth cylinder, rising higher than the rooftops around it. Somewhere nearby, a cat moves across a sunlit step. The wind from the Caspian Sea finds its way through these narrow lanes, and it carries a faint smell of salt. You are standing at the foot of the Maiden Tower, in the heart of old Baku.

This quarter is called Icherisheher, which means the Inner City. It is the walled old town of Baku, and it sits within the district of Sabail. Thick fortress walls still wrap around it. Inside, the streets twist and narrow, climb and drop. The tower stands near the lower edge of the old town, close to where the land slopes down toward the sea. For centuries, it was the tallest structure that sailors saw as they approached the shore.

In the year 2000, UNESCO added the walled city of Baku to its World Heritage list. The listing names this tower together with the Palace of the Shirvanshahs, the old royal complex further up the hill. The Shirvanshahs were a dynasty of rulers who governed this region for many centuries. So the tower is not a lone monument. It is one part of a whole medieval city that people still live in today.

Now, here is the first surprise. Nobody agrees on exactly how old this tower is. Many historians link its main construction to the twelfth century. Others argue that the lower part is far older, and that the medieval builders worked on top of an earlier structure. The debate is still open. For a monument this famous, that is unusual. It means that when you look at these stones, you are looking at a real puzzle.

Look at the shape of the tower from the side. It is round, but it is not a simple cylinder. A long, solid projection runs down one side, like a thick rib or a buttress. From some angles, the tower looks almost like a giant keyhole. This shape is rare. Defensive towers usually have a plain, regular form. The odd profile is one reason scholars keep returning to the question of what this building was really for.

Some people say it was a defensive tower, the last place of safety in a siege. Others suggest it was a watchtower or a signal tower, where fire could be lit to warn ships and the city. A third idea links it to ancient religious practice, perhaps to fire worship, which has deep roots in this land. Azerbaijan is sometimes called the land of fire, because natural gas once burned openly from the ground here. None of these theories is fully proven.

The name itself carries a story. In Azerbaijani, the tower is called Qız Qalası. Qız means girl or maiden, and qala means tower or fortress. One common explanation is practical rather than romantic. In this reading, maiden means untouched, a fortress that no enemy ever captured. It is a little like calling a mountain a virgin peak because no one has climbed it. This explanation is popular, but it is also an interpretation, not a certainty.

Then there is the legend, and locals tell it often. According to the most popular tale, a ruler wanted to marry his own daughter. She asked him to build a tall tower first, hoping to delay him. When the tower was finished, she climbed to the top and threw herself into the sea below. It is a sad story, and it is folklore, not history. But it has shaped how people here feel about this place for generations.

That story has travelled far beyond these walls. It has inspired poems, paintings, and stage works in Azerbaijan. The tower has also appeared on national currency and on countless souvenirs. You may notice its outline on signs and logos as you walk through the city. In a sense, this round stone building has become a symbol of Baku itself, much like a famous bridge or clock tower in other capitals.

Inside, the tower is now a museum. The interior is divided into several levels, stacked one above the other. A narrow stone staircase connects them, and it hugs the inside of the thick outer wall. The walls are so deep that the rooms feel quiet, even when the lanes outside are full of voices. Small window openings let in thin beams of light. On each level, displays explain the history of the tower and the city.

Here is the second surprise, and it waits at the top. When you reach the roof, the whole of Baku opens around you. To one side, you see the flat roofs and domes of the old town. Beyond them rise modern towers of glass and steel. And in front of you lies the Caspian Sea, wide and grey-blue. Many people believe the shoreline once came much closer to this tower than it does today.

From up there, you can also trace the old city walls. Follow their line with your eyes, and you will see how the medieval town was shaped and protected. You may spot the minaret of an old mosque, small courtyards, and the long seaside boulevard below. It is one of the best places in Baku to understand how the old and the new city sit side by side. The contrast is sharp, and it is worth a slow look.

Now come back down to the street, and take a moment before you move on. Stand back a few steps and look up again. Notice how the pale stone changes colour as the light moves, warmer in the evening, flatter at midday. Listen to the sounds around you, the footsteps, the shop doors, the sea wind. This tower has watched this corner of the world for many centuries. As you walk away into the narrow lanes, it will stay behind you, still standing, still keeping its secrets.
