The guide

The story

Run your eyes along the pale stone façade before you go inside. The surface is smooth and creamy, carved with careful detail, and it holds the warm light of Baku's sun. Traffic moves past on the street behind you, and somewhere close by the wind off the Caspian Sea pushes through the trees. This building was not made to be a museum. It was made to be a home, and a very grand one.

You are standing at the National Museum of History of Azerbaijan. Its collections tell the story of this land from its earliest settlements to the modern era. The house that holds them tells a second story, one about oil, money, and a remarkable man. Before we look at the objects, it helps to understand the walls around them, because here the building is as much an exhibit as anything in the glass cases.

This was the mansion of Haji Zeynalabdin Taghiyev. In the late nineteenth century, Baku became one of the great oil cities of the world. Fortunes appeared almost overnight. Taghiyev came from modest roots. Local accounts say he started as a stonemason's apprentice. He invested in oil land on the Absheron Peninsula, the flat, dry tongue of land that reaches out into the sea around Baku. When oil came up from his wells, he became one of the richest men in the Russian Empire.

With that wealth, he built this house at the turn of the twentieth century. The design is usually credited to the Polish architect Józef Gosławski, who shaped much of Baku's look in that period. The style is European on the outside, with columns, balconies, and classical proportions. That choice was deliberate. Baku's new oil elite wanted their city to look like Paris or Vienna. The stone itself is local limestone, the same honey-coloured material you see across the city centre.

Now here is the first surprise. Taghiyev is remembered in Azerbaijan less for his money than for how he spent it. In 1901 he opened a school for Muslim girls in Baku. It is often described as the first secular school of its kind in the Muslim East. At that time, the idea was controversial. Many conservative voices opposed it. Taghiyev went ahead anyway, and families still speak his name with respect for that decision.

He also funded a water pipeline that brought clean mountain water to Baku from the Shollar springs, far to the north. Before this, fresh water was a constant problem in the dry city. He paid for the translation of the Quran into the Azerbaijani language, and he supported theatre, students, and charities. So as you walk through his rooms, keep this in mind. You are inside the home of a man who tried to change how his society lived.

Inside, the mood changes from room to room. Each hall was decorated in a different style, almost like a tour of world fashions under one roof. Look up as you move. The ceilings carry gilding, painted panels, and carved plaster. Many visitors focus only on the display cases and miss the decoration above their heads. Slow down in the doorways. The transitions between rooms show how carefully the house was planned for guests and for display.

One hall stands out above the others. It is often called the Oriental Hall, and its design draws on Moorish architecture, the Islamic style of medieval Spain, best known from the Alhambra palace in Granada. Here you see delicate arches, dense geometric patterns, and rich colour. After the European rooms, it feels like stepping into a different world. Notice how the patterns repeat and interlock, with no single focal point. Your eye keeps moving across the surface.

Then history turned. In 1920 the Red Army entered Baku, and Azerbaijan became part of the Soviet Union. Private fortunes were taken by the state. Taghiyev's mansion was nationalised, and the history museum was founded in the same period. Taghiyev himself died in the early 1920s at his country house outside the city. His home became a public space. There is a quiet irony in that. The rooms built for one family's prestige now belong to everyone who walks in.

The collections cover a very long span of time. You can find archaeological finds from ancient settlements, tools and pottery from early farming communities, and objects from the medieval kingdoms that rose and fell in this region. There are coins, weapons, jewellery, and household items. Azerbaijan sits on old routes between Persia, Russia, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, and you can see those many influences meeting in the material on display.

Pay attention to the ethnographic sections too. These show how people dressed, cooked, wove, and worked across the regions of Azerbaijan. Carpets and textiles carry strong local traditions, with patterns that differ from one area to another. Look at the metalwork and the embroidered clothing. These everyday objects often say more about ordinary life than the grand weapons or royal pieces. They show the hands of farmers, weavers, and craftsmen across many generations.

Here is a second detail worth holding onto. This house sits only a short walk from the old walled city, Icherisheher, with its medieval lanes and the Maiden Tower. Within a few minutes on foot, you move from centuries-old stone streets to an oil baron's European palace. That short distance shows how quickly Baku changed. In just a few decades, oil money pulled a small fortress town into the age of electric light, tramways, and grand boulevards.

When you step back outside, pause on the pavement for a moment. Look again at the façade, now that you know whose house this was and what he paid for. The same stone that greeted you at the start now carries a fuller story. Listen to the city around you, the cars, the voices, the sea wind. Taghiyev's Baku was loud and new and full of ambition. In many ways, it still is. Take that thought with you as you walk on.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least ninety minutes to see both rooms and collections.
Look up in every hall; ceilings hold much of the decoration.
Combine your visit with a walk to the nearby Old City.
Ask at the desk whether photography needs a separate permit.
Visit the European rooms first, then the Oriental Hall.
