The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the stone. It is warm when the sun has been on it, and slightly rough, like coarse sand pressed into a block. This is local limestone, cut from the land around Baku, and it has a soft honey colour that turns almost gold in late afternoon. Above you, the wall rises in a steady line, broken by rounded towers. Listen for a moment. On one side, traffic moves along a modern boulevard. On the other side, the narrow lanes of the Old City are quieter.

You are standing at the edge of Icherisheher, which means the Inner City. For centuries, this wall decided who was inside Baku and who was outside it. Today the line still holds. Cars, glass towers and wide avenues stay on one side. Stone houses, mosques, small courtyards and crooked alleys stay on the other. Few cities keep their old boundary this clear. Here, you can walk across it in a few steps.

Most of what you see dates back to the twelfth century, when this coast was ruled by the Shirvanshahs. They were a long line of local rulers who controlled the region of Shirvan, in what is now northern and eastern Azerbaijan. Baku became more and more important to them over time. A strong wall made sense. The city sat on a natural harbour of the Caspian Sea, and that harbour brought trade, wealth and danger at the same time.

The wall was built to face threats from both land and sea. Historians generally agree that it once had a double line of defence, with an outer wall in front of the inner one. The inner wall is the one that survives today. Much of the outer line is gone. If you imagine a second barrier of stone a short distance in front of you, you begin to see how serious this fortress once was.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The sea used to come much closer. On the southern side, the walls once ran close to the waterline of the Caspian. Ships could approach almost to the foot of the defences. Over the centuries, the shoreline moved, and the city filled the land in between. Today a wide seaside park lies between the Old City and the water. When you stand on that side, you are standing on ground that the defenders never knew.

Look at the towers. They are mostly rounded rather than square. A curved surface was harder to break with stones and battering rams, and it gave defenders a wider view along the wall. Near the top you can see openings and narrow slits. From these places, guards could watch the approaches, shoot arrows, or drop things on attackers below. The design is practical. Every curve and gap had a job to do.

The gates are where the wall becomes most human. The best-known entrance is the Double Gate, sometimes called the Shamakhi Gate, after the old road that led inland toward the town of Shamakhi. Its pair of arches makes it easy to recognise. For centuries, merchants, camels, soldiers and families passed through openings like this one. Stand under the arch for a moment. The air feels cooler in the shade, and the street noise changes as soon as you step through.

The wall did not stay untouched. In the early nineteenth century, Baku came under the control of the Russian Empire. Later in that century, oil changed everything. Baku became one of the most important oil cities in the world, and money poured in. New streets, banks, mansions and theatres grew outside the old walls. Parts of the fortifications were taken down, opened up or rebuilt as the city spread. The wall you see today carries the marks of that period too.

Here is a second surprise. Much of the elegant architecture just outside the wall, with its carved stone balconies and tall facades, is not medieval at all. It belongs to the oil boom of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. So when you look from inside the wall to the outside, you are often looking from the old Shirvanshah city to the city that oil built. Two very different eras face each other across a single line of stone.

In 2000, the walled Old City was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, together with the Palace of the Shirvanshahs and the Maiden Tower. UNESCO is the United Nations body that recognises places of outstanding value to humanity. The listing covers the whole historic ensemble, not only the famous monuments. The wall matters because it holds everything together. Without it, the Old City would simply be another neighbourhood. With it, the city keeps a clear shape and a clear story.

Restoration has been a long and careful process. Old stone weathers in the wind and salty air of the Caspian, and Baku is known for strong winds. Look closely and you will often see a difference between older blocks and newer ones. Some stones are darker, smoother or more worn. Others are sharp-edged and pale. This mix shows centuries of repair. It is not a flaw. It is the normal life of a wall that people have used, damaged and cared for.

Walk a short distance along the outside of the wall if you can. The view changes every few metres. A tower appears, then a gate, then a stretch of plain stone with a café or a small garden at its base. Inside, the lanes turn and narrow. You may hear the call to prayer from a nearby mosque, the clink of tea glasses, or the voice of a shopkeeper inviting you in to look at carpets. The wall frames all of this.

At night, the wall takes on a different character. Lights shine up along the stone, and the honey colour turns warm orange against the dark sky. The modern towers outside light up too, sometimes with moving patterns. Standing between the two, you can feel how the city has grown around its old core without erasing it. Families walk along the paths. Couples sit on the benches. The old defences have become a place to stroll.

Before you move on, look up once more at the line where the stone meets the sky. For hundreds of years, people stood on top of this wall and watched for ships, riders and storms. Now the threats are gone, but the wall still marks the edge of something. As you walk away, notice the moment the ground under your feet changes from old stone to smooth pavement. That small step is the border between two Bakus, and you have just crossed it.

Oil-boom mansions facing the wall from outside.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the outer perimeter to see towers from different angles.
Come in late afternoon when the limestone glows gold.
Return after dark to see the walls lit up.
Bring a light jacket, as Baku winds can be strong.
Wear comfortable shoes for the uneven stone lanes inside.
