The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The stone under you is pale, sandy limestone, worn smooth by centuries of shoes and sandals. It holds the warmth of the sun long after the afternoon has passed. Around you, the walls are the same honey colour as the ground. On a windy day, and Baku has many, you can hear the air move through the narrow lanes of the old town below. Up here, at the top of the hill, the city noise fades into a low murmur.

You are standing in the Palace of the Shirvanshahs, in the heart of Icherisheher, the walled Old City of Baku. The Shirvanshahs were a line of rulers who governed the region of Shirvan, in what is now Azerbaijan, for many centuries. Their story is long, and it moved between several cities. This palace belongs to its later chapter, when the court settled here on the shore of the Caspian Sea. Most of what you see dates to the fifteenth century.

The ruler most closely linked with this complex is Shirvanshah Khalilullah the First. During his long reign, the court moved its centre from the inland city of Shamakhi to Baku. Many accounts connect the move to the earthquakes that troubled Shamakhi. Baku also had clear advantages. It sat on the sea, it had trade, and it had strong walls. A ruler who builds on the highest point of a fortified port city is sending a message about security and control.

Notice that the complex is not one single building. It is a group of structures arranged on terraces that step down the slope. There is the main residential palace, a court pavilion, a mosque with its minaret, a family burial vault, a bathhouse, and a small mausoleum for a scholar. These buildings were not all finished at the same moment. They grew over time, and that is why the courtyards feel slightly uneven, as if each one has its own logic.

Here is the first surprise. This was never a palace of vast halls and mirrored ballrooms. The rooms are modest, the corridors are narrow, and the decoration is concentrated in a few places. The builders put their effort into carved stone portals, the entrances, rather than into large interiors. In this tradition, the doorway was where a building spoke to the visitor. So, as you walk, look less at the walls and more at the doors.

The finest example is the Divankhana, usually described as the court or council pavilion. It sits in its own small courtyard, ringed by a stone arcade. The pavilion itself is octagonal, with a domed top. Its portal is covered in deep, interlacing carving: geometric patterns, plant forms, and Arabic inscriptions cut into the limestone. The stone here is soft enough to carve finely, and the craftsmen used that freely. Stand close, then step back, and the pattern changes with distance.

Historians still discuss exactly what the Divankhana was for. Some describe it as a hall for meetings and judgment. Others suggest it may have had a ceremonial or memorial purpose. Parts of the decoration also appear unfinished, which adds to the puzzle. It is a good reminder that even well-studied monuments keep some questions open. The building is clearly important. Exactly how the court used it is less certain.

Walk on to the burial vault of the Shirvanshah family. This was where members of the ruling household were laid to rest. Its entrance carries another carved portal with inscriptions. There is a well-known story told to many visitors here. Guides often say that the name of the architect is hidden in the decoration, written in a way that must be read in reverse, as if in a mirror. Whether that reading is certain or not, it is a lovely idea: a craftsman quietly signing his work where only careful eyes would find it.

Nearby stands the palace mosque, with a slim minaret rising above the roofline. An inscription on the minaret links it to the reign of Khalilullah, in the 1440s. The mosque is plain compared with the portals around it. That plainness is part of the design. Prayer spaces in this tradition often value calm and proportion over ornament. Listen for a moment. Even with visitors around, the stone walls absorb sound and keep the space quiet.

Down the slope you will find a smaller mausoleum, connected with Seyid Yahya Bakuvi. He was a Sufi scholar who lived and taught at the court in the fifteenth century. Sufism is the mystical, inward-looking tradition within Islam, focused on personal closeness to God. His tomb has an eight-sided body and a pointed roof. Small openings let in thin beams of light. Locals have long treated the site with respect, and it is sometimes called the dervish mausoleum.

Here is the second surprise. Look for a large carved gateway called Murad's Gate. It is much later than most of the complex, from the late sixteenth century, during a period of Ottoman control in the region. So the palace you see is not frozen in one moment. It carries layers from different powers. Later, after the Russian Empire took Baku in the nineteenth century, parts of the complex were used for military purposes. Restoration work in the twentieth century brought it back into its present form, and it now serves as a museum.

In the lower courtyard, look also for a row of carved stone blocks. Many of these come from a medieval fortress that once stood in the Bay of Baku and was later covered by the sea. The stones carry long friezes of inscriptions and figures. They were recovered from the water and brought here for display. In 2000, the palace, together with the Walled City and the Maiden Tower, was listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site.

Now take a breath before you leave. Look up at the domes and the minaret against the sky. Look down at the steps, worn into soft curves by centuries of feet. As you walk back into the lanes of the Old City, notice how the same pale stone continues into the houses, the shops, and the walls around you. The palace is not separate from Baku. It is the same stone, the same hill, the same wind from the Caspian, simply shaped with more care.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to enjoy the courtyards quietly.
Wear shoes with grip for the smooth stone steps.
Walk the terraces downhill to follow the site's layout.
Allow extra time in the Divankhana courtyard.
Combine your visit with a walk to the Maiden Tower.
