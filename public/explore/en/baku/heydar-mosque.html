The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look up. Out here in Binagadi, the wind off the Absheron Peninsula rarely stops. It carries dust, a faint smell of the sea, and the low hum of traffic on the roads around you. Then your eyes reach the mosque. Pale stone rises out of a flat, open landscape, and four slender minarets climb into the sky. Welcome to Heydar Mosque, one of the largest places of worship in Azerbaijan, and a building that tries to connect modern Baku with a much older idea of beauty.

First, a little orientation. You are not in the old walled city that most visitors see first. Binagadi is a district to the north of central Baku, a place of residential blocks, wide roads and patches of dry, open ground. For much of the twentieth century this part of the peninsula was shaped by the oil industry, not by monuments. That makes the mosque feel even more striking. It stands here as a deliberate statement, placed among ordinary neighbourhoods rather than among the tourist sights downtown.

The mosque opened in 2014. It carries the name of Heydar Aliyev, who served as the third president of independent Azerbaijan and who remains a central figure in the country's public life. You will see his name on streets, parks and buildings across the country. Naming a large mosque after him was a choice with a clear message: a national leader linked with a national place of faith. Whatever a visitor thinks about politics, the building is first and foremost an active mosque, and local people come here to pray.

Now look closely at the shape of the building. The design draws on the architectural traditions of Shirvan, a historic region of Azerbaijan that once had its own dynasty of rulers. If you have visited the Palace of the Shirvanshahs inside Baku's Old City, you may recognise the family resemblance. Think of carved stone, pointed arches, careful geometry and a sense of calm weight. Here, those old forms are rebuilt at a much larger scale, with modern engineering hidden behind a traditional face.

Here is something many visitors miss. The pale colour of the stone is not only decoration. On the Absheron Peninsula, light-coloured limestone has been the main building material for centuries. You can see it in the old houses of the Old City and in village mosques across the peninsula. So the colour connects this new building with very old local habits. Run your eyes along the walls and notice how the stone changes during the day — creamy in the morning, almost gold in late afternoon, and soft grey under cloud.

The four minarets are the most obvious feature. In many Islamic traditions, a minaret is the tower from which the call to prayer is given. Older mosques in this region often had only one minaret, or sometimes none at all. Four towers is a bolder, more monumental choice. It places the mosque in conversation with large mosques across the wider Muslim world. From a distance, the minarets work as a landmark. You can often see them from the roads of Binagadi long before you arrive.

Between the minarets sits the central dome. Domes have a long history in mosque architecture. They create a large open space for prayer without many columns to block the view. From outside, the dome crowns the building and gives it a clear centre. From inside, it creates a feeling of height and quiet. If you are able to enter, stand under it for a moment. Notice how sound behaves — footsteps soften, voices drop, and the space seems to slow everyone down.

As you walk around the outside, look for the inscriptions. Like many mosques, this building uses Arabic calligraphy as decoration. In Islamic art, the written word often takes the place of human or animal images. Verses from the Quran, the holy book of Islam, become patterns in stone. Even if you cannot read Arabic, you can follow the rhythm of the letters. Notice how the lines stretch, curve and repeat, almost like a melody written in stone.

A second detail worth knowing. Azerbaijan is a majority Muslim country, and most believers here follow the Shia tradition of Islam. At the same time, the country has a long secular history, shaped by decades of Soviet rule in the twentieth century. During that period, many mosques were closed, damaged or used for other purposes. Building large new mosques after independence was one way the country reconnected with its religious heritage. This mosque belongs to that story of return and rebuilding.

So what should you expect as a visitor? This is a working place of prayer, not a museum. Dress modestly. That means covered shoulders and legs for everyone, and a headscarf for women. You will remove your shoes before entering the prayer hall. Move quietly, and avoid walking in front of people who are praying. Friday is the main day of communal prayer, so it may be busier then. At other times, the courtyard and grounds are often calm, with only a few people coming and going.

Photography is usually possible outside, but inside it is polite to ask first, and never photograph people at prayer without permission. The open space around the building is helpful here. You can step back far enough to fit all four minarets into one frame. Early morning and late afternoon give the warmest light on the stone. At midday, the Absheron sun can be harsh, and the pale walls may look flat and white in pictures.

Take a moment to notice the people around you as well. You might see families walking slowly across the grounds, older men talking near the entrance, or a few visitors from other parts of Baku. Some come to pray. Others come simply to look at the building or to spend a quiet hour away from the city centre. That mix is part of the character of this place. It is both a religious centre and a new public landmark for the district.

It is also worth thinking about scale. Most historic mosques in Baku are small, built into narrow streets and surrounded by houses. This one is the opposite. It stands in open space, designed to be seen from far away. That contrast tells you something about modern Azerbaijan. The old forms of Shirvan architecture are still respected. But they are now used to express national confidence, at a size that older builders could not have imagined.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look up at the minarets. Feel the wind again, and listen. Perhaps you will hear the call to prayer, or perhaps only traffic and birds. Look at the pale stone, the same kind of stone Baku has used for centuries. As you walk away into the streets of Binagadi, turn around once. Watch the four towers grow smaller behind you — a very old idea, built again for a new century.
