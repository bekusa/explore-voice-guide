The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, on the high ground above Baku, the wind is almost never still. It moves through the grass and the trees, it tugs at your jacket, and it carries a faint taste of salt from the Caspian Sea below. Now look up. A single pale shaft of concrete rises straight into that wind, narrowing as it climbs, until it ends in a thin mast against the sky. This is the Baku TV Tower, and it is the tallest structure in Azerbaijan.

The tower stands about 310 metres tall. That is roughly the height of a hundred-storey building, though it holds only a small fraction of that in usable floors. Most of its height is solid structure and antenna. Its job is simple and practical: to send television and radio signals across the city and far beyond it. From this hill, a signal can travel over the rooftops, across the bay, and out toward the towns of the Absheron Peninsula, the long arm of land that holds Baku.

The story of this tower begins in the Soviet era. In those decades, Azerbaijan was one of the republics of the Soviet Union, and Moscow planned large broadcasting towers for many of its major cities. Work on this one began around 1979. Engineers chose reinforced concrete, the standard material for such towers, because it could carry great weight and still stand firm against strong winds. And in Baku, the wind matters. The city's name is often linked by locals to the idea of a windy place.

Then history interrupted the builders. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, the Soviet Union came apart. Azerbaijan became an independent country in 1991, in a period of war, economic hardship, and deep uncertainty. Large construction projects stopped across the region, and this tower was no exception. For several years, an unfinished concrete shaft stood on the hill. It was a visible reminder that one state had ended and another was still finding its feet.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The tower is often thought of as a Soviet monument, but it was actually finished by independent Azerbaijan. Construction was completed in 1996, five years after independence. So the structure carries two stories at once. Its design and foundations belong to the Soviet plan. Its completion belongs to the new republic. When you look at it, you are looking at a bridge between those two worlds, built in concrete.

Let your eyes travel slowly up the shaft. Near the top, the smooth column swells into a wider section. This is where the tower holds its working rooms, its viewing levels, and its restaurant. Above that, the structure becomes thinner and more technical, finishing with the antenna mast. The overall form is clean and functional. There is very little decoration here. The beauty, if you find it, comes from proportion, from height, and from the way light moves across the concrete during the day.

That restaurant is the second surprise. High up in the tower, on around the sixty-second floor, a restaurant floor slowly turns in a full circle. If you sit at a table there, the view changes without you moving at all. First the sea, then the old city, then the hills and the newer districts. A full turn takes long enough that you barely feel the motion. Diners often notice it only when they look up and realise the coastline has shifted.

Access to the upper levels can change over time, and visits may need a reservation or an organised booking. It is worth checking locally before you plan around it. But even from the ground, this hill gives you a strong sense of the city's shape. Baku curves around a wide bay. Its older heart sits close to the water, and the city climbs in terraces up the slopes behind. You are standing near the top of those terraces now.

This high ground also holds deep meaning for the people of Baku. Nearby is the Alley of Martyrs, a memorial cemetery for those who died in the tragic events of January 1990 and in the conflict that followed. Many Azerbaijani families come to this hill to remember. If you walk toward the memorial, you will notice that the mood changes. Voices drop. Fresh red carnations are often laid on the graves. It is a place for quiet respect.

From many points on this ridge, you can also see the Flame Towers, three curved glass buildings shaped like tongues of fire. Fire is an old symbol in this land. Azerbaijan has long been called a land of fire, because natural gas escapes from the ground here and has burned in some places for a very long time. Seen together, the older concrete TV Tower and the newer glass towers show two different eras of ambition on the same skyline.

Come back at night if you can. After dark, the TV Tower becomes a column of light. It is illuminated with changing colours, and on national occasions it often shows the blue, red, and green of the Azerbaijani flag. From the seafront boulevard far below, the lit tower is one of the easiest ways to find your bearings. Many locals use it exactly that way. If you can see the tower, you know which direction the hills are.

For photographs, the base of the tower is not always the best spot. The shaft is so tall that it is hard to frame from close up. Try stepping back toward the edges of the hill, or look for it later from the waterfront, where the tower rises above the city in one clean vertical line. In the late afternoon, the low sun warms the pale concrete. In the blue hour just after sunset, the lights come on while the sky still holds some colour.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Feel the wind again, the same wind the engineers planned for more than four decades ago. Look up at the long grey shaft and the thin mast at its tip. Somewhere inside, equipment is quietly sending pictures and voices across the city right now. Then turn toward the sea, and as you walk away, notice how often the tower appears again between buildings, keeping watch over Baku from its hill.
