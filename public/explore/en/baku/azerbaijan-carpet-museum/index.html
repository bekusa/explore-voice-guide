The guide

The story

Start outside, on the seafront boulevard. The wind comes off the Caspian Sea and carries a faint smell of salt. Joggers pass, and gulls cry over the water. Then look at the building in front of you. It curves in a long, low sweep, like a carpet half-rolled on a shop floor. Its surface carries a woven pattern, as if someone pressed a giant rug into the walls. Inside, the air is cooler and quieter. Here, wool is the main material.

The building opened in 2014, and its shape is deliberate. The Austrian architect Franz Janz designed it to look like a rolled carpet. Most museums give their collection a neutral box. This one makes the building a picture of what it holds. From the boulevard, the patterned panels change with the light. They look silver in the morning haze, and warmer as the sun drops toward the sea. Walk along the length of the building before you go in. It reads very differently from each end.

The museum itself is much older than this building. It was founded in 1967, when Azerbaijan was part of the Soviet Union. It is often described as one of the first museums anywhere devoted entirely to carpets. Its first home was not a modern gallery at all. It was the Juma Mosque inside the walled Old City, a short walk from where you stand now. Imagine carpets displayed under a mosque roof. In that space, many of these pieces would have felt at home.

The collection kept moving. After Azerbaijan regained independence in the early 1990s, the carpets moved to a large building that had served as the city's Lenin museum. A gallery built to honour the Soviet founder became the home of a much older national craft. Then, in 2014, the collection moved again, here to the water's edge. There is a gentle irony in this. Carpets were made to travel. They were rolled, tied, and carried across mountains and plains. This collection has done the same, in its own way.

One name stays close to this museum: Latif Karimov. He was a twentieth-century carpet artist and scholar. He spent his life studying, drawing, and cataloguing Azerbaijani carpets. The museum carries his name today. Karimov did more than collect patterns. He gave people a way to read them. He grouped the carpets of the region into distinct schools, each tied to a place. Much of the way these galleries are organised today follows the system he built.

So what are these schools? Karimov named them after regions and towns. They include Guba in the north, Shirvan, Baku and the Absheron Peninsula around this city, Ganja and Gazakh in the west, Karabakh, and Tabriz to the south. Each school has its own habits of colour, motif, and composition. A weaver in a mountain village and a weaver in a city workshop worked with different traditions. Once you know this, the galleries stop looking like a wall of rugs. They start to look like a map.

Try a small game as you walk. Look for carpets with large, bold geometric shapes — strong hooks, stepped diamonds, deep reds. Then look for carpets with dense, flowing floral designs. The bold geometry often belongs to village and mountain traditions in the west. The more curving, garden-like patterns often show a different, more urban hand. These are broad tendencies, not strict rules. But your eye will learn quickly. Within a few rooms, you may start guessing a carpet's region before you read the label.

Now look closely at how the carpets are made. There are two great families here. The first is the pile carpet. A weaver ties thousands of small knots onto the threads of a loom, one by one, and then trims them into a soft surface. The second family is the flat-woven carpet, with no pile at all. In Azerbaijan these include types with local names such as kilim, sumakh, zili, and verni. Each uses a different technique. Where a carpet edge is visible, notice how flat the weave lies.

Then think about colour. For most of their history, these carpets were dyed with natural materials. The madder plant produced reds. Indigo produced blues. Other plants, roots, and barks gave yellows and browns. Notice how a single red can shift slightly across one carpet. This happens when wool was dyed in separate batches. The weaver simply kept going with the next skein. Collectors sometimes see these soft colour bands as a sign of hand work. They are a small human trace in a very large object.

Carpets were never only for the floor. In homes across the region, they covered walls and divans. They became saddlebags, cushions, cradle covers, and bags for storing bread or bedding. Some were made as part of a bride's dowry. Keep this in mind as you pass the smaller woven pieces. They were part of daily life. The museum also holds other crafts, including metalwork, jewellery, embroidery, and ceramics. Together they show the wider world in which carpets were made and used.

Here is a fact that many visitors miss. Carpet weaving in Azerbaijan is not only a museum subject. In 2010, UNESCO inscribed the traditional art of Azerbaijani carpet weaving on its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage. That list protects living traditions, not old objects. The skill is still passed from one generation to the next, often within families. Many weavers have been women, working at home looms. Locals say some motifs were believed to protect a home from the evil eye. Treat that as folklore, but it is a living folklore.

Inside the galleries, the light is low for a reason. Strong light fades natural dyes over time, so the museum keeps the rooms dim and the carpets behind glass. Give your eyes a minute to adjust. Then try two distances with each carpet. First, stand well back and take in the whole composition — the border, the central field, the large medallions. Then step close and look at the small figures. You may find tiny animals, combs, lamps, or birds. These little figures are easy to miss from a distance.

When you step back outside, the wind will meet you again. The Caspian is grey or blue, depending on the day. Look along the boulevard toward the Old City walls, and notice the patterns around you. You may see them in paving stones, carved balconies, and tiled facades. After an hour with these carpets, the city may look a little more woven. Take that eye with you as you walk. Pattern is everywhere in Baku, and now you know how to read it.

Practical tips

View each carpet from afar, then up close.
Combine your visit with a walk to the Old City.
Avoid flash photography to help protect the dyes.
