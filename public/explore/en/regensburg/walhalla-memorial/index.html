The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Far below, the Danube moves slowly through flat Bavarian farmland. Up here the wind carries over the hilltop, and your footsteps sound sharp against pale stone. In front of you rises a long row of columns. It is a Greek temple, white against the sky, standing on a wooded slope east of Regensburg. It looks as if someone carried it here from Athens.

This is Walhalla, a hall of fame in marble. It sits above the small village of Donaustauf, a short journey downriver from Regensburg's old town. The building does not hold a god or a tomb. Instead, it holds faces. Inside are rows of marble busts and inscribed plaques. Each one honours a person from the German-speaking world, chosen for achievement in art, science, politics or faith.

The idea began with one man. Ludwig, the crown prince of Bavaria, imagined this memorial in the early nineteenth century. At that time, Napoleon's armies dominated much of Europe, and the German lands were divided into many separate states. Ludwig wanted a place that would show a shared cultural identity. He began commissioning busts years before any building existed. When he became King Ludwig the First of Bavaria, he finally had the power to build his temple.

He gave the work to his favourite architect, Leo von Klenze. Klenze shaped much of nineteenth-century Munich in a classical style. For Walhalla, he took the Parthenon in Athens as his model. The Parthenon is the great temple on the Acropolis, built for the goddess Athena in the fifth century before Christ. Construction here started in 1830, and the hall opened in 1842. Both ceremonies took place on the eighteenth of October, the anniversary of the Battle of Leipzig, where Napoleon was defeated in 1813.

The name itself comes from a very different world. In Norse mythology, Valhalla is the great hall of the god Odin. Fallen warriors feast there after death. Ludwig borrowed that northern legend and placed it inside a southern, Greek shell. That mix of Germanic myth and ancient Greek form tells you a lot about the nineteenth century. Many educated Europeans then saw classical Greece as the highest standard of beauty, and they wanted to connect their own history to it.

Before you go inside, look up at the two triangular gables at each end of the roof. These are called pediments. They carry groups of sculpted figures by Ludwig Schwanthaler. One side shows the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest, where Germanic tribes defeated Roman legions in the year 9. The other side shows Germania, a female figure who represents the German lands, after the end of the Napoleonic wars. Both scenes carry the same political message as the hall.

Now step through the doors. The change is sudden. Outside, everything is pale and calm. Inside, the walls glow with warm, reddish marble, and the floor shines underfoot. Light falls from openings in the roof rather than from side windows. This gives the room a soft, even glow. Voices drop to a murmur here. Even school groups tend to go quiet for the first few seconds.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look high along the walls, just below the ceiling. A long sculpted frieze runs around the room. It tells the story of early Germanic peoples, from their migrations to their conversion to Christianity. Below it, standing figures of Valkyries hold up the roof structure. In Norse legend, Valkyries are the women who choose which warriors go to Valhalla. Here, they guard the room instead.

Then there are the busts themselves. They stand on shelves and pedestals along the walls, white faces turned toward the centre. You may recognise some names: composers, poets, scientists, emperors. Some of the people honoured lived many centuries ago, and nobody recorded what they looked like. For them, there is no face at all. Instead, you will find simple marble plaques with their names. Look for them high on the walls. They are easy to walk past.

The second surprise is that this collection is not finished. Walhalla was designed to grow, and new busts are still added from time to time. The Bavarian state decides who enters, often after public suggestions. In the later twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, the hall welcomed Albert Einstein and Sophie Scholl. Scholl was a young student who resisted the Nazi regime and was executed in 1943. Her presence shows how the meaning of this place has slowly changed.

That change matters. A memorial built for national pride in the nineteenth century carries a heavier weight after the twentieth. For many years, historians and visitors have debated who belongs here and what the hall should say. You do not need to settle that question. But it is worth asking as you walk along the rows. Who was chosen, who was left out, and who did the choosing?

Outside again, walk to the front of the temple. A wide stone terrace and long flights of steps run down the hillside toward the river. This is where the building makes the most sense. Klenze placed the temple high above the Danube so travellers on the water would see it from far away. From the top step, the view opens across the river valley. On a clear day, the fields and villages stretch to the horizon in soft greens and browns.

Take a little time on these steps before you leave. Feel the sun on the stone, or the cool air if the clouds have come in. Below you, the Danube keeps moving east toward Vienna and, eventually, the Black Sea. Behind you, rows of marble faces look into the quiet hall. As you walk back down, turn around once. The columns will catch the light, and the temple will look small and white above the trees.

What to look for

Sculpted battle scene in the triangular pediment above the columns.
Warm reddish marble lining the interior walls.
Name-only plaques for people with no known likeness.
Valkyrie figures supporting the roof inside the hall.
Long sculpted frieze running just below the ceiling.
Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the riverside steps for the full view of the temple.
Visit on a clear day for long views across the valley.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep hillside paths.
Combine the visit with a Danube boat trip from Regensburg.
Speak quietly inside; the hall carries sound easily.
