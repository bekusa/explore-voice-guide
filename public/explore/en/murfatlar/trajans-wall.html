The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. Here the earth rises gently into a long, grassy swell, then dips into a shallow hollow beside it. In summer the grass is dry and pale, and the air carries the smell of warm dust and wild thyme. Wind moves across the open plateau of Dobruja with very little to stop it. It does not look like much at first... and that is exactly the point. You are standing beside one of the oldest defensive lines in this corner of Europe.

This is Trajan's Wall, a system of ramparts that crosses the Dobruja region of eastern Romania. It is not one wall but several. Together they run roughly from the Danube, near Cernavodă, to the Black Sea coast near Constanța. Murfatlar lies along this corridor, in the low valley between the river and the sea. Here the land is narrowest and easiest to cross, so whoever controlled this valley controlled the road between the two waters.

Scholars usually describe three main lines. There is a small earthen wall, a larger earthen wall with a ditch, and a stone wall. In places they run close together. In other places they drift apart across the fields. Each line was built as a bank of earth, or a core of stone, with a ditch dug along one side. Today most of what survives are softened mounds. Centuries of rain, ploughing and grazing have rounded the sharp edges into gentle curves.

Now for the first surprise. Despite the name, nobody can say with certainty that the Roman emperor Trajan built any of this. Trajan ruled in the early second century and fought major wars against the Dacians, the people of the lands north of the Danube. His fame in this region is so strong that many old earthworks carry his name. Romanians call this one Valul lui Traian, which simply means Trajan's Rampart. The name may say more about memory than about the builders.

Archaeologists and historians have argued about the dates for a long time. Some link parts of the system to the Roman period. Others connect parts to the Byzantine Empire, the eastern continuation of Rome. Still others suggest a link with the early medieval Bulgarian state, which also contested this frontier. It is quite possible that different lines belong to different centuries. One builder may have reused, extended or answered the work of another. The honest answer is that the debate is still open.

What is clear is why someone wanted a barrier here. Dobruja sits between the lower Danube and the Black Sea. For many centuries it was a frontier zone. Peoples moving from the northern steppe often came this way, and empires to the south tried to hold the line. A rampart with a ditch would not stop a determined army. It could, however, slow riders, channel traffic toward guarded points, and give defenders warning and time.

Try to imagine the wall when it was new. The bank would have stood much higher than it does now, with steep sides of freshly turned earth. The ditch would have been deeper and wider, perhaps holding water after rain. On the stone line, cut blocks would have formed a hard face against the plain. Guards may have watched from small forts along the route. Remains of such positions have been studied along the lines, though much is still unclear.

Here is the second surprise. The same narrow valley that made the wall useful has attracted builders again and again. In the nineteenth century, a railway linked the Danube with the port of Constanța along this route. In the twentieth century, the Danube to Black Sea Canal was dug through the same corridor. Roads and modern infrastructure followed too. In several places, these newer works cut straight through the ancient ramparts. So the wall shares its valley with every later attempt to join river and sea.

That is why the line can be hard to follow on the ground. You may see a clear mound in one field, then lose it under a road or a set of tracks. Farmland presses right up against it in places. Vineyards spread across the slopes around Murfatlar, a town long known in Romania for its wines. The chalky soil and dry, sunny climate suit the vines. On a quiet day, you can look from the old rampart straight across rows of grapes.

Murfatlar has another layer of deep history close by. Near the town is a complex of small churches and chambers cut into the soft white chalk. It is usually dated to roughly the tenth century, and its walls carry carved crosses, inscriptions and figures. It is a separate site from the wall, but it belongs to the same frontier story. Both show how many different communities passed through, prayed and fought in this valley.

As you walk, look closely at the profile of the land. A rampart usually shows as a long, straight or gently curving ridge, with a hollow running beside it. Changes in the grass can help. The ditch often holds more moisture, so plants there may stay greener longer into summer. Low evening light is your best friend here. When the sun is low, shadows fall along the edges of the bank and make its shape much easier to read.

Please treat the mounds with care. They are protected historical monuments, and they have already survived a great deal of damage. Walk on established paths where you can, and avoid digging, climbing loose slopes or taking any stones. Bring water, a hat and good shoes, because there is little shade on the open plateau. If you can, carry a map or use satellite view on your phone. From above, the long lines across the fields are often clearer than from the ground.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look along the line of the bank. Feel the wind coming across the plain, the same open wind that riders and guards once faced. Listen for the traffic on the road and perhaps a train in the distance, moving along the same valley. The mound beside you is quiet and low now. But it still marks a choice someone made, long ago, about where one world should end and another begin.
