The story

You will hear the market before you see it. Taxis honk. Sellers call prices. Somewhere a radio plays coupé-décalé. Then the smell arrives: smoked fish, ripe mango, fresh herbs, and hot oil. Welcome to Treichville, one of the oldest quarters of Abidjan.

Treichville lies just south of the Plateau, across the Ébrié Lagoon. Two bridges connect it to the towers of the business district. The Houphouët-Boigny Bridge opened in 1957. The Charles de Gaulle Bridge followed a decade later. Water buses also cross the lagoon, and the ride gives fine views of both shores. Cross by any route, and the city changes completely. Offices give way to workshops, courtyards, and streets full of trade.

The name is older than the country. Marcel Treich-Laplène was a young French explorer who signed treaties along this coast in the 1880s. Colonial planners later named this district after him. Under colonial rule, Treichville was the African quarter, where dockworkers, traders, and craftsmen lived. The Plateau held the offices. Treichville held the life.

That history still shapes the streets. They follow a strict grid, with numbered avenues and rues. Ask for Rue Douze, and anyone can point the way. The houses are low, the courtyards busy, and commerce spills from every doorway. Treichville has been a place of arrival for generations. People from every region of Côte d'Ivoire, and from across West Africa, have settled here.

Listen as you walk. French mixes with Dioula, Baoulé, and the street slang called nouchi, which was born in the streets of Abidjan. Music is everywhere too. Zouglou and coupé-décalé, the great Ivorian styles, pour from shop radios and taxi windows.

The market is the heart of it all. The Grand Marché de Treichville fills a large building and overflows into the streets around it. Hundreds of stalls press close together, and the paths between them are narrow. Morning is the busiest and freshest hour; late afternoon is calmer. Give yourself time, and accept that you will get lost. Getting lost is part of the visit.

Start with the fabric sellers. Bolts of wax-print cotton rise in walls of pattern and color. Sellers unfold them with a snap of the wrist. Six yards is the standard cut. Around the fabric stalls sit tailors with pedal sewing machines. Choose a cloth in the morning, and some tailors can fit a garment by evening.

Then follow your nose to the food halls. Women preside over pyramids of tomatoes, onions, chilies, and okra. There are basins of rice, sacks of dried beans, and mounds of attiéké, the fermented cassava couscous that Ivorians eat with everything. Smoked fish lies stacked like silver firewood. Snails the size of a fist wait in wide bowls.

Bargaining is the local language of respect. The first price is an opening line, not an insult. Answer with a smile and a lower number. Take your time. Buy, or walk away politely. If you take several items from one stall, a discount usually follows. A few words of French help, and a greeting works wonders. Say bonjour before you say anything else.

Treichville is more than its market. The Palais de la Culture stands at the edge of the lagoon, a great angular hall where the city stages concerts, plays, and festivals. Sports fans know the commune for its stadiums and boxing gyms. And when night falls, Rue Douze fills with grills, music, and conversation that lasts until morning.

Come hungry. Around the market, small restaurants called maquis serve grilled fish and chicken with attiéké, fried plantain known as alloco, and pepper sauce with real heat. Eat where the crowd gathers; turnover keeps the food fresh. Finish with bissap, a cold hibiscus juice, deep red and lightly sweet.

Keep a little care with you. The crowd is dense, so carry your bag in front and leave valuables at your hotel. Take photos only after you ask; a seller who says yes will often pose with pride. If the press of people tires you, step into a side street, breathe, and start again.

Markets like this one are older than any skyline. Money changes hands, but so do news, jokes, recipes, and blessings. Somewhere in the noise, a tailor threads a needle, a fish seller laughs, a child sleeps through it all on a mother's back. Stand still for one minute and let it move around you. This is Abidjan at full strength, and it has room for you in it.
