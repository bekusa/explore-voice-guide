The guide

The story

Listen for the soft hum of air conditioning, then the music. It drifts out of hidden speakers, sweet and slow, like a music box that never quite winds down. The light inside is warm and even. There is no sun here, no wind, no rain. You have stepped out of a quiet hillside suburb and into a building where every surface has been designed to feel gentle. Welcome to Sanrio Puroland, the indoor home of Hello Kitty and her many friends.

First, a word about where you are. Tama sits on the western edge of Tokyo, in rolling hills that were once farmland and woods. From the 1960s onward, planners turned this area into Tama New Town, one of the largest planned residential districts in Japan. It was built to house families from the crowded city centre. So this theme park did not grow out of an old tourist district. It was placed among apartment blocks, schools and shopping streets, for the families who lived nearby.

Puroland opened its doors in December 1990. That timing matters. Japan was at the very end of its great economic boom, and large leisure projects were appearing across the country. Many of them struggled in the decade that followed. This park also had difficult years. But it survived, and today it draws visitors from across Japan and from abroad. Some of them travel very far just to stand where you are standing now.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. This is an indoor theme park, almost entirely under one roof. The decision was practical. Tokyo summers are hot and humid, and the rainy season can last for weeks. Indoors, the shows run in any weather. The park can control every colour, every sound, every beam of light. That control is part of the experience. Nothing here is left to chance, and that makes the space feel calm and closed off from the world outside.

The company behind all of this is Sanrio. It began in the early 1960s as a small business selling silk and then small gifts. Its founder, Shintaro Tsuji, built the company around a simple idea: small, cute gifts can help people show friendship to each other. The company has said that the name Sanrio comes from Spanish words meaning holy river. Whatever its origin, the idea of friendship through small gifts still guides everything you see here.

Hello Kitty arrived in the mid-1970s. She first appeared on a small vinyl coin purse. She was a simple design, a white cat with a red bow and two dots for eyes. Here is the second surprise. In her official story, Kitty is not Japanese. Sanrio describes her as a cheerful girl who lives in the suburbs of London with her family. Her twin sister is called Mimmy. And Sanrio has said that Kitty is not really a cat at all, but a little girl.

Look closely at her face on any sign or costume around you. Notice that she has no mouth. This is deliberate. Sanrio has explained that without a mouth, people can project their own feelings onto her. If you feel happy, she looks happy with you. If you feel sad, she seems to share it. It is a quiet piece of design, and it may explain why the character has lasted for so many decades.

As you walk deeper inside, you will reach the large central hall. At its heart stands a great tree, an artificial one, built to look old and kind. It rises toward the ceiling, and small houses and walkways surround it. The lighting changes through the day. Many visitors stop here first, because this hall is where big performances and parades often take place. Find a spot along the edge, look up at the branches, and take a moment before the next show begins.

Hello Kitty is the star, but she is far from alone. Around the park you will meet My Melody, a white rabbit in a pink hood. You will see Kuromi, her mischievous rival in black. There is Cinnamoroll, a puppy who flies with his long ears. There is Pompompurin, a golden retriever in a brown beret. And there is Gudetama, a very lazy egg yolk. Each year, Sanrio fans vote for their favourite characters, and the results are watched closely across Japan.

The heart of a visit here is not rides. It is shows. There are musical performances, character greetings and a gentle boat ride past scenes from the Sanrio world. The performers sing and dance with real energy, and the costumes are made with great care. Visitors often notice how many adults are in the audience. Many of them came as children. Now they return in matching outfits, with their friends, or with their own children beside them.

That adult audience says something about Japan. The word kawaii means cute, but it carries more than that. It can mean gentle, harmless and lovable. In Japan, kawaii culture is not only for children. Office workers carry Sanrio pens. Adults hang small character charms from their bags. Here, nobody feels strange in a pink hairband. Watch the crowd around you for a few minutes, and you will see grown people relax in a way they may not on a busy city train.

The food and shops follow the same logic. Desserts, plates and even small snacks often carry a character face. Seasonal events change the decorations, so the park looks different at the start of spring, in summer and at the end of the year. Shops sell items you may not find elsewhere. If you collect anything at all, you may leave with more than you planned. The shelves are arranged carefully, almost like a gallery of soft colours and small shapes.

Before you go, pause near the entrance once more. Listen again to that music box melody under the voices of the crowd. Feel the cool, steady air. Outside, the hills of Tama are waiting, with their apartment blocks, their trees and their ordinary afternoon light. As you walk back toward the station, look at the bags and badges around you. A small white cat with a red bow is travelling home with many of them... and perhaps with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk from Tama Center Station, uphill through the planned town.
Check the daily show schedule as soon as you arrive.
Claim a spot early along the central hall for parades.
Visit on a weekday to avoid the heaviest crowds.
Bring a bag for seasonal goods sold only here.
