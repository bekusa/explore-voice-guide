The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. Above you, a huge dark eye floats in the Curitiba sky. It rests on a single yellow column, and its glass face holds a reflection of the clouds passing over the city. Under your feet the paving is wide and open, and a shallow pool of water lies between you and the building. Curitiba people have a simple name for this place. They call it the Eye.

This is the Oscar Niemeyer Museum. It sits in the Centro Cívico, the civic district of Curitiba, the capital of the southern Brazilian state of Paraná. Around you are government buildings, lawns, and quiet avenues lined with trees. The museum is dedicated to visual art, architecture, and design. It is also a monument to one man's long career. Oscar Niemeyer was the architect best known for shaping Brasília, the modernist capital of Brazil.

So who was Niemeyer, for listeners who do not know the name? He was born in Rio de Janeiro in 1907, and he worked for almost his whole very long life. He believed that concrete could curve freely, like the shape of a hill or a body. Straight lines, he once argued, were cold and man-made. Curves felt Brazilian to him. You can see that idea above you right now, in the soft outline of the Eye.

Here is the first surprise. The museum you see today actually joins two buildings from two very different moments in Niemeyer's life. The long, low structure beside the Eye came first. Niemeyer designed it in the late 1960s, and it was completed in the following decade. For years it held offices of the state government. It was not built as a museum at all.

Now look at that older building closely. It is a long horizontal block that seems to hover above the ground. Wide supports lift it, so the space underneath stays open and light. This was a key idea of modernist architecture — free the ground, and let people walk through. The concrete is plain and unpainted. Its strength comes from scale and proportion, not from decoration.

At the start of the 2000s, the state decided to turn this government building into a home for art. Niemeyer was asked to adapt it. By then he was in his nineties. Many people would have retired decades earlier. Instead, he added the newest and boldest part of the complex. The museum opened in 2002 under another name, and soon afterwards it was renamed in his honour.

And so we come back to the Eye. This is the part that most visitors photograph. The yellow tower is the only support for the whole raised gallery. From some angles, the gallery looks almost too heavy for it. That tension is deliberate. Niemeyer liked forms that seemed to defy their own weight. The yellow colour helps too. It stands out sharply against the grey concrete and the green lawns.

Here is the second surprise, and many people miss it. You do not climb straight up into the Eye from the plaza. The route takes you through the main building first, and then along a passage below ground level. After that, a ramp and stairs carry you up into the gallery inside the Eye. The journey is part of the design. You move from the low, horizontal past into the high, curved present.

Inside the Eye, the light is soft and filtered. The large glass face changes the way daylight enters the room. On a bright day the space feels calm and slightly shaded. On a cloudy day it feels like being inside a lantern. Exhibitions change over time, so what hangs on these walls today may be different next season. The room itself, though, stays the same, and it is worth a slow look on its own.

The main building holds most of the galleries. The museum shows Brazilian and international art, from painting and sculpture to photography and design. There is a permanent space about Niemeyer himself, with drawings, models, and images of his projects across Brazil and beyond. The museum also keeps a notable collection of Asian art, which surprises many first-time visitors. Temporary shows rotate through the large halls, so each visit can feel new.

Take a moment to notice how people use the outside spaces. Families walk along the lawns. Students sit on the steps with notebooks. Skaters and joggers pass along the edges of the plaza. The museum is not only a container for art. It is also a public place where the city gathers on weekend afternoons. That idea mattered to Niemeyer. He wanted architecture to belong to everyone, not only to people inside the galleries.

The setting also shows something about Curitiba. This city is known in Brazil for its parks, its bus system, and its long tradition of urban planning. The museum fits that picture. It sits in an open green district, not squeezed between towers. From the plaza you can see sky in almost every direction. The building has room around it, and that space gives the Eye its strong outline.

If you have time, walk a full circle around the complex. From the front, the Eye looks like a wide shape on a column. From the side, it becomes thinner and sharper. Late in the afternoon, the low sun turns the yellow tower warm and bright. After dark, lights shine on the concrete, and the glass face becomes a dark mirror again. The same building gives you several different pictures.

Before you leave, stand once more by the water and look up. Notice the clouds moving across the glass. Notice the single yellow column holding everything up. Somewhere behind you, the older building waits in its long, quiet line. Together they tell the story of one architect across many decades. As you walk away, look back once over your shoulder. The Eye will still be there, watching the city.
