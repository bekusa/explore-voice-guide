The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the gateway, the city traffic of Aurangabad fades into birdsong and the soft scrape of sandals on stone. Ahead of you, a white dome rises over a long garden of water channels and clipped lawns. In the late light, its surface turns warm, almost honey-coloured. Many visitors stop here and think of somewhere else entirely. That reaction is part of this monument's story. Let us walk slowly toward it together.

This is Bibi Ka Maqbara, which means the Tomb of the Lady. It is the resting place of Dilras Banu Begum, the first wife of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb. She died in 1657, and her son, Prince Azam Shah, raised this mausoleum in her memory in the second half of the seventeenth century. She is also remembered by a title, Rabia-ud-Daurani, roughly meaning the Rabia of her age.

So why does it look so familiar? Because it was modelled on the Taj Mahal in Agra. That tomb was built by Shah Jahan for his wife Mumtaz Mahal. Mumtaz Mahal was Azam Shah's grandmother. In other words, a grandson echoed his grandfather's tribute to a wife and mother. Today, people often call this place the Taj of the Deccan. The Deccan is the high plateau that covers much of central and southern India.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. As you approach the main gateway, look at the large door. It is covered in metal plates with fine floral patterns. An inscription at the site names the architect as Ata-ullah. He is traditionally described as the son of Ustad Ahmad Lahauri, the master builder linked with the Taj Mahal itself. So the family connection here may run through the builders as well as the patrons.

Now walk into the garden. The layout follows the Mughal charbagh, a garden divided into four parts by water channels. For the Mughals, this design suggested the gardens of paradise described in Islamic tradition. Long straight paths lead your eye directly to the tomb. Fountains and pools sit along the central axis. When the water is still, the dome appears a second time, upside down, at your feet.

As you get closer, look carefully at the surfaces. This is the second surprise. The Taj Mahal is faced almost entirely in white marble. Here, marble covers the dome and the lower parts of the main building. Much of the rest is finished in a fine lime plaster, polished to a smooth white sheen. From a distance, the two materials look nearly the same. Up close, you can see the difference in texture and in the way they weather.

Why the change? A popular account says that Aurangzeb gave his son only a modest budget. The emperor was known for his austere habits and his dislike of lavish display. According to this story, the prince had to work with far less money than his grandfather once spent in Agra. Treat this as a well-loved explanation rather than a settled fact. Still, the mix of marble and plaster is real, and you can see it with your own eyes.

Notice the proportions too. The tomb stands on a high square platform, with a slender minaret at each corner. Many visitors feel the building is taller and narrower than its famous model. The minarets seem closer to the central structure. Some critics have called this a weaker copy. Others see it as a building with its own character, shaped by a different time, a different region, and a different purse. Your own eye is the best judge here.

Now climb onto the platform and look for the opening in the floor. Here is perhaps the most unusual feature of all. The grave of Dilras Banu lies on a lower level. You do not walk down to it. Instead, you look down through an octagonal opening surrounded by a carved marble screen. The screen is pierced with delicate lattice work. From above, the simple grave sits in soft, filtered light, quiet and plain.

Take a moment with the carving on that screen. The patterns are geometric and floral, cut so finely that light passes through the stone. This kind of pierced screen is called a jali. In Mughal buildings, a jali allowed air and light to move while still giving privacy. Here, it also creates a sense of distance. You are close to the grave, yet you look at it the way you might look into a well.

Walk around the outer walls of the tomb. Look for the plaster panels with carved flowers and vines. Look up at the small domed pavilions near the roofline. On the western side of the enclosure, you will find a mosque. Mosques often stand beside Mughal tombs, so that prayers can be offered for the dead. The enclosure walls themselves have small pavilions and bastions, so it is worth a full circuit, not only a straight walk to the dome.

Now turn and look outward, beyond the walls. On a clear day, you can see the hills to the north of the city. Those hills hold the Aurangabad Caves, a group of carved Buddhist shrines far older than this tomb. Aurangabad itself carries the name of Aurangzeb, who spent long years in the Deccan. This tomb is one of the clearest marks his family left on the city. Today it is cared for by the Archaeological Survey of India.

As the afternoon moves on, watch the colour of the dome change. It starts bright and almost chalky. Then it softens to cream, and then to a pale gold. Families settle on the lawns. Students sketch the minarets. Couples pose for photographs along the water. The place feels less like a monument to power, and more like a garden that people actually use.

Before you leave, stand once more on the central path and look back. Behind you is a tomb built by a son for his mother, in the shadow of a more famous love story. It is smaller, simpler in places, and honest about its limits. Listen again to the birds in the trees and the water in the channels. Then, as you pass back through the gateway, notice how quickly the city sounds return... and how the white dome stays in your mind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the dome.
Walk the full enclosure, not just the central path.
Stand at the long pools for reflection photographs.
Wear comfortable shoes for the platform steps and stone paths.
Combine with the Aurangabad Caves in the nearby hills.
