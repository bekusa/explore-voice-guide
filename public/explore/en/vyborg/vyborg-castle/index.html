The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves against the rocks below you, and gulls call over the bay. The air carries salt, wet granite and a little pine from the Finnish woods to the north. In front of you, grey stone walls rise straight out of a small rocky island. Above them stands a single tall tower, plain and heavy, with a pointed roof. This is Vyborg Castle. For more than seven centuries, it has watched the water between Russia and the Baltic world.

The story begins in 1293. That year, Swedish forces arrived here during a military campaign that later writers called the Third Swedish Crusade. Their leader was the Swedish marshal Torgils Knutsson. They chose this island in the bay with care. Water surrounded it, so attackers could not easily reach the walls. Boats could still bring supplies. The Swedes built a stone fortress, and that fortress gave the town of Vyborg its first shape.

Why here? Look at the water around you. This bay sits at the northern edge of the Gulf of Finland, near old trade routes between the Baltic Sea and the Russian lands to the east. For centuries, Sweden and Novgorod, a powerful medieval Russian trading republic, competed for this coast. Whoever held this rock could control the boats, the furs and the fish moving past it. So the castle was never a quiet residence. From its first day, it was a frontier fortress.

Now look up at the main tower. It is called the Tower of Saint Olaf. The name honours Olaf, a Norwegian king of the eleventh century who became a saint and was popular across Scandinavia. The tower is the oldest and most important part of the castle. Its lower walls are very thick and built from rough granite blocks. Over the centuries, builders raised it, repaired it and changed its roof. So the tower you see today carries the work of many different generations.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The castle was not built in one piece. Fires, sieges and storms damaged it again and again. After each disaster, new owners rebuilt sections in the style of their own time. Walk slowly and compare the walls. Some stone is rough and irregular. Some walls are smoother, with later brick and plaster. The castle is not a frozen medieval scene. It is a long record of repair.

The most famous story about this place comes from 1495. A Russian army besieged the castle, and the Swedish commander Knut Posse led the defence. What happened next became a legend known as the Vyborg Bang. According to the popular tale, the defenders set off a huge explosion in one of the towers. Some versions say the attackers saw a cross appear in the sky and ran away in fear. Historians treat the details as folklore. But the siege itself was real, and the castle held.

That legend still matters in Finland. Finnish writers and artists retold the story for centuries, and it became part of the region’s identity. So when you stand here, you are looking at a place that belongs to several national memories at once. Swedish, Finnish and Russian histories all pass through this small island. Each tradition tells the story a little differently. That mix of voices is part of what makes Vyborg unusual.

The Swedish era ended in 1710. In that year, the army of the Russian tsar Peter the Great captured Vyborg after a long siege. Peter wanted to protect his new capital, Saint Petersburg, which he had founded only a few years earlier further east along the gulf. Vyborg became a shield on its northern side. After this, the castle slowly lost its military role. Over time, its rooms were used for storage, offices and other practical purposes.

The twentieth century brought more change. After Finland became independent, Vyborg was a Finnish city called Viipuri. It was the second largest city in the country and an important port and cultural centre. Then came the wars of 1939 to 1944. After the Second World War, the city became part of the Soviet Union, and most Finnish residents left. Many of their families still remember Viipuri today. Some come back to walk these streets and look at this tower.

Today the castle houses a museum. Inside, you can find rooms with exhibitions about the history of the castle, the town and the region. Displays change, so check what is open when you arrive. The rooms are often cool, even in summer, because of the thick stone walls. Notice how small many of the windows are. They were built for defence, not for views. Light enters in narrow beams, and the floors feel uneven under your feet.

If you can, climb the tower. The stairs are narrow and steep, and the climb is not easy. But the reward is the view. From the top, you can see the bay, the islands, the harbour and the rooftops of the old town. Look for the old church towers and the long line of the shore. On a clear day, the water looks silver and the forests look almost black. Up here, the castle’s purpose becomes obvious. You can see everyone coming.

When you come back down, take some time on the bridge to the mainland. From there, you can see the whole island at once: the walls, the tower and the rocks at the waterline. Behind you, the old town climbs the slope with its narrow streets. Many buildings there show Swedish, Finnish and Russian influences side by side. Some are well kept. Others are worn and quiet. Together, they show how many owners this town has had.

Before you leave, stop and look at the castle one more time. Notice the colour of the stone in this light, the moss in the cracks, the water around the base. The same rock has stood here through crusades, sieges, empires and borders that moved again and again. Listen to the gulls and the water once more. Then walk slowly into the old town, and look for the tower above the rooftops behind you.
