The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves slap against the pebbles of a tiny bay. A boat engine idles somewhere offshore. The water at your feet shifts from pale green to deep blue within a few strokes of the shore. Somewhere beneath that blue surface, a bronze figure stands with its arms raised toward the light. You cannot see it from here, but it is close. This is the cove of San Fruttuoso, on the Portofino promontory, a short boat ride from Camogli.

The statue is called the Christ of the Abyss, or in Italian, il Cristo degli Abissi. It stands on the seabed of this bay, at a depth of roughly fifteen to seventeen metres. That is shallow enough for recreational divers to reach comfortably. On a calm, clear day, snorkellers floating above can sometimes make out a pale shape below them. The figure looks upward, face tilted toward the surface, both arms lifted as if reaching for the sky... or for whoever swims down to meet it.

The story behind the statue begins with loss. In the years after the Second World War, Italy was a pioneering country for underwater exploration. Diving equipment was still new, and divers took real risks. One of those divers was Dario Gonzatti, who died while diving in the late 1940s. His friend and fellow diver, Duilio Marcante, wanted a lasting memorial. He imagined something unusual — not a plaque on land, but a figure placed in the sea itself.

Marcante's idea grew into a tribute not only to his friend, but to everyone who had lost their life at sea. That includes fishermen, sailors, and divers. The sculptor chosen for the work was Guido Galletti, an artist based in Genoa, the large port city just along the coast. He shaped a Christ figure in bronze, designed to survive in salt water and to be seen from below, by people suspended in the water.

The statue was lowered into this bay in 1954. Think for a moment about what that meant. There was no tradition of underwater monuments to follow. The team had to choose a site, prepare a base on the seabed, and guide a heavy bronze figure into place from a boat. They chose San Fruttuoso, a sheltered cove with clear water and no road access. The quiet of the place was part of the point.

Here is a detail many visitors never hear. According to the story often told about the casting, the bronze was made partly from donated metal objects. People gave medals, pieces of ships, and bells. If that account is right, the figure carries small fragments of many lives connected to the sea. It is a fitting idea for a memorial that belongs to the whole maritime community, not to one family.

Over the decades, the sea took its toll. Salt, currents, and marine growth slowly covered the bronze. At one point the statue was damaged, and one hand had to be repaired. In the early 2000s, the figure was lifted out of the water for a full restoration. Conservators cleaned it and repaired it, and it then returned to the seabed on a new base. The Christ you could see today has already lived two lives — one before restoration, and one after.

Now look around you, at the bay itself. San Fruttuoso is one of the most remote corners of this coast. No car can reach it. You arrive either by boat, from Camogli or nearby harbours, or on foot, along steep paths over the wooded promontory. That isolation shapes the experience. Even in summer, when boats come and go, the cove feels enclosed, held between green slopes and grey rock.

Behind the beach stands the old abbey of San Fruttuoso, with its stone arches and its low dome. Above it, on the slope, rises a square watchtower. That tower was built to guard against raids from the sea, at a time when this coast feared pirates. So the bay holds two kinds of memory side by side. On land, monks and watchmen. Under water, a bronze figure for those the sea did not return.

Every year, usually in the summer, a ceremony takes place here. Boats gather in the bay. A religious service is held, and a wreath is lowered or laid in the water above the statue, in memory of those lost at sea. Divers descend to the figure. If you visit at that time, the cove fills with people, flowers, and quiet attention. On ordinary days, the memorial is simply there, under the water, without crowds.

A second surprise. The figure in this bay is not alone in the world. Copies made from the same original design were later placed in other waters. One stands off Key Largo, in Florida, in a protected reef area. Another stands in the harbour area of Grenada, in the Caribbean. So divers on two other continents can meet the same raised arms. But this cove near Camogli is where the idea began.

If you dive here, you will usually join a local dive centre, since the bay has boat traffic and the area is part of a marine protected zone. Divers often describe the moment the figure appears out of the blue. First a vague shape. Then the head, the shoulders, the open hands. Small fish move around the base. Light falls in moving bands across the bronze. For many people, it is the reason they learned to dive.

If you stay on the surface, the experience is different, but still real. Watch the colour of the water over the spot where the statue stands. Notice the boats that slow down here, and the swimmers who pause, face down, looking. Many people come to this bay without diving at all. They come because they know the figure is there, just below, and that knowledge changes how the water looks.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the edge of the beach. Hear the pebbles roll back with each wave. Look at the tower, the abbey, and the green slopes above them. Then look out at the water one more time. Somewhere out there, a bronze figure is reaching toward the same light that falls on your face right now. As your boat pulls away from the cove, you might glance down... and wonder how close you passed.

Practical tips

Reach San Fruttuoso by boat from Camogli or by hiking trail.
Book dives through a licensed local dive centre.
Pack snorkel gear to scan the water on calm days.
Wear sturdy shoes if hiking over the Portofino promontory.
Visit outside peak summer for a quieter cove.
