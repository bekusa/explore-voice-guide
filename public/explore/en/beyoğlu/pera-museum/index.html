The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the old red tram rattles along İstiklal Avenue. Gulls call from the direction of the Golden Horn. In front of you stands a pale, ornate facade with tall windows and carved stone details. It looks like a grand hotel from another age, and that is exactly what it once was. Today the building holds paintings, tiles, and some of the strangest little objects you will see in Istanbul.

This is the Pera Museum. It sits on Meşrutiyet Avenue, on the slope that runs down towards Galata and the water. The name Pera comes from Greek and means something like "beyond" or "across". For centuries, people in the old walled city used it for the hills on the far side of the Golden Horn. This side of the inlet was where foreigners lived and traded. It became the home of embassies, merchants, and the city's many minorities.

By the late nineteenth century, Pera was the most European-looking corner of Istanbul. It had gas lamps, cafés, theatres, and shops selling the latest fashions from Paris. Travellers arrived by train and by steamship, and they needed places to sleep. Grand hotels rose along these streets to serve them. This building opened in 1893 as the Bristol Hotel. It was designed by Achille Manoussos, an architect of Greek origin who worked in Istanbul. Its neighbour up the road, the Pera Palace Hotel, belongs to the same world of travel.

Look up at the facade. The architects of Pera liked classical European styles, and here you can see that taste clearly. There are framed windows, balconies, and decorative stonework arranged in calm, even rows. The building was later converted for museum use, and the restoration kept the historic front largely intact. Behind it, the interior was rebuilt with modern galleries. So the outside belongs to the age of steamships, while the inside belongs to the twenty-first century.

The museum opened in 2005. It was founded by the Suna and İnan Kıraç Foundation, a private cultural foundation in Turkey. Its mission is twofold. It shows its own permanent collections, and it brings international exhibitions to Istanbul. Over the years, the temporary shows have covered painters, photographers, and designers from many countries. That is worth knowing before you go in. What fills the upper galleries today may be completely different from what a friend saw last year.

Now for the painting that draws many visitors here. It is called The Tortoise Trainer, and it was painted by Osman Hamdi Bey in the early twentieth century. An old man in a long red robe stands in a tiled room. He holds a small drum, and a reed flute hangs on his back. At his feet, several tortoises graze on leaves. He seems to be trying to teach them something. The tortoises do not appear to be listening.

Who was Osman Hamdi Bey? He was one of the most important figures in late Ottoman culture. He studied in Paris and became a painter. He was also an archaeologist and a museum founder. He played a central role in creating what became the Istanbul Archaeology Museums, across the Golden Horn. Many people read The Tortoise Trainer as a quiet joke about slow reform in the Ottoman state. That reading is popular, but it is an interpretation, not a statement the artist left behind.

Here is something many visitors miss. When the foundation bought this painting in 2004, the sale made headlines in Turkey. It was described at the time as a record price for a Turkish painting. Today the work is one of the most recognised images in the country. You will see it on posters, books, and shop souvenirs. Standing in front of the real canvas, notice how small details carry the story. Look at the patient face, the bright robe, and the cool tiles behind him.

The painting belongs to the museum's Orientalist collection. Orientalism, in art, means works by mostly European painters who pictured the Middle East and the Ottoman world. Many of them painted scenes of markets, mosques, palaces, and daily life. Some were careful observers. Others painted fantasies for buyers back home. This collection lets you compare outside eyes with Osman Hamdi's own view. He was an Ottoman painter who used a European style to show his own culture.

Now comes the surprise. Not everything here is a painting. One of the permanent collections holds Anatolian weights and measures. These are scales, weights, and measuring tools gathered from many periods of Anatolian history. Some are small and plain. Others are shaped or stamped with care. They look modest beside the large canvases. But think about what they did. Every fair trade, every sack of grain, every handful of spice depended on objects like these.

Another collection focuses on Kütahya tiles and ceramics. Kütahya is a town in western Anatolia with a long tradition of pottery. Its workshops produced plates, cups, tiles, and vessels painted in blues, greens, yellows, and reds. Look closely at the brushwork. You can often see the hand of the painter in the flowers and lines. These pieces were made for churches, mosques, homes, and daily tables. They show how one craft served many communities across the empire.

The building has a life beyond the galleries. The museum runs a film programme and organises talks and events. There is a café on the ground floor, a good place to rest between floors. Outside, the street drops steeply towards the water. Walk downhill and you will reach the Galata quarter, with its narrow lanes and its old stone tower. Walk uphill and you reach İstiklal Avenue, where Pera's old shops and passages still line the street.

As you stand here, think about everyone who passed through this doorway. First came hotel guests with trunks and travel papers. Then came workers, owners, and decades of change in the neighbourhood. Today people come to see an old man with his tortoises, or a shelf of brass weights. The building has changed its purpose, but it still receives visitors from all over the world.

When you step back out onto the pavement, take one more look at the facade. Notice the light on the stone and the rows of windows above you. Listen for the tram again, and for the traffic heading down towards Galata. This is still Pera, the district across the water. Take your time as you walk on. The streets around you have their own long story, one doorway at a time.

Hand-painted flowers in blue and green on Kütahya ceramics.
