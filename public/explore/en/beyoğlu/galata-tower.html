The story

The stone beneath your feet is worn smooth by six centuries of footsteps. You are standing at the base of a tower that has watched Istanbul transform through empires, fires, and revolutions — a cylinder of Genoese limestone that rises sixty-seven metres above the Golden Horn, its conical cap visible from nearly every corner of the old city.

The Genoese built this tower in 1348, anchoring the northern edge of their walled trading colony in what was then Constantinople. They called the district Galata, and they needed height — a watchtower to scan the Bosphorus for incoming ships, a landmark to guide merchants through the maze of warehouses and dockyards below. The walls are three and a half metres thick at the base, tapering as they climb. Stand close and you can see the seams where medieval masons fitted each block, no mortar in some joints, just the weight of stone on stone.

The tower changed hands in 1453 when Mehmed the Conqueror took the city. The Ottomans kept the structure but repurposed it — first as a fire watchtower, then briefly as a prison, later as a naval depot for storing equipment. In the seventeenth century, a man named Hezarfen Ahmed Çelebi supposedly strapped wooden wings to his arms, launched himself from the top gallery, and glided across the Bosphorus to land in Üsküdar. Whether the flight actually happened is debated, but the story stuck, and a small plaque near the entrance commemorates the attempt.

Fire gutted the upper floors in 1794. The Ottomans rebuilt the conical roof you see today, sheathing it in lead and raising the total height slightly. Another fire in 1831 damaged the interior staircases, prompting a second restoration. By the early twentieth century, the tower had become a curiosity — too old to demolish, too iconic to ignore. The Turkish Republic restored it again in the 1960s, adding the observation deck and the restaurant that now occupies the top floors.

Climb the spiral staircase — or take the lift if your knees protest — and the city unfolds in layers. To the south, the domes and minarets of the old peninsula: Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque, Topkapı Palace. To the north, the modern sprawl of Beyoğlu and Taksim. Directly below, the terracotta rooftops of Galata, steep lanes threading between nineteenth-century apartment blocks. The Bosphorus cuts through the middle distance, ferries tracing white lines between continents.

The observation deck is open-air, ringed by a low stone parapet. Wind funnels up from the water, carrying the smell of diesel and salt. On clear mornings, you can see the Princes' Islands on the horizon, faint green humps in the Sea of Marmara. Late afternoon light turns the Golden Horn bronze, and the call to prayer echoes from a dozen mosques at once, overlapping in waves.

Inside, the tower is less romantic. The ground floor holds a small ticket hall and a gift shop selling postcards and Turkish delight. The restaurant on the upper levels serves tourist-oriented meals — acceptable meze, overpriced wine, views that do most of the work. The real attraction is the climb itself, the way the city shrinks and clarifies as you rise, the way the stone walls hold the cool even on hot August afternoons.

Galata has always been a threshold — Genoese watching Byzantine ships, Ottoman fire brigades scanning for smoke, modern visitors framing photographs of two continents at once. The tower does not explain Istanbul; it offers a vantage point, a moment of altitude in a city built on seven hills. The stone is the same stone the Genoese quarried. The view has changed, but the act of looking has not.

When you descend, take a moment in the small plaza at the base. There is a café on the corner that serves strong Turkish coffee. The streets radiating outward — Galip Dede, Serdar-ı Ekrem — are lined with music shops selling ouds and violins, antique dealers, bookstores with shelves of dusty French novels. The tower casts a long shadow in the late afternoon, a dark line that sweeps across the cobblestones as the sun drops toward Eyüp. You are standing in the middle of it, looking up at the stone, the same stone six centuries of travelers have looked up at, wondering what the city looks like from the top.

Key facts

Built by the Genoese in 1348 as a watchtower
Stands sixty-seven metres tall with walls three and a half metres thick
Hezarfen Ahmed Çelebi allegedly flew from here to Üsküdar in the 1600s
Destroyed by fire twice, in 1794 and 1831
Restored in the 1960s and opened to the public

What to look for

The seams between medieval stone blocks at the tower's base
The conical lead-sheathed roof added after the 1794 fire
The plaque commemorating Hezarfen's legendary flight
The view of the Golden Horn turning bronze in afternoon light
The long shadow the tower casts across the plaza at sunset

Practical tips

Arrive early morning or late afternoon for softer light and smaller crowds
Take the lift if available; the spiral staircase is narrow and steep
Bring a light jacket; the observation deck is windy year-round
Explore the surrounding Galata streets after your visit
