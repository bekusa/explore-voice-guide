The guide

The story

A bell rings twice — sharp, metallic — and the crowd parts without looking up. A red tram slides past, close enough to touch. Under your feet, granite paving stones carry the sound of thousands of shoes. Somewhere nearby, chestnuts roast on a cart, and the smoke drifts across the avenue. Music comes from three directions at once — a busker's guitar, a shop speaker, a street drummer. This is Istiklal Street, and it never really pauses. Stand still for one minute and let the current of people move around you.

The avenue runs about one and a half kilometres through the Beyoğlu district, from Taksim Square at the top to the Tünel area near Galata at the bottom. It is closed to normal traffic. Only the tram, delivery carts, and the occasional police vehicle share the space with pedestrians. On busy weekend evenings, the crowd can swell to hundreds of thousands of people in a single day. Locals cross the street daily without a glance. Visitors walk it slowly, reading the facades above the shopfronts.

In the nineteenth century, this was the Grande Rue de Péra — the main street of Istanbul's European quarter. Foreign embassies built their mansions here. Bankers, traders, and diplomats from across Europe filled its cafes and theatres. The architecture still shows it. Look up and you will see Neoclassical columns, Art Nouveau ironwork, and ornate stone balconies — styles imported from Paris and Vienna. At street level, global brands and phone shops occupy the ground floors. The avenue holds both layers at once — the old above, the new below.

After the Turkish Republic was founded in 1923, the street received its current name. Istiklal means independence. The embassies became consulates when the capital moved to Ankara, but most stayed in their old buildings. Several still stand directly on the avenue behind iron railings — you can spot their flags between the shops. The renaming marked a shift. A street once defined by foreign powers became a symbol of the new nation. Today it belongs, above all, to whoever happens to be walking along it.

The red tram is a survivor. Electric trams first ran here in the early twentieth century, disappeared for decades, and returned as a heritage line in 1990. The tram travels the full length of the avenue at walking pace. At the lower end waits something older — the Tünel, a short underground funicular that opened in 1875. It climbs the steep slope between the Galata waterfront and the avenue. It is one of the oldest underground passenger railways in the world, and it still runs every few minutes.

Partway along, an ornate arched entrance opens into Çiçek Pasajı — the Flower Passage. A great fire swept through Pera in 1870 and destroyed the theatre that stood here. In its place rose an elegant arcade with apartments above and shops below, modeled on the covered passages of Paris. Flower sellers later filled it, which gave the passage its name. Today it holds a row of restaurants and taverns under a glass roof. Step inside for a moment. The noise of the avenue drops away almost instantly.

Right beside the passage runs the Balık Pazarı — the fish market. Crushed ice, silver mackerel, lemons stacked in pyramids. Vendors call out prices while cats patrol the gutters with total confidence. The side streets here branch into a web of meyhanes — traditional taverns where mezes, small shared plates, crowd the tables. This is where the avenue eats. If Istiklal is a river, these alleys are its side pools — slower, saltier, and full of conversation. Follow your nose down any of them and you will find a table.

Back on the main avenue stands a large church of dark red brick — Saint Anthony of Padua. It is the largest Roman Catholic church in Istanbul, completed in 1912 in neo-Gothic style, the pointed-arch style of medieval European cathedrals. It sits back from the street behind a courtyard, so many people walk past without noticing it. Mass is still held in several languages. The church is a reminder that Beyoğlu was, and remains, one of the most mixed neighbourhoods in the city — Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish, and Muslim, side by side.

Near the avenue's midpoint you reach the tall iron gates of Galatasaray High School. A school has operated on this site for centuries, and instruction here has long been in both Turkish and French. The school gave its name to the surrounding square — and to one of Turkey's most famous football clubs, founded by its students in 1905. The gates rarely open to visitors, but pause and look through the railings at the quiet gardens. Generations of writers, statesmen, and athletes passed their teenage years behind them.

Istiklal rewards the walker who slows down. Narrow passages branch off between the shops — some hold secondhand bookstores, others record shops, cafes, or workshops. Street musicians claim their corners by evening, and the repertoire runs from Anatolian folk songs to jazz. Overhead, strings of lights span the avenue in winter. The street has seen fires, riots, decline, and revival — Beyoğlu faded in the mid twentieth century and filled again decades later. Change is its only constant habit. Whatever you find here today will be slightly different next year.

Before you move on, step to the edge of the avenue and stand under one of the old facades. Let the tram pass one more time. Watch how the crowd flows — students, families, vendors, tourists, all moving at their own speed in the same channel. People have promenaded along this exact line for well over a century — in fezzes, in top hats, and now in hooded jackets. The clothes change. The walk stays the same. When you are ready, pick a direction and rejoin the current.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the tram one way, then walk back slowly
Come early morning to photograph the avenue almost empty
Explore the meyhane alleys off the fish market for dinner
Keep valuables zipped; the crowd gets dense on weekend evenings
