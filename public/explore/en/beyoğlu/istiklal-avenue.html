The story

The pavement beneath your feet is smooth stone, polished by millions of footsteps. Above, the avenue stretches nearly two kilometres in a gentle arc, lined with buildings whose facades shift from neoclassical to art nouveau to early modern as you walk. This is Istiklal Avenue, the beating heart of modern Istanbul, and it hums with a particular energy — not the tourist rush of the old peninsula, but the everyday rhythm of a city living its life.

The street was laid out in the sixteenth century as the Grand Rue de Péra, the main artery of the European quarter when this hill across the Golden Horn was home to foreign embassies and merchant families. The name Péra means 'beyond' in Greek — beyond the old city, beyond the walls. For centuries, this was where Istanbul's cosmopolitan life unfolded: French schools, Italian churches, Greek theatres, Armenian jewellers, Levantine traders.

Look at the buildings flanking you now. Many date from the late eighteen-hundreds, when the Ottoman Empire was modernizing and European architects were reshaping the skyline. The ornate stonework, the wrought-iron balconies, the tall windows — these were statements of wealth and modernity. Some facades still carry the scars of the great Péra fire of eighteen seventy, visible as patched stonework or mismatched brickwork on upper floors.

The red vintage tram that rattles past every few minutes is not original — the first tram here ran in eighteen seventy-five, pulled by horses, then electrified in nineteen fourteen. The current heritage tram was reintroduced in the nineteen nineties as a moving monument, running the full length of the avenue from Taksim Square down to the Tünel funicular. Locals rarely ride it; it is slow and stops frequently. But it gives the street a rhythm, a punctuation mark every eight minutes.

Pause at one of the narrow side streets that branch off to your left or right. These steep lanes — Asmalımescit, Nevizade, Çukurcuma — are where the avenue's real texture lives. Small meyhanes serving rakı and meze, vintage furniture shops, hole-in-the-wall bookstores, art galleries in converted apartments. The avenue itself has surrendered to international chains in recent decades, but the side streets hold onto older layers.

The avenue was pedestrianized in the early two-thousands, which changed its character entirely. Before, it was a gridlocked artery choked with buses and taxis. Now, on a weekend afternoon, it can feel like a slow-moving river of people — families, students, street musicians, vendors selling roasted chestnuts in winter or fresh-squeezed pomegranate juice in summer. The density can be overwhelming, especially between four and eight in the evening.

Notice the churches as you walk. There are at least half a dozen along the avenue or just off it — Catholic, Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic. The red-brick neo-Gothic spire of Sant'Antonio di Padova rises near the midpoint of the avenue, built by Istanbul's Italian community in the early nineteen-hundreds. On Sunday mornings, you will hear Mass in Italian, Turkish, and Polish. These buildings are quiet reminders of the city's layered identity, still active, still holding services.

The avenue ends at Taksim Square, where the Monument of the Republic stands — a bronze ensemble of figures commemorating the founding of the Turkish Republic in nineteen twenty-three. From here, the modern city spreads north and east: business districts, shopping malls, residential towers. But Istiklal Avenue remains the threshold, the place where old Péra and new Istanbul meet and blur.

Toward dusk, the light softens and the avenue takes on a different quality. The shop windows glow. The smell of simit — sesame-crusted bread rings — drifts from street carts. The tram bell clangs in the distance. This is when the street feels most itself: not a museum, not a tourist set piece, but a living avenue where the city gathers, argues, flirts, eats, and keeps moving.

As you walk, pay attention to the small details the crowd overlooks. The tile work in a doorway. The faded painted sign on a third-floor wall advertising a long-closed tailor. The way the cobblestones dip slightly in the centre, worn down by a century of wheels and feet. These are the textures that outlast the shops and the crowds, the quiet evidence of all the lives that have crossed this same stone.
