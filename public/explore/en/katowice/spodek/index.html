The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the plaza and look up. A wide, grey disc rests above you, tilted gently toward the sky. Its sides are covered in overlapping metal panels, like the scales of a fish. Traffic hums somewhere below your feet, and the wind moves freely across the open ground. It looks less like a building and more like something that landed here one quiet night and decided to stay.

That feeling is exactly where the name comes from. In Polish, Spodek means saucer. Some people think of a teacup saucer, others of a flying saucer from an old science fiction film. Both readings work. The arena opened in 1971, and from the start the people of Katowice gave it this nickname. The nickname became the official name, and today it is hard to picture the city without it.

To understand why the arena matters, think about where you are. Katowice is the heart of Upper Silesia, a region of southern Poland built on coal and steel. For generations, the skyline here was mine towers, chimneys and slag heaps. Smoke and soot were part of daily life. A shining, futuristic arena was a bold statement in that setting. It said that this industrial city could also be modern, ambitious and a place for crowds to gather for joy.

The idea took many years to become real. The project grew out of a design competition in the post-war decades, when Poland was rebuilding under a communist government. A team of architects and engineers developed the design, and construction stretched through the 1960s. Much of the political push came from regional leaders who wanted Silesia to have a showpiece. The roundabout next to the arena still carries the name of General Jerzy Ziętek, a regional leader closely linked with the project.

Now look again at the roof. Here is the first surprise. The roof is remarkably light for its size. It does not rest on a forest of columns. Instead, it uses a system of steel cables held in tension, balanced against a ring of rigid structure. Engineers call this approach tensegrity, which means a structure that holds its shape through a careful balance of pulling and pushing forces. When it was built, this was a daring piece of engineering anywhere in the world.

The ground beneath the arena added another challenge. The site was not clean, solid earth. It was former industrial land, shaped by the waste of mining and heavy industry. Builders had to account for unstable soil, and in Silesia there is also the constant question of old mine workings below the surface. The foundations and the slanted supports you see around the base were part of the answer to that difficult ground.

All of this made people nervous. Locals say that while the arena was under construction, rumours spread through the city that the roof would collapse. According to one popular tale, the doubts grew so loud that the builders had to prove the structure was safe in front of everyone. Whether every detail of that story is true is hard to say. But the arena has stood for more than half a century, and the fears are now part of its folklore.

Inside, the space is a large bowl of seating around a central floor. Over the decades it has hosted ice hockey, volleyball, boxing, handball and athletics. It has also hosted many concerts, from Polish rock and jazz legends to big international tours. For many people in Silesia, their first live concert happened right here. Ask an older local about the arena, and you may hear a very personal memory rather than a fact.

Here is the second surprise. In recent years, the arena became famous far beyond Poland for something its builders could never have imagined. Since 2013, it has hosted Intel Extreme Masters, one of the largest video game tournaments in the world. Fans travel from many countries to watch professional players compete on giant screens. A building designed in the age of slide rules now fills with the sound of keyboards, commentators and cheering gamers.

Turn around and take in the neighbourhood. The arena now sits at the edge of the Culture Zone, a district built on the site of a former coal mine. Nearby you will find the Silesian Museum, much of it built underground, the home of the Polish National Radio Symphony Orchestra, and the International Congress Centre. In 2018, this complex hosted the United Nations climate conference. A coal region hosting climate talks was a striking image for many observers.

If you have time, walk toward the Congress Centre. Its roof is covered in grass and slopes down to the ground, so you can walk up onto it like a small hill. From there, you get one of the best views of the saucer. You see its full round shape against the sky, with the city stretching out behind it. It is a calm place to sit after the noise of the roundabout.

Notice how the old and the new speak to each other here. The concert hall and the museum use dark brick, glass and clean lines. The arena uses metal and curves. Between them, you may still spot a preserved mine tower, a reminder of what stood on this land before. Within a few hundred metres, you can read the whole story of the city, from coal to culture.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Listen to the traffic circling below, the footsteps on the plaza, the wind against those metal scales. The arena is not a museum piece. It is still a working building, still filling with crowds, still part of ordinary life in Katowice. As you walk away, glance back once. The saucer will still be there, quietly resting, as it has since 1971.
