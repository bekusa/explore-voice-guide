The guide

The story

Step out of the street noise and into the shade of the portal. The marble here is cool to the touch, even on a warm afternoon. Above you, the stone is carved into tiers of small niches, like honeycomb hanging from the arch. Inside, the light is softer still. It falls on walls covered in tiles of deep green and turquoise, and the colour seems to hold the light. This is the Green Mosque of Bursa, and it rewards slow looking.

To understand why this building matters, picture Bursa six centuries ago. This city was the first great capital of the Ottomans, the dynasty that would later rule from Istanbul. In the early fifteenth century, the young state was in crisis. A crushing defeat in 1402 had left the sultan captured and his sons fighting each other for the throne. For more than a decade, the empire was close to breaking apart.

One of those sons, Mehmed the First, finally won that struggle and reunited the state. The Green Mosque was his statement of recovery. It was built around 1420, near the end of his short reign. Its design is credited to Hacı İvaz Pasha, a statesman and builder at his court. The mosque was not only a place of prayer. It was a declaration that the dynasty had survived, and that its home was here in Bursa.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The building was never finished. Look at the front facade again as you leave. Many Ottoman mosques have a covered porch, a row of columns and domes in front of the entrance. Here, there is none. Mehmed died soon after the main structure rose, and the porch was simply never built. You are looking at a facade that was meant to sit behind something else.

Now look at the plan of the space around you. It is shaped like an upside-down letter T. You entered into a domed central hall, and ahead of you a second domed hall holds the prayer niche. On either side are smaller rooms. Historians link this layout to buildings that also served travellers and members of religious orders, who could rest and gather here. So this was a mosque, a meeting place, and a guesthouse, all at once.

In the middle of the first hall, notice the fountain. Water in the centre of a domed room is an old idea in this region, and it changes how the space feels. Listen for a moment. Even when the hall is quiet, the stone carries small sounds — footsteps, a low voice, the shuffle of a carpet. The dome above you has an opening for light, and on a bright day the beam moves slowly across the floor through the afternoon.

And then there are the tiles. This is the reason many people travel to Bursa. The interior holds some of the finest early Ottoman tilework anywhere. An inscription in the building credits the decoration to masters from Tabriz, a great city of art in what is now Iran. These craftsmen brought a technique where coloured glazes sit side by side on a single tile, separated by thin dark lines, so that each colour stays clean and bright.

Walk forward to the prayer niche, the mihrab, which shows worshippers the direction of Mecca. It is tall and covered entirely in tile. Look closely, and you will see layers of pattern. There are vines and flowers, geometric stars, and bands of Arabic script. Some panels have touches of gold. Up close, the surface is not perfectly flat. You can see where the glaze pooled slightly, and where a craftsman's hand placed each piece.

Now turn around and look up above the entrance. There is a raised gallery set into the wall. This was the sultan's private box, where the ruler could pray apart from the congregation. Many visitors walk straight past it, facing the mihrab the whole time. It is one of the most richly decorated parts of the whole building. The ceiling and walls there glow with tile, a small jewel box hidden over the door you came through.

You may wonder about the name. The mosque is called green because of its tiles, and once the outside shone too. Accounts describe glazed tiles on the exterior and on the minarets. Today, almost all of that outer colour is gone. The building suffered badly in a powerful earthquake in 1855. In the following years, a French architect named Léon Parvillée led a major restoration, and much of what you see outside comes from that work.

When you step back out, cross the street and look up the slope. On the hill opposite stands the Green Tomb, the mausoleum of Mehmed the First himself. Its turquoise tiles still cover the outside, so it gives you a sense of how the mosque might once have looked from a distance. The two buildings were meant to be seen together, the prayer hall and the resting place of the man who built it.

The area around you is known as Yeşil, which simply means green in Turkish. It sits a little east of the busier city centre, higher up, with views over the rooftops toward the plain below. There are tea gardens and small shops selling tiles and souvenirs nearby. On a clear day, look south and you may see Uludağ, the large mountain that rises behind the city and is often white with snow in winter.

Before you go, take one more look at the marble portal. Find the carved niches in the arch, and the panels of fine stone on either side. Notice how the sunlight changes their shadows as you move. Six centuries ago, this doorway welcomed a nation that had nearly fallen apart. Today it welcomes you. As you walk away down the hill, listen for the call to prayer, which may still rise from this building above the city.
