The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the silence has a texture. A raven's wingbeats carry across open air. A dry wind moves through the juniper. Under your boots the sandstone is warm, gritty, and the colour of rust. In front of you the ground simply ends, and the land drops away in layers of red, cream and grey. Below lies a maze of canyons that seems to go on forever. Welcome to Canyonlands National Park, in the high desert of southeastern Utah.

The first thing to understand is that this is not one place. It is several. Rivers carved the park into separate districts, and they barely connect by road. Island in the Sky is a huge mesa, a flat-topped plateau, in the north. The Needles lies to the southeast, full of striped rock spires. The Maze sits to the west, one of the most remote corners of the lower forty-eight states. The rivers themselves form a fourth district.

Those rivers are the reason this landscape exists. The Green River and the Colorado River wind toward each other through deep gorges and meet near the centre of the park. That meeting point is called the Confluence. For millions of years, water has cut down through the rock layers here. Each layer is a page of ancient time. There are old sand dunes turned to stone, the floors of vanished seas, and mud from long-gone rivers. The canyons you see are what remains after all that cutting.

If you are standing at Island in the Sky, you are on top of an enormous block of rock. Its edges fall in two great steps. First comes a sheer wall of pale-red Wingate sandstone. Then comes a wide, flat bench capped by a lighter band of rock, called the White Rim. Below that, the land drops again toward the rivers. From Grand View Point, at the southern tip of the mesa, you can follow these steps for a very long way. On a clear day, the view runs across many kilometres of open desert.

Now look down at the ground near the trail edges. You may notice dark, lumpy, almost crunchy-looking soil. It is not dirt. It is a living crust, made of cyanobacteria, lichens and mosses that bind the sand together. It holds moisture, feeds plants and stops the desert from blowing away. A single footstep can crush it, and it can take decades to recover. Rangers here have a short phrase for this: don't bust the crust. Stay on the rock or the marked path.

Here is something many visitors miss. Canyonlands became a national park in 1964, under President Lyndon Johnson. But the push for protection came from people on the ground. A local park superintendent named Bates Wilson spent years guiding officials and journalists through these canyons. He cooked for them over campfires, drove them down rough tracks and let the land make the argument. He is often called the father of Canyonlands. The park you see today owes a great deal to those slow, dusty trips.

One of the strangest features on the mesa is Upheaval Dome. It is a crater-like bowl of twisted, broken rock, quite unlike anything around it. For a long time, geologists argued about its origin. One idea says that a deep layer of salt pushed upward and deformed the rock above. Another says that a meteorite struck here long ago. Many researchers now favour the impact theory, but the debate has not fully closed. Either way, you are looking at a puzzle that scientists still study.

Travel south to the Needles district, and the mood changes. Here the rock rises in tall spires and fins striped red and white, like layered candy. Trails wind through narrow slots and open into grassy meadows ringed by stone. People lived among these rocks long before the park existed. Ancestral Puebloan farmers, ancestors of modern Pueblo peoples, built small stone granaries tucked under overhangs. They stored corn and seeds in them, safe from rain and animals. Some of these structures still stand today.

The Maze, across the rivers to the west, is a different world again. Reaching it takes long hours on rough dirt roads and a capable high-clearance vehicle. Few people make the trip. Nearby is a detached section called Horseshoe Canyon. Its walls hold the Great Gallery, a long panel of painted human-like figures, some taller than a person. They are in a style known as Barrier Canyon. Archaeologists believe they are very old, possibly thousands of years, though their exact age is still debated.

That remote western country also carries outlaw legends. Locals say Butch Cassidy and his gang hid out in the canyons near the Maze in the late nineteenth century. The area is still known as Robbers Roost. The stories are colourful, and some are surely stretched over time. But stand at the edge of these canyons, and you understand the appeal. A person could vanish into this landscape. Searchers could ride for weeks and never find them.

Now let your eyes rest on the colours around you. In the middle of the day, the light can flatten everything into one dusty orange. But at dawn and dusk, the canyons come alive. Shadows stretch out and show the shape of every ledge and butte. At Mesa Arch, on Island in the Sky, early risers gather to watch sunrise light the underside of the arch. For a few minutes, the stone glows a deep, warm red.

When night comes, the show continues. Canyonlands has been recognised as an International Dark Sky Park. There are very few towns nearby, so very little artificial light reaches the sky. On a moonless night, the Milky Way stretches clearly from one horizon to the other. Stars appear in numbers that city dwellers rarely see. If you can stay after sunset, turn off your torch for a while. Let your eyes adjust slowly. The desert becomes a different place in the dark.

Before you leave, take one more look out over the edge. Find the thin white line of the White Rim far below. Then trace the dark gash where a river runs, hidden from sight. Everything in front of you was shaped by water, in a place that now sees very little rain. Listen again for the wind and the raven. When you walk back to the car, step lightly on the rock. The crust beneath your feet has been growing here far longer than any of us have been visiting.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive before sunrise to see light under Mesa Arch.
Carry far more water than you think you need.
Stay on rock or marked trails to protect the soil crust.
Use a high-clearance vehicle for Maze and White Rim roads.
Stay after dark for some of the clearest stargazing anywhere.
