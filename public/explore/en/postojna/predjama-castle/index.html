The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the voices and footsteps there is a cool, steady movement of air coming out of the rock. It smells of wet stone and moss. In front of you, a pale Renaissance facade rises straight out of a cliff, as if someone pressed a house into the mountain and the mountain closed around it. This is Predjama Castle, a short drive from Postojna, in the limestone country of southwestern Slovenia.

The name tells you almost everything. In Slovenian, jama means cave, and Predjama means roughly in front of the cave. That is exactly where the castle sits. It does not stand on a hilltop like most medieval fortresses. Instead, it fills the mouth of a large cave halfway up a sheer wall of grey rock. The cliff above it climbs more than a hundred metres. Look up and you will see the stone hanging over the roof like a giant awning.

This landscape is called karst. The word describes limestone country where water dissolves the rock over thousands of years. The result is sinkholes, underground rivers and caves. A small river, the Lokva, flows toward the base of the cliff and disappears underground beneath the castle. So the building stands on top of a hidden world of passages. That hidden world shaped its whole history, and it is the key to the most famous story told here.

People used this cave long before there was a castle. A natural shelter with water nearby and a cliff for protection was too useful to ignore. Written records mention a fortification here in the thirteenth century, under the German name Luegg, which the noble family that held it also took as their name. That first castle was smaller and sat deeper in the cave. Over the centuries, it grew outward toward the light, until it reached the shape you see now.

Now to the story that every visitor hears. In the fifteenth century, the castle belonged to a knight called Erazem of Predjama, known in German as Erasmus Lueger. According to the account, he killed a nobleman connected to the court of the Habsburg emperor, Frederick the Third. The Habsburgs were the dynasty that ruled this region for centuries. Erazem fled to Predjama. The emperor sent soldiers after him, and they surrounded the cliff and settled in for a long siege.

The soldiers expected hunger to do their work. Months passed. Then, so the tale goes, Erazem stood on the walls and threw fresh cherries down at the men below. He was mocking them. How could a trapped man have fresh food? The answer was the cave. Behind the castle, a narrow natural passage led through the mountain to the surface far away. His servants could slip out, collect supplies in the villages, and carry them back without being seen.

The ending of the story is less heroic and more human. Legend has it that a servant betrayed Erazem. The servant signalled to the besiegers when his master went to the toilet, a small room in the weakest, most exposed part of the walls. A cannon fired at that spot, and the stone collapsed on the knight. This is usually dated to 1484. Historians agree that Erazem was real and that a siege took place, but the cherries and the toilet belong to folklore.

Locals say Erazem was buried near the small church below the castle, and that a linden tree was planted beside his grave. The linden is a tree with deep meaning in Slovenia. It traditionally marks village squares, meeting places and important graves. When you walk back down toward the road, look for the old church and the trees around it. Whether or not the knight lies there, the spot carries the memory of him.

The facade in front of you is not the castle Erazem knew. His fortress was badly damaged in the siege. In the sixteenth century, a new owner rebuilt it in the Renaissance style, with flatter walls, regular windows and a more residential look. Later, the castle passed to the Cobenzl family, and then to the Windischgrätz family. Each owner adapted the rooms. Today the building is a museum, and the rooms show how people lived inside the rock.

As you walk through, notice how the castle changes from house to cave. In the front rooms, the walls are plastered and the windows look out over the valley. Further back, the rock itself becomes the wall. In places, the ceiling is raw stone, rough and damp, with water marks running down it. The air gets cooler with every step. It is a strange feeling — a living room on one side, a mountain on the other.

Here is something many visitors miss. The castle is not only one building. It is several levels connected to the cave system. Above the castle, a higher passage leads toward the escape route of the Erazem story. Below it, a larger cave runs underground, and parts of it can be visited on separate tours in the warmer months. Bats live in these caves. In winter they hibernate here, which is why some underground sections close in the colder season.

Another surprise is how well the cliff protected the castle. The overhang above shields the roof from falling rock and much of the rain. Attackers could not easily approach from above, and the steep slope below made any direct assault very hard. This is why Predjama is often described as the largest cave castle in the world. The cave was not just a backdrop. It was the strongest wall the builders had, and they did not have to build it.

When you leave, stop on the road below and turn around once more. From a distance, you can see the whole composition: green meadow, white walls, dark cave mouth, grey cliff rising above it all. Watch how the light changes on the stone as the clouds move. Listen for the river and for the birds that nest on the cliff face. Five centuries ago, soldiers stood near this spot, waiting for a man inside the mountain who seemed to have food without end.
