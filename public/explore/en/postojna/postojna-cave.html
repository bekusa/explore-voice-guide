The guide

The story

The air changes first. A few steps past the entrance, the summer warmth falls away and a cool, damp stillness settles on your skin. It smells of wet stone and old water. Somewhere in the dark, a single drop hits a pool. Then another. This is the sound that built everything you are about to see, one drop at a time, over a span of time that is hard to picture.

You are standing at the mouth of Postojna Cave, in southwestern Slovenia. The landscape above your head is limestone, a soft rock that slowly dissolves in rainwater. Here, the Pivka River sinks underground and has spent ages cutting tunnels, halls and galleries through the stone. The result is one of the longest cave systems open to visitors anywhere in Europe, with more than twenty kilometres of known passages.

Here is a detail that most visitors miss. The word "karst", which geologists around the world use for this kind of limestone landscape, comes from this region. The Kras plateau sits nearby, and scientists who studied the sinking rivers and hollow hills here gave the whole field its name. So in a sense, every cave scientist on the planet is using a word from this corner of Slovenia.

People have known about the cave's entrance for a very long time. Deep inside, on the walls of one passage, you can find old names scratched into the rock. The oldest of these signatures dates back to the thirteenth century. Medieval visitors came with torches, saw only a small part of what lay ahead, and left their mark. The far deeper chambers stayed hidden for centuries more.

That changed in 1818. A local lamplighter named Luka Čeč was helping prepare the cave for a royal visit. He climbed across a ledge, disappeared into the dark, and came back with news of whole new halls beyond. Soon the cave became a destination for travellers from across the Austrian Empire. Paths were cut, guides were trained, and the long story of Postojna as a place to visit began.

You will probably begin your journey on the cave train. It is a small open railway that carries visitors several kilometres into the mountain. The first rails were laid in the 1870s, and in those early years guides pushed the carriages by hand. Today the ride feels a little like a fairground attraction. Rock walls slide past close enough to touch, and the cool wind carries the smell of damp earth.

Another surprise waits in the ceiling above you. Postojna Cave had electric lighting in the 1880s. That was before many large European cities had it, including Slovenia's own capital, Ljubljana. Imagine a nineteenth century traveller stepping off a horse-drawn coach and walking into a mountain lit by electric bulbs. For many people at that time, this cave was the first place they ever saw electric light.

When you step off the train, the walking section begins. The path winds through a series of halls, each with its own character. In some places the formations are thin, white and pale, like strands of uncooked pasta hanging from the roof. Locals call one of these chambers the Spaghetti Hall. Elsewhere, the stone turns red and orange, coloured by iron and other minerals carried in by the water.

Keep an eye out for a short bridge over a deep ravine. It is known as the Russian Bridge. Russian prisoners of war built it during the First World War, when this region was still part of Austria-Hungary. Further on, in another section, the walls look blackened, as if burned. They were. During the Second World War, Slovene resistance fighters set fire to a German fuel store kept inside the cave. The soot from that fire still stains the rock.

Then comes the cave's most famous resident. In a dimly lit tank, you may see a pale, slender creature with pink frills on its head. This is the olm, a blind salamander that lives only in the underground waters of this part of Europe. It has no working eyes, its skin has almost no colour, and it can survive for years without food. Studies suggest it can live for a century or more.

Legend has it that long ago, heavy rains washed these strange animals out of underground springs. Local people, seeing them for the first time, believed they were baby dragons from a hidden lair deep below. Today, scientists simply call the olm a living window into life without light. In Slovenia, people often call it the "human fish", because its pale skin looks a little like our own.

The olm is not the only scientific story here. In the nineteenth century, a small beetle was found in this cave and formally described by naturalists. It became one of the very first cave animals ever studied by science. That discovery helped launch a whole new field, the study of life underground. Researchers still come here today to learn how animals adapt to total darkness and constant cold.

Near the end of the walk, the path opens into a vast chamber known as the Concert Hall. It is large enough that concerts and events really have been held here, with the stone itself shaping the sound. Stand still for a moment and listen. Voices fade quickly into the space. The temperature in the cave stays at around ten degrees all year, so the air feels the same in July as it does in January.

As you head back toward the train and the daylight, take one last look at the formations around you. Many of them are still growing, slowly, from water that fell as rain on the hills above. A single drop lands, leaves a trace of mineral, and moves on. When you step outside again, notice how warm and bright the world feels, and how loud. The cave keeps its own quiet pace.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer since the cave stays around ten degrees.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp, sloping paths.
Book a guided tour time in advance during summer.
Sit near the front of the train for open views.
Combine your visit with nearby Predjama Castle for a full day.
