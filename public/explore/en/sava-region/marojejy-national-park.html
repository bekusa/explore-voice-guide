The guide

The story

Listen first. Water is moving somewhere close by, over stone, under leaves. The air is heavy and warm, and it smells of wet earth and crushed green stems. Above you the canopy drips, even when it is not raining. Your boots press into soft red soil, roots and moss underfoot. This is Marojejy, in the far northeast of Madagascar, and almost everything around you lives nowhere else on Earth.

Marojejy is a mountain massif, a tight cluster of steep ridges and peaks. Its highest summit rises to a little over two thousand metres. That sounds modest, but the mountains climb fast from the lowlands, so the slopes are sharp and the trails are demanding. Clouds gather on the upper ridges most days. Rain falls here through much of the year, and the rivers that start up there run down toward the vanilla farms of the valleys below.

The region you are in is called Sava. The name is an acronym, built from four towns: Sambava, Antalaha, Vohemar and Andapa. This corner of Madagascar is famous for vanilla, and you may have passed vines climbing support trees on your way here. Andapa is the nearest town to the park. From there, most visitors travel to the village of Manantenina, at the forest's edge, where the walk in begins.

The protection of this forest is older than many people expect. Marojejy was first set aside as a strict nature reserve during the colonial period, in the early 1950s. In those days, access was limited mostly to scientists. In 1998 it became a national park, and it opened to visitors who come to walk, watch and learn. Today local guides lead every group, and many of them grew up in the surrounding villages.

Here is the first surprise. In 2007, Marojejy became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site called the Rainforests of the Atsinanana. That site joins several separate parks along Madagascar's eastern side. Together they protect the last large blocks of the island's eastern rainforest. Marojejy is one of the wildest pieces of that chain, and many naturalists consider its slopes among the richest in species on the whole island.

The reason is height. As you climb, the forest changes around you, band by band. Low down, there is tall lowland rainforest with thick trunks and buttress roots. Higher up, the trees grow shorter and the air grows cooler. Then comes cloud forest, where moss covers branches like green wool and ferns grow on the trees. Near the summit, the forest gives way to low, tough scrub, open to wind and mist.

Each of these zones has its own community of plants and animals. Botanists and zoologists have returned here again and again, and new species are still being described from these mountains. Frogs, chameleons, insects and orchids are all part of that list. So when you walk the trail, you are moving through several different worlds in a single climb. A short distance uphill can mean a completely different set of neighbours.

Now the animal that brings many people here — the silky sifaka. A sifaka is a type of lemur that moves by leaping upright between tree trunks. The silky sifaka has long, soft, cream-white fur, and it is one of the rarest primates in the world. It is listed as critically endangered. Marojejy is one of very few places where it lives, and one of the best places to see it in the wild.

Seeing one is never guaranteed. The groups move through the mid-elevation forest, and the guides often spend hours tracking them. When it happens, it is usually quiet. A pale shape sits high in a tree, chewing leaves, watching you with calm orange eyes. Then it pushes off with its powerful legs and is gone, crossing a gap you could never cross. Most people remember the silence of that moment.

The forest holds other lemurs too, along with a remarkable list of birds. One bird that keen birders hope for is the helmet vanga. It has dark plumage and a huge, curved, bright blue bill, almost too large for its body. Down at your feet, look for small frogs on wet leaves, and for chameleons resting on thin branches. Their colours are easy to miss. Your guide will often spot them first.

The second surprise is a darker story. Around 2009, during a period of political crisis in Madagascar, illegal logging of rosewood spread through parts of this region. Rosewood is a slow-growing tree with dense, deep red timber, highly valued overseas. Marojejy was one of the parks affected. Conservation groups, park staff and local communities pushed back, and the issue drew international attention. It is a reminder that this forest still needs active protection.

The visitor experience here is simple and close to the land. The trail climbs through a series of camps with basic shelters, each at a different height in the forest. Many people stay several nights, moving up slowly. Evenings bring the sounds of frogs and insects, and sometimes rain on the roof. Porters and cooks from nearby villages often travel with groups, and their work is part of what keeps the park open to visitors.

The effort shapes the experience. The paths are steep, roots and rocks are slippery, and leeches may find your ankles after rain. But the reward comes in small, steady moments. A waterfall appears between trees. A view opens across ridges wrapped in cloud. A bird calls once and does not call again. The forest asks you to slow down, and when you do, you notice much more.

So take a moment where you are. Look at the light coming through the canopy, green and broken into small patches. Listen to the water, still running somewhere nearby. Feel how damp the air is on your skin. Every leaf around you is part of a forest that has been protected for decades. As you walk on, step carefully, look up often, and let the mountain show you what it decides to show.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan several nights to climb gradually through the trail camps.
Pack rain gear; showers are frequent at every elevation.
Wear boots with good grip for steep, slippery trails.
Bring leech socks or long trousers tucked into socks.
Let your guide set the pace when tracking silky sifakas.
