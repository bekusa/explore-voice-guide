The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the wide stone square and feel how flat and open it is underfoot. Pale grey slabs run toward the lake, and on a calm day you can hear the trams of Bellevue ringing behind you. Gulls call from the water. In front of you rises a cream-coloured facade with columns, balconies, and rows of sculpted heads. This is the Zurich Opera House, and it has looked out over this corner of the city for more than a century.

The building you see opened in 1891. It replaced an earlier theatre on a nearby site that burned down in 1890. In the nineteenth century, fire destroyed theatres across Europe with grim regularity. Stages were lit by flame, scenery was made of canvas and wood, and a single spark could end everything. So when Zurich rebuilt, the city wanted something grand. It also wanted something safer.

The city turned to two architects from Vienna, Ferdinand Fellner and Hermann Helmer. Their firm was something like a theatre factory of its age. They designed dozens of playhouses and opera houses across Central Europe, from Austria to the Balkans. If you have travelled in that part of the world, this facade may feel familiar. Here is the first surprise... cities hundreds of kilometres apart share near relatives of this building, all drawn by the same Viennese hands.

The style is often called neo-Baroque. That means it borrows the drama of seventeenth and eighteenth century palaces — the curves, the ornament, the sense of theatre on the outside as well as the inside. Look at the central section with its tall windows and paired columns. Look up at the roofline, where sculpture breaks the skyline. The whole front is designed to be read from across the square, like a stage set for the city itself.

Now bring your eyes down to the heads along the facade. These are busts of great figures from music and literature, set into the stone like an audience watching you. Take a slow walk along the front and try to spot names you recognise. It is a quiet statement of values. The people of Zurich in the eighteen-nineties wanted their new theatre to stand in a European tradition, not a provincial one.

For most of its life the building had a different name. It opened as the Stadttheater, which simply means the city theatre. It hosted opera, but also spoken drama and operetta. Only in the 1960s did it take the name Opernhaus Zürich, the Zurich Opera House. Today it is home to the opera company and also to the ballet company of the city, so the stage carries dance as well as song.

Here is the second surprise, and it is not a gentle one. In 1980, this elegant building became the centre of street riots. The city planned to spend a large sum on renovating the opera house. Many young people in Zurich were angry. They felt the city funded high culture for the wealthy, while offering almost nothing for youth culture, music, and independent art. Protests began right here, outside these doors.

The clashes became known as the opera house riots, and they spread through the city over many months. They shook a country famous for order and calm. In the end, they changed Zurich. The unrest pushed the city toward supporting alternative cultural spaces, and parts of the lively independent scene that visitors enjoy today trace their roots to that time. So this facade is not only a symbol of tradition. It is also a reminder of a fight about who culture is for.

The renovation did go ahead, and the house reopened in the mid-1980s with an extension that added modern working spaces behind the historic front. That is worth knowing as you look at it. Opera needs enormous backstage machinery — rehearsal rooms, workshops, storage for scenery and costumes. The old front is the face the public sees. Behind it, a working theatre runs like a small industry.

If you go inside for a performance, expect the classic horseshoe shape of a nineteenth century auditorium. Tiers of balconies curve around the hall, so the audience can see each other almost as well as the stage. Gold ornament, red tones, and a decorated ceiling create a warm, close room. Compared with the huge opera houses of some capitals, this hall feels intimate. Many visitors are struck by how near the singers seem.

Now turn around and look at the square where you are standing. This is the Sechseläutenplatz, named after the spring festival of the old Zurich guilds. Each year the guilds parade through the city, and on this square they burn the Böögg, a figure of a snowman packed with fireworks. Locals say that the faster its head explodes, the better the summer will be. It is folklore, of course, but people here follow it closely, and the crowd counts the minutes.

The square was redesigned in the 2010s and paved with quartzite stone from the Swiss Alps. That is why the surface has that cool, slightly glittering grey tone, especially after rain. In summer, people sit here on movable chairs, children run across the open space, and the opera house becomes a backdrop to ordinary city life. It is one of the few places in central Zurich where you can stand in a large open space and simply breathe.

Before you leave, walk a few steps toward the water. Lake Zurich opens out only a short distance away, and on clear days the Alps appear faintly on the horizon. Then look back at the opera house one more time. Notice how the light falls across the columns and the carved heads. Notice the trams, the cyclists, the people crossing the square without a second glance. This building has seen fire, celebration, and riot... and still, every evening, it waits quietly for the curtain to rise.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book performance seats well in advance for popular productions.
Visit the square at dusk when the facade is lit.
Walk two minutes to the lakeshore for Alpine views.
Come during Sechseläuten in spring to see the Böögg burn.
Check the ballet programme too, not only the opera schedule.
