The guide

The story

Listen first. A breeze moves in from the sea, dry grass hisses against old stone, and somewhere beyond the trees there is a low industrial hum. Underfoot, the ground is pale and uneven, scattered with the outlines of walls that stand only knee-high. This is Megara Hyblaea. It is one of the oldest Greek cities in Sicily, and it sits beside the modern refineries on the Gulf of Augusta.

That contrast is the first thing most visitors notice, and it is worth taking a moment with it. On one horizon you can see steel towers, storage tanks and pipelines. Around you there are foundations laid by people who arrived here more than 2,700 years ago. Few ancient sites in the Mediterranean sit this close to heavy industry. It is strange, but it is also honest. This coastline has always drawn people who wanted a harbour, flat land and a way to make a living.

The city's story begins in the eighth century before Christ. Settlers sailed from Megara, a Greek city between Athens and Corinth, and looked for land on Sicily's eastern shore. The ancient historian Thucydides wrote about their journey centuries later. In his account, they tried several places before a local ruler named Hyblon gave them land to settle. That is where the double name comes from. Megara is for the homeland they left behind, and Hyblaea is for the Sicilian king who let them in.

It helps to picture what that meant. Sicily was not empty when the Greeks arrived. The Sicels, an indigenous people of the island, lived in the hills inland. The newcomers needed their permission, or at least their tolerance, to stay. So this city was born from negotiation as much as conquest. Ancient writers said it was a gift of land. Historians still discuss what that arrangement looked like on the ground.

Now look at the layout around you. Archaeologists have traced a network of straight streets here that cross in a regular pattern, and some of it goes back to the earliest generations of settlers. This matters more than it might seem. Many people link planned grid cities with later Greek and Roman towns. Here you can see an early version of that idea, laid out by colonists who were dividing new land into fair plots.

At the centre of the old town is the agora, the public square where Greek citizens traded, argued and made decisions together. Today it is an open space framed by low foundations. Around it, excavators found traces of shrines, public buildings and porticoes, which are long covered walkways. Try to imagine it full of voices, market stalls and the smell of fish and olive oil. The stones are modest, but they mark the civic heart of a working city.

Here is the first surprise. This small city sent out its own colonists. About a century after it was founded, people from Megara Hyblaea sailed to the far southwest of Sicily and founded Selinunte. Selinunte later became famous for huge temples that still stand on its hill. Many visitors who admire those temples never learn that the settlers came from this quieter place, with its knee-high walls beside the gulf.

The city's first life ended violently. In the early fifth century before Christ, Gelon, the powerful ruler of nearby Syracuse, captured Megara Hyblaea. Ancient sources say he moved the wealthy citizens to Syracuse and sold the ordinary people into slavery. In a single campaign, a community that had lived here for more than two centuries was taken apart. The houses emptied, and the agora went silent.

But the story did not end there. In the fourth century before Christ, people settled here again, and a smaller town grew over the ruins. The sturdy defensive walls and towers you may see near the edges of the site belong mostly to these later centuries. Then came Rome. During the Roman war against Syracuse in the late third century before Christ, Megara was destroyed again. After that, it never fully recovered as a city.

So what survived? The best answer is in Syracuse, at the regional archaeological museum named after Paolo Orsi, the pioneering archaeologist who worked here. Two of the finest pieces from this site are displayed there. One is a seated limestone mother nursing two infants, a moving and unusual image from archaic Sicily. The other is a marble statue of a young man, a type the Greeks called a kouros. If you visit Syracuse, look for them. They put faces on this ground.

The second surprise is how much of this site we owe to a long partnership. Italian archaeologists began serious work here, and later the French School at Rome led decades of careful excavation. Their focus was not only on treasures. They recorded house plots, street lines and the slow growth of a town. Because of that work, Megara Hyblaea became a key site for understanding how Greek colonies were actually organised, beyond their temples.

As you walk, pay attention to small things. Look at the different kinds of stone and how the blocks are cut. Notice how narrow some of the old house rooms are. Look for places where a doorway or threshold has survived. In spring, wildflowers grow between the foundations. In summer, the light is harsh and white, and shade is scarce, so pace yourself. The site is quiet. You may have long stretches entirely to yourself.

Pause before you leave. Turn toward the sea and listen again to the wind, the grass and the hum of the refineries. The first settlers came here for a sheltered coast and good flat land, and people today come for many of the same reasons. Look once more at the straight line of an ancient street running into the weeds. Then follow it with your eyes, and walk back toward your own century.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring water and a hat; shade is scarce across the site.
Visit in spring for wildflowers among the foundations.
Pair with the Paolo Orsi museum in Syracuse.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and dry grass.
Come early morning for softer light and cooler air.
