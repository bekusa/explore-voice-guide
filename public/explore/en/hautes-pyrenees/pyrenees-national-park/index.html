The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, water is falling. It may be a thin silver thread on a cliff face, or a torrent roaring under a stone bridge. In these mountains the sound of water rarely stops. The air smells of wet granite, crushed grass, and sometimes the faint warmth of sheep. Underfoot the path shifts from springy turf to loose scree. Welcome to the Pyrénées National Park, a long ribbon of high country along the border between France and Spain.

The park was created in 1967. That makes it one of the oldest national parks in France. It runs for roughly one hundred kilometres along the frontier, across two departments: the Hautes-Pyrénées, where you are now, and the Pyrénées-Atlantiques to the west. It is a narrow park, not a wide one. Think of it as a spine of peaks, lakes, and glacial valleys, with villages sitting just outside its edges rather than inside it.

That design was deliberate. The park has a protected heart, where the rules are strict, and a wider surrounding area where people live and farm. Inside the heart, there are almost no permanent residents. Here is something many visitors miss: the border with Spain does not mark the end of the protected landscape. On the far side, the Ordesa and Monte Perdido National Park continues the same mountains. Together, the two parks form one of the largest protected areas in the range.

The park gathers several valleys, each with its own character. In the Hautes-Pyrénées you find the valleys of Cauterets, Luz and Gavarnie, Azun, and Aure. Further west lie the Ossau and Aspe valleys. Each valley has its own villages, its own dialect of mountain life, and its own way into the high country. Many walkers return year after year, and choose one valley at a time, rather than trying to see the whole park at once.

The most famous place in the park is the Cirque de Gavarnie. A cirque is a great bowl of rock, carved out by ancient glaciers. At Gavarnie the walls rise in tiers, like the seats of a stone amphitheatre. Snow often lingers on the ledges well into summer. The great waterfall of Gavarnie drops more than four hundred metres down the back wall, which places it among the tallest waterfalls in mainland Europe. On a still day you hear it long before you reach its foot.

The nineteenth-century writer Victor Hugo visited Gavarnie and wrote about it with real astonishment. He was not alone. Painters, scientists, and early mountaineers came here in growing numbers through that century. In 1997 UNESCO listed the wider landscape of Pyrénées and Mont Perdu as a World Heritage site. Here is the second surprise: the listing honours not only the rock and the ice, but also the pastoral culture of the shepherds who have used these slopes for centuries.

High on the rim above Gavarnie, there is a square notch in the ridge. It is called the Brèche de Roland, or Roland's Breach. Legend has it that Roland, the knight of Charlemagne, tried to break his sword Durendal against the rock so that no enemy could take it. The blade did not break. Instead, according to the tale, it split the mountain open. Geologists give a less dramatic answer, but the story has stuck to the stone for many centuries.

East of Cauterets rises the Vignemale, the highest summit on the French side of the range, at 3,298 metres. Its north face carries one of the last glaciers in the French Pyrenees. Those glaciers are shrinking fast, and old photographs show how much ice has gone. The Vignemale also has a strange human story. In the late nineteenth century the Irish-French mountaineer Henry Russell leased the mountain for ninety-nine years. He had small caves dug into the rock near the summit, and he slept in them, hosting friends at altitude.

Near Cauterets, the road ends at the Pont d'Espagne, a stone bridge above a churning river. From there, paths climb through pine forest to the Lac de Gaube. On a calm morning the lake mirrors the Vignemale at its far end. This is one of the gentler ways to feel the high mountains without serious climbing. Expect company in summer. The lakes, forests, and bridges here attract families, anglers, and hikers from all over France.

Now look up, and look carefully at the slopes. The park is home to the isard, the Pyrenean chamois, a nimble goat-antelope with curved black horns. Marmots whistle from the boulder fields, a sharp warning call that carries far. Overhead you may see griffon vultures circling on warm air. With luck you might spot the bearded vulture, a rare bird that feeds largely on bones. It drops them onto rocks to crack them open, then eats the marrow and fragments.

The brown bear also lives in the Pyrenees, though it is rare and shy, and most visitors will never see one. In the rivers lives an even stranger creature, the Pyrenean desman. It is a small aquatic mammal with a long, mobile snout, a little like a mole adapted for swimming. It is very hard to see in the wild. Its presence is one reason the park watches water quality so closely. Down at your feet, look for wild flowers in early summer, including irises, gentians, and orchids.

The human layer matters as much as the wild one. In summer, shepherds still move cattle, sheep, and horses up to the high pastures, a practice called transhumance. You will pass stone huts, called cabanes, where herders shelter. You may hear bells long before you see the animals. Please keep your distance from large guard dogs, which protect flocks from predators. Remember too that dogs are not allowed in the heart of the park, even on a lead.

So pause where you are. Notice the colour of the rock, grey granite here, layered limestone there. Notice how the light changes as clouds cross the peaks, and how quickly the temperature drops in shadow. Listen again for the water, still running somewhere above you. When you walk on, take only photographs and memories, and leave the flowers where they grow. These mountains have taken many thousands of years to look the way they do today.

Tiered rock walls of the Cirque de Gavarnie.
Marmots standing upright on sunny boulder fields.
Stone shepherds' huts scattered across high pastures.
The Vignemale reflected in Lac de Gaube on calm mornings.
Before you go

Practical tips

Start popular walks early to avoid summer crowds.
Leave dogs at home; they are banned in the park's heart.
Carry warm layers, since mountain weather changes very quickly.
Keep a calm distance from flock guard dogs.
Choose one valley per trip rather than rushing the whole park.
