The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes across the flat ground here from the Firth of Forth, and it carries a faint metallic hum as it moves past the steel. Under your feet the path is smooth and level. Ahead of you, two enormous horse heads rise out of the landscape. One bends down toward the water. The other lifts its head high, as if it has just heard something in the distance.

These are the Kelpies. They stand in Helix Park, on the open land between Falkirk and Grangemouth, in Scotland's central belt. Each head rises about thirty metres above the ground. That is roughly the height of a ten-storey building. From far away they look almost solid, like dark silver casting. Up close, the surface breaks apart into hundreds of separate pieces of stainless steel.

The sculptor was Andy Scott, a Scottish artist known for large public figures in metal. He did not invent fantasy creatures here. He modelled the heads on real working horses — Clydesdales, the heavy draught breed that comes from the Clyde valley in western Scotland. Two of these horses, called Duke and Baron, stood as his models. Many visitors know the sculptures only as the Kelpies, and they miss the fact that two real animals gave their faces to the steel.

So why that name? In Scottish folklore, a kelpie is a water spirit. According to the old tales, it often takes the form of a horse and lives in rivers and lochs. Legend has it that the kelpie could tempt a traveller onto its back and then carry them down into the water. These are stories, not history. But the name gives the sculptures a second layer: half working animal, half creature from the deep.

The working-horse side of the story is the one rooted in fact. Before engines took over, horses powered much of Scotland's industry. They pulled barges along canals, hauled carts, and worked on farms across this region. The canals around Falkirk were busy routes for goods moving between the east and west coasts. The sculptures are a tribute to that muscle and that labour, which built so much of the modern country.

Now look at the water beside you. The Kelpies stand next to a stretch of canal and a lock, created as part of the wider Helix project. This new section connects the Forth and Clyde Canal toward the River Carron and the Forth. Boats can pass right between and beside the heads. Imagine stepping off a narrowboat and finding these two figures towering over the lock gates.

Walk closer and study the structure. The heads are not hollow shells of smooth metal. Instead, an inner steel frame holds an outer skin of overlapping stainless steel plates. Gaps between the plates let light and air pass through. Because of this, the sculptures change with the weather. On a grey day they look heavy and dark. When the sun breaks through, they shine almost white.

Here is a detail that many people miss. Stand directly beneath one head and look up. From below, the horse disappears. You see only a pattern of plates, beams, and sky — more like the inside of a ship or a bridge than an animal. Then step back fifty paces, and the face returns. The ears, the flared nostrils, the line of the mane all come back into focus.

The Kelpies were completed in the 2010s and opened to visitors soon after. Since then they have become a symbol of Falkirk and a common sight from the nearby motorway. Smaller versions, called maquettes, have travelled to other places, so some people meet the horses at a reduced scale long before they come here. Nothing at that size prepares you for the real thing.

As evening falls, the experience changes again. The sculptures are lit after dark, and the lights shift in colour across the steel. The reflections in the canal double the heads. The park grows quieter, the wind seems louder, and the two figures look less like monuments and more like animals standing watch over the water. If you can stay until dusk, it is worth the wait.

The parkland around you is part of the experience too. Helix Park is a wide green space with paths for walking and cycling, open lawns, wetland edges, and a play area for children. Families spread out on the grass. Dog walkers move along the canal towpath. Runners pass under the heads without slowing down. For local people, this is an everyday park, not only a visitor attraction.

If you have more time, the Falkirk Wheel lies a short drive to the west. It is a rotating boat lift that raises vessels between two canals, and it carries the same idea as the Kelpies: Scotland's waterways, rebuilt for a new century. Together, the two sites tell a story of canals that fell into decline and then came back to life.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the lowered head, close to the water, and then at the raised one, alert and listening. Notice how the light moves across the steel plates as clouds pass. Listen to the wind, the ducks on the canal, and the distant traffic. As you walk away, turn around once more. From each new angle, the horses seem to shift — as if they are still working, still waiting for the next barge to come.
