The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the trees, the Thames slides past, slow and green. Underfoot, the grass rises and falls in long, uneven swells. They look natural, but they are not. Every bump and hollow around you was dug, piled, and shaped by human hands. You are standing on the bones of one of the most important castles in medieval England.

Today this place is a quiet public garden. Dog walkers pass. Children roll down the slopes. A few broken pieces of flint and stone wall stand among the lawns. It is hard to imagine that for nearly six hundred years, kings, queens, and armies treated this patch of riverbank as a prize worth fighting for. But the shape of the ground still holds that story.

The story starts before the castle. Wallingford was one of the fortified towns built by the Anglo-Saxons, the English kingdoms before the Norman Conquest. These towns were called burhs, and they defended Wessex against Viking raids. The reason was the river. Here, the Thames could be crossed, and whoever held the crossing controlled movement across southern England. The town's old earthen banks still run around its edges today.

Then came 1066. After his victory at Hastings, William the Conqueror did not march straight into London. Instead, he swung west and crossed the Thames here, at Wallingford. A local lord named Wigod is said to have welcomed him and submitted to the new king. It was an early sign that the English would not stop the Normans. Soon afterwards, a castle began to rise beside the crossing.

The first castle was built of earth and timber. Its builder is usually named as Robert D'Oyly, a Norman baron who also raised the castle at Oxford. The design was a motte and bailey. That means a tall mound of earth, the motte, topped with a wooden tower, beside one or more fenced yards, the baileys. Over the following centuries, the timber gave way to stone, and the castle grew into a large complex with several rings of ditches.

The castle's greatest drama came in the twelfth century, during a civil war later called the Anarchy. Two cousins fought for the English crown. One was King Stephen. The other was the Empress Matilda, daughter of the previous king. Wallingford stayed loyal to Matilda. Its lord, Brien FitzCount, held the castle for her through years of hardship. Stephen's forces besieged it more than once, but the walls did not fall.

Here is one of the best stories of the whole war. In the winter of 1142, Matilda was trapped by Stephen's army in Oxford Castle. According to the chroniclers, she slipped out at night and crossed the frozen landscape on foot through the snow. Many tellings add that she wore white, so she would vanish against the snowfields. Her escape eventually brought her to safety here, behind Wallingford's walls.

Wallingford also played a part in ending that war. In 1153, the armies of Stephen and Matilda's son, Henry, faced each other near the town. Instead of fighting, the leaders talked. The meeting helped lead to a settlement that let Stephen keep the crown for life, with Henry to follow him. Henry became King Henry the Second, the first of the Plantagenet kings. So a peace that shaped English history began on these meadows.

In the thirteenth century, the castle passed to Richard, Earl of Cornwall, the younger brother of King Henry the Third. Richard was one of the richest men in Europe, and he spent heavily here. He strengthened the defences and made the castle a grand royal residence. Later, King Edward the Second gave it to his favourite, Piers Gaveston, a choice that angered many nobles. The castle stayed close to the royal family for generations.

Royal women lived here too. Joan of Kent, the mother of King Richard the Second, died at Wallingford Castle in the late fourteenth century. Richard's very young second wife, Isabella of Valois, a French princess who was still a child when she married, also spent time here. Imagine the sounds that once filled this space — horses, servants, music, the smoke of great kitchens — where now you mostly hear birds and the river.

The last chapter came with the English Civil War in the seventeenth century, the conflict between King Charles the First and Parliament. Wallingford was a Royalist stronghold, guarding the approaches to the king's base at Oxford. In 1646 it came under siege. The garrison held on even after Oxford itself surrendered, making it one of the last Royalist strongholds to give in. That stubbornness had a price.

Parliament did not want such a strong fortress to be used against it ever again. A few years after the war, the castle was deliberately pulled down. This was called slighting. Much of its stone was carted away and reused in other buildings, so pieces of this castle may sit unnoticed in walls elsewhere. What remains here are fragments of masonry, the great earthworks, and the outline of the old ditches.

As you walk on, look at the ground with new eyes. The steep slopes, the deep dips filled with grass and trees, the sudden ridges — these are defences, not decoration. Notice the flint in the surviving walls, a stone common in this chalk country. Then walk toward the river. Stand where the Thames runs past, and you are looking at the reason this castle existed at all... a simple place to cross the water, which shaped nearly a thousand years of history.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full gardens to understand the castle's huge scale.
Wear sturdy shoes; the earthwork slopes can be steep and slippery.
Finish at the riverside to see the old Thames crossing.
Walk part of the old Saxon town banks afterwards.
Visit in winter when bare trees show the earthworks clearly.
