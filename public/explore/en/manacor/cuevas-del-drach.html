The guide

The story

The air changes first. A few steps below the entrance, the warm Mallorcan breeze is gone. In its place is a cool, damp stillness that settles on your skin and stays there. Water drips somewhere ahead. You cannot see it yet, but you can hear it: a slow, patient tapping on stone. Underfoot, the path is smooth and slightly slick. The light is low and golden. Welcome to the Cuevas del Drach, the Caves of the Dragon, on the eastern coast of Mallorca.

You are inside the municipality of Manacor, close to the small harbour town of Porto Cristo. The Mediterranean is only a short walk away. That closeness matters, because the sea is part of this cave's story. Above your head, the island is limestone. Below it, over a very long span of time, water has dissolved that stone and hollowed it into chambers. What you are walking through now is not one cave but a connected system of several caves, joined into a single route.

The name sounds like something from a fairy tale, and in a sense it is. Drach is the old Catalan word for dragon. Locals say a dragon once guarded a treasure deep in these passages. Other versions of the tale speak of hidden loot left by pirates, or by Templar knights. None of this is documented history. It is folklore, the kind of story people tell about any dark place that seems to go on forever. Still, as you look at the shadows ahead, the idea of a dragon is easy to understand.

The four caves in the system each carry their own name. There is the Black Cave and the White Cave, named for the colour of their stone. There is the Cave of the French, and there is a cave named after Luis Salvador, an Austrian archduke. That last name is a clue. In the late nineteenth century, Archduke Ludwig Salvator was deeply in love with Mallorca. He studied the island, wrote about it, and encouraged scientists to explore it.

One of those scientists was the Frenchman Édouard-Alfred Martel, often called the father of modern cave science. In 1896, at the archduke's invitation, Martel came here to survey the caves. He crawled, climbed, and rowed by candlelight through passages that few people had seen. And in the deepest part of the system, he found something remarkable — a wide underground lake. Today it carries his name. You will reach Lake Martel near the end of your walk.

As you move forward, look up. The ceilings hang with stalactites, the stone icicles that grow down from above. Some are thinner than a pencil. Others are as thick as tree trunks. Each one grows from a single drop of water at a time. The water carries dissolved minerals, and when a drop falls, it leaves a tiny trace behind. Repeat that for thousands of years, and you get the forest of stone around you. Below, stalagmites rise to meet them.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Near the waterline, some formations look strange. Instead of being smooth and pointed, they bulge into lumpy, rounded shapes, almost like candle wax gathered at one height. These are not random. They form where cave water meets the surface of brackish water, a mix of fresh water and seawater. Scientists study these growths because they mark old sea levels. In other words, the walls here hold a record of how the Mediterranean rose and fell over long ages.

That is the second surprise. The lake ahead of you is not sealed off from the outside world. It connects, through cracks in the rock, with the sea itself. So when you finally see the water, remember that it is part fresh and part salt. Its level shifts very slightly with the sea. The water is also strikingly clear. In places you can see straight down to the bottom, and the reflection of the stalactites makes it hard to tell where the stone ends and the water begins.

Notice how quiet the people around you become. Caves do this to visitors. Voices drop. Children stop running. The constant temperature, the dampness, and the soft lighting slow everyone down. The lighting is carefully placed to show the shapes of the rock without flattening them. Pay attention to the colours too. The stone moves from cream to honey to deep amber, and in the darker chambers, it turns almost grey. The Black Cave and the White Cave earn their names here.

At Lake Martel, the walk opens into a large chamber with rows of seats facing the water. This is where the cave's most famous ritual happens. The lights dim almost to darkness. Then, out on the lake, small lit boats appear and drift slowly across the surface. Musicians on board play classical music, and the sound of strings fills the chamber. This underground concert has been part of the visit for many decades. It is short, but in this setting, a few minutes feel very long.

Listen to how the music behaves. The rock walls do not echo it harshly. Instead, the sound seems to soften and spread, as if the whole cave were holding it. After the concert, many visitors can choose to cross the lake by boat themselves. Sitting low on the water, you see the ceiling from a new angle. Drops occasionally fall from above and ring on the surface. It is one of the few places where you can float through the inside of an island.

It helps to keep a few practical things in mind. The route involves many steps, both up and down, and the ground can be wet. Wear shoes with good grip. The air stays cool even in high summer, so a light layer is welcome. Visits are guided in groups and follow a set timing, so arriving early gives you a calmer start. Photography rules can change, so check the current guidance at the entrance before you raise your camera.

When you climb back toward daylight, take a moment before you leave. Turn and look once more at the formations behind you. Every point of stone you passed today is still growing, one drop at a time, far too slowly for any person to see. The cave you visited this afternoon will look almost exactly the same for your grandchildren. And yet it will not be quite the same. A few more traces of mineral will have settled.

Now the warm air returns. You may smell pine and salt, and hear the cicadas from the trees outside. Porto Cristo and its harbour are just down the road, where the same sea that seeps into Lake Martel laps against the boats. As you walk away, think of the water beneath your feet — quietly moving through the limestone, shaping new chambers in the dark, where no dragon, and no visitor, has yet been.
