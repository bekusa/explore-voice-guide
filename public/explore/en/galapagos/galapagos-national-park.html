The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wind, there is a low, steady crackle as waves pull back over black lava rock. A sea lion grunts somewhere close. The air smells of salt, warm stone, and something sharper, the musky scent of animals that have no reason to run. Look down, and the ground is not soil but cooled volcanic rock, folded like rope in places. This is Galápagos National Park, and almost everything you are about to see was made by fire.

The islands sit on the equator in the Pacific Ocean, roughly a thousand kilometres west of mainland Ecuador. They are volcanic, and they are still forming. The western islands are the youngest, and some of their volcanoes have erupted in recent decades. The eastern islands are older and more worn down. So as you travel across the archipelago, you are also travelling through geological time, from raw new rock to weathered land with deeper plant life.

The park itself was created in 1959. That year was chosen with care. It marked one hundred years since Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species, the book that changed how people understand life on Earth. It was Ecuador's first national park. Today the park protects about ninety-seven percent of the islands' land. The rest is the towns and farms where people live. In 1978, the islands were among the first places ever listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. For most of human history, nobody lived here. The first recorded visit came in 1535, almost by accident. A Spanish bishop was sailing from Panama to Peru when his ship was becalmed and carried off course by the currents. His crew found dry, rocky islands, very little fresh water, and huge tortoises. The name Galápagos comes from an old Spanish word linked to those tortoises and the shape of their shells.

Those tortoises paid a heavy price. For centuries, pirates and later whalers stopped here and took them on board as living food. A tortoise can survive many months without eating or drinking, so sailors stacked them in ships' holds. Many thousands were taken this way. Goats, rats, and other animals that arrived with people did further damage. Some tortoise populations vanished completely. Others came very close to the edge.

Then, in 1835, a young naturalist came ashore from a British survey ship called the Beagle. Charles Darwin spent only about five weeks in the islands. He collected birds, plants, and rocks, and he heard that local people could tell which island a tortoise came from by the shape of its shell. Only later, back in England, did the full meaning of his notes become clear. Similar animals on neighbouring islands had changed in different ways to fit their own small worlds.

You can see that idea in the animals around you. Look at the finches, small brown birds that are easy to ignore. Their beaks differ from species to species, some thick for cracking seeds, some thin for picking insects. Look at the marine iguanas lying in dark heaps on the rocks. They are the only lizards in the world that feed in the sea. They dive to graze on algae, then lie in the sun to warm up again.

The ocean is the reason this place works. Cold currents rise from the deep and meet warmer water near the surface. This mix brings nutrients up to the light, and life follows. That is why a penguin can live here, right on the equator. The Galápagos penguin is one of the smallest penguins in the world. Nearby, the flightless cormorant has small, stubby wings. With few predators on land, it simply had no need to fly.

And then there are the blue-footed boobies. Their feet really are that bright, a clear turquoise blue. During courtship, the male lifts one foot, then the other, in a slow, careful strut, showing his colour to the female. It looks comic, but it matters. Brighter feet can signal a healthier bird. If you are lucky enough to see this display, stay still and simply watch. It is one of the quieter wonders of the islands.

You may notice that the animals here barely react to you. A sea lion may sleep across the path. A bird may sit on its nest a short step from your feet. Because they evolved without large land predators, many species never learned to fear people. This calm is a gift, but it is also a responsibility. Park rules ask visitors to keep about two metres from wildlife, never to touch or feed animals, and to stay on marked trails.

That is why most visitor sites can only be visited with a certified naturalist guide. Group sizes are controlled, and trails are carefully laid out. In 1998, Ecuador also created a large marine reserve around the islands, protecting the waters as well as the land. Much of the park's work today is restoration. Teams have removed introduced goats and rats from several islands, giving native plants and birds room to recover.

One of the best known stories is about a single tortoise. Lonesome George was the last known tortoise from Pinta Island. For decades he lived at the research centre on Santa Cruz, and for many people he became a symbol of extinction. He died in 2012. There is hopeful news too. On Española Island, a breeding programme helped the giant tortoise population grow from just a handful of animals to more than a thousand.

If you are on Santa Cruz, the breeding centres near Puerto Ayora let you see young tortoises at different ages. Some are small enough to fit in two hands. In the highlands, you can often see adult tortoises moving slowly through wet grass and mud pools. The highlands feel very different from the coast. The air is cooler, the ground is green, and mist often hangs low over the trees.

Before you move on, take one more moment where you are. Notice the sound of the water, the texture of the rock under your shoes, the animal nearby that is watching you with little concern. This place was not designed for people. It is a working laboratory of life that still runs on its own rules. As you walk away, keep to the path, leave the stones where they lie, and let the islands stay exactly as you found them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book visitor sites through tours with certified naturalist guides.
Keep about two metres between yourself and any wildlife.
Stay on marked trails at every visitor site.
Bring strong reef-safe sunscreen for the equatorial sun.
Pack sturdy closed shoes for sharp, uneven lava rock.
