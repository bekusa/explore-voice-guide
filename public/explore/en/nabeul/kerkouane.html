The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes in off the Mediterranean, low and steady, and moves through dry grass between ancient walls. Somewhere below the cliff edge, waves break against pale rock. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of sandy soil and worn stone thresholds. There are no towers here and no columns reaching into the sky. Instead, you see a low, orderly maze of walls at knee and waist height, laid out under a wide sky. This is Kerkouane, and its quiet is part of what makes it unusual.

You are standing on the tip of Cap Bon, the long finger of land in northeastern Tunisia that points toward Sicily. The ruins in front of you belonged to the Punic world. Punic is the word historians use for the Phoenician culture of the western Mediterranean, centred on the great city of Carthage. Phoenician sailors came originally from the coast of what is now Lebanon. Over centuries, they and their descendants built ports and towns across North Africa. Kerkouane was one of them.

Here is the first surprise. Nobody knows what the people who lived here called their town. The name Kerkouane is modern, taken from the local area. The original Punic name has not survived in any record that scholars can confidently link to this site. So you are walking through a real town, with streets and homes and workshops, whose own name is lost.

And here is the second surprise, which explains why this place matters so much. Most Punic cities were later rebuilt by the Romans. Carthage itself was destroyed and then covered by a Roman city. Kerkouane was different. It was abandoned, most likely in the third century before Christ, during the long wars between Carthage and Rome. Afterwards, no one built over it. That is why its Punic street plan survives so clearly, and why it is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site, together with its necropolis.

The ruins stayed hidden for a very long time. They were recognised in the twentieth century, in the early 1950s, and archaeologists have studied the site in stages ever since. Look around at the layout. The streets run in fairly straight lines, and the houses sit in neat blocks. This was a planned town, not a random cluster of huts. Even today, without roofs or upper floors, you can read where one home ends and the next begins.

Walk into one of the houses and look closely at the floor plan. Many homes were organised around an open central courtyard. Light and air came in from above. Rooms opened off this courtyard, and a narrow corridor often led from the street, so passers-by could not look straight inside. A well or a cistern helped collect precious water. It is a design that still feels familiar in older North African houses today, built around privacy and shade.

Now look for something very personal. In many houses you will find small, built-in bathtubs. They are compact, with a seat moulded into one end, so a person sat rather than lay down. Many are coated in a reddish waterproof plaster, and some still keep that warm colour. Private bathing rooms in ordinary homes were not common in this era. Their presence here suggests that the people of Kerkouane cared a great deal about cleanliness, and that many households lived reasonably well.

Keep your eyes on the floors as you walk. Some rooms are paved with a hard surface of mortar mixed with crushed pottery, scattered with small pieces of white stone. In at least one doorway, these white pieces form a simple figure, a triangle topped by a horizontal line and a circle. This is the sign of Tanit, a major goddess of the Punic world. To find her symbol set into a household floor, right where people stepped every day, brings faith down to a very human scale.

Kerkouane was also a working town. Archaeologists found evidence linked to the production of purple dye, made from sea snails gathered along this coast. Phoenician purple was famous across the ancient Mediterranean, and it was valuable, because each snail produced only a tiny amount of colour. The process was also known to be smelly, which may be one reason dye works were often kept toward the edges of settlements. Fishing, pottery and other crafts also seem to have supported life here.

The town was protected by defensive walls on the landward side, while the cliffs and the sea guarded the other edge. Excavations also uncovered a sanctuary, a sacred space with rooms and courts used for worship. Its exact rituals are not fully understood, so treat any confident story about them with care. What you can see clearly is how closely the town was tied to the water. The sea was its road, its larder and its view.

A short distance away lies the necropolis, the burial ground of the town. Tombs were cut into the rock, and some contained grave goods such as pottery and small personal objects. One of the best-known finds is a carved wooden coffin lid shaped like a woman, often called the Lady of Kerkouane. Wood rarely survives for so many centuries, which makes it a remarkable object. Some finds from the site are displayed in the small museum here, which is worth a visit to put faces and objects to the stones.

As you walk, notice the materials of the walls themselves. Much of the masonry is local sandstone, laid in courses, with some walls built of smaller stones held together with mortar. The colour shifts through the day, from cool grey in the morning to honey and rose in late afternoon. Lizards dart between the stones. Wild herbs grow in the cracks, and on a warm day you may smell them in the air as you brush past.

It is worth stepping to the seaward edge and simply looking out. The water here changes from deep blue to pale turquoise over the shallows. On a clear day, the horizon feels very wide. Sicily is not visible from here, but it is not far across that sea, and that short crossing helps explain why this coast became such contested ground between Carthage and Rome.

Before you leave, stand for a moment in one of the courtyards. Picture a roof overhead, a cooking fire, voices in a language that few people today can read. Then look down at the threshold under your feet, worn smooth by people who crossed it more than two thousand years ago. As you walk back toward the entrance, listen again to the wind and the waves. They are the same sounds the people of this nameless town heard every day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early or late to avoid the strongest midday sun.
Bring a hat and water; shade is very limited.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and sandy paths.
Visit the small site museum to see the finds.
Allow extra time to walk to the clifftop sea edge.
