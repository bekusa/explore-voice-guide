The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind carries fine grit across the ground, and it ticks softly against stone and dried mud. The air smells of dust and warm earth. Pale brown mounds rise from the desert floor around you, and under a bright sky they almost blend into the hills. Then your eye catches a straight edge, a stepped terrace, a wall too regular to be natural. This is Cahuachi, and people shaped these hills by hand.

Cahuachi sits in the valley of the Nasca River, in southern Peru, west of the modern town of Nazca. It belongs to the Nasca culture. That society flourished on this coastal desert roughly two thousand years ago, in the early centuries of the first millennium. The Nasca left no written records. What we know comes from their buildings, their pottery, their textiles, and the patient work of archaeologists who have dug here for decades.

Here is the first surprise. Many of the mounds around you are not built from the ground up. The Nasca took existing natural hills and remodelled them. They cut terraces into the slopes, faced them with adobe walls, and added platforms and rooms on top. So each mound is part geology and part architecture. From a distance, the line between the two is hard to see. Up close, look for the layers of mud brick sitting on the natural rock.

The site is large. Researchers have counted dozens of these modified mounds, spread across the river terrace. The most important is often called the Great Temple. It is a stepped structure that rises in levels above the plain. Around it sit plazas, open spaces where large groups of people could gather. The scale tells you that Cahuachi mattered. Moving this much earth and brick in a desert takes many hands and a strong shared purpose.

And that purpose is the second surprise. For a long time, people assumed a place this big must have been a city. But careful excavation found little evidence of ordinary daily life. There were few signs of permanent homes, workshops, or the everyday rubbish that crowded towns produce. The archaeologist Helaine Silverman argued that Cahuachi was mainly a ceremonial centre. In her view, the site stood mostly empty for much of the year.

Imagine it, then, on a festival day. People would arrive from settlements along the valleys, walking across the desert toward these mounds. They would gather in the plazas, share food and drink, and take part in rituals whose exact meaning we no longer know. Then they would go home, and the wind would return to the empty terraces. Many scholars describe Cahuachi as a pilgrimage centre, a place people visited rather than a place they lived.

What did they leave behind? Excavations have recovered fine Nasca pottery, painted in many colours. The Nasca were skilled potters. Their vessels show birds, fish, plants, and fierce mythical beings, painted in reds, oranges, creams, and blacks. Archaeologists have also found broken panpipes made of clay. That detail brings sound back into this quiet place. Picture thin, breathy music rising over the crowd in the plazas.

Some finds are harder to look at. The Nasca are known for so-called trophy heads. These are human heads that were specially prepared, often with a cord passed through the forehead so they could be carried or hung. Examples have been found in the Nasca region, and the motif appears often in Nasca art. Their meaning is still debated. They may relate to warfare, to ancestors, or to ideas about fertility and the renewal of crops.

Much of the modern work at Cahuachi has been led by the Italian archaeologist Giuseppe Orefici. His team has excavated here over many years, uncovering walls, rooms, and offerings. They have also worked to protect the fragile adobe. Mud brick does not survive rain well, and even in this dry place, rare heavy storms and floods can do serious damage. Some of the walls you see have been consolidated to keep them standing.

Why did Cahuachi decline? There is no single agreed answer. Some researchers point to natural disasters, such as floods and earthquakes, which may have damaged the buildings. Others see changes in Nasca society and religion. What seems clear is that, over time, the site lost its central role. Parts of it appear to have been deliberately covered over. Later, other people used the area as a burial ground.

Now turn and look toward the wider desert. The famous Nazca Lines lie on the plains in this same region. These are giant figures and long straight lines, made by clearing the dark surface stones to expose lighter ground beneath. Some researchers have suggested links between the lines and Cahuachi, perhaps as ritual paths. That idea is still discussed, so treat it as a possibility, not a fact. But the two belong to the same culture and the same landscape.

Water is the quiet theme of this whole region. The Nasca River often runs low, and for parts of the year its surface can be nearly dry. In a place like this, water decides who lives and who leaves. Much Nasca art and ritual seems concerned with water, rain, and fertile fields. Standing here, between bare mounds and the green strip of the valley, you can feel how close the line is between desert and life.

As you walk, notice how the light changes the site. In the middle of the day, the mounds look flat and pale. When the sun is lower, shadows fall into the terraces, and the stepped forms appear sharply. The colour shifts too, from washed-out beige to warm gold and brown. Take a moment to stay on the paths and let your eyes adjust. Small details, like a line of bricks or a corner, slowly come forward.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Feel the grit under your shoes and the dry wind on your skin. Two thousand years ago, crowds came across this same ground, carrying painted pots and playing clay pipes. Then they went home, and silence returned. Today the mounds are quiet again. As you walk back, look over your shoulder. The hills will start to look like ordinary hills again... unless you know where to look.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early or late in the day to avoid harsh heat.
Bring plenty of water, sun hat, and strong sunscreen.
Stay on marked paths to protect fragile adobe walls.
Combine your visit with a trip to the Nazca Lines.
Hire a local guide or driver for the desert road.
