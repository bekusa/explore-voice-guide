The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the loudest sound is often a cowbell somewhere below, or the wind moving through the spruce. The air is thin and cool, even in high summer. Your boots have carried you a long way to reach this small wooden house on a green shoulder of rock. It looks almost ordinary at first. A pale timber chalet with a low roof and a carved balcony. Nothing about it suggests that a king once climbed here to escape his own kingdom.

This is the King's House on Schachen, high in the Wetterstein mountains above Garmisch-Partenkirchen. It stands at roughly 1,860 metres above sea level. It was built for King Ludwig the Second of Bavaria, the ruler most people know from the fairy-tale castle of Neuschwanstein. Work on this retreat took place in the early 1870s. Compared with his other palaces, it is tiny. But in many ways, it may be the most personal place he ever made.

Ludwig came to the throne very young, in 1864, at just eighteen. He was shy, romantic, and deeply uncomfortable with crowds, ministers, and public duty. As the years passed, he withdrew further into the mountains and into worlds of his own design. Some of those worlds were medieval, some were French, some were drawn from the operas of Richard Wagner, the composer he admired and supported. Here on the Schachen, he chose something different. He chose the East.

From the outside, though, the house gives nothing away. The exterior follows the style of a Swiss chalet, a wooden mountain house of the kind that was fashionable in the nineteenth century. The ground floor holds a set of modest rooms: a study, a bedroom, a dining room, and spaces for daily life. They are panelled in wood and furnished simply by royal standards. If the tour ended here, you might think the king had simply wanted a comfortable hunting lodge.

Then you climb the stairs. And everything changes. The entire upper floor is one room — the Turkish Hall. It is an explosion of colour after the quiet wood below. Here you find rich fabrics, gilded detail, deep reds and blues, coloured glass, and the shimmer of peacock feathers. Low cushioned divans line the walls. At the centre, a fountain once added the sound of running water. It is an imagined palace of the Ottoman world, the Turkish empire, placed on top of a Bavarian alpine hut.

This is the first surprise most visitors carry home. The contrast is deliberate. Ludwig loved stage sets and illusions. Climbing from plain timber into this glowing hall was meant to feel like walking from the real world into a dream. In the nineteenth century, many Europeans were fascinated by an idealised, romantic picture of the Orient. That picture was more fantasy than fact. This room shows that fantasy in its purest form, built for an audience of one.

Accounts from the time say the king sometimes asked his servants to dress in oriental costume here. He would sit on the cushions, read, and perhaps smoke a water pipe while the evening fell outside. Treat the finer details of these stories with some caution. Ludwig attracted rumour all his life, and many tales grew larger after his death. Still, the room itself makes the spirit of those stories easy to believe. It was built for long, private evenings.

The house also has a strong link to one date. Ludwig was born on the 25th of August, and he is said to have liked spending his birthday up here, far from court ceremony. Think about what that meant. While Munich prepared official celebrations, the king could be sitting in this hall, high above the valley, with only mountains for company. For a man who found people exhausting, this solitude was perhaps the best gift.

Now step back outside and look at the setting. This is the second surprise. Walk a few minutes to the edge of the plateau, to the small viewing pavilion, and the ground drops away dramatically. Below you lies the deep valley of the Reintal. Across it rise the grey limestone walls of the Wetterstein range, with the Zugspitze massif, Germany's highest mountain, among them. The king did not build here by accident. He built where the view would stop his breath.

Near the house you will also find an alpine botanical garden. It was laid out around 1900, after the king's lifetime, and it is cared for by the Munich Botanical Garden. Hundreds of mountain plants from the Alps and from high ranges around the world grow among the rocks. In early summer the beds fill with small flowers in white, yellow, and violet. It is a quieter pleasure than the Turkish Hall, but it rewards slow walking.

There is something important about how you arrived here. No road brings ordinary visitors to the Schachen. You came on foot, on a long mountain path, probably for several hours. In Ludwig's day, the king travelled up by horse or carriage along a track built for him. For you, the effort is part of the meaning. You have earned this view in the same way the house was meant to be reached — slowly, and away from everyday life.

Ludwig's story ended sadly. In 1886, his government declared him unfit to rule, and a few days later he died in Lake Starnberg, in circumstances that are still debated today. His great projects had left heavy debts. Yet the places he built are now among the most visited in Bavaria. This one remains the hardest to reach. Because of that, it keeps a little of the privacy he wanted so badly.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Look back at the plain wooden walls of the chalet. Remember the colours hidden behind them, on the floor above. Listen again to the wind, the cowbells, maybe the voices of other hikers at the mountain inn nearby. As you start the walk down, the house will disappear behind the trees quite quickly. But you now know its secret: a quiet mountain cabin, holding a king's private dream of somewhere far away.
