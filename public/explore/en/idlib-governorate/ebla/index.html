The guide

The story

The wind arrives first. It moves across the flat fields of northern Syria, lifts a little pale dust from the slopes, and carries it over the low mound where you are standing. Underfoot, the ground is uneven. It is packed earth, broken limestone, and fragments of old mudbrick, all baked by long summers. Around you are olive groves and open farmland. Nothing here is tall or showy. Still, under this quiet hill lay one of the great cities of the ancient Near East.

The hill has a local name, Tell Mardikh. In Arabic, a tell is an artificial mound. It forms over many centuries as people build, collapse, and rebuild on the same spot. Each layer of houses becomes the floor for the next. A tell is like a stack of pages, and the oldest pages lie at the bottom. Today we know this one by an older name. It is Ebla, a city that rose to power in the third millennium before Christ.

For a long time, Ebla was only a name. Scholars had seen it in Mesopotamian inscriptions. Kings in distant Mesopotamia boasted of defeating it, but nobody knew where it was. Then, in the 1960s, an Italian archaeological team from the University of Rome began digging here. Paolo Matthiae led the team. They uncovered walls, gates, and buildings, but the identity of the site was still uncertain.

The answer came from a fragment of stone. In 1968, the team found the lower part of a statue with an inscription. The text named a local ruler and mentioned Ebla. For the first time, a name from old texts could be linked to a real place on the map. It was a quiet moment that changed what historians knew about ancient Syria.

The greatest surprise came a few years later. In the mid-1970s, archaeologists were excavating a large building on the central height of the city. They now call it Royal Palace G. Inside, they found thousands of clay tablets and fragments, covered in wedge-shaped cuneiform writing. Many still lay where they had fallen. Some had collapsed from wooden shelves when the room burned. It was one of the largest archives ever found from the third millennium before Christ.

Here is the irony. Fire destroyed the palace, but fire also saved its records. Clay tablets were usually left to dry in the sun, so they could crumble over time. When the palace burned, the heat baked many of them hard, like pottery. The disaster that ended this royal court preserved its paperwork for more than four thousand years. Without that fire, much of what we know about Ebla would be gone.

So what did the tablets say? Most of them are practical records. They count textiles, metals, livestock, food rations, and gifts between rulers. They show a state that managed wealth on a large scale and traded across a wide region. There are also word lists and school texts. Some of these lists pair Sumerian words with words in the local language. Sumerian was the old written language of southern Mesopotamia, so these lists worked like an early bilingual dictionary.

That local language is now called Eblaite. It belongs to the Semitic family, the same broad family that later includes Arabic, Hebrew, and Aramaic. Before these finds, many scholars thought that Syria in this period lived in the shadow of Mesopotamia. The archive told a different story. Ebla had its own scribes, its own administration, and its own diplomacy. It was an urban power in its own right, not only a distant trading partner.

As you walk, notice the shape of the land. The central rise is the acropolis, the high citadel where the palace and important buildings stood. Around it spreads the lower town, a wide ring of lower ground. At the edges, you can see long earthen ramparts. These massive embankments once surrounded the city. Gaps in them mark ancient gateways. From the top, the plan of the city is still easy to read, even after so many centuries.

Ebla did not end with the burning of Palace G. Historians still debate who destroyed the palace in the third millennium. Several Mesopotamian rulers claimed victories over Ebla, and the evidence is not simple. Afterwards, the city recovered and was rebuilt. In the Middle Bronze Age, it grew again, with new temples, palaces, and those great ramparts. Around the middle of the second millennium before Christ, it was destroyed again. After that, it never returned to its old importance.

The site's recent history is also part of its story. During the Syrian conflict, excavation stopped, and the mound suffered damage. Reports describe illegal digging and military use of the high ground. Some of the scars you see may be recent, not ancient. Many tablets and objects were taken off the site long ago and placed in museums in Syria. That is why the mound itself feels so bare. Its treasures are mostly kept elsewhere.

That bareness can be powerful. There are no glass cases here, and there are no reconstructed towers. Instead, you see stone foundations, the outlines of rooms, and steps that lead nowhere now. Look at the base courses of the walls. Large, rough-cut limestone blocks form the lower part. Above them, people once built in mudbrick, and most of that brick has returned to the soil. The earth that covers the hill is partly the old city itself.

Pause and listen for a moment. You may hear the wind in the olive trees and perhaps a distant tractor or a dog in a nearby village. Life here today is mostly farming. The same plain once fed a royal city with grain, oil, and herds. Thousands of years ago, scribes recorded these same kinds of goods on clay. Some of the rhythms of this landscape have changed very little.

Before you leave, turn slowly in a full circle. Find the high centre of the acropolis, then follow the line of the ramparts around the horizon. Somewhere beneath your feet, people once counted cloth, taught children to write, and sent messages to distant kings. As you walk back down the slope, look at the pale fragments in the soil. Some may be ordinary stones. Others may be small pieces of a city that was lost and then found again.
