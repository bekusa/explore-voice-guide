The guide

The story

Limestone rises above you, pale and streaked with rust where water has run down its face for thousands of years. Behind you, traffic moves along the river road. Beyond that, the Mississippi slides past, broad and brown, with the slow weight of a river that drains half a continent. On the cliff wall, a strange creature looks down. It has horns, wings, scales, and a long tail. This is the Piasa, the river monster of Alton, Illinois.

Before we look closely at the painting, it helps to know one thing. What you see today is not the original. It is a modern recreation, painted in the late twentieth century and refreshed since. The first Piasa was painted on these bluffs long before any European arrived. That older image is gone. So this place is really about two things at once. One is a lost work of Indigenous art. The other is the long human effort to remember it.

The story we can document begins in 1673. That summer, the French Jesuit priest Jacques Marquette and the explorer Louis Jolliet paddled down the Mississippi in birchbark canoes. They were searching for the river's course and its people. Somewhere along these bluffs, they looked up and saw painted figures on the rock. Marquette described them in his journal. His account is the earliest written record of the image, and every later version comes back to his words.

What did he see? Marquette described two monsters, not one. Each was about the size of a calf. They had horns like a deer and red eyes. They had a beard like a tiger's and a face something like a man's. Their bodies were covered in scales. A very long tail wrapped around the body, passing over the head and between the legs, and ended like a fish's tail. The colours were red, green, and black.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Marquette's description does not mention wings. The winged version you see on the wall today came later, through nineteenth-century drawings and retellings. Over generations, the creature changed shape as people redrew it from memory and imagination. So when you look at this painting, you are looking at an image shaped by a long chain of copies. Each artist added something, and each left something out.

Marquette was also clearly impressed by the skill of the work. He wrote that the painting was so well made that he doubted good painters in France could easily do better. For a priest from Europe, this was high praise. It tells us something important. The people who painted these bluffs were skilled artists working in a living tradition. They chose this cliff on purpose, where anyone travelling the river would see it.

Who were those painters? Nobody can say for certain. Many scholars connect the image to the Mississippian culture, the mound-building society that flourished in this region for several centuries. Its great centre, Cahokia, lies not far south of here, across the floodplain near modern Saint Louis. Mississippian art often shows powerful creatures that combine features of different animals. One of these is sometimes called the Underwater Panther, a spirit of rivers and the world below. The Piasa may belong to that family of beings.

Now for the part of the story that needs a gentle warning. You may hear a dramatic legend about the Piasa. In this tale, a terrible bird carried off villagers, until a brave chief offered himself as bait while his warriors shot the creature with poisoned arrows. It is a gripping story. But historians generally treat it as a nineteenth-century invention. It was published in the 1830s by a local writer, and it does not appear to come from a documented Indigenous source.

The same caution applies to the name itself. You will often hear that Piasa means something like the bird that devours men. That meaning comes from the same nineteenth-century writing, and its origin is uncertain. Marquette never used the word Piasa at all. So the name, the wings, and the famous legend all arrived long after the first painting. The creature on the wall is part ancient art, part American folklore.

And what happened to the original? As Alton grew in the nineteenth century, the bluffs became valuable for their limestone. Quarrying cut into the cliffs along this stretch of river. The painted rock face was destroyed in that period. Some early travellers had sketched the image before it vanished, and those sketches helped later artists rebuild it. The loss is a reminder of how easily a place can disappear while a town is busy building itself.

Take a moment to look at the setting itself. These bluffs run for kilometres along the Illinois side of the river. Upstream, not far from here, the Illinois River joins the Mississippi, and a little farther south the Missouri joins it too. For thousands of years, this meeting of rivers made the area a crossroads for travel and trade. A painting placed here would have been seen by many people passing by. It worked almost like a sign on a great highway of water.

Today, the scene around you is quieter. Barges push slowly against the current. Gulls and, in colder months, bald eagles drift over the water, as they often gather along this part of the river in winter. Cyclists pass on the riverside path. The old river town of Alton sits just downstream, with brick buildings climbing its steep streets. The Piasa watches over all of it, bright against the grey stone.

Before you leave, look once more at the creature's face, its horns, and that long, curling tail. Try to picture the cliff as Marquette saw it, with no road and no town, only forest, water, and a painted face high on the rock. Then listen for a moment to the river behind you. It is the same river that carried his canoe. The painting has changed many times. The river is still here, and so is the wish to remember.
