The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you move. Somewhere ahead, a chain lift is clicking, slow and steady, like a giant clock winding up. Then the clicking stops. A second of quiet... and a wave of screams rolls across the park. Under your feet the pavement is warm, and the air carries the smell of popcorn, funnel cake, and sunscreen.

Welcome to Six Flags Great America. You are in Gurnee, in the far north of Illinois, roughly between Chicago and Milwaukee. This flat stretch of land near the interstate has drawn thrill seekers from both cities for decades. Today it is one of the largest amusement parks in the American Midwest. Let us walk through it together, one land and one ride at a time.

The park did not begin under the Six Flags name. It opened in 1976 as Marriott's Great America. Yes, that Marriott, the hotel company. In the 1970s Marriott built two parks with almost the same layout. This one is in Illinois, and its twin is in Santa Clara, California. The opening year was chosen with care. It was America's bicentennial, the two hundredth anniversary of independence. That is why the original theme was a tour through the history of the United States.

That theme is still visible if you look for it. The park is divided into themed lands, each modeled on a piece of the American past. Near the gate is Hometown Square, styled like a small Midwestern town from the early twentieth century. Further on you will find County Fair, Yankee Harbor with its New England harbor look, and Yukon Territory, with its Gold Rush and lumber camp style. There is also Orleans Place, inspired by the French Quarter of New Orleans.

Marriott's time here was short. In 1984 the company sold the park, and the Six Flags era began. The new owners slowly changed its character. The gentle history lesson stayed in the architecture. But the skyline began to fill with taller, faster, and stranger roller coasters. If you look up from almost anywhere in the park, you can read this history in steel and timber, one coaster after another.

Before you rush to the big rides, look at the carousel near the entrance. This is Columbia, a double-decker carousel that has been here since the park opened. It is one of the tallest carousels of its kind in the world. It has two full floors of horses and painted panels, all lit by rows of small bulbs. It is a nice surprise. In a park built for speed, one of its oldest treasures turns slowly in circles.

Now follow the sound of wooden tracks. American Eagle opened in 1981. It is a racing coaster, which means two trains leave the station side by side and race each other down the course. On a busy day, riders in one train shout across the gap to riders in the other. Wooden coasters feel different from steel ones. The ride is rougher, louder, and full of rattles. Many fans think that is exactly the point.

Here is a fact many visitors miss. Batman: The Ride, which opened in 1992, was the first inverted roller coaster in the world. On an inverted coaster, the train hangs below the track. Your legs swing free, with nothing under your feet. Engineers in Switzerland designed it, and the idea soon spread to parks around the globe. So when you see riders dangling through loops anywhere today, the story partly starts right here in Gurnee.

The late 1990s and the decades after brought even bigger machines. Raging Bull opened in 1999. It is a hyper coaster, a word for coasters built for great height and long, fast drops. Its yellow and orange track runs across the southern edge of the park. Later came X-Flight, a wing coaster, where seats extend out to the sides of the track. With no track above or below you, it feels a little like flying.

In 2014 the park opened Goliath, a wooden coaster with a remarkably steep first drop. When it opened, it set records for wooden coasters, including the steepest drop. It mixes old and new methods. It uses a wooden structure, but the track is made with modern techniques. It can even turn riders upside down, which is rare for wooden coasters. Stand nearby and watch the train crest the top. For a moment it seems to hang in the air.

Then there is Maxx Force, which opened in 2019. Instead of a slow climb, it uses a launch. The train is fired forward from a standstill in a fraction of a second. You will hear it before you see it, a sharp rush of air and then a roar. At its debut, it was known for one of the fastest accelerations of any coaster in North America.

This place is not only about coasters. Next door is a water park, Hurricane Harbor, which is busy in the summer heat. Inside the main park there are also gentler rides, kids' areas, live shows, and arcade games. In autumn the park usually changes its mood. Fog machines, costumed performers, and decorated paths transform the place for its Halloween season. Families with small children and serious coaster fans share the same paths, often in the same group.

Notice the people around you. You may hear Chicago accents next to Wisconsin ones, and teenagers comparing which ride scared them most. A grandparent might wait by a bench while grandchildren run to the next queue. Amusement parks are among the few places where adults are allowed to scream in public. For many families in this region, a day here is a summer tradition passed down for several generations.

Before you move on, stand still once more. Look up at the tangle of tracks against the sky, wooden lattice beside steel loops. Listen again for that clicking lift hill and the pause before the drop. Fifty years of American fun stand on this ground, from a bicentennial history theme to some of the fastest rides on the continent. Now choose your next ride... and enjoy the rest of your day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the biggest coasters first, before afternoon queues grow.
Bring a separate swimsuit for the Hurricane Harbor water park.
Use lockers for loose items on high-speed coasters.
Check seasonal event schedules before visiting in autumn.
Keep a refillable water bottle for hot summer days.
