The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through tall grass, and somewhere below you a bird calls once, then again. The soil under your shoes is red laterite, an iron-rich earth that colours your boots and the hem of your trousers within minutes. The light here is wide and soft, especially in the early morning, when the savannah looks pale gold and the air still holds a little cool. Welcome to Mole National Park, the largest wildlife refuge in Ghana.

The park lies in the north of the country, a short drive from the town of Damongo. It covers several thousand square kilometres of open woodland and grassland. That makes it larger than many small countries' entire protected areas combined. Most visitors only see a thin slice of it, near the park headquarters on the escarpment. Beyond that ridge, the land runs on for a long way with few roads. It stays largely the domain of animals, rangers, and the rivers that cross it.

This landscape is called Guinea savannah. Think of it as a mix of grass and scattered trees, not a dense forest and not an empty plain. In the wet season, the grass grows tall, sometimes above a person's head. In the dry season, it turns brown and brittle, and controlled fires clear some of it. Look at the trees around you. Many have thick, cracked bark. That bark helps them survive the regular fires that pass through this country.

One of the most common trees here is the shea tree. Its nuts produce shea butter, which women in northern Ghana have processed by hand for generations. The butter is used for cooking, for skin care, and for trade. Today it also travels around the world in cosmetics. So the same tree that shades an antelope in this park may connect, in a small way, to a jar of cream on a shelf in another country. That is a surprising thread to follow.

Mole became a protected area in the second half of the twentieth century. It was first set aside as a game reserve, and later it gained full national park status. Before that, people lived in parts of this land. Some communities were moved when the park was created. That history still matters to the villages along the boundary. Today, several of those communities work with the park through tourism projects, guiding, and crafts.

Now, the animals. Mole is best known for its elephants. Several hundred of them live in the park, and they often come to the waterholes below the escarpment. Many visitors are surprised by how close you can get on foot. Here, the classic experience is a walking safari with an armed ranger, not only a drive in a vehicle. You walk in a line, quietly, through the grass. When an elephant appears among the trees, you feel its size in a very direct way.

Please follow the ranger's instructions at every moment. Elephants are calm here, but they are wild animals. Keep your voice low. Do not wear bright colours. Stay behind the guide, and never try to approach for a better photograph. If the ranger tells you to stop or move back, do it at once. The rules are simple, and they protect both you and the elephants. A good ranger reads the wind, the ears, and the posture of each animal.

Elephants are only part of the story. You may see warthogs trotting with their tails straight up like small flags. Kob and bushbuck, both types of antelope, graze at the forest edges. Waterbuck stand near the water. Buffalo live here too, though they are harder to find. Predators such as leopards and hyenas are present, but they are shy and rarely seen. Birdwatchers often come for the birds alone, because hundreds of species have been recorded across the park.

And then there are the baboons. Olive baboons and other monkeys live around the headquarters, and they know exactly what a tourist's bag can hold. They will open a door, climb onto a table, or grab a sandwich from your hand. Locals and staff laugh about it, but they also warn every guest. Keep food out of sight. Close your windows and doors. This is perhaps the most common surprise for first-time visitors. The wildest moment of the day sometimes happens at breakfast.

From the escarpment, look down over the waterholes. In the dry season, roughly from late in the year into early spring, water becomes scarce across the savannah. Animals gather at these pools, and viewing is often at its best. This is also the season of the harmattan, a dry, dusty wind that blows from the Sahara. It can turn the sky a hazy white and soften the sun. In the wet season, the land turns green, the animals spread out, and some tracks become muddy.

The area around the park adds another layer. Close to the main road sits the village of Larabanga. It is home to a small white mosque built of mud and timber, in the Sudano-Sahelian style. That style comes from the Sahel, the dry belt south of the Sahara, and uses earthen walls with wooden poles sticking out. Local tradition says the mosque is many centuries old, and it is often described as one of the oldest in Ghana. Locals also tell a legend about a mystic stone nearby.

Other community projects sit near the park boundary. In some villages, you can join a canoe trip on a river, walk with a local guide, or watch shea butter being made. These visits are a way to see the human side of this landscape. They also give the people who live beside the park a direct benefit from its protection. Ask at the park headquarters which projects are running when you visit, because arrangements change.

Before you leave, take one more moment on the escarpment. Look at the line where the grass meets the trees. Listen for the snap of a branch or the low rumble that might be an elephant somewhere out of sight. Feel the red dust on your hands. As you walk back toward the road, notice how quickly the sounds of the savannah close behind you... and how much of this land still lies quietly beyond the ridge.

Before you go

Practical tips

Join a ranger-led walking safari for close elephant sightings.
Go early morning, when animals are active and air is cool.
Keep all food hidden from baboons around the headquarters.
Wear muted colours instead of bright clothing on walks.
Visit in the dry season for animals at the waterholes.
