The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here in the hills of western Pennsylvania, the loudest thing is often the wind. It moves through the tall grass and across the open field. If you are near the entrance, you may also hear something else: long, low tones rising and falling, like bells that never quite finish ringing. Under your feet, the ground feels solid and ordinary. But this is not an ordinary field, and the quiet here is not accidental.

On the morning of September 11, 2001, United Airlines Flight 93 took off from Newark, New Jersey, bound for San Francisco. On board were thirty-three passengers and seven crew members. Four hijackers were also aboard. Not long after takeoff, they seized control of the plane and turned it back toward the east, toward Washington. It was one of four aircraft hijacked that morning in coordinated attacks on the United States.

What happened next is the reason this memorial exists. Over phone calls from the cabin, passengers and crew learned that other hijacked planes had already struck the World Trade Center and the Pentagon. They understood what their own flight was meant to do. They talked with family members. They talked with each other. And then, together, they decided to act. They rushed toward the cockpit to try to take back the plane.

The struggle was recorded by the cockpit voice recorder. Shortly after ten in the morning, the aircraft crashed into this field, near the small borough of Shanksville in Somerset County. Everyone on board was killed. Investigators concluded that the hijackers likely intended to strike a target in Washington, possibly the United States Capitol or the White House. The plane came down roughly twenty minutes of flying time from the capital. The forty people on board are remembered here as heroes.

Now, here is something many visitors do not realise. This land was once a surface coal mine. The open slopes and the wide, bowl-shaped field you see around you were shaped partly by mining and then by reclamation. That is why the landscape feels so broad and so exposed. Before the memorial, local people knew this ground as a working site, not a sacred one. In a single morning, its meaning changed completely.

In the days after the crash, people began to arrive. They left flags, notes, flowers, hats, and small objects on a fence near the site. A temporary memorial grew out of that spontaneous grief. Volunteers, many of them local residents, helped look after it and spoke with visitors. The permanent memorial you are visiting today grew from that same impulse. It is managed by the National Park Service, and it was designed through a public competition.

The Memorial Plaza was dedicated in 2011, on the tenth anniversary of the attacks. Its centrepiece is the Wall of Names. It is made of forty separate panels of white marble, one for each passenger and crew member. Each name is carved on its own panel. The wall does not form one continuous surface. Small gaps divide the panels, so every person stands as an individual, not as part of a single mass.

The Wall of Names follows the final flight path of the aircraft. Beyond it lies the crash site itself. A large sandstone boulder marks the point of impact. Visitors do not walk out to the boulder. The area beyond the wall is the final resting place of the forty, and it is reserved for their families. You look across the grass toward it. That distance is part of the design. It asks you to stop, to stand still, and to look.

If you came in from the visitor centre, you already walked along that same line. The visitor centre opened in 2015, and its walkway is aligned with the path of the plane as it came down. The building frames a view out over the field toward the impact site. Inside, exhibitions tell the story of that morning through objects, timelines, and the recorded voices of the people involved. Some of it is difficult to hear. Take your time, and step outside when you need to.

Now, back to those long tones in the wind. They come from the Tower of Voices, near the memorial entrance. It is a tall concrete tower, dedicated in 2018, and it holds forty wind chimes. Each chime is tuned to a different note. Together they stand for the forty voices of the passengers and crew, voices that were heard on those final phone calls. The tower has no keyboard and no player. Only the wind sounds the chimes, so on a still day you may hear almost nothing.

That is the second detail many visitors miss. The memorial changes with the weather. On a bright summer afternoon, the field can feel calm and open. On a grey, windy day in autumn, the chimes are louder and the grass moves in waves. Groves of trees have been planted around the site, and they will keep growing for decades. In a sense, this place is still being finished. Each season adds something to it.

The families of the forty played a central role in shaping the memorial. Many of them took part in planning, and they have helped decide how the story is told. Every year on September 11, a ceremony takes place here. Names are read aloud, and bells are rung. Between those ceremonies, the site belongs mostly to ordinary visitors: school groups, veterans, travellers who drove a long way on quiet country roads, and local people who remember the smoke rising from the field that morning.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look along the white panels of the wall. Follow the line of the flight path out across the grass, toward the boulder at the edge of the trees. Notice the hemlocks behind it, dark against the lighter field. Listen for the wind, and for the chimes if they are sounding. As you walk back, you are walking a path that forty people never finished. Carry a little of this quiet with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the visitor centre to follow the flight path.
Bring a warm layer; the open field gets very windy.
Visit the Tower of Voices on a breezy day.
Allow quiet time between the exhibits and the wall.
Wear comfortable shoes for the walkways between sites.
