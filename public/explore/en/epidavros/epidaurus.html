The guide

The story

Stand still on the warm limestone for a moment. Pine resin hangs in the air, and cicadas fill the hills with a steady, dry hum. The pale seats above you curve away in a perfect bowl, row after row, rising toward a ridge of dark green trees. Somewhere high up, a visitor drops a coin on the stone circle below... and you hear it land.

This is Epidaurus. It is the ancient theatre, and it is also the sanctuary of Asklepios that surrounds it. Most people arrive for the theatre, and that is fair. But the theatre was not built as a stand-alone attraction. It was part of a healing centre, one of the most important in the ancient Greek world. People travelled here because they were sick, and they hoped a god would make them well.

That god was Asklepios. In Greek myth he was a son of Apollo and a gifted healer. His symbol was a staff with a single snake wrapped around it. You still see that image today on ambulances and pharmacy signs across Europe. Snakes were kept at sanctuaries like this one, and they were treated as sacred. Long before Asklepios became the main figure here, Apollo himself was worshipped on a nearby hill.

Now look at the theatre itself. Ancient writers praised it above others. The Greek travel writer Pausanias, who described Greece in the second century of our era, admired its harmony and beauty. He named the builder as Polykleitos. Modern scholars usually date the theatre to the late fourth century before Christ. Its upper section of seats was added later, which extended its capacity to roughly 13,000 spectators.

The lower tier and the upper tier are separated by a wide walkway. Look up and you will see it as a clear horizontal break in the rows. Narrow stairways climb between wedge-shaped blocks of seats, like slices of a fan. The front row held carved seats with backs, meant for priests and important guests. Everyone else sat on plain stone. Bring a scarf or a cushion if you plan to stay a while.

At the bottom lies the orchestra — a full circle of beaten earth bordered by stone. This complete circle is rare. In many later theatres, the circle was cut or reshaped, but here it survives whole. The chorus once sang and danced in this space. Behind it stood the stage building, now reduced to foundations. Picture actors in masks entering from either side, their voices carrying up the slope.

And those voices really did carry. This is the theatre's best-known quality. From the top rows, an actor speaking at normal volume can be heard clearly. Researchers have studied why. One respected explanation is that the stepped rows of limestone help reduce low background noise, like wind and murmur, while letting the higher tones of the human voice travel upward. In other words, the seats themselves may work as a kind of sound filter.

Here is something many visitors miss. This theatre is not a museum piece. Every summer it becomes a working stage again. The Athens and Epidaurus Festival has staged ancient Greek drama here since the 1950s. Audiences climb these same steps at dusk to watch tragedies by Sophocles and Euripides, often in modern Greek. If your visit falls in the festival season, an evening performance is a very different experience from a daytime walk.

Now leave the theatre and walk down into the sanctuary. The ground is flatter here, scattered with foundations, column drums and low walls. It takes some imagination. This was once a busy complex with temples, guest houses, baths and a stadium for athletic games. Festivals in honour of Asklepios brought crowds from across Greece. Healing here was never only medical. It mixed religion, rest, diet, exercise and entertainment.

The heart of the cure was sleep. Patients made offerings, purified themselves, and then spent the night in a long hall called the abaton, a sacred sleeping place. They hoped Asklepios would visit them in a dream. In the morning, priests helped interpret what the dream meant for treatment. Stone tablets found at the site record cures that patients believed they received. Some accounts are moving. Others are hard to believe, and they read more like miracle stories than medical notes.

Search for the round building known as the Tholos. Only its circular foundation remains in place, but it is one of the most puzzling structures here. Its foundations form rings of walls with passages between them, almost like a small maze. Nobody is fully sure what happened inside. Some think sacred snakes lived below. Others suggest special rituals. Archaeologists still debate it, and restoration work on the building has continued in recent years.

A second surprise — there are two Epidauruses. The sanctuary you are standing in sits inland, among hills. The ancient city of Epidaurus was down on the coast, near the modern village of Palaia Epidavros. That town has its own smaller theatre, close to the water. The whole area became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1988. Before you leave, visit the small site museum. It holds inscriptions, architectural fragments and pieces of the Tholos ceiling.

Before you go, climb back toward the theatre if you have the energy. Sit on one of the upper rows. Watch the shadows move across the stone and listen. Notice the cicadas, the footsteps, the quiet voices of strangers far below. Thousands of people sat here to watch, and thousands more came to this valley hoping to heal. As you walk out through the pines, take a final look back at that pale curve in the hillside.

Practical tips

Carry a cushion or scarf for sitting on stone.
Visit the site museum before walking the sanctuary ruins.
Check the festival programme for evening performances in summer.
