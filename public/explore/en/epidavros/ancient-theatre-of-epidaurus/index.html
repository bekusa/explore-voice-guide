The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, a cicada is buzzing in the pines. Below you, someone in the round stone floor of the theatre is speaking in a normal voice, and you can hear every word. Under your feet, the limestone seats are warm from the sun and smooth from centuries of use. This is the Ancient Theatre of Epidaurus. People have climbed these rows for more than two thousand years. Many come back for the same reason you came today.

First, a little orientation. You are in the hills of the Argolid, in the northeastern Peloponnese, a short drive from the modern village of Lygourio. The theatre sits in a natural hollow, and the hillside holds the curved rows of seats like a cupped hand. Pine forest rises behind the top rows. In front of you, the land opens toward green slopes and low mountains. The builders chose this place for its slope, and also for the way it frames the sky.

The theatre dates to the late fourth century before Christ. That is the period after the great age of Athenian tragedy, when theatre was already a mature art across the Greek world. The traveller Pausanias wrote about Greece in the second century of our era. He credited the design to an architect named Polykleitos, usually understood today as Polykleitos the Younger. Pausanias also said that no other theatre could match this one for harmony and beauty. Modern scholars still debate the attribution. Few people disagree with his opinion of the result.

Now look down at the orchestra, the flat circle at the bottom. Its full circle is rare. Many ancient theatres were later cut down or reshaped by the Romans, but this circle remained largely intact. The chorus danced and sang here. The actors performed nearby, in front of a stage building called the skene. Today only the foundations of that building remain. In the centre of the orchestra, look for a round stone. Visitors often stand on it to test the sound.

And that sound is the famous part. Guides like to drop a coin, tear a sheet of paper, or strike a match on that central stone. Then the people in the upper rows hear it clearly. It feels almost like a trick — but it is geometry. In 2007, two researchers studied the acoustics. They suggested that the stepped rows of seats work like a filter. The rows reduce low background noise, such as wind and murmuring crowds. At the same time, they let the higher sounds of the human voice travel upward.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The theatre was not built in one single campaign. The lower section of seats is the original theatre. The upper section was added later, in the Hellenistic period, when the sanctuary needed more space. Count the rows if you have patience. There are fifty-five in total, divided by a wide walkway that runs around the middle. Estimates suggest the full theatre held more than 12,000 people. That is a crowd the size of a small town, all sitting on stone.

Look at the front row closest to the orchestra. These seats are different. They have backrests and a more carefully carved shape. They were seats of honour, called proedria — places reserved for priests, officials, and important guests. Ancient Greek society was very aware of rank, even at a festival. Further up, the ordinary seats are simple blocks. Still, every seat has a clear view. That fairness of sightline is part of the design.

Now the second surprise. This theatre was not built mainly for city entertainment. It belonged to a sanctuary of Asklepios, the Greek god of medicine and healing. Epidaurus was one of the most important healing centres in the ancient Mediterranean. Sick people travelled here from far away. They made offerings, bathed, and rested. Some slept in a special sacred building, hoping the god would appear in a dream and show them a cure. Inscriptions from the site record stories of these healings.

So why a theatre at a healing sanctuary? The ancient Greeks did not separate the body from the mind in the way we often do. Music, drama, and festival were part of wellbeing. The sanctuary held games and performances in honour of the god. Watching a tragedy could be an emotional release, something the Greeks connected with the idea of catharsis, a kind of cleansing of feeling. Sitting here, a patient was still inside the healing world of the sanctuary.

After antiquity, the sanctuary declined, and the hills slowly covered the theatre. Earth and vegetation buried much of it, and that burial helped protect the stone. Systematic excavation began in the late nineteenth century, led by the Greek archaeologist Panagiotis Kavvadias. Restoration work has continued in different stages since then. Today the theatre is part of the Sanctuary of Asklepios, which UNESCO added to its World Heritage List in 1988. The ruins of the healing complex lie a short walk away.

The theatre is also a working stage again. In the twentieth century, Greek directors and actors brought ancient drama back to this orchestra. Since the 1950s, the summer festival has staged plays by Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, and Aristophanes here. Famous performers have stood where that central stone sits. The opera singer Maria Callas sang here in 1960. On a summer night, thousands of people still climb these rows to watch the same stories the ancient audience knew.

Before you leave, try the experiment for yourself. Climb toward the top rows, slowly, because the steps are uneven and can be slippery. Sit down, catch your breath, and look back. The orchestra looks small from here, almost like a coin itself. Ask a friend to speak quietly from the centre. Then notice how the curve of the hill seems to hold the whole space together. The view also widens up here — the pines, the hills, and the long Peloponnesian light.

When you walk back down, run your hand along the edge of a seat. Feel the tool marks and the places where stone has been worn smooth. Listen again to the cicadas and the voices of strangers testing the echo. Each person here is doing what audiences have done for centuries — sitting together, facing the same small circle, waiting for a voice. As you head toward the sanctuary ruins, keep that sound in your ears for a little while longer.
