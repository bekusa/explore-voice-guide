The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see a single ride, you hear it. There is a low rumble of steel wheels on track, then a wave of screams that rises and fades on the wind. The wind never really stops out here. It crosses the flat land of Flevoland with nothing to slow it down. It carries the sounds of Walibi Holland long before the park gates come into view.

You are standing on ground that, not so long ago, was the bottom of a sea. This part of the Netherlands is a polder, land that engineers reclaimed from water by building dikes and pumping it dry. Eastern Flevoland was drained in the mid-twentieth century. That means the soil under your feet is younger than many of the grandparents walking through these gates. The park sits on what was once the seabed of the Zuiderzee, a shallow inland sea.

This flatness shapes everything you see. There are no hills here, no old forests, no medieval towns on the horizon. Just straight roads, drainage canals, rows of poplar trees and enormous skies. Against that backdrop, the steel of the roller coasters rises like nothing else around it. On a clear day, the tallest lift hills stand out from far across the fields. They are some of the highest points in this whole landscape.

The story of this site did not begin with thrill rides. It began with farming. In the early 1970s, an attraction called Flevohof opened here. It was an agricultural park, built to show visitors how modern Dutch farming worked on the new land. Families came to see animals, crops and machinery. In a way, it celebrated the polder itself. It was a showcase for what reclaimed land could produce.

Flevohof struggled financially and eventually closed in the early 1990s. The site then passed to the Belgian Walibi group, which turned it into an amusement park. It opened as Walibi Flevo in 1994. Over the following years the name changed several times. For a period it carried the Six Flags brand, after the American company bought the Walibi parks. Later it became Walibi World, and finally Walibi Holland. Since the mid-2000s, it has belonged to the French leisure group Compagnie des Alpes.

Those name changes are worth knowing, because you can still see the layers. Look closely at the park and you will notice that different areas feel like they come from different eras. Some rides arrived under one owner, some under another. Walking through the park is a bit like reading a timeline of European theme park fashions — from the gentle family attractions to the extreme steel that came later.

The ride that dominates the skyline is Goliath. It is a hyper coaster, a type built for great height, long drops and moments of near weightlessness. It was built by the Swiss manufacturer Intamin and opened in the early 2000s, during the Six Flags years. Riders talk about airtime, that feeling of rising out of your seat as the train crests a hill. On Goliath, those moments come again and again over a long, sweeping layout.

Then there is Lost Gravity, a much stranger machine. It was built by the German company Mack Rides and opened in 2016. Its track twists and dives in ways that look almost impossible from the ground. The theme suggests a city knocked off balance, with debris and wreckage scattered around the ride. Stand near the exit and watch riders step off. Most of them are laughing, slightly unsteady, and already looking for their friends.

Here is something many visitors miss. Untamed, one of the park's best-known coasters, stands on the site of an older wooden coaster called Robin Hood. The American firm Rocky Mountain Construction rebuilt it in 2019 as a hybrid. That means it kept a wooden support structure but received a new steel track on top. So when you look at Untamed, you are looking at two coasters in one — the timber skeleton of the past, carrying the steel of the present.

Not everything here is about speed and height. There are water rides that soak riders on warm days, spinning rides, and gentler attractions for younger children. Walibi the kangaroo, the park's mascot, appears around the grounds and in the family areas. The mascot comes from the original Belgian park, and the name is a play on wallaby, a small cousin of the kangaroo. It is a cheerful link to the company that first built this place.

A second surprise lies just beyond the park itself. The large fields next to Walibi Holland host Lowlands, one of the biggest music festivals in the Netherlands. Every August, tens of thousands of people camp here for a weekend of concerts. The same flat, open land that suits roller coasters also suits festival stages. If you visit in late summer, you may notice the signs of preparation, with fences, tents and stages rising nearby.

In autumn, the park often changes character again. Its Halloween event, Fright Nights, turns parts of the grounds into scare zones and haunted mazes after dark. Fog drifts between the rides, and actors in costume move through the crowds. The polder wind adds its own effect, pushing mist across the paths. Families tend to visit during the day, while older visitors stay for the evening. It is the same park, but it feels like a different place.

Before you move on, pause and look up. Follow the line of a coaster track against the sky, from the lift hill down to the first drop. Then lower your eyes to the flat horizon behind it. Everything you see — the rides, the paths, the trees, the canals — stands on land that was once open water. As you walk away, listen again for that rumble of wheels and that rising wave of voices, carried on the wind across the old seabed.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the most popular coasters early before queues build.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed polder breezes.
Pack a change of clothes if you plan water rides.
Check the park calendar for Fright Nights dates in autumn.
Watch Lost Gravity from the ground before deciding to ride.
