The guide

The story

Out here, the ground is soft and pale, almost like flour. It shifts under your shoes. Low mounds rise from the flat land, sand-coloured and worn smooth by wind. The Euphrates is close. You may not see it at first, but you feel it in the air, a faint coolness and the green of palms along its banks. This quiet field of earth northwest of Fallujah holds the remains of Anbar, one of the oldest towns in this stretch of Mesopotamia.

At first glance, there is very little to see. No towers, no gates, no columns. Only ridges of earth and scattered fragments. But that is how most cities of ancient Iraq survive. They were built of mudbrick, made from the same river clay you are standing on. When a mudbrick city falls, it does not leave a ruin of stone. It melts slowly back into the land, and the mounds you see are the city itself, folded into the ground.

Before it was called Anbar, this place had a Persian name — Peroz-Shapur, often translated as victorious Shapur. It was a city of the Sasanian Empire, the last great Persian dynasty before the arrival of Islam. The Sasanians ruled a vast territory from their capital at Ctesiphon, on the Tigris to the southeast. A fortified town here, on the Euphrates, guarded the western approach to the heart of their empire. It faced the frontier with Rome.

That frontier was not quiet. In the fourth century, the Roman emperor Julian marched down the Euphrates with a large army, heading for Ctesiphon. Roman writers recorded that his forces besieged and took a strong Persian city on the river, which they called Pirisabora. Most historians identify it with this place. The campaign ended badly for Rome. Julian died in Mesopotamia, and his army withdrew. But for a short time, Roman soldiers stood on these same mounds.

Now, a detail that most visitors miss. The name Anbar is usually explained as a Persian word meaning a storehouse or a granary. That fits the landscape well. This was rich farmland, watered by the river and by canals cut across the plain. Grain from these fields fed armies and cities. So the town may carry, in its very name, a memory of full barns and stored harvests. It is a practical name, for a practical place.

The canals matter here. Between the Euphrates and the Tigris, the two rivers come relatively close together in central Iraq. For thousands of years, people dug channels to link them, and to spread water over the fields. Medieval geographers described a major canal that left the Euphrates in this region and ran east toward the Tigris. Anbar sat at a junction of water, roads, and trade. That position is the key to its whole story.

In the seventh century, the armies of the new Islamic state moved north from Arabia into Iraq. Anbar was one of the towns that fell to them early in the conquest. Arab historians tell a striking story about the siege. According to the traditional accounts, the Muslim archers aimed at the eyes of the defenders on the walls, and the battle became remembered by a grim nickname — the battle of the eyes. Treat the details as tradition, but the town's capture is well recorded.

Here is the second surprise. For a brief moment, this modest-looking place stood near the centre of the Islamic world. In the eighth century, the Abbasid dynasty overthrew the Umayyads and took power. The first Abbasid caliph, known as al-Saffah, settled at Anbar and built a residence nearby. He died here. His successor, al-Mansur, later chose a site on the Tigris and founded Baghdad. So before Baghdad existed, the government of a vast empire was run from the area around these mounds.

There is a quieter cultural thread too. Some early Muslim scholars wrote that Arabic script spread to Arabia from Iraq, and they named Anbar and the nearby city of al-Hira as its source. Modern scholars debate how much of this is true. The roots of Arabic writing are complex. But the tradition itself shows how people remembered Anbar — as a place of learning and of writing, where Arab, Persian, Aramaic, and Jewish communities lived side by side.

That mix is real. This region of the Euphrates was home to important Jewish communities for many centuries. Great academies of Jewish learning stood in towns along the river, and scholars there helped shape the Babylonian Talmud, a central text of Jewish law and thought. Christians, Persians, and Arabs also lived and traded here. Standing on these mounds, you are standing in a landscape where many languages were spoken in the markets and fields.

After Baghdad rose, Anbar slowly faded. Trade and power moved to the new capital. Rivers in Mesopotamia also change course over time, and canals silt up without constant care. Towns that depended on that water could empty within a few generations. By the later medieval period, Anbar was no longer a major city. Its name survived, however. Today the whole western province of Iraq, the largest in the country, is called Anbar Governorate after this ancient town.

If you visit, come with local guidance. This area has seen hard years of conflict, and conditions can change. Archaeological sites in Iraq are protected, and you should not dig, pick up, or remove anything you find on the surface. Wear sturdy shoes, because the ground is uneven and dusty. Carry plenty of water and cover your head. In summer, the sun here is fierce, and there is almost no shade across the open mounds.

Take a moment now to look around you. Notice the way the mounds catch the low light, with soft shadows on one side and pale dust on the other. Look toward the line of palms that marks the river. Listen for the wind, and perhaps a distant road. This field once held Persian walls, Roman soldiers, caliphs, scholars, and farmers carrying grain. As you walk away, look down at the earth under your feet. It is the same clay the city was made of.
