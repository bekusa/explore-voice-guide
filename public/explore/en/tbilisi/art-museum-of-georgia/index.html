The guide

The story

Push open the heavy door and the noise of the street drops away. The air inside is cool and still, and it smells faintly of old plaster and polished wood. Your footsteps sound louder here than they did outside, and they rise along the walls of a building that has seen more than one life. Welcome to the Art Museum of Georgia, a short walk from Freedom Square in the heart of Tbilisi.

Many people know this place by a longer name. It is the Shalva Amiranashvili Museum of Fine Arts. Amiranashvili was a Georgian art historian. He spent much of the twentieth century studying, cataloguing and defending the country's medieval art. Today the museum belongs to the Georgian National Museum, a network of institutions across the country. But the name on the door still honours one scholar's lifetime of quiet work.

Now, a first surprise. Before this building held paintings and icons, it trained priests. It was the Tiflis Theological Seminary — Tiflis being the old name for Tbilisi. In the late nineteenth century, a young student from the town of Gori studied within these walls. His name was Ioseb Jughashvili. The world later knew him as Joseph Stalin. He left the seminary without finishing, and he took a very different path from the one his teachers planned.

So pause for a moment in the corridors. These are rooms where a future dictator once sat through lessons in scripture and Church history. Nothing here marks that fact loudly. There is no shrine and no plaque in every hall. The building simply carries on with its new purpose. And perhaps that is the most fitting response — the rooms that shaped him now hold the faith, craft and beauty of the country he later ruled with fear.

Look at the building itself as you move through it. The walls are thick, and the windows are deep-set, so the light arrives softly and at an angle. The proportions feel institutional and orderly, the way a nineteenth-century school often felt. Staircases are wide. Ceilings are high. It was built for discipline and study, not for display. Yet the calm rhythm of the rooms suits art well, because nothing in the architecture competes with the objects.

The heart of the collection is medieval Georgian art, and especially the work of goldsmiths and enamellers. For centuries, Georgian craftsmen produced icons covered in chased silver and gold. They decorated processional crosses, book covers and altar pieces with fine metalwork. Much of this survived in remote mountain churches, far from invading armies. Over time, scholars brought many of these pieces to the capital for protection and study.

The most famous object here is the Khakhuli Triptych. A triptych is a picture or icon in three panels, usually with two side wings that fold toward the centre. This one is very large, and it glows with gold. At its heart was a revered icon of the Virgin. Around it, craftsmen set dozens of small enamel plaques, some Georgian and some Byzantine, from the Greek-speaking Christian empire centred on Constantinople. Georgia's medieval rulers treated it as a national treasure.

Come close to the enamels if the display allows it. This technique is called cloisonné. The artist bends thin gold wire into tiny walled cells, then fills each cell with coloured glass paste and fires it. The result is hard, bright and almost permanent. Look for deep blues, leafy greens and a warm red. The faces of saints are built from lines finer than a thread. Every plaque is a small window, no bigger than your palm.

Here is a second surprise. Several of these treasures have travelled much further than you might expect. During the upheavals of the early twentieth century, part of Georgia's Church and state treasury was taken abroad for safekeeping. It spent years in Europe before it came home. Some pieces show damage — missing stones, empty settings, worn gilding. Those gaps are part of the story. They show where precious materials were pried away or lost during centuries of war and raids.

Beyond the medieval treasury, the museum holds a wider range of art. Collections have included Persian painting and decorative objects, which reflect Georgia's long and complicated relations with Iran. There are also works by Russian and Western European artists, and by Georgian painters of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Not every gallery is open at every visit, and exhibitions rotate. So treat each room as its own small discovery rather than a fixed checklist.

As you walk, notice how the mood changes from hall to hall. The treasury feels hushed and almost sacred, with low light that protects fragile surfaces. Other galleries feel brighter and more relaxed. Families talk quietly. A guard may follow your path with patient eyes. Students sometimes sit with sketchbooks in front of a single icon for a long time. This is a working museum for the city, not only a stop for visitors.

Step outside afterwards and look back at the facade. Around you, the streets slope toward Freedom Square and the old town below. Traffic hums along nearby roads, and the smell of fresh bread or coffee often drifts from a corner shop. It is worth remembering that the gold you just saw came from small churches in high valleys. Those valleys are many hours away, yet their art now sits here in the capital.

Before you leave, take one more breath of this place. Think of the layers inside a single address — a seminary, a student who changed the century, a scholar who protected medieval craft, and the anonymous artists who bent gold wire by candlelight. Now look at the street in front of you. People walk past these walls every day without a second glance. You have seen what they hold... and you will carry that with you down the hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with the treasury while your attention is fresh.
Combine your visit with a walk around Freedom Square.
Ask at the desk which galleries are currently open.
Allow at least ninety minutes for the medieval collection.
Check photography rules before raising your camera inside.
