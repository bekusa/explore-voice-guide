The guide

The story

From the ridge above the old town, Tbilisi opens out all at once — a tide of terracotta roofs, the river threading through the middle, church domes catching the light, and the modern city climbing the far hills. This is the viewpoint by the Narikala fortress, the single best place to grasp the shape of the city, and getting up here is half the pleasure.

The quickest way up is the aerial cable car that lifts from Rike Park, by the river, to the fortress ridge. The cabins are glass, the ride takes only a couple of minutes, and the view widens with every metre as the old town drops away beneath your feet. It opened in 2012 and has become a small attraction in its own right, as much for the ascent as for the destination.

Step out at the top and the fortress is right there. Narikala's stone walls follow the spine of the hill, many centuries old and rebuilt again and again through the city's violent history. You can climb sections of the walls for an even higher vantage, so the viewpoint and the fortress are really one visit, not two.

Beside the walls stands the tall aluminium figure known as the Mother of Georgia, holding a sword in one hand and a bowl of wine in the other — a blunt summary of the national character, ready to fight enemies and to welcome friends. She is visible from much of the city below, and up close she marks the high point of the ridge walk.

The view itself is the reason most people come. The paths along the ridge give a long sweep over the old town, the bends of the river, the brick domes of the bathhouse quarter, and the large cathedral on the far hill. On a clear day the gaze runs all the way out to the mountains beyond the city.

Come for the change of light if you possibly can. Late afternoon warms the roofs to gold; at dusk the streetlamps come on and the fortress is floodlit; after dark the city dissolves into scattered points of light below you. Many people ride up before sunset and walk down afterwards, catching the whole transition in one visit.

One point of confusion is worth clearing up, because the name joins two different things. Tbilisi also has an older funicular railway, but that one climbs a separate hill, Mtatsminda, to a park and a terrace on the other side of the centre. If you specifically want this Narikala panorama, take the Rike cable car; the Mtatsminda funicular is a separate trip with its own, higher view.

Getting down is part of the fun. You can ride the cable car back, or walk down through the lanes of Abanotubani and the old town in fifteen or twenty minutes, passing the bathhouse domes on the way. The downhill route on foot is one of the better short walks in the city.

A little planning helps. Carry water in summer, because the ridge is exposed and there is little shade; bring something warmer for the evening, when the wind picks up. The platform by the statue is busiest right at sunset, so arrive a little early if you want a clear spot at the railing.

Beyond the headline view, take a moment for the smaller things up here — the texture of the old fortress stone, the wildflowers on the slope in spring, the way sound from the city rises faintly to the ridge. The viewpoint rewards a slow half hour as much as a quick photograph.

The fortress has watched over every chapter of the city's history. Its walls have been held by Georgians, Persians, and others in turn, damaged by war and earthquake and patched back together, so the stone you touch is a patchwork of many centuries rather than a single build. The viewpoint is built on top of all of that.

Down in Rike Park, at the foot of the cable car, there is more to see while you wait. The riverside park has lawns, fountains, and modern structures, and the Peace Bridge arches across the water nearby, so the lower station makes a natural start or end to the trip up.

Give yourself time at the railing to pick out the landmarks one by one and trace the river through the city. From up here the whole tangled, much-fought-over history of Tbilisi lies open in a single glance. Then ride or walk back down into the very streets you were just looking at from above, carrying the map of the city in your head.
