The guide

The story

The water moves slowly below you, wide and grey green, carrying the light of the sky across its surface. This is the Mtkvari River. It cuts straight through the middle of Tbilisi and splits the whole city into two halves. Stand at its edge for a while, and you feel the old town gather quietly around you on both banks, leaning toward the water as if to watch it pass.

The Mtkvari is the longest river in the South Caucasus. It begins high in the mountains of northeastern Turkey, crosses the length of Georgia, and flows on through Azerbaijan before reaching the Caspian Sea, far away to the east. Tbilisi grew up along these banks over many centuries, using the moving water at once as a road, a border, a defense, and a steady source of life.

Look up to one side and you will see a church standing on a high rock above the water. This is Metekhi, one of the older churches in the city. Beside it stands the dark statue of a man seated on a horse, the king the city remembers as its founder. From this cliff, rulers once watched over the river crossing and the crowded houses on the banks below.

The banks here are made for walking. Stone embankments hold the river firmly in its course, and paved paths run along the top of them on both sides. On a clear day, people stroll slowly, lean on the iron railings, and watch the current pull steadily past. The water is never still for a moment, and neither, it seems, is the city that presses in around it.

Several bridges link the two sides of the river. The oldest crossing point sits down near Metekhi, where a bridge has stood in one form or another for a very long time. People on foot, carts, horses, and now cars have all passed over the same narrow neck of the river, the place where the two banks lean in and come closest together.

One bridge looks nothing at all like the others. It is built from glass and steel, curved like a long wave frozen in mid air, and it carries only people on foot. It opened in 2010 and is called the Bridge of Peace. At night, small lights run in patterns along its frame, and the whole structure glows softly above the dark, moving water.

On one bank lies a green park, opened in recent years, with fountains, broad lawns, and young trees. On the other bank rise the tight, climbing streets of the old town, leaning toward the fortress on the hill above. The river holds these two faces of the city gently apart, and at the very same time it quietly joins them together.

The light changes everything along this water. In the early morning the river is pale, flat, and quiet, barely awake. By evening it turns to gold, then to a deep and heavy blue, and the buildings along the banks light up one by one. Their reflections stretch downward and break apart on the moving surface, never once holding still long enough to be caught.

This stretch of river has seen the whole long story of the city. Tbilisi has been burned, rebuilt, conquered, and freed more times than its own people can easily count. Through every one of those turns, the Mtkvari kept flowing in the same patient direction, quietly marking the center of the city that it had helped to make in the first place.

Find a bench or a low stone wall and sit for a while. Boats are rare on this water, so the river belongs almost entirely to itself. You can hear it move beneath the noise of the traffic and the voices, a low and steady sound running under everything else, far older than the bridges, the railings, and the walls.

From the waterfront you can reach much of the old town on foot. The sulfur baths, the hilltop fortress, the small churches, and the covered markets all lie a short walk back from these banks. The river is the thread that ties them quietly together, the long line around which everything else in the old city has slowly arranged itself.

Watch the current carry the light away from you, around the slow bend, and out of sight. The same water once passed kings, travelers, and ordinary people standing exactly where you stand now. It does not wait for anyone, and it does not return. It simply keeps moving, quiet and certain, on and on through the patient heart of the city.
