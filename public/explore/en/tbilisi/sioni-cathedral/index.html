The story

Step inside and the air changes — cooler, faintly sweet with beeswax and the smoke of thin brown candles. Your eyes take a moment to adjust to the dim stone. Somewhere ahead, gold catches the small flames. This is Sioni Cathedral, in the heart of Tbilisi's old town, a short walk from the river. For many centuries it stood as the main cathedral of the Georgian Orthodox Church, and it still serves as a working church today.

The name reaches back to Jerusalem. Sioni comes from Mount Zion, and churches across Georgia carry the name in its honour. This one is dedicated to the Dormition of the Virgin Mary — the falling-asleep, as the Orthodox tradition calls her death and passing. Keep that dedication in mind. It shapes the icons on the walls and the feast days that fill the building with people at certain times of the year.

Sioni is old, and its age is written in its scars. A church has stood on or near this spot since roughly the sixth and seventh centuries. Tbilisi sat on a fault line of empires, and the cathedral was burned, broken, and rebuilt more times than any single guide can list — by Arab, Mongol, and Persian armies, by fire, and by earthquake. What you see now is the sum of many repairs across many centuries, not a single moment frozen in place.

The plan is a cross beneath a central dome, the classic Georgian form. The stone is a warm yellowish grey, cut and stacked by hand. Outside, the walls are plain and solid, with narrow windows. Most of the colour waits inside. The bell tower nearby has its own long history of damage and rebuilding, so do not expect every part of the group to match in age or style.

Sioni holds one of the most treasured objects in the country: the cross of Saint Nino. Nino was the woman who, by tradition, brought Christianity to Georgia in the fourth century. Her cross has an unusual shape — the two horizontal arms droop slightly, bending downward at the ends. According to the old account, it was made from vine wood and bound with strands of her own hair. Look for it near the iconostasis, framed and protected.

The interior was painted and repainted over generations. Some of the wall paintings you see are relatively recent work, replacing far older images lost to damage. Above and around the altar, the iconostasis — the screen of icons that divides the sanctuary from the congregation — carries saints in deep reds, blues, and gold. Let your eyes travel upward into the dome, where daylight falls through the high windows.

This is not a museum piece. Step in on an ordinary morning and you may find people lighting candles, pressing their foreheads to an icon, or standing in silent prayer. Services here are sung, never played — Georgian church music is unaccompanied, three voices moving together. If you arrive during a service, stay near the back and move gently. Women often cover their hair, and both men and women dress modestly.

For most of its life, Sioni was the seat of the Catholicos-Patriarch, the head of the Georgian Church. That role moved to the much larger Holy Trinity Cathedral after it opened in 2004, up on the hill across the river. But Sioni keeps its weight in the city's heart. Generations were baptised, married, and mourned within these walls, and that long, unbroken use is part of what you feel standing here.

When you step back out, you are in the oldest part of Tbilisi. The lanes are narrow and uneven, lined with carved wooden balconies and small shops. The river is close. Sioni Street runs past the door, and within a few minutes on foot you can reach the sulfur bath district, the older Anchiskhati Basilica, and the bridges across the water.

Listen, if the building is quiet. Stone of this kind holds sound in a particular way — a single chanted line seems to linger after the voice has stopped, and three voices together fill the space without any instrument behind them. This is one of the oldest living musical traditions in Europe, and Sioni is one of the rooms it was shaped to fit. Even an empty cathedral has a faint readiness to it, as if waiting for the next service.

Outside, the cathedral anchors a small knot of the old town. The river runs close by, and from the bridges nearby you can see how the church sits low among tiled roofs and balconies rather than rising over them. For most of its history this was the most important building in the neighbourhood; today it keeps its place quietly, ringed by cafes and small shops that pull down their shutters by night.

Mornings are calmer than late afternoons, and the light through the windows is softer then. Photography is sometimes allowed and sometimes not, depending on the moment and whether a service is underway — watch for signs and follow what local worshippers do. There is no ticket. This is a place of prayer, open to anyone who enters with respect.

Before you leave, stand for a moment just inside the door and let the noise of the street fall away behind you. The candles, the dark stone, the faint singing if you are lucky enough to catch it — this is close to what a traveller would have felt here many hundreds of years ago. When you walk back out into the daylight and the sound of the city returns, you carry a little of that quiet with you down the old streets.

Practical tips

Dress modestly and cover your hair during services.
Stay near the back if a service is underway.
Check for signs before taking any photographs.
