The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, on this open hillside above Tbilisi, the wind moves without anything to stop it. It runs over dry grass, across the stone steps, and around a ring of enormous bronze pillars. On a warm day the metal holds the heat of the sun. Lay your palm against it and you can feel it. On a grey day it turns cool and dark, the colour of old coins. Before any history, simply stand here for a moment and look up.

This is the Chronicle of Georgia. Some people call it a memorial to the history of the nation. Many visitors simply call it the Georgian Stonehenge. The nickname makes sense once you see it. Tall vertical slabs stand in a group on a high place, open to the sky. But these pillars are not rough stone. They are covered, almost from top to bottom, with sculpted figures in bronze. The whole country's story is told here in metal.

The monument is the work of Zurab Tsereteli. He is one of the best-known Georgian artists of the past century. He was a sculptor, painter and designer who created huge public works across Georgia, Russia and beyond. Admirers praised his ambition. Critics often called his work too large and too grand. Both views are common, and you will hear both in Tbilisi. Here, though, the scale feels right. The site is wide and empty, and the pillars need that space around them.

Work on the Chronicle began in the 1980s, in the last years of the Soviet Union. Then the Soviet state collapsed, and Georgia went through hard years of conflict and economic crisis. Large art projects were not a priority. The work slowed for a long time. Even now, many visitors notice that the monument does not feel fully finished. Some surfaces look rough. Some areas around the base look plain. In a way, that unfinished quality matches the subject. Georgia's own story is still being written.

Now look at the pillars themselves. Each one rises roughly thirty metres or more above the platform. That is about the height of a ten-storey building. Walk close and the figures seem to lean out over you. Faces, hands, swords, crowns and robes push out from the metal surface. Step back and they merge again into one dense pattern of bronze. It is worth doing both. The monument looks completely different from close up and from a distance.

The pillars carry two kinds of story. The upper sections show the history of Georgia. You will see kings and queens, warriors and saints, rulers in long robes. Among the figures, guides usually point out King David the Builder, a medieval ruler who united and strengthened the Georgian kingdom. Queen Tamar is here too. She ruled during what Georgians call their golden age, a time of strong government, poetry and church building. Many Georgians still name her with deep affection.

The lower sections tell a different story. Here the scenes come from the life of Christ and from the Bible. This choice matters. Georgia adopted Christianity very early, in the fourth century. For Georgians, faith and national identity grew up together. Through centuries of invasion, the church helped keep the language and culture alive. So the monument places religious scenes and national history side by side. For many local visitors, they are two parts of the same story.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Most people photograph the monument from the front and leave. But the pillars are sculpted on more than one face. Walk all the way around the group. The light changes as you move. A figure that looks flat in shadow suddenly gains depth when the sun hits it from the side. Late afternoon light is especially kind to the bronze. It brings out a warm, golden tone in the metal.

Now turn around and look outward. Below you lies a large body of water. Locals call it the Tbilisi Sea. It is not a real sea, of course. It is a reservoir, created in the Soviet era. On summer weekends, families come to its shores to swim and picnic. From up here the water looks calm and wide, and on a clear day it reflects the colour of the sky. Behind it, the hills roll away toward the edge of the city.

This is the second surprise of the place. Many people expect a monument in a city centre, with traffic and crowds around it. Instead, the Chronicle stands on the edge of Tbilisi, far from the old town. Often you may share the platform with only a few other people. You might see a couple taking photos, a family with children running up the steps, or a lone visitor sitting quietly. The silence is part of the experience.

You probably climbed a long stone staircase to reach the top. Those steps are part of the design. They make you slow down. By the time you arrive, you have left the road and the cars behind. Near the monument you may also notice a small church. Its modest size makes a strong contrast with the towering bronze. Georgians often stop to light a candle in churches like this. If you go inside, dress modestly and keep your voice low.

It is worth asking what this place means to people today. For some Georgians, the Chronicle is a proud statement of a long and stubborn national history. For others, it is mainly a quiet place for a walk and a view. Some find the style heavy. Others find it moving. You do not need to decide. Simply notice how you feel standing between these huge figures, under an open sky, with the city spread out below.

Before you leave, take one last slow look. Notice the wind again, the dry grass, the warm or cool touch of the bronze. Look at one face on the nearest pillar. Then look out at the water and the low hills. As you walk back down the steps, turn around once halfway. From there, the pillars stand together against the sky, the way they will stand long after today's visitors have gone home.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon for warm light on the bronze.
Bring a windbreaker because the hilltop is very exposed.
Wear comfortable shoes for the long stone staircase.
Walk fully around the pillars, not just the front.
Dress modestly if you plan to enter the small church.
