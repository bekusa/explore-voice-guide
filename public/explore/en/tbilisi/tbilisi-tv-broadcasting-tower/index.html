The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Up here, the wind moves through steel, and the lattice above you answers with a low, steady hum. The city's traffic is far below now, softened to a murmur. The air is cooler than in the old town. It often carries the smell of pine and warm dust. Look up, and the tower rises in a web of beams and cross-braces, narrowing as it climbs toward the sky.

This is the Tbilisi TV Broadcasting Tower. It stands on the summit of Mtatsminda, the ridge that rises along the western edge of the city. The name Mtatsminda means Holy Mountain in Georgian. That is a fitting name for a peak that has looked down on Tbilisi for as long as people have lived beside the river. The tower is simply the newest thing to crown it, and by far the tallest.

The tower was completed in the early 1970s, during the Soviet period. Its job was practical. Television and radio signals travel best from high ground, and Mtatsminda is the highest point close to the city centre. Engineers placed a tall steel mast on top of an already tall mountain. That gave the transmitters a clear line across the valley and the surrounding hills. For decades, much of what Georgians watched at home passed through the equipment around you.

The structure itself is a steel lattice tower, and it reaches roughly 270 metres. That makes it one of the tallest structures in Georgia. Notice that it is not a solid concrete shaft, like many television towers in other cities. Instead, it is an open frame. The wind passes through the gaps instead of pushing against a wall. That is one reason lattice towers can be light and very tall at the same time.

Here is something many visitors do not notice from the city below. The tower does not rise in one smooth line. Look carefully, and you will see that it changes shape as it climbs. There are platforms and wider sections where equipment once sat, and still sits. Each level was built for a technical purpose. The overall form looks almost decorative, but almost every beam is there to hold something up or keep something steady.

Now turn around and look out. From this ridge, the whole of Tbilisi spreads across the valley of the Mtkvari River. You can trace the old town by its tight clusters of roofs and church domes. Newer districts stretch outward in long lines of apartment blocks. On clear days, the hills on the far side of the city fold into one another. Sometimes, on the very clearest days, the high Caucasus peaks appear far to the north.

The second surprise comes when you head back down into the city. Watch how often the tower appears. It shows up at the end of streets, above courtyards, behind church bell towers, and in the background of wedding photographs. Because the mountain is so close to the centre, the tower works as a kind of compass. Locals and visitors use it without thinking. If you can see the tower, you know which way the western ridge lies.

At night, the tower changes character completely. Lights run up its frame, and they shift through colours and patterns after dark. From the old town, the lit tower floats above the black outline of the mountain. Many people who never come up here know the tower best this way. To them, it is a column of light on the skyline, more than a piece of broadcasting equipment.

It is worth remembering that this is still a working transmission site. It is a technical facility, not a viewing tower, so most visitors enjoy it from the ground and from the park around it. That is no loss. The best way to understand its scale is to stand close to the base and look straight up. From here, the beams seem to converge into a single point high above your head.

Around the tower lies Mtatsminda Park, an amusement park spread across the summit. There are rides, cafés, walking paths, and a large Ferris wheel. The wheel gives you a view that rises almost to the level of the lower tower sections. On weekends, families fill the paths. The sound of children and fairground music mixes with the wind in the steel. The contrast between the playful park and the serious engineering is part of the charm.

Many people reach the summit by funicular, a cable railway that climbs the steep slope from the city below. The ride is short but very steep. Partway up, the funicular passes near the Church of Saint David and the Mtatsminda Pantheon. This is a quiet cemetery where some of Georgia's most respected writers, artists, and public figures are buried. It is worth stopping there on the way down. The calm of the pantheon feels far removed from the fairground at the top.

Think about how layered this mountain is. Below you lie graves of poets and a church that pilgrims have climbed toward for generations. Around you is a park built for fun. Above you rises a Soviet-era steel mast that sent pictures and voices into homes across the country. Each era added something to Mtatsminda. None of them removed what came before. That mix is very typical of Tbilisi, a city that rarely tidies away its past.

Before you leave, find a spot at the edge of the summit where you can see both the tower and the city in one view. Notice how the light changes on the steel as clouds pass. Listen again for that hum in the frame. As you walk away, look back once more. You will probably see the tower again tonight, from a street corner far below, glowing above the dark ridge.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the funicular up for the steep, scenic approach.
Stop at the Mtatsminda Pantheon on your way down.
Come back at dusk to see the tower lights begin.
Bring a light jacket; the summit is windier than town.
Ride the Ferris wheel for a higher view of the frame.
