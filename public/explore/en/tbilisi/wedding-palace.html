The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. Pale concrete curves away above you in long, folded shapes, like fabric caught by the wind and frozen in place. The surfaces are smooth in some places and rough in others, marked with the grain of the wooden moulds that once held the wet concrete. Below the hill, the Mtkvari river runs through Tbilisi, and the sound of traffic rises faintly from the city. Up here, though, the building feels oddly quiet.

This is the Wedding Palace, known in Georgian as the Palace of Rituals. It was built in the final decade of the Soviet Union, and it was completed in 1984. Its purpose was simple and very ambitious at the same time. The Soviet state wanted a grand place for civil weddings, a ceremony without priests. And it wanted that place to feel as moving as any church.

That goal tells you a lot about the period. In Soviet Georgia, the state was officially atheist, and religion was pushed to the edges of public life. Yet weddings still needed ritual, music, and a sense of occasion. Across the Soviet Union, authorities built special halls where couples could register their marriage with ceremony. Most of them were plain. This one in Tbilisi was not plain at all... and that is the first surprise.

The building was designed by the Georgian architect Victor Djorbenadze, working with a colleague, Vazha Orbeladze. Djorbenadze did not copy a church, but he borrowed the feeling of one. Look at the tall, narrow tower rising beside the main volume. It recalls a bell tower. Look at the rounded forms that swell outward. They suggest apses, the curved ends of a church where the altar usually stands. The language is sacred, but the purpose was secular.

Many people describe the palace as Brutalist. Brutalism is a style of architecture from the mid twentieth century that uses raw, exposed concrete and heavy, sculptural forms. But this building is softer than most Brutalist works. There are few straight lines and almost no sharp corners. Instead, the walls billow and turn, and the roof seems to flow from one shape into the next. Some visitors think it looks like a ship. Others see flower petals, or a sail.

Walk slowly around the outside if you can. The building changes with every few steps. From one angle it looks heavy and closed, like a fortress. From another it looks light, almost floating above the slope. This is deliberate. The palace was designed as sculpture as much as architecture, and it is meant to be seen in motion. Notice how the sunlight moves across the curves. Shadows gather in the folds and soften at the edges.

Inside, the ceremonial spaces were designed to match the drama of the exterior. Couples moved through the building in a sequence, almost like a procession. There were halls for the ceremony itself, spaces for families, and rooms for celebration afterwards. The interiors included decorative art and large wall paintings, which gave the rooms colour and a sense of story. For many Georgian couples in the 1980s, a wedding here was an event the whole family remembered.

Now for the second surprise. The palace did not stay a public wedding hall for long. The Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, only a few years after the building opened. Georgia became independent, and the following years were very hard, with conflict, economic crisis, and frequent power cuts. Large state buildings like this one became difficult to maintain. In the early 2000s, the palace was sold to a private owner, the well-known Georgian businessman Badri Patarkatsishvili.

Under private ownership, the building changed its role. It was used as a residence and as a place for private events and receptions. The church-like silhouette that once hosted Soviet civil ceremonies now hosted a very different world, one of new money and new politics. After his death in 2008, the building continued its life as a venue. Today it is still used for weddings and celebrations, so in a way it has returned to its original purpose.

The palace has also found a second audience far beyond Georgia. In recent years, photographers and architecture lovers from around the world have become fascinated by late Soviet buildings. This building appears in a widely known photographic book about unusual Soviet architecture by the French photographer Frédéric Chaubin. Because of that interest, people now travel to Tbilisi partly to see structures like this one, together with other daring concrete buildings across the city.

It helps to place the palace among its neighbours in time. Tbilisi in the 1970s and 1980s was a city of experiments. Georgian architects had more creative freedom than many colleagues elsewhere in the Soviet Union. They mixed modern engineering with references to Georgian tradition, such as old church towers and mountain fortresses. You can see that mix here. The tower carries a quiet echo of medieval Georgian bell towers, translated into poured concrete.

As you look at the walls today, you will see the marks of age. There are stains where rain has run down the concrete for decades. There are patches and repairs in slightly different shades of grey. Some people see this as decay. Others see it as part of the building's character. Concrete like this is honest about time. It does not hide its weather, and every season leaves a trace on the surface.

If a wedding is taking place while you are here, you may hear music drifting out, or see a couple posing for photographs on the steps. That is perhaps the best way to understand the building. It was made for moments like these, for nerves and laughter and families in their best clothes. Please give them their space. The palace is a working venue, and for the people inside, today may be one of the most important days of their lives.

Before you leave, take one more look at the tower against the sky. Notice the hill around you, the river below, and the city spreading out in every direction. This building was born from a state that wanted rituals without religion. It survived that state, changed owners, and found new meaning. As you walk away, listen for the city again... and let the curves of the concrete stay with you a little longer.
