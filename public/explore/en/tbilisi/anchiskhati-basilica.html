Anchiskhati Basilica

The story

Tucked into a quiet corner of the old town, a little below the noise of the main lanes, stands a small brick-and-stone church that most people walk straight past. Push the door and step into the cool dimness inside. You are now in the oldest surviving church in Tbilisi — Anchiskhati Basilica, a building that has watched the city grow up around it for some fifteen hundred years.

Anchiskhati dates to the early sixth century. Tradition links it to King Dachi of Iberia, the ruler who, the old chronicles say, moved the Georgian capital to Tbilisi and ringed the young city with walls. Whether or not every detail of that story holds, the church's great age is not in doubt. It has outlasted dynasties, invasions, and empires while keeping its modest size.

Look at the shape, because it is unusual here. Most old Georgian churches are built as a cross with a dome rising over the centre. Anchiskhati is a basilica — a plain rectangular hall divided into three long aisles by two rows of columns, with no central dome. That older, simpler plan is one reason historians read the building as early. Standing inside, you feel length drawing you forward toward the altar, rather than height pulling you up.

The name carries a journey. Anchiskhati means the icon of Ancha. Ancha was a monastery far to the south-west, in a region called Klarjeti that now lies inside Turkey. In the seventeenth century, to keep a revered icon of Christ safe, it was carried here for protection, and the church took the icon's name. The original icon itself is no longer kept in the church; it is preserved in a museum in the city.

Just outside stands a separate bell tower of brick, added long after the church itself, in the late seventeenth century, under a church leader named Domenti. It is one of the older surviving bell towers in Tbilisi. Notice how its warm brick differs from the stone of the church body. The two structures are centuries apart, standing side by side in the same small yard.

Like everything old in this city, Anchiskhati has been damaged and patched many times. Walls were rebuilt, the roof changed, sections refaced. If you look closely at the masonry you can see different periods of work stacked together — older cut stone low down, later brick above. The building is honest about its repairs. It does not pretend to be untouched.

Inside, the light is low and the air is still. Fragments of wall painting survive, though much has been lost over the centuries. The columns march down the hall in two rows. There is little of the gilded grandeur of the big cathedrals here. The appeal is the plainness — the sense of a very old room that has been in steady use far longer than almost any building you will ever enter.

Anchiskhati has a particular place in Georgian culture: it is closely tied to traditional Georgian chant. The choir linked to this church is among the best-known keepers of the country's old church singing — three independent voice lines moving together, a form recognised around the world as a treasure of human music. If you can time a visit to a sung service, the sound in this small stone hall is the real reason to come.

It remains an active church, not a monument. People come in to pray, light a candle, kiss an icon, and leave. Because the space is small, your presence is felt — move quietly, dress modestly, and women may wish to cover their hair as local worshippers do. Photography may be limited, especially during services, so take your cue from those around you.

The contrast with the surroundings is part of the experience. A few minutes' walk away, glass footbridges and new parks line the river, all built within the last decades. Here, behind a low wall, sits a hall raised when the city itself was young. Standing in the yard, you can hold both versions of Tbilisi at once — the very old and the very new — separated by a short walk and about fifteen centuries.

In a building this small and this old, the senses do the work. The stone keeps the air cool even in summer. Candle smoke and the smell of warm wax hang near the entrance. Footsteps stay quiet on the worn floor. When the choir rehearses or a service begins, the unaccompanied voices fill the hall completely, with nowhere for the sound to escape — which is exactly how this music was meant to be heard.

The church sits near Baratashvili Street, at the lower edge of the old town, an easy addition to a walk that also takes in Sioni Cathedral and the river. It is small, so a visit need not be long — ten or fifteen unhurried minutes is enough to take it in. Mornings tend to be the quietest. There is no entrance fee.

When you step back out into the yard, look once more at the low church and the brick tower beside it, then at the modern city rising just past the rooftops. Few places let you stand so plainly inside the sixth century and the present at the same time. As you rejoin the busier lanes, it is worth remembering that this quiet hall was already old when most of what surrounds it was only beginning.

Key facts

The oldest surviving church in Tbilisi.
Built in the early sixth century.
A three-aisle basilica, not a domed church.
Named after an icon brought from Ancha monastery.
Its brick bell tower dates to the seventeenth century.

What to look for

Two rows of columns dividing the long hall.
Older stone below, later brick patched above.
The separate brick bell tower in the yard.
Surviving fragments of old wall painting inside.

Practical tips

Come in the morning for the deepest quiet.
Time a visit to a sung service if you can.
Dress modestly; women may cover their hair.
Ten to fifteen minutes is enough to take it in.
