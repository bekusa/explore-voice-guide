The guide

The story

Halfway across, stop and put your hand on the steel rail — warm in the sun, faintly humming when the wind moves through the frame. Above you curves a long canopy of glass and metal, and below, the green water of the Mtkvari slides past. This is the Peace Bridge, a footbridge that has become one of the most photographed objects in Tbilisi since it opened in 2010.

The bridge is for walking only — no cars. It links the old heart of the city on one bank with Rike Park and the lower station of the cable car on the other. In a few minutes on foot it stitches together the medieval lanes, the river, and the newer riverside park, which is exactly why so many visitors cross it without ever planning to.

The form is deliberately modern, and it was meant to stand apart from the old town. It was designed by the Italian architect Michele De Lucchi, with a lighting scheme by the French designer Philippe Martinaud. The shape is often described as a bow: a single sweeping curve of steel arches carrying a roof of glass panels, set down between centuries-old buildings on one side and newer city on the other.

The canopy is the heart of the design — thousands of glass panels held in a steel web, letting daylight through while throwing patterned shadows onto the deck. Walk it at different times of day and the light underfoot changes completely: sharp and bright at noon, long and soft toward evening. The transparency was the point, so the bridge would feel light rather than block the view of the river.

Here is the detail most people miss. The bridge carries an interactive lighting system built into its rails and canopy. After dark, thousands of small lights switch on, and they do not simply glow — they run in slow sequences along the parapets. The installation was designed to send a repeating message in pulses of light, a quiet idea about life and connection coded into the structure itself. Few of the people crossing realise the lights are saying anything at all.

That is why the bridge is worth seeing twice — once by day and once after sunset. At night the glass canopy glows from within, and the moving lights turn the whole span into a soft band of brightness over dark water. From the riverbanks and the bridges nearby, it becomes one of the defining shapes of the modern city skyline.

The bridge was not loved by everyone at first. Some residents felt a sharp piece of glass and steel sat awkwardly beside the old town's brick and carved wood, and the design drew its share of nicknames. Others welcomed it as a sign that the city was investing in itself again. Standing on it now, you can decide for yourself how the new structure speaks to the old skyline around it.

The crossing is one of the better free viewpoints in the centre. From the middle you can see the Metekhi church on its cliff, the statue of the city's founder, the old town climbing the far slope, and the modern concert halls downstream. Turn slowly through a full circle before you walk on; the bridge gathers most of central Tbilisi's landmarks into one sweep.

On the far side lies Rike Park, a riverside strip of lawns, fountains, and walking paths. From there the cable car lifts up to the Narikala fortress on the ridge, so many people treat the Peace Bridge as the first step in a longer route: cross the river, walk the park, ride up to the fortress, then look back down on the bridge they started from.

Walking it tells you things a photograph cannot. The deck has a faint give underfoot, the slight, designed flex of a long modern span. The handrails are smooth metal, warm where the sun has reached them and cool in the shade of the canopy. On a windy day you can feel the structure working. It is a short crossing, but an oddly physical one.

The bridge belongs to a wave of bold building along this stretch of river. Nearby stand a curved glass public hall and the steel tubes of a riverside concert venue, all from roughly the same era. Together they mark a deliberate push to set modern architecture directly against the medieval core, rather than keeping it to the edge of town. You may admire the result or argue with it, but it is hard to ignore.

Crossing is free and takes only a few minutes, but give it more time than that. Come once in daylight for the shadow patterns and the views, and again after dark for the lights. The deck can grow warm and bright under the midday sun, so morning or evening is more comfortable. The surface is smooth and level, easy for wheelchairs and strollers.

When you reach the far bank, turn around and look back at the long curve you have just walked. It is a young structure in a very old city, and that contrast is the whole experience — a thread of glass and light tying the medieval streets to the modern parks across the water. Whether you find it beautiful or strange, it has already become part of how Tbilisi sees itself.
