The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of Rustaveli Avenue for a moment and listen. Car tyres hiss on the wide road, a trolley of roasted corn rattles past, and somewhere a street musician tunes a guitar. Then lift your eyes. A pale, ornate facade rises above the traffic, with curved windows, carved garlands and balconies that look almost too delicate for a city street. This is the Rustaveli Theatre, the oldest and best-known drama stage in Georgia.

The building went up at the turn of the twentieth century, when Tbilisi was a growing imperial city on the edge of Europe and Asia. Wealthy patrons and artistic societies wanted a cultural home that could match the opera houses of Vienna or Saint Petersburg. The result is this facade in a revived Baroque and Rococo style. Baroque is the theatrical, curving style of seventeenth-century Europe, and Rococo is its lighter, more playful cousin. Notice how almost no surface is left plain.

The theatre carries the name of Shota Rustaveli, and so does the avenue outside. Rustaveli was a poet of the twelfth century. He wrote The Knight in the Panther's Skin, a long epic about friendship, loyalty and love. For Georgians it holds roughly the place that Shakespeare holds in English. Families once gave a copy to a bride as part of her dowry. Many Georgians can still recite lines from memory, and the theatre took his name in the early Soviet years.

Now look closely at the stonework above the entrance. The decoration is generous: scrolls, shell shapes, swags of carved fruit and flowers. The colours are soft, mostly cream and warm grey, and they change with the light. In the morning the facade looks cool and calm. In the late afternoon the sun moves down the avenue and the carving casts deep shadows, so each garland seems to stand out from the wall.

Here is something many visitors walk straight past. Below the theatre, in the basement, there is a space that was once an artists' café called Kimerioni. Around 1919, during Georgia's short period of independence, a group of young painters decorated its walls. Lado Gudiashvili, one of the great Georgian painters of the twentieth century, was among them. Their murals show dreamlike figures and fantastic creatures, and they belong to a brief, bright moment of freedom before the Soviet era.

Those years matter for understanding the stage upstairs. After 1921, Georgia became part of the Soviet Union. In the 1920s, a brilliant director named Sandro Akhmeteli led the theatre. He wanted a bold, physical, national style, with strong movement, large crowds on stage and a sense of Georgian epic. His productions made the Rustaveli company famous far beyond Tbilisi. In the purges of 1937, Akhmeteli was arrested and executed. His name was silenced for years.

Even so, his ideas did not disappear. Georgian theatre kept its love of rhythm, music and big gesture. Actors trained here learned to fill a stage with their whole body, not only with their voice. When you watch a performance today, even without understanding the language, you can often follow the story through movement alone. That physical clarity is one of the quiet strengths of this house, and one reason foreign audiences have responded to it so strongly.

The second great chapter came with the director Robert Sturua. From the 1970s onward, he staged productions that toured across Europe and beyond. His version of Bertolt Brecht's The Caucasian Chalk Circle and his Richard the Third by Shakespeare drew attention from critics in London and elsewhere. Brecht's play is actually set in Georgia, which gave the Tbilisi company a special connection to it. For many people outside the Soviet Union, these tours were their first view of Georgian theatre.

Step inside if the doors are open, and the mood changes. The street noise drops away. The foyer is quieter and cooler, and the old floors and staircases carry the polish of more than a century of feet. The main auditorium follows the classic European horseshoe shape, with tiers of seating curving around the stage. Red and gold tones are common in halls of this period. Theatres like this were built for people to watch the stage, and also to watch each other.

The theatre is not a museum. It is a working company with a regular season, rehearsals and new productions. Performances are usually in Georgian. Some evenings attract an older crowd who have followed the company for decades. Other nights fill with students who come for a new young director. If you buy a ticket, arrive a little early. The ritual of coats, programmes and quiet conversation in the foyer is part of the experience.

When you come back outside, look along the avenue in both directions. Rustaveli Avenue is the main ceremonial street of Tbilisi. It runs between Freedom Square and the area around Rustaveli metro station. Along it you will find the Parliament building, museums, galleries, and the Opera and Ballet Theatre. Plane trees shade the pavements in summer. This street has seen parades, celebrations and protests, so the theatre stands in the political as well as the cultural heart of the city.

That location gives the building a double life. On ordinary days it is a place of art, rehearsals and evening shows. On days of national tension, crowds gather on the avenue outside, and the theatre facade becomes a backdrop to public life. Georgians have often seen culture and national identity as closely connected. A theatre named after their national poet, on their main avenue, naturally sits at the meeting point of both ideas.

Before you move on, take one more look at the facade. Find a single carved garland and follow its curve with your eyes. Notice the windows, the balconies, the small details the builders added for people who would rarely look up. Then listen again to the avenue: the traffic, the voices, perhaps music from a nearby café. Somewhere inside, actors may be rehearsing right now. As you walk away, the stage behind these walls keeps working, as it has for more than a hundred years.
