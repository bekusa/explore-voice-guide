The guide

The story

West of the old centre, the streets widen, the traffic eases under rows of tall trees, and the cafes fill with students and families rather than tour groups. This is Vake, the leafy district that shows you the everyday, modern side of Tbilisi, well away from the historic lanes and the souvenir shops. It is where much of the city actually lives.

Vake grew into its character in the twentieth century. It was developed largely during the Soviet decades as a desirable residential area, and it has kept that comfortable, settled feel ever since. Solid apartment blocks and older houses line broad, tree-shaded streets, and the pace is noticeably slower and more local than in the old town.

The spine of the district is Chavchavadze Avenue, a long, busy street named after Ilia Chavchavadze, a celebrated nineteenth-century Georgian writer and public figure honoured as a national hero. Along it you find bookshops, bakeries, cafes, boutiques, and the buildings of the main university, and the wide pavements fill with people through the late afternoon.

This is one of the best parts of the city for simply sitting and watching. The cafes here are where Tbilisians actually spend their time, meeting friends, working, and passing the afternoon, and an hour at an outdoor table tells you more about how the city lives now than another church or fortress would. It is ordinary life, on display and easy to join.

Trees define the district as much as any building. The streets are lined with them, casting deep shade in summer, and the whole area runs up toward Vake Park, a large green space that gives the neighbourhood its breathing room and its name recognition. Greenery and unhurried streets are, between them, the whole appeal.

There are few formal sights here, and that is precisely the point. You do not come to Vake to tick off monuments; you come to walk, eat, drink coffee, browse a bookshop, and feel the rhythm of the present-day city. It is the antidote to a day spent among ancient stones.

The district also marks a social contrast worth noticing. Vake has long been one of the more prosperous parts of Tbilisi, and the cafes, boutiques, and well-kept streets show it. Set against the worn grandeur of the old town, it gives you a fuller, more honest picture of how varied the modern city is.

It pairs naturally with the park at the top of the avenue. A walk up Chavchavadze, a coffee along the way, and a climb into Vake Park make an easy, pleasant half day, and the park's wooded slopes lead on toward Turtle Lake higher up the hill for those who want to keep going.

Getting here is straightforward. The district is a short taxi ride or a metro-and-walk from the centre, and once you arrive it is best explored on foot. Prices in the smarter cafes on the main avenue can match the centre, but the quieter side streets hide cheaper and often more characterful local spots.

Time your visit for the late part of the day. Late afternoon and early evening are the liveliest and most pleasant hours to stroll Chavchavadze Avenue, when the heat has eased, the pavements fill, and the cafes hit their stride. Mornings are quieter and good for a slower coffee.

Vake also tells part of the city's recent story. As one of the more sought-after addresses through the late twentieth century and into the present, it has drawn new cafes, shops, and renovations, and walking it gives a sense of where modern, comfortable Tbilisi has chosen to settle.

For visitors, the district is an easy place to eat and rest well. The cafes and bakeries are aimed at locals rather than tourists, the coffee is good, and a meal here often costs less and feels more genuine than its equivalent in the busier old town.

Take a table, order a coffee, and let the comfortable life of the district go by for a while. After the weight of history in the old town, Vake is a useful reminder that Tbilisi is also just a place where people live, study, work, and meet their friends. That contrast is worth a slow hour of any trip.
