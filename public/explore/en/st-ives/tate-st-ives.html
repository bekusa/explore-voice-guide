The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, the Atlantic rolls onto Porthmeor Beach in long, steady breaths. Gulls call overhead. The air tastes of salt, and the light has a sharp, clean quality, almost white on a clear day. This is the light that painters came here to find. The building in front of you was made to hold their work, and it sits close enough to the sand that the sea is never really out of view.

This is Tate St Ives, one of the regional galleries of the Tate, the British national collection of art. It opened in 1993. Its purpose was simple and quite unusual for its time. Instead of showing art from everywhere, it would show the art that grew from this particular town, beside the very beach and harbour that shaped it. Few galleries in Britain stand so close to the subject matter of the paintings inside them.

Look at the shape of the building. White walls, clean curves, and a large round form at the front. That rounded entrance is a deliberate echo. Before the gallery, this site held a gasworks, and the curve recalls the round gasholder that once stood here. The architects were Eldred Evans and David Shalev. They took an industrial plot on the edge of a fishing town and gave it a form that feels at home among the white-washed cottages and the sea.

So why St Ives? Why would a small Cornish fishing town, near the far western tip of England, become a name known in modern art? Part of the answer is the light. The town sits on a narrow headland with water on several sides. Sunlight reflects off the sea and the pale sand, and the effect is bright and soft at the same time. Artists had visited since the nineteenth century, but something new began in the twentieth.

Here is the first surprise. One of the most important painters connected with St Ives was not a trained artist at all. Alfred Wallis was a retired fisherman and marine scrap dealer. He started painting in old age, after his wife died, using ship paint on scraps of cardboard. In 1928, the painters Ben Nicholson and Christopher Wood walked past his cottage and saw his pictures. They were struck by how direct and honest they were. Wallis painted boats and harbours from memory, with no interest in perspective.

That meeting mattered. Nicholson took ideas from Wallis back into his own work. Later, around the start of the Second World War, Nicholson and the sculptor Barbara Hepworth moved to St Ives with their family. Other artists followed or were already here. Names you may see on the gallery walls include Patrick Heron, Terry Frost, Peter Lanyon and Wilhelmina Barns-Graham. Together, people came to call them the St Ives School, although they never worked as one tidy group.

What connects them? Many of these artists moved towards abstraction — art that does not try to copy the visible world directly. But look closely and you will often find the landscape still there. A sweep of colour may be the curve of a bay. A rough texture may be granite or a field wall. A band of blue may be the horizon you can see from the window. The gallery invites you to compare the paintings with the view outside.

Now for the second surprise. Much of the gallery you walk through today is underground. In 2017, a major extension opened, designed by Jamie Fobert Architects. Rather than build a large new block that would tower over the town, the architects dug into the hillside behind the original building. The new main gallery sits below ground level, lit from above by deep roof lights. It gave the gallery a large, flexible space for changing exhibitions without crowding the streets around it.

Outside, look for the exterior of that extension. The walls are covered in glazed ceramic tiles, made by hand. Their colours shift between grey, green and blue, and they catch the changing light from the sea and sky. Ceramics are a fitting choice here. St Ives has a long pottery tradition, connected especially with Bernard Leach, who set up a pottery in the town in 1920 with the Japanese potter Shoji Hamada. His influence on studio pottery spread far beyond Cornwall.

Inside, in the original building, pay attention to colour. Patrick Heron, one of the leading painters of the St Ives group, designed a large stained glass window for the gallery. On a bright day, colour from the glass falls across the space. Heron loved strong, saturated colour, and his window turns sunlight itself into part of the artwork. It is worth pausing there to watch how the patterns move as clouds pass across the sun.

The gallery also has a close partner nearby. Tate looks after the Barbara Hepworth Museum and Sculpture Garden, a short walk away through the old town. This was Hepworth's own home and studio, Trewyn Studio, where she lived and worked for decades. Her bronze and stone sculptures stand among the plants of her garden, close to where she made them. Seeing both places on the same visit helps you understand how closely art and daily life were woven together here.

The gallery's role has changed over time. Today it shows not only the St Ives artists but also modern and contemporary art from around the world, often placed in conversation with the town's own story. In 2018, it was named Art Fund Museum of the Year, a major British award for museums and galleries. That recognition came soon after the extension opened, and it brought fresh attention to this small coastal town and its long relationship with artists.

If you go up through the building, look for the views. Windows frame the beach, the surfers and the long line of the sea. The upper floor café looks out over Porthmeor, and many visitors find the view as memorable as anything on the walls. It is a reminder that the gallery was designed to look outward, not to shut the world away. The town, the water and the art are meant to be seen together.

As you leave, take a moment on the beach or the steps outside. Look at the colours in front of you — the pale sand, the green-blue water, the white foam, the grey granite of the headland. These are the same colours you may have just seen on canvas. Notice how quickly the light changes as clouds move in from the Atlantic. The painters who worked here watched exactly this. Now, for a few minutes, you are watching it too.
