The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the silence has weight. A raven calls somewhere high above, and a dry wind moves across the slickrock. Under your boots, the sand is fine and red, almost like powdered brick. The stone around you is warm, even after the sun has passed. This is Arches National Park, in the high desert of eastern Utah, just north of the town of Moab.

The name is honest. More than two thousand natural stone arches have been documented inside the park. That is the greatest known concentration of natural arches anywhere on Earth. Some are tiny, barely wide enough for a hand to pass through. Others span the length of a football field. Each one began as a crack in the rock, and each one will one day fall.

To understand why this place exists, you have to look underground. Deep below the surface lies a thick layer of salt. It was left behind by an ancient sea that evaporated many millions of years ago. Over time, layers of sand and mud buried that salt and pressed down on it. But salt behaves strangely under pressure — it flows, slowly, like a very thick liquid.

As the salt shifted and swelled, it pushed the rock above it upward. The sandstone bent and cracked into long, parallel fractures. Rain and frost worked into those cracks. Water froze, expanded, and pried the stone apart. Over enormous spans of time, the cracks widened into narrow walls of rock called fins. When the softer layers inside a fin wore away, light broke through... and an arch was born.

Most of the arches here are carved from a rock called Entrada Sandstone. Its colour comes from iron oxide — essentially rust — coating the grains of sand. That is why the cliffs glow orange in the early morning and deep red at sunset. Look closely at the base of many formations and you will see a paler band of rock. These layers wear away at different speeds, and that difference shapes the landscape you see today.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The arches are not permanent. In 2008, a formation called Wall Arch collapsed during the night, leaving two broken stumps of stone. Landscape Arch, in the Devils Garden area, has dropped large slabs since the late twentieth century. Its ribbon of rock is remarkably thin for its length. It is one of the longest natural stone spans in the world, and you are seeing it in a single moment of its life.

People were here long before the arches had names. Ancestral Puebloan and Fremont peoples moved through this country, and later the Ute. Near the trailhead for Delicate Arch, you can find a panel of rock art pecked into the dark varnish of a cliff. It shows figures on horseback alongside bighorn sheep. The horses tell us something important — this art was made after horses arrived in the region, so it is relatively recent.

Close to that rock art stands a small, weathered log cabin. It belonged to John Wesley Wolfe, a Civil War veteran who settled here with his son in the late nineteenth century. He ran cattle on this dry, unforgiving land. Imagine raising a family with the nearest supplies a long ride away. The cabin is modest, built from rough timber. It shows how hard daily life was in this valley.

Protection came in the twentieth century. The area was first set aside as a national monument in 1929, and it became a national park in 1971. In the 1950s, a young seasonal ranger named Edward Abbey worked here. He later wrote a book called Desert Solitaire, about his months alone in this landscape. His writing helped many readers see the desert not as empty ground, but as a place worth defending.

If there is one shape that people connect with Utah, it is Delicate Arch. It stands alone on the rim of a sandstone bowl, with the snowy La Sal Mountains on the horizon behind it. The arch appears on Utah licence plates. The trail to reach it climbs across open slickrock with very little shade. At the top, the view opens suddenly. Many people go quiet when they first see it.

There is life here, even where the ground looks bare. Look down at the dark, lumpy soil between the shrubs. That is biological soil crust, a living layer of cyanobacteria, lichens, and mosses. It holds the sand in place and feeds the plants around it. A single footstep can crush it, and it may take decades to recover. Rangers have a simple phrase for this — do not bust the crust.

The park changes completely after dark. Arches has been recognised as an International Dark Sky Park, because the night sky here stays so free of city light. On a clear, moonless night, the Milky Way rises over the fins and towers. The arches become black outlines against the stars. The temperature drops quickly, and the desert fills with the small sounds of animals that hide from the daytime heat.

So take a breath, wherever you are standing right now. Feel the dry air, the warmth still held in the stone. Look at the nearest crack in the rock — it may be the beginning of an arch that no one living will ever see. As you walk on, stay on the trail and watch the colour of the cliffs shift with the light. This landscape is still being made, one grain of sand at a time.
