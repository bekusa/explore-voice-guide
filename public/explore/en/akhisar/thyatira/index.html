The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums along the streets of Akhisar. A tea glass clinks somewhere nearby. A moped coughs past. And here, just below street level, lie pale blocks of marble, broken column shafts, and the low outlines of old walls. You are standing at the edge of a city that is more than two thousand years old. It sits in the middle of a modern town that grew right on top of it.

This is Thyatira. Today the site is known locally as Tepemezarı, which roughly means "the hill cemetery". It is a fenced, open-air patch of ruins wrapped by apartment blocks, shops, and busy roads. There is no grand approach here. There is no long avenue of temples. That is part of its charm. Ancient and modern life press directly against each other, and you can see both in a single glance.

The city's story as a Greek town begins in the Hellenistic era, the period after Alexander the Great, when his generals divided his empire. One of them, Seleucus the First, established Thyatira as a military colony, settling Macedonian soldiers here in the early third century before Christ. The location made sense. Thyatira sat on a natural route between the ancient kingdoms of the region, and whoever held the road held influence over the plain.

The name itself carries a small story. According to one popular tale, Seleucus received news here that a daughter had been born to him. The name "Thyatira" has often been linked to a Greek word for daughter. Treat this as legend rather than certain history. Still, it is a gentle origin story for a town that was founded by soldiers.

Thyatira never became a political capital. Its real strength was work. Inscriptions found in and around the city mention a remarkable number of trade guilds. Among them were wool workers, linen weavers, leather workers, potters, bakers, and bronze smiths. Few towns in this part of the ancient world have left such a long list. Imagine the streets around you filled with workshops, the smell of wet wool, and the sound of hammers on metal.

The most famous of these trades was dyeing. Thyatira was known for its coloured cloth, especially a rich purple-red. In the Bible, the Book of Acts mentions a woman named Lydia from Thyatira. She was a seller of purple cloth, and she met the apostle Paul in the city of Philippi in Greece. Many readers know her name without ever realising that her hometown lies under these streets.

That biblical link leads to Thyatira's greatest claim to fame. It is one of the Seven Churches of Asia named in the Book of Revelation, the last book of the New Testament. Each of the seven cities receives a short letter. Thyatira's letter is the longest of the seven, which surprises many visitors, since this is the smallest and least known city on the list.

The letter praises the community for its love, faith, and patience. It also warns against a figure it calls "Jezebel", a woman accused of leading people astray. Scholars still debate who, or what, this name stood for. One common idea connects the warning to the guilds. Guild members often shared feasts honouring pagan gods. For early Christians who depended on these trades, joining in or staying away was a hard choice.

The city also had its own religious life. Ancient coins and inscriptions point to a local sun god called Tyrimnos, who was closely linked with the Greek god Apollo. Festivals and games were held in his honour. None of his temple is clearly visible here today, but his presence on the city's coins shows how proud Thyatira was of its own traditions.

Now look closely at the ruins in front of you. What remains belongs mostly to the Roman and later Byzantine periods. You can see the stumps of columns and the line of a colonnaded street, once covered by a roofed walkway with shops behind it. There are also the foundations of a large building with a rounded end, often interpreted as a basilica. Many of the stones show tool marks and old cracks. Some have been reset by archaeologists after excavation.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Look at the ground level of the ruins, then look at the street around you. The ancient pavement sits noticeably lower. Centuries of rebuilding, rubble, and soil slowly raised the town. Much of Thyatira is still buried under the houses of Akhisar. That is why the site feels so small. You are seeing only a window into a city that once stretched far beyond this fence.

Later history passed through here too. In the fourth century, a battle near Thyatira helped decide a struggle for the Roman throne. Over the following centuries the town changed hands many times, and the Turkish name Akhisar, meaning "white castle", eventually replaced the Greek one. Today the region is known for its olives and its olive oil. On your way out, you may notice shops selling them in the streets nearby.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at a single column, then lift your eyes to the balconies and laundry lines above the fence. Soldiers, dyers, and early believers walked this same ground. Now children walk home from school beside it. As you step back into the noise of Akhisar, listen again. The work and the life of this town never really stopped.
