The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through olive leaves, and somewhere below, a car hums along the lakeshore road. Under your feet the ground is uneven, a mix of grass, dark volcanic earth, and old stone. Look downhill and you will see the wide grey-blue water of Lake Bolsena. This hillside was once a Roman city, and the lake was the first thing its people saw each morning.

The city was called Volsinii. Its story begins somewhere else, with a violent ending. Before the Romans, this region belonged to the Etruscans. They were a powerful people of central Italy who ruled here long before Rome controlled the peninsula. One of their greatest cities was Velzna, which the Romans called Volsinii. Most scholars place that older city on the high rock where Orvieto stands today, to the northeast of here.

Ancient writers tell a remarkable story about that older city. In the third century before Christ, they say, the old aristocratic families lost power to people of lower status, often described as former slaves. The nobles could not take the city back on their own. So they asked Rome for help. That was a fateful choice. In 264 BCE, Roman armies besieged the city, captured it, and destroyed it.

Rome did not simply leave the survivors alone. According to the ancient accounts, the people were moved away from their fortified rock to a new, more open site. That site is the hillside around you. Historians often call it Volsinii Novi, meaning New Volsinii, to separate it from the destroyed Etruscan city. A rock fortress was hard to conquer. A gentle slope above a lake was much easier to control.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder said that the conquerors carried away an enormous number of bronze statues. He gave a figure of about two thousand. Whether that number is exact or exaggerated, it shows how wealthy the Etruscan city must have been. The Roman general who won the victory celebrated a formal triumph in Rome. Etruscan wealth helped decorate the growing Roman capital.

The new city did well. It stood close to the Via Cassia, the great Roman road that ran north from Rome into Etruria and beyond. Today's road through Bolsena still follows much of that old route. Travellers, traders, and soldiers passed this lake for centuries. For the people of Volsinii, the lake also gave them fish, water, and a mild local climate. Those things kept a town alive long after the empire changed.

Here is a second surprise. Volsinii gave Rome one of its most feared men. Lucius Aelius Sejanus was born here. He became commander of the Praetorian Guard, the emperor's personal soldiers, under the emperor Tiberius in the first century. For several years Sejanus held huge influence in Rome. Then he fell from power suddenly and was executed. A boy from this quiet lakeside town once came very close to the centre of the Roman world.

Much of what we know about Roman Volsinii comes from careful excavation in the twentieth century. French archaeologists from the École française de Rome began systematic digging here after the Second World War. They worked season after season on the slopes above the modern town. Their work uncovered public spaces, streets, and private houses. It also showed that the city had earlier layers, so people lived on this ground before the Roman resettlement.

As you walk, look at the building materials. This whole landscape is volcanic. The lake itself fills an ancient volcanic basin, and it is one of the largest volcanic lakes in Europe. Builders here used the stone the volcanoes left behind: soft, workable tuff and hard, dark basalt. Romans often paved their roads with tight-fitting blocks of hard volcanic stone. The foundations around you came directly from the ground beneath them.

Try to picture the city as it once was. There was a forum, the open public square where citizens met, traded, and heard announcements. There were temples and halls for business and law. There were homes with painted walls, some with richly decorated rooms. Today you see mostly low walls and foundations. But the plan of the city is still in the ground. Streets, doorways, and room outlines are all there for a careful eye.

You may find the site very quiet. Many travellers come to Bolsena for the lakeshore, the medieval streets, or the church of Saint Christina in the town below. That church is linked to a famous medieval event, the Miracle of Bolsena in the thirteenth century. Fewer visitors climb up to the Roman ruins. That means you may have the stones mostly to yourself, with lizards on the warm walls and birds in the trees.

If you want to see objects found here, visit the castle on the hill above the old town. It holds the local museum of Lake Bolsena and its territory. Inside, finds from the area help explain how people lived around this lake across many periods. Seeing the objects after walking the ruins helps a lot. The pottery, inscriptions, and fragments make the empty foundations feel inhabited again.

Before you leave, stand still and look at the lake one more time. It is the same water the Etruscan exiles saw when they arrived here, uprooted from their old city. They built new homes, and their grandchildren became Romans. Their stones are still under the grass. Notice the dark colour of the earth on your shoes as you walk down. It is volcanic ground, the same ground that held this city for more than two thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and loose stone.
Bring water and a hat on summer afternoons.
Visit the castle museum to see finds from the area.
Combine the ruins with a walk through medieval Bolsena.
Come early morning for quiet paths and softer light.
