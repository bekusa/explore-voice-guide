The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the wind on this high bluff. Below you, two rivers meet. The Mississippi comes down from the north, and the Minnesota River joins it from the west. On a quiet day you can hear the traffic on the highways, the hum of planes from the nearby airport, and under it all, the slow movement of water. Long before any wall stood here, people came to this point to look down at exactly what you are seeing now.

The Dakota people call this place Bdote. The word refers to a meeting of waters, and for many Dakota it is a place of origin, connected to their creation stories. Families gathered here, traded here, and buried their dead nearby. That is why the site today carries a longer name: Historic Fort Snelling at Bdote. The name reminds visitors that the fort is only one chapter in a much older story about this landscape.

The American chapter began in the early nineteenth century. In 1805, the explorer Zebulon Pike negotiated a land agreement with Dakota leaders here, an agreement that historians still debate for its fairness. Construction of a military post started around 1820. The fort was later named for Colonel Josiah Snelling, the commander who oversaw much of the building. For the young United States, this was the far frontier — a post meant to control river traffic, the fur trade, and relations with Native nations.

Look at the walls. They are built of local limestone, pale grey and cream, cut from the rock beneath your feet. The outline of the fort follows the shape of the bluff, so it does not form a neat square. Instead, it narrows toward the point where the cliff drops to the rivers. Soldiers on the walls could watch both valleys at once. The builders used the ground itself as part of the defence.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Round Tower, with its thick stone walls and narrow openings, is often described as the oldest standing building in Minnesota. Most of what you see around you was rebuilt in the twentieth century, but the tower survived from the original fort. When you touch its stone, you are touching something that stood here before Minnesota was even a state.

And that rebuilding is itself part of the story. By the mid-twentieth century, much of the old fort had crumbled or been taken down. A highway was even planned through the area. Local people campaigned to save it, and the Minnesota Historical Society led a long reconstruction based on archaeology and old records. So the walls, barracks, and storerooms you walk past are careful reconstructions — a historical argument made in stone.

Now for a story that surprises many people. In the 1830s, an enslaved couple named Dred Scott and Harriet Robinson Scott lived at this fort. Slavery was banned in this territory, yet army officers brought enslaved people here anyway. Years later, the Scotts sued for their freedom, partly because they had lived on free soil. Their case reached the United States Supreme Court in the 1850s. The court ruled against them, and that decision pushed the country closer to civil war.

The fort holds darker memories too. After the US–Dakota War of 1862, roughly 1,600 Dakota people, mostly women, children, and elders, were held in a camp on the river flats below this bluff. They spent a hard winter there in crowded, cold conditions. Hundreds are believed to have died from disease and hunger. In the spring, the survivors were forced out of Minnesota. For many Dakota families, this ground carries deep grief.

The fort kept changing with the country. During the Civil War, thousands of Minnesota soldiers trained here before heading south. Later, in the Second World War, Fort Snelling became a major induction centre, where many young men began their military service. The post was finally decommissioned in the mid-1940s. Its role as an army base ended, but its role as a place of memory was just beginning.

One more surprise from that war. During the Second World War, a military language school moved to Fort Snelling. Its students were mostly Japanese Americans, trained to read and translate Japanese for the army. Some of them had families held in American internment camps at the very same time. Their work in the Pacific was considered extremely valuable. It is a quiet, complicated story that many visitors never hear.

As you walk through the reconstructed buildings, notice how small and plain the rooms are. Barracks held rows of simple beds. The officers' quarters are a little more comfortable, with fireplaces and more space. The storerooms and the sutler's store — the shop where soldiers bought goods — show what daily life required out here. The difference between ranks shows in the size of a room and the quality of a window.

In recent years, the site has been reshaped to tell more voices at once. Exhibits and signs now include Dakota perspectives, the stories of enslaved people, soldiers, and immigrants who passed through. Some visitors find this uncomfortable, and some find it honest. Either way, the fort no longer tells one heroic frontier tale. It asks you to hold several truths in the same place.

Before you leave, walk back toward the edge of the bluff. Look down again at the meeting of the two rivers, where the water changes colour as the currents mix. Listen for the birds along the wooded banks, and for the steady sound of the city around you. The stone walls behind you are about two hundred years old. The rivers in front of you are far older. Standing between them, you are part of the long line of people who came to this point to look, and to remember.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk to the bluff edge first to see the river confluence.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and uneven stone floors.
Bring a layer; wind on the open bluff can feel cold.
Read the Dakota perspective exhibits before touring the barracks.
Visit the Round Tower early before groups gather inside.
