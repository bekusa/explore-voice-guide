The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath your feet, the wind moves through the grass of the clifftop. Far below, the sea pushes into the rocks with a deep, hollow thump. Gulls call somewhere out of sight. The air tastes of salt, and on most days it carries a damp chill, even in summer. Ahead of you, a narrow line of planks and cable crosses open air to a small island of dark rock. This is Carrick-a-Rede, on the north coast of County Antrim.

The name comes from the Irish language. It is usually translated as the rock in the road. But the road here was never a road for people. It was a road for fish. Every summer, Atlantic salmon swam along this coast on their way back to the rivers where they were born. The small island sat directly in their path. For fishermen, that made this lump of rock very valuable indeed.

So the bridge began as a working tool, not an attraction. Fishermen strung a rope crossing to the island so they could reach the best spot to set their nets. They worked here for centuries, through generations of the same local families. The early bridges were simple and frightening. Accounts describe a single handrail, widely spaced slats, and plenty of room to look straight down. These men carried nets, ropes and catches across it as part of an ordinary working day.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The bridge was not a permanent structure. The fishermen took it down at the end of each salmon season and put it up again the following spring. Winter storms on this coast are fierce, and a rope bridge left hanging through them would not last. So for most of its history, this crossing existed only in the warm months, when the salmon were running.

The salmon, sadly, did not keep running. Over the twentieth century, catches along this coast fell sharply. Fewer fish came back each year, and eventually the fishery here came to an end. The bridge outlived the trade that created it. Today, the National Trust looks after the site, and the crossing you see is a modern one. It uses steel cables and solid timber, and it is inspected carefully. Still, it moves.

The bridge runs for roughly twenty metres. It hangs about thirty metres above the water. Those numbers sound modest on paper. Standing at the edge, they feel very different. The gap is a deep, narrow channel, and the sea inside it is often a startling colour. On bright days it turns clear turquoise over pale rock. On grey days it becomes a dark, heavy green, with white foam working at the base of the cliffs.

Now look at the rock itself. This coastline was shaped by volcanic activity tens of millions of years ago. The island of Carrick-a-Rede is the remains of an old volcanic vent, a place where molten rock once rose to the surface. Wind and sea have worn away the softer material around it. What is left stands apart from the mainland, black and rough, with grass and sea pinks clinging to its top.

As you wait your turn to cross, watch the people ahead of you. Almost everyone does the same thing. They grip both handrails. They look down once, then look firmly at the far side. Some laugh nervously. A few stop in the middle to take a photograph. The bridge has a gentle sway, and it bounces slightly with each step. That movement is normal. It is part of the experience, and staff manage how many people cross at once.

On the island, the world opens up. The wind is usually stronger here, so hold on to your hat. Turn and look north. The large island close by is Rathlin, Northern Ireland's only inhabited offshore island. On a clear day, you can see further still. The low shapes on the horizon belong to Scotland. That short stretch of water has carried people, trade and stories between the two lands for thousands of years.

The cliffs around you are also a home for seabirds. In spring and early summer, you may see fulmars gliding stiffly along the cliff face, almost without moving their wings. Kittiwakes, razorbills and guillemots also use this coast. Listen for the noise of them on the ledges below. If you look down carefully, you may notice white streaks on the rock. Those mark the places where birds return again and again to nest.

There is one more reminder of the fishing years. On the island and around the coast, look for traces of the old fishery, including a small fisherman's hut. It is a plain, low building, made for shelter rather than comfort. Imagine spending a long day here, watching the water for salmon, with only this hut between you and an Atlantic squall. The view was the same. The life was much harder.

When you walk back along the cliff path, take a moment at the old quarry nearby, called Larrybane. Limestone was once cut from these white cliffs. The contrast is striking — pale chalky stone next to dark volcanic rock, side by side on the same coast. In recent years, this quarry has also appeared as a filming location in the television series Game of Thrones, so some visitors come here for that reason too.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the top of the path. Look back at the bridge, now small against the cliffs. Hear the sea again, steady and patient, moving in and out of the channel. For centuries, people crossed here because they needed to. Today, people cross because they want to. As you walk away, notice the wind on your face and the salt on your lips — the same things the fishermen felt every summer morning.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a timed crossing slot ahead, especially in summer.
Check the weather; strong winds can close the bridge.
Wear sturdy shoes for the clifftop path and steps.
Bring a windproof layer, even on sunny days.
Arrive early in the day to avoid long queues.
