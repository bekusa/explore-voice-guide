The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the wide square. Underfoot, the paving stones hold the day's heat, and the dry air carries a faint taste of dust from the desert beyond the city. In front of you rises a long wall of pale yellow brick, cut through by a tall arched gateway. On either side, a slender minaret climbs toward a sky that, on most days in Kashgar, is a hard and cloudless blue.

This is the Id Kah Mosque. It is often described as the largest mosque in China, and for many centuries it has been the spiritual centre of this oasis city. Kashgar sits at the far western edge of China, close to the borders of Central Asia. For travellers on the old Silk Road, this was a place to rest, trade, and pray before or after crossing some of the hardest terrain on Earth.

The name gives you a clue to the building's purpose. Id Kah comes from words that mean, roughly, the place of the festival. In the local Uyghur language, the mosque is called Heytgah. Uyghurs are the Turkic-speaking Muslim people who have long made up most of the population of this region. Here, the great festivals of the Islamic year, especially Eid, were marked with mass prayers that spilled out of the courtyard and onto the square.

The mosque is traditionally dated to the 1440s. A local ruler is usually credited with the founding, although some accounts say a smaller prayer site stood here even earlier. What you see today is not a single moment in time. The complex has been extended, rebuilt, and restored many times over the centuries. Think of it less as one building and more as a long conversation between generations of builders.

Look closely at the gateway. The facade is made of brick, finished in that warm yellow tone, with white trim that outlines the arch and the panels around it. The design is simple compared with the tiled mosques of Samarkand or Isfahan. There are no great blue domes here. Instead, the beauty comes from proportion — the balance of the tall arch between its two towers, and the clean lines against the sky.

The minarets are worth a second look. They are not as tall as minarets in Istanbul or Cairo, and they do not dominate the skyline. Their slim shape and small lantern-like tops are typical of the Central Asian style found across this region. In older times, a call to prayer from these towers would have carried across the flat rooftops of the old city, over courtyards, markets, and narrow lanes.

Now step through the gate. Many first-time visitors are surprised here. The outside wall suggests a grand interior hall, but instead you enter a large, open courtyard. Trees give shade, paths cross the ground, and the noise of the square drops away quickly. The courtyard is the real heart of the mosque. On festival days, it was the space that allowed thousands of people to pray together under the open sky.

At the far end of the courtyard stands the main prayer hall. It is long and shallow rather than deep, with an open front facing the yard. Its roof rests on rows of slender wooden columns, painted green and carved near the top. The ceiling above carries painted decoration in soft colours and floral patterns. This open, columned design suits the climate — shade from the summer sun, but air that can move freely.

Here is a second detail that many people miss. The prayer hall is built mostly of wood, brick, and plaster, materials that need constant care in this dry, dusty climate. The fresh paint you may notice on the columns and ceiling is part of a long cycle of repair. Each restoration renews the colours, so the hall can look newer than its long story suggests. The design, though, follows a very old local tradition.

It is also honest to say that the mosque today is not quite the place it was a few decades ago. Reporters and visitors in recent years have noted far fewer worshippers than in the past. This reflects tight state controls on religious life across the region. You may see security checks, cameras, and a calm, ordered atmosphere. As a visitor, it helps to move quietly, and to remember that this is still a place of prayer for local people.

When you walk back out to the square, notice how the mosque connects to the city around it. The square is a meeting point. Families gather here in the evening, children run across the open paving, and older men sit on benches in the shade. The streets around the mosque lead into the old parts of Kashgar, where you can find bakeries, tea houses, and workshops making copper pots, hats, and musical instruments.

If you can, come back at a different time of day. In the late afternoon, the low sun turns the yellow brick a deeper, honey-gold colour, and the minarets throw long shadows across the square. Because Kashgar runs on Beijing time, even though it lies far to the west, the light here comes late. Sunset can arrive much later in the evening than you might expect, which gives you long, warm hours on the square.

Before you leave, stand once more in front of the gateway. Look up at the arch, then at the two minarets, and then at the sky above them. For hundreds of years, merchants, pilgrims, and ordinary families stood on this same ground and looked at this same view. The city around you has changed many times. As you walk away, listen to the sounds of the square — voices, footsteps, the bells of bicycles — and let this quiet yellow gateway stay with you.

Practical tips

Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered.
Carry your passport for frequent security checks nearby.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers.
Ask permission before photographing worshippers or local residents.
