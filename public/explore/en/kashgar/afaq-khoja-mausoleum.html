The guide

The story

Listen first. Poplar leaves move overhead, and the sound is thin and dry, like paper. Underfoot, the path is smooth and dusty. Ahead of you, a broad tiled facade catches the light. Its surface is a patchwork of green, with touches of blue, yellow and white. Above it sits a wide, rounded dome. This is the Afaq Khoja Mausoleum, on the edge of Kashgar. It is a family tomb, a place of prayer, and a place of stories.

Kashgar sits at the far western edge of China, close to the mountains that divide Central Asia. For centuries, caravans on the Silk Road passed through this oasis. They brought silk and spices, and they also brought ideas, languages and faiths. Islam arrived here many centuries ago and shaped the city deeply. The mausoleum belongs to that long history. Its tiles, arches and minarets look west, toward Persia and Central Asia, more than east.

The mausoleum is linked to a family of religious leaders known as the Khojas. They were Sufi masters. Sufism is the mystical tradition within Islam, focused on a personal, inward path toward God. The family followed the Naqshbandi order, one of the most influential Sufi brotherhoods in Central Asia. The tomb was first built in the seventeenth century for a respected teacher named Muhammad Yusuf. Later, his son became even more famous. That son was Afaq Khoja, and the building now carries his name.

Afaq Khoja was more than a spiritual guide. In the late seventeenth century, he also held real political power in the region around Kashgar. His followers became known as the White Mountain faction, and they were rivals of another Khoja group. These rivalries shaped the politics of the oasis cities for generations. For many local Uyghurs, Afaq Khoja is still remembered as a holy man. People have come here to pray and pay respects for a very long time.

Now look carefully at the main building. It is almost square, with a minaret at each of the four corners. The minarets are slender towers, and they are also covered in glazed tiles. Notice that the tiles are not perfectly uniform. Shades of green change from panel to panel. Some areas look newer than others. This shows centuries of repair. The building has been restored more than once, and each repair added its own colour to the surface.

The dome is the crown of the whole complex. It is wide and fairly low, and it is covered in green tiles. In Islamic tradition, green is often linked with paradise and with the Prophet. Look at how the dome sits on the square base below. The builders had to solve a classic problem — how to put a round roof on a square room. Inside, you can see how the walls step and curve to carry the weight upward.

Step inside, and the temperature drops. The light becomes soft and indirect. In the centre of the hall, you will see a raised platform crowded with tombs. They are covered in tiles or draped in cloth. There are dozens of them, belonging to members of the family across several generations. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The tombs are different sizes. The smallest ones belong to children. It is a quiet reminder that this is, above all, a family grave.

Now for the story most people have heard before they arrive. Many visitors know this place as the Tomb of the Fragrant Concubine. According to a popular legend, a young woman from this family was taken to the imperial court in Beijing. She was said to carry a natural, sweet fragrance. In the tale, the emperor adored her. Locals sometimes call her Iparhan. The story says that after her death, her body was carried all the way back home to Kashgar.

Here is the second surprise. Historians generally agree that the real imperial consort connected to this story is buried far away, in the Eastern Qing Tombs near Beijing. So the legend and the historical record do not match. Still, the story has power. It appears in plays, paintings and songs. Inside the complex, you may see a wooden sedan chair. Guides often say it carried her body home. Treat that as part of the legend, not as documented fact.

Beyond the main tomb, the complex has more to explore. There are prayer halls with carved and painted wooden columns. Look up at the ceilings, where geometric patterns repeat in careful rhythm. Islamic decoration often avoids images of people. Instead, it uses geometry, plants and calligraphy. The repeating patterns suggest something endless and ordered. Spend a moment with one column. Follow the carving from the base to the top, and notice how many small, separate cuts it took.

Around the buildings, there is a cemetery. Simple earthen graves stretch out in rows, the colour of the dry ground. They are very different from the bright tiles of the mausoleum. This contrast is worth noticing. The famous family rests under glazed domes. Ordinary people rest close by, under mud and brick. Being buried near a holy person was considered a blessing. Walk slowly here, keep your voice low, and remember that these are real graves, not decoration.

The garden offers a pause. Trees give shade, and in summer the air smells of dust and leaves. Kashgar is a desert oasis, so shade and water have always mattered deeply. A garden near a tomb also echoes an old idea in Islamic culture. Paradise is often imagined as a garden. Sit for a few minutes if you can. Watch how the light moves across the tiled facade as clouds pass. The colours change surprisingly quickly.

As you prepare to leave, turn back once more toward the main building. Look at the four minarets and the green dome against the sky. Think about the layers you have seen today. A Sufi family. A rivalry for power. A legend about a fragrant young woman. Repair after repair, tile after tile. Then listen again. The poplars are still moving overhead. The dry leaves still sound like paper, just as they did when you arrived.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered.
Keep voices low when walking through the cemetery.
Ask before photographing people who are praying.
Visit early morning for softer light on the tiles.
Bring water and sun protection for the open grounds.
