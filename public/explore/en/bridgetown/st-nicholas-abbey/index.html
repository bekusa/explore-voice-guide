The guide

The story

A long avenue of mahogany trees closes over the road like a green tunnel. At its end, white curved gables rise against a bright sky. The air feels cooler here, in the high north of Bridgetown. You have arrived at St. Nicholas Abbey, one of the oldest houses in the Americas.

The house was built around 1658, less than forty years after English settlers first landed on this island. It is a Jacobean mansion, a style named for King James the First of England. Only three houses of this kind survive in the whole Western Hemisphere. Two stand in Bridgetown. The third, Bacon's Castle, stands far away in Virginia.

Despite the name, no monks ever lived here. The house was never an abbey. It began as a sugar planter's home, and the name St. Nicholas appears only in later records. Where it came from is still a puzzle. Even the owners will tell you that nobody knows for certain.

Look up at the roofline as you approach. Three curved gables, in the Dutch fashion, crown the front of the house. At the corners sit chimneys. Chimneys, on a tropical island. The house was built from plans drawn in England, where every fine home needed its fireplaces. Here, no fire was ever needed for warmth.

The first owner was Colonel Benjamin Berringer. His neighbor was John Yeamans, and the story of these two men is a dark one. The records say Yeamans grew close to Berringer's wife, Margaret. Berringer died suddenly, and people whispered of poison. Yeamans married the widow and claimed the estate. The courts later took it back and returned it to Berringer's children. True in every detail or not, the tale has clung to this house for more than three centuries.

For more than three hundred years, this was a working sugar estate. That work rested on the labor of enslaved African men, women, and children, who planted the cane, cut it, and fed the boiling house. Their names rarely appear in the estate books. An honest telling of this place must hold their memory too. Slavery ended in Bridgetown in the 1830s. The estate went on growing sugar for another hundred years and more.

Step inside, and the rooms are cool and dim. Cedar panels line the walls. The furniture is old, worn smooth by generations of hands. One treasure is a home movie from 1935, filmed by a member of the Cave family, who owned the estate for nearly two hundred years. The flickering black-and-white film shows cane fields, workers, and the harbor at Bridgetown as they looked ninety years ago. It plays for visitors in a small theater room.

By the early 2000s, the great house needed rescue. In 2006, a Barbadian architect named Larry Warren bought the estate. He restored the buildings and set out to make the old plantation pay for its own survival. It became a family project, run together with his wife and their sons.

Their answer was rum. Cane from the estate is crushed in a working mill, and the fresh juice is fermented on site. A copper pot still turns it into clear spirit, drop by drop. The rum then sleeps in oak barrels inside the old sugar works. Production is small and slow, measured in barrels, not tankers. You can watch each step, then taste the result.

Around the yard stand the older bones of the estate. There is a boiling house, a curing house, and the remains of the machinery that once crushed cane in far greater volume. Steam power arrived in the nineteenth century and replaced the windmills. Rust and repair sit side by side here, and both tell the truth.

In 2019, the estate opened a railway. A narrow line loops out through the fields, pulled by a small steam locomotive. The ride climbs gently to Cherry Tree Hill, one of the highest points in the north of the island. From the open carriages, you look across the Scotland District, a wild green landscape that falls away toward the Atlantic coast.

Give yourself time for the grounds as well. Paths lead through gardens and into a shaded gully, where green monkeys sometimes move through the branches. Herbs and orchids grow near the house. The avenue of mahogany trees you passed on arrival was planted long ago to announce this place. It still does its job.

Before you leave, stand once more before the gables. Houses like this were built to display wealth, and that wealth came from sugar, and from the people forced to make it. The house has outlived them all, planter and enslaved alike. What remains now is something quieter. A place to walk slowly, to remember, and to listen.
