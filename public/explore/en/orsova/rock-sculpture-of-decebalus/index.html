The guide

The story

Listen first. The Danube here does not rush. It slides past the limestone in wide, green, silent folds, and the air carries the cool smell of wet rock. Then you look up at the cliff on the Romanian bank, and a face looks back at you. It is huge, bearded, and stern. It is carved straight out of the mountain.

This is the rock sculpture of Decebalus, cut into a cliff face a short distance upstream from Orșova. It is usually described as about fifty-five metres tall. That makes it the tallest rock relief in Europe. Its scale is hard to judge from the water, because there is nothing beside it for comparison. A single eye is larger than a person.

So who is this man? Decebalus was the last king of Dacia. Dacia was an ancient kingdom that covered much of what is now Romania. At the end of the first century and the start of the second century, he was the most dangerous enemy of Rome on the Danube frontier. He fought the emperor Domitian. He then fought Trajan, one of the most capable soldiers Rome ever produced.

Trajan fought two wars against Dacia. The first ended in 102 with a peace that neither side trusted. The second ended in 106, when Roman armies took the Dacian capital. Ancient sources say that Decebalus took his own life rather than be led through Rome as a prisoner. Dacia became a Roman province. Romanians often count that meeting of Dacians and Romans as the beginning of their own story.

Now here is something many visitors do not realise. Despite its ancient subject, this sculpture is very recent. Work began in the 1990s and was finished in the early 2000s. It took roughly a decade. Teams of climbers and stone workers hung on ropes against the cliff and worked with drills and controlled blasting, shaping the face section by section.

The project came from Iosif Constantin Drăgan, a Romanian businessman who made his fortune abroad. He was also a passionate, and often debated, promoter of theories about the Dacian past. He paid for the work himself. Look below the face, and you will find a plaque cut into the rock. It reads in Latin: Decebalus Rex, Dragan Fecit. That means: King Decebalus, made by Drăgan.

That inscription surprises people. Ancient emperors put their names on monuments, and here a modern patron did the same. Some visitors find it bold. Others find it a little vain. Either way, it ties the sculpture to the long Roman habit of writing a name into stone. And that habit has a famous example right here — just across the water.

Turn and look toward the far bank. That is Serbia. On the Serbian side of the gorge, a Roman plaque called the Tabula Traiana is fixed into the cliff. It commemorates the road that Trajan's engineers cut along the river, ahead of his campaigns. So the Roman emperor and the Dacian king face each other across the Danube. One is carved in the second century, the other in our own time.

The setting itself is part of the story. You are in the Iron Gates, a long gorge where the Danube pushes between the Carpathian Mountains and the Balkan Mountains. This stretch is known as the Cauldrons, because the water once churned in deep whirlpools here. Today the area is protected as a natural park. Its cliffs, forests and caves shelter rare plants and many birds of prey.

The river looks different from the one the Romans saw. In the early 1970s, Romania and Yugoslavia completed a huge hydroelectric dam downstream, at the Iron Gates. The water rose by many metres. Old villages, roads and islands disappeared under the lake. Even Orșova's old town was flooded, and the town was rebuilt on higher ground. So when you look at the waterline, remember that history lies beneath it as well.

Close by, at the mouth of a small valley, you will see the white walls of the Mraconia Monastery. An older monastery once stood lower down, and the rising waters of the dam lake covered it. The present church was built in more recent years, on the bank above. Its bells and the stone king share this same quiet bend of the river.

Most people see the sculpture from a boat, and that is the best view. Boats leave from Orșova and from the nearby villages along the shore. From the water, the face rises straight above you, and the cliff looks almost vertical. You can also glimpse it from the road that runs along the Romanian bank. But a road view is brief, and the angle is much flatter.

Take a moment with the stone itself. The face is not smooth and polished. You can see drill lines, rough patches and changes in colour where the limestone was cut. Water and weather have already begun to mark it, with dark streaks and small plants in the cracks. In the rough texture, the work of human hands stays visible.

As you move on, watch the king slowly turn back into a cliff. From a distance, the face disappears into the grey rock and the green trees. The river carries on, quiet and deep, past the Roman plaque on one bank and the Dacian king on the other. Listen once more to the water against the hull, and notice how small your boat looks below him.

Practical tips

Bring a zoom lens to catch the carved inscription.
