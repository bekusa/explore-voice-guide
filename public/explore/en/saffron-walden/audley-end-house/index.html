The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your feet, rooks call from the tall trees, and somewhere nearby water moves slowly over stone. In front of you, a long front of pale stone rises from the lawns, its rows of windows catching the light. The grass runs down to a quiet river. This is Audley End, just outside the market town of Saffron Walden in Essex. What you see today is large. What stood here four centuries ago was far larger.

Before the great house, there was a monastery. This ground once belonged to Walden Abbey, a community of Benedictine monks, members of a Christian order that lived by prayer and work. In the sixteenth century, King Henry the Eighth broke with Rome and closed the monasteries across England. The abbey and its lands went to Thomas Audley, the King's Lord Chancellor. He turned the monastic buildings into a private home. His name stayed with the place, and it still does.

The house in front of you belongs to the next chapter. In the early seventeenth century, Audley's grandson, Thomas Howard, rebuilt the site on a huge scale. Howard was the first Earl of Suffolk, and he became Lord Treasurer to King James the First. He wanted a house that could receive a royal court. Historians call buildings like this prodigy houses — vast, showy homes built by courtiers to impress the monarch. The style is called Jacobean, after the Latin form of the King's name, James.

A story is often told about a royal visit here. King James is said to have looked at the house and remarked that it was too big for a king, but might do for a Lord Treasurer. Whether he really said it, nobody can be certain. The joke had a sharp edge, though. Howard's money was soon questioned. He was later tried for embezzlement, fined heavily, and imprisoned for a time. The house had cost far more than he could honestly afford.

Here is a surprise many visitors miss. For a period in the later seventeenth century, Audley End was a royal palace. King Charles the Second bought it from the Suffolk family. It suited him well. The racecourse at Newmarket is not far away, and the King loved the races. The Crown never fully paid for the house, however. Around the start of the eighteenth century, the property went back to the Suffolk family, along with a large problem — the upkeep.

So the house began to shrink. Over the eighteenth century, large parts were pulled down. Whole courtyards and wings disappeared. What remains is only a fraction of the Jacobean palace. As you walk around the outside, try to picture more buildings running out across the lawns. The lines of the old house once enclosed open courts where carriages turned and servants hurried across the stone. Today the grass covers that ground.

The next great change came from Sir John Griffin Griffin, who later became Lord Braybrooke. Yes, his name really did repeat itself. From the 1760s, he set out to modernise the house. He brought in Robert Adam, one of the most fashionable architects of the age. Adam was known for elegant interiors with delicate plasterwork, soft colours, and motifs taken from ancient Rome. Several rooms inside still carry his work.

Outside, the park was reshaped by Lancelot Brown, the landscape designer everyone called Capability Brown. He liked open grass, clumps of trees, and water that looked natural, even when it was not. The river that runs below the house was widened and shaped to fit his picture. Look out across the park and you will see small classical buildings placed on the views. These are not homes. They are eye-catchers, designed to give the eye somewhere to rest.

Inside, the Great Hall is one of the oldest surviving spaces. It keeps a massive carved oak screen from the Jacobean house. The screen is dark, heavy, and covered in detail. Take a moment to stand beneath it. Then notice how the later owners mixed their own styles around it. The house is a conversation between centuries. Jacobean wood, Georgian plaster, and Victorian comforts all sit side by side.

The Braybrooke family left another mark, this time on English literature. In the early nineteenth century, the third Lord Braybrooke published the first edition of the diary of Samuel Pepys. Pepys was a naval official in London during the 1660s. He wrote about the Great Fire, the plague, and his own private life, often in a kind of shorthand code. Braybrooke's edition, published in 1825, brought the diary to the public for the first time.

For many visitors, the real heart of the house is downstairs. The Victorian service wing has been restored to show how the household actually ran. There is a kitchen with copper pans, a dairy, a laundry, and storerooms. In the 1880s, the cook here was a woman named Avis Crocombe. English Heritage, the charity that now cares for the house, has made her well known through a popular series of cooking videos. Her recipes come from a real notebook.

There is one more chapter, and it is a quiet one. During the Second World War, the house was used by the Special Operations Executive, the secret British organisation that trained agents to work behind enemy lines. Here, Polish soldiers trained for dangerous missions in occupied Europe. Many of them never came home. A memorial in the grounds honours them. It is easy to walk past. It is worth finding.

Beyond the house, look for the walled kitchen garden. It still grows fruit and vegetables using organic methods, and the long glasshouse runs along one wall. The stable block nearby is older than much of the house itself. And now, as you stand here, look back at that pale stone front once more. Monks, courtiers, a king, cooks, and secret soldiers have all crossed this gravel. As you walk away, listen again — the rooks, the river, and your own footsteps.
