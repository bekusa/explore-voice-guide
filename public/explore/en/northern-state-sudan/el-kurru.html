The guide

The story

Feel the sand shift under your shoes. It is fine and pale, and it runs down toward a line of date palms where the Nile flows out of sight. The air is dry enough to make your lips tighten. Around you, low mounds of rubble rise from the desert floor. At first they look like nothing much... just piles of broken stone. But you are standing in the first royal cemetery of the kings of Kush.

This is El-Kurru, in the Northern State of Sudan, a short drive from the town of Karima and the sandstone mountain of Jebel Barkal. Kush was an ancient African kingdom along the middle Nile. For centuries it traded with Egypt, fought Egypt, and borrowed from Egypt. Then, in the eighth century before Christ, its kings did something remarkable. They marched north and ruled Egypt itself.

Historians call these rulers the Twenty-fifth Dynasty, or the Kushite pharaohs. For a few generations, kings from this stretch of the Nile wore the double crown of Egypt. They ruled from Memphis and Thebes, far to the north. But when they died, many of them came home. Their bodies travelled back up the river, past the cataracts, to be buried here. El-Kurru was their ancestral ground, the place that tied them to their family line.

The oldest burials here are not pyramids at all. They are simple tumuli, which are round earth and stone mounds over a grave. These belonged to the ancestors of the later kings, local chiefs whose names are mostly lost. Over the generations, the graves became more formal. Square stone superstructures appeared, then true pyramids. So as you walk this ground, you are walking through the story of a family becoming a dynasty.

Here is the first surprise. The Kushite kings built pyramids long after the Egyptians had stopped. In Egypt, royal pyramid building had ended many centuries earlier. The Kushites revived the idea and made it their own. Their pyramids were smaller and steeper than the famous ones at Giza. Most of the pyramids at El-Kurru have collapsed into the mounds you see today. Stone robbers, wind, and time took them down.

Several famous names were buried in this cemetery. Piye, the king who led the conquest of Egypt, lies here. So do his successors Shabaka and Shebitku, and the last Kushite ruler of Egypt, Tanwetamani. One great king is missing. Taharqa, perhaps the best known of them all, chose a new burial ground at Nuri, across the river. After him, most kings were buried there, and El-Kurru grew quiet.

The cemetery was excavated in the years around 1918 and 1919 by the American archaeologist George Reisner. He worked for a joint expedition of Harvard University and the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. Many of the objects he found travelled to Boston. His records gave the world its first clear picture of these royal tombs. Much of what we know about the layout of this ground comes from his notes and plans.

Now the second surprise. Reisner also found graves for horses. The Kushite kings loved horses, and Egyptian texts describe Piye's deep concern for the animals of his enemies. Here at El-Kurru, rows of horses were buried near the royal tombs. According to the excavation reports, some were laid to rest with beads, amulets, and fine trappings. These were not work animals. They were companions chosen to follow their kings into the afterlife.

If the site guardian is present, you may be able to go underground. Two tombs here are usually shown to visitors. One belongs to Tanwetamani. The other belongs to Queen Qalhata, who is thought to be his mother. A steep stairway cut into the rock leads down into the cool dark. The temperature drops as you descend. The smell changes from dust to old stone.

At the bottom, colour waits for you. The walls of the burial chambers are painted with scenes in the Egyptian style. You see the king and queen among gods, figures in procession, and lines of hieroglyphic text. The paint has survived more than two and a half thousand years in the dry darkness. The reds, yellows, and blues still hold. Standing there, with the bright desert just above your head, the contrast is hard to forget.

Archaeology here did not end with Reisner. In recent years, a team led by the University of Michigan has returned to El-Kurru. They studied an unfinished rock-cut funerary temple and found many graffiti scratched into its walls. Some show boats, others show animals and people, made by visitors over many centuries. The team also traced the remains of a large ancient wall near the cemetery. These finds suggest that El-Kurru was more than a burial ground. People lived and gathered here too.

Look up from the tombs toward the village. The modern settlement of Kurru sits between the cemetery and the river. You will see mud-brick houses, painted doors, and goats picking through the shade. Palm groves and small fields follow the line of the water. Farmers here still lift water to their crops much as people have done along this river for thousands of years. The kings chose a living landscape for their dead.

The light at El-Kurru changes the place completely. At midday the sun flattens everything, and the mounds lose their shape. In the early morning and late afternoon, long shadows return. Then you can read the square outlines of fallen pyramids and the edges of old stone foundations. The desert turns from white to gold to soft pink. This is the best time to understand the ground as a planned royal cemetery.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind moving across the sand, and perhaps a donkey or a call from the village. Look at the nearest mound and picture it whole, sharp, and pale against the sky. Kings who once ruled from the Mediterranean to this desert were carried here to rest. As you walk back toward the palms, notice the stones under your feet. Some of them were once part of a pharaoh's pyramid.
