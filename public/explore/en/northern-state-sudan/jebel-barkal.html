The guide

The story

Start with the sand. It is fine, warm, and the colour of weak tea. It gathers in soft drifts against the base of the rock in front of you. Look up, and a flat-topped mountain of sandstone rises out of the desert plain. It stands alone, with no range around it. On a still morning the only sounds are the wind and, perhaps, a donkey somewhere toward the town of Karima. This is Jebel Barkal, and for more than three thousand years people have believed that a god lived inside it.

The mountain is not very tall. It is under one hundred metres high, a mesa, which means a hill with steep sides and a flat summit. Its power comes from its isolation and its position. The Nile runs close by, on a long bend through northern Sudan. For anyone travelling the river in ancient times, this dark block of stone appeared from far away. It marked the landscape the way a cathedral spire marks a European town. People could not miss it, and they gave it meaning.

Now walk a little way along the southern face and look for the pinnacle. A tall column of rock has partly separated from the cliff. From certain angles it looks like a rearing cobra. From others it looks like a standing figure wearing a tall crown. To the ancient Egyptians, the rearing cobra, called the uraeus, was the symbol of royal power. It sat on the forehead of every pharaoh. Here, it seemed, nature itself had carved the sign of kingship into a mountain.

Egyptian armies of the New Kingdom pushed south into this region, which they called Kush, in the fifteenth century before Christ. When they reached this mountain, they decided it was sacred. They called it the Pure Mountain. They believed it was a home of Amun, their great state god, in a southern form. In this belief, kingship itself came from inside the rock. A pharaoh who controlled this place could claim that the god had chosen him.

The Egyptians built a temple to Amun at the foot of the cliff. Over the centuries it grew larger, with new halls, courtyards, and gateways. Today you see mostly low walls, column bases, and scattered blocks of sandstone. But the plan is still easy to follow on the ground. Walk the long axis of the temple, and notice how it points toward the mountain. The building was designed to lead worshippers toward the god's home.

Here comes the first surprise. The story did not end when Egypt withdrew. In the centuries that followed, a local kingdom grew strong in Kush, with its capital at Napata, close to this mountain. In the eighth century before Christ, a Kushite king named Piye marched north and conquered Egypt. For several generations, kings from this part of Sudan ruled Egypt as pharaohs. Egyptian history calls them the Twenty-Fifth Dynasty. The southern land had taken control of its former master.

These Kushite kings did not see themselves as foreigners. They saw themselves as the true guardians of Amun's worship, perhaps more faithful than the Egyptians of their own day. Piye greatly enlarged the temple at Jebel Barkal. He set up a large stone monument here that recorded his campaign into Egypt. For these rulers, the mountain was proof of their right to rule. The god had given them the crown from inside this rock.

One of the most famous of these kings was Taharqa. He also appears in the Hebrew Bible, which is the Christian Old Testament. He commissioned a temple cut directly into the cliff. It was dedicated to the goddess Mut, and some of its columns were carved in the form of Hathor, a goddess with the ears of a cow. Step close to the rock face and look for the dark doorways. The rooms behind them were carved into the mountain itself, as if the builders wanted to enter the god's house.

The second surprise is at the top of the pinnacle. Archaeologists found that the Kushite kings had placed an inscription near the summit of that rock column. It was very high and hard to reach. Scholars believe the panel was once covered with precious metal, so it would catch the sun. Imagine the scene: a cobra of stone, crowned with a flash of light, visible from the river below. Today you will see only bare rock from the ground, but the idea alone changes how the pinnacle looks.

Now turn toward the west side of the mountain. There you will find a group of pyramids. They are steep, narrow, and much smaller than the famous pyramids of Giza in Egypt. Many of them have lost their tops, and the stone has weathered to a soft brown. Royal burials continued in this region over a long period. Other royal cemeteries are not far away, at El-Kurru and Nuri. Sudan, in fact, has more pyramids than Egypt does.

In 2003, UNESCO listed Jebel Barkal and the nearby sites of the Napatan region as a World Heritage Site. The listing covers the mountain, its temples, and several cemeteries along this stretch of the Nile. It recognises a civilisation that is often left out of the story of the ancient world. Kush was not simply a southern neighbour of Egypt. It was a powerful African kingdom with its own kings, its own gods, and its own long history.

For many visitors, the best moment comes late in the day. The sun drops toward the desert, and the sandstone turns orange, then red, then a deep brown. The pyramids throw long shadows across the sand. The heat eases, and the wind often picks up. If you are able to climb the gentle slope on the back of the mountain to the flat summit, you can look out over the river, the palm groves, and the town of Karima.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the foot of the cliff. Touch the sandstone if you can. It is rough and slightly warm, and it crumbles a little at the edges. Look once more at the pinnacle, the cobra, rising from the face of the rock. Kings from two great civilisations stood near this spot and believed a god was watching them. As you walk back across the sand, listen to the wind against the mountain. It is the same sound they heard.
