The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the water near Wadi Halfa and listen. There is wind across the lake, the slap of small waves on sand, and very little else. The light here is hard and white at midday, and the far shore shimmers. Somewhere beneath this wide, quiet surface lies one of the most carefully engineered fortresses of the ancient world. This is Buhen... or rather, this is where Buhen once stood.

That is the first surprise, and it is an honest one. The fortress of Buhen is no longer visible. In the 1960s, the Aswan High Dam was built downstream in Egypt. The river rose behind it and formed a vast reservoir. Egyptians call it Lake Nasser, and Sudanese call their part of it Lake Nubia. The mudbrick walls of Buhen went under that water. What you are looking at today is a landscape that swallowed its own history.

So why come here at all? Because for roughly four thousand years, this stretch of the Nile mattered enormously. Just upriver lies the Second Cataract, a long belt of rocks and rapids. Boats could not pass easily. Goods had to be unloaded, carried, and guarded. Whoever controlled this bottleneck controlled the flow of gold, ivory, ebony, and cattle from the south. The rulers of ancient Egypt understood this very well.

People were working here even before the great fortress existed. Excavators found traces of an earlier settlement from the Old Kingdom, the age of the great pyramids. Evidence suggests that copper was processed at this site. Think about that for a moment. Egyptian workers travelled far south, into Nubia, to smelt metal on the banks of this river. It is one of the earliest signs of Egypt reaching deep into lands beyond its southern border.

The fortress itself belongs to the Middle Kingdom, during the Twelfth Dynasty. In that period, Egyptian kings built a chain of strongholds along the Second Cataract. Their names may be unfamiliar — Mirgissa, Uronarti, Shalfak, Semna, Kumma. Buhen sat at the northern end of this chain. Together, these forts formed a frontier system. They watched the river, recorded traffic, and controlled who could move north into Egypt.

Now picture the walls. They were made of mudbrick, the same sun-dried material still used in villages along the Nile today. But do not imagine something fragile. The main walls were thick and tall, strengthened with timber, and lined with towers. Around them ran a deep, dry ditch cut into the rock. Beyond the ditch rose a sloping outer rampart. An attacker would have to cross open ground, descend into the ditch, and climb again — all under fire.

Here is the detail that surprises many visitors. The defences of Buhen look almost medieval. There were rounded bastions projecting from the walls. They had narrow openings, arranged in rows, so archers could shoot in several directions at once. The main gate was a heavy, fortified structure, with a bridge across the ditch that could be pulled back. Ideas that Europe would use thousands of years later were already working here, beside the Nile.

Inside the walls was not only a garrison. Buhen held streets, houses, storerooms, workshops, and administrative buildings. Officials lived here with their households. Scribes kept records of ships and cargo. Grain was stored for the soldiers. This was a small walled town, a piece of Egypt set down in Nubian territory. Life here would have mixed military routine with everyday tasks — baking bread, mending tools, writing reports for distant officials.

The fortress changed hands over the centuries. When central power in Egypt weakened, Buhen came under the influence of the Kingdom of Kerma, a powerful Nubian state based further south. Later, in the New Kingdom, Egyptian armies returned and pushed far beyond the cataracts. Buhen was rebuilt and used again. The frontier it once guarded had moved south, but the site kept its importance as a temple town and a centre of trade.

From that later period came a small temple dedicated to Horus of Buhen. Horus was the falcon-headed god, closely linked to Egyptian kingship. The temple was associated with Queen Hatshepsut and her successor Thutmose the Third, two of the best-known rulers of the New Kingdom. Its walls carried carved and painted scenes. Some of the colours survived for more than three thousand years in the dry desert air.

And here is the second surprise. That temple was not lost to the flood. Before the waters rose, an international rescue campaign, supported by UNESCO, worked across Nubia to save what it could. Archaeologists, led at Buhen by the British Egyptologist Walter Bryan Emery, excavated and recorded the fortress in great detail. The temple was taken apart, block by block, and moved. Today it stands in the grounds of the National Museum in Khartoum.

The excavations produced other finds that still spark debate. Among them was the skeleton of a horse. Some scholars have argued that it is one of the earliest horses known from the Nile Valley, while others question its exact date. It is a good reminder that archaeology is not always settled. Buhen still raises questions, even decades after the water covered it, and researchers continue to study the records Emery left behind.

It is worth thinking about what the rescue work achieved, and what it could not. The plans, photographs, and drawings of Buhen survive. The temple survives. But the walls themselves, the streets, the gates, and the long ditch could not be moved. They are now part of the lakebed. The flooding of Nubia also displaced many living communities, whose villages and fields lay along the old river. Their loss is part of this story too.

So take one more look across the water. The surface is calm, and the colours change as the sun moves — pale blue, silver, then gold in the evening. Somewhere out there, beneath the reflections, lie the foundations of a frontier town that guarded this river for centuries. As you walk away, notice the dry hills and the mudbrick houses nearby. They are built from the same earth that once made Buhen.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the National Museum in Khartoum to see the temple.
Come early morning or late afternoon to avoid harsh heat.
Carry plenty of water; shade along the shoreline is scarce.
Ask locally in Wadi Halfa about boat trips on the lake.
Read Emery's excavation plans beforehand to picture the walls.
