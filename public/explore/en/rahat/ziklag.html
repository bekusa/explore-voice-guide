The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. In this part of the northern Negev, the soil is loess: a fine, pale dust carried here by desert winds over thousands of years. When it is dry, it rises around your shoes like flour. After winter rain, it turns soft and dark, and the fields around Rahat turn green with wheat. This is the landscape where the name Ziklag belongs. It is open, low and wide, with a huge sky.

Ziklag is a name from the Hebrew Bible. It is a small town on the southern edge of the land, close to the border between the Philistine cities of the coast and the hill country of Judah. The town would be easy to forget. It was never a capital or a great fortress. But for a short and dramatic period, it became home to one of the most famous figures in the biblical story... a young fugitive named David.

Here is the story as the Book of Samuel tells it. David had fallen out with King Saul and was running for his life. In a bold move, he crossed over to the enemy. He offered his services to Achish, the Philistine king of Gath. Achish gave him Ziklag as a base. David settled here with his followers and their families. According to the text, he lived in Philistine territory for a year and four months.

Think about how strange that arrangement was. The future king of Israel lived under the protection of a Philistine ruler. From Ziklag, the story says, David raided desert tribes further south. He told Achish he was attacking Judah instead. It is a story of double loyalties and careful survival. This edge-of-the-map town suited it well. Ziklag was far enough from both powers to keep secrets.

Then comes the most vivid episode. David and his men marched north with the Philistine army, but the Philistine commanders did not trust him. They sent him back. When the men returned to Ziklag, they found it burned. Amalekite raiders had taken the women and children. The text describes the men weeping until they had no strength left. Some even spoke of stoning David. It is one of the rawest scenes in the whole account.

David's men chased the raiders into the desert. They found them celebrating and recovered every captive. On the way back, David made a rule about the spoils. Men who stayed behind to guard the supplies would receive the same share as those who fought. The text says this became a lasting custom. It is a small detail, but a human one. Fairness began in a burned town on the frontier.

Ziklag holds one more famous moment. Back in the ruined town, a messenger arrived with news from the north. Saul and his son Jonathan had died in battle on Mount Gilboa. Jonathan had been David's closest friend. Here, according to the Second Book of Samuel, David composed his lament for them. Many readers consider that lament one of the most moving poems in the Bible. Its first sorrow belongs to this dry southern country.

Now for a surprise most visitors do not expect. Nobody knows for certain where Ziklag was. The Bible lists the town among the settlements of Judah and of the tribe of Simeon. It also mentions people living there again after the return from exile. But the ancient name did not survive on any single mound. Archaeologists have proposed several candidates across the northern Negev and the southern coastal plain, and the debate continues.

One long-standing candidate is Tel Sera, an ancient mound beside the Gerar stream, not far from this area. Excavations there in the twentieth century uncovered layers from many periods. Another candidate came into the news more recently. In 2019, archaeologists working at a site near Kiryat Gat suggested it might be biblical Ziklag. Other scholars disagree. So when you hear the name Ziklag here, you are hearing a question as much as an answer.

That uncertainty is part of the appeal. Biblical geography in this region is detective work. Scholars compare ancient texts, pottery styles, burn layers and the distances between towns. A layer of ash might match a story of a fire. Or it might belong to a different fire entirely. Each new season of digging can shift the argument. The landscape around you is a working puzzle, not a finished museum label.

Look at the setting itself. The northern Negev sits between desert and farmland. To the south and east, the land gets drier. To the west, it opens toward the coastal plain and the sea. In biblical times, this was a meeting zone between peoples, trade routes and ways of life. Herders and farmers shared it, sometimes peacefully, sometimes not. You can still feel that in-between quality in the wide fields and dry streambeds.

Today, the city of Rahat gives this region its human texture. Rahat is the largest Bedouin city in Israel. Its neighbourhoods, mosques and markets sit close to the open country. Bedouin families have long connections to this land and its seasons. In the morning you might hear the call to prayer carry across the fields. Shepherds may still move flocks along the edges of the farmland. Ancient and modern life share the same horizon here.

Before you leave, pause and look around. Notice the colour of the soil and the line where the fields meet the sky. Notice how far you can see in every direction. A small band of fugitives once lived somewhere in country like this. They lost everything here and won it back. As you walk away, listen to the wind moving across the loess. It is the same wind that carried this soil here, long before any town had a name.

Practical tips

Bring water and sun protection for the open Negev landscape.
Read First Samuel chapters 27 and 30 before you visit.
Dress modestly when walking through Rahat's neighbourhoods.
Wear closed shoes; the loess dust gets everywhere.
