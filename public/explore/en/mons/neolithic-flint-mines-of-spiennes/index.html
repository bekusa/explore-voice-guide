The guide

The story

Look down at the ground near your feet. Among the grass and the turned earth, you may see small pale shards with sharp edges, grey and white, some glinting dully in the light. Pick one up and run a thumb along it. That edge was made by a human hand, thousands of years ago. You are standing on one of the oldest industrial sites in Europe, and it is still scattered across the fields.

This is Spiennes, a quiet village a short drive southeast of Mons, in the Belgian province of Hainaut. Above ground there is very little to see: gentle chalk hills, farmland, a few hedges, the valley of the small river Trouille. Below ground is another world. Under these fields lie thousands of mine shafts, dug by Neolithic farmers searching for one thing, flint. The site covers more than one hundred hectares, an area larger than many old town centres.

Why dig for a stone? To understand Spiennes, picture a world without metal. In the Neolithic, the New Stone Age, people were clearing forests, building houses and planting crops. For all of that they needed tools that could cut and chop. Flint was the answer. It is hard, it breaks in predictable ways, and a skilled worker can shape it into a blade sharper than many modern knives.

The chalk under Spiennes holds flint in layers, like raisins in a cake. The nodules near the surface were often cracked or poor in quality. The best flint lay deeper, in thick, clean seams. So the miners did something remarkable. Somewhere in the fifth millennium before Christ, they began to sink vertical shafts straight down through the chalk to reach those deep layers. Archaeologists date activity here across many centuries, roughly from around 4400 to around 2200 BC.

Think about what that means. The shafts could go down more than ten metres, and some reached even deeper. The miners had no metal picks, no pulleys of iron, no electric light. They worked with tools of antler and flint, and likely with wooden ladders, ropes and baskets. At the bottom of a shaft, they cut horizontal galleries outward, following the seam. They worked in near darkness, in chalk dust, on their knees.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The great flint blocks they freed underground were sometimes enormous, heavy slabs the miners could split right there in the gallery. And when one shaft was exhausted, they often filled it with the waste from the next one. Over generations, the hill became a honeycomb of filled and refilled pits. That is why the surface is covered in debris today. You are walking on the spoil heaps of an ancient workshop.

What did they make? Above all, axe heads. Workers on the surface roughed out the flint into axe shapes, striking it with hammer stones. The polishing that gave these axes their smooth finish often happened elsewhere, sometimes far away. Axes made from Spiennes flint travelled widely across the region. In a sense, this hillside was part of a trade network long before roads, coins or writing existed.

The modern story begins in the nineteenth century. In 1867, workers digging a railway cutting through the area sliced into the hidden shafts. Suddenly, the cross-sections of ancient pits appeared in the walls of the trench, filled with chalk and flint debris. Scholars quickly understood the importance of the find. Excavations continued on and off for more than a century, and research here is still ongoing.

In 2000, UNESCO added the Neolithic flint mines of Spiennes to the World Heritage List. The listing recognises them as the largest and earliest concentration of ancient mines in Europe. It also honours something less obvious: the range of technical solutions the miners invented. Different parts of the site show different ways of reaching the flint, from shallow pits to deep shafts. It is a record of human problem-solving, written in chalk.

Today, the main way to understand the site is through the interpretation centre on the plateau, known as SILEX'S. Silex is the French word for flint. The building is low and modern, designed to sit lightly on the land. Inside, displays explain the geology, the tools and the people. And here is the second surprise: with a booking and a guide, visitors can climb down into a real Neolithic shaft and stand in the galleries themselves.

Going underground changes everything. The air turns cool and still. The walls are bright white chalk, and in places you can see the dark bands of flint running through them. You may notice marks left by antler picks, scratches made by hands that stopped working thousands of years ago. The space is tight. It is easy to imagine the dust, the heat of bodies, and the dim glow of whatever light the miners carried.

Back on the surface, take a moment to read the landscape. The plateau is open and windy, with long views over farmland toward Mons. The fields still carry dips and bumps where shafts collapsed or were filled. After ploughing, the white flint fragments become much easier to see. Please leave them where they lie. The scatter itself is part of the protected heritage, and every piece helps researchers map the ancient activity.

It is worth remembering who these miners were. They were not slaves or specialists from some distant empire. They were members of early farming communities, people who also kept animals, grew grain and raised children. Mining was probably seasonal work, fitted around the farming year. Their village life left fewer traces than their mines. So, strangely, we know their tools and tunnels better than we know their homes.

Before you leave, look once more at the ground and the wide sky above it. Listen to the wind moving over the fields, the distant traffic, maybe a tractor somewhere on the slope. Beneath all of it, the galleries are still there, dark and silent. Six thousand years ago, someone stood very close to where you are now, holding a fresh piece of flint, testing its edge with a thumb, just as you might have done a few minutes ago.
