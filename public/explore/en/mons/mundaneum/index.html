The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Inside this building, the loudest sound is often paper. Drawers slide open on wooden runners. Cards brush against each other with a dry, soft whisper of their own. Under your feet the floor is smooth and quiet, and the light falls in long, even bands. It feels less like a museum and more like the inside of a very old, very patient machine. In a sense, that is exactly what it is.

Welcome to the Mundaneum, here in the Belgian city of Mons. The name sounds grand, and the idea behind it was grander still. Two men in late nineteenth century Brussels wanted to gather all human knowledge in one place. Not just books. Every fact, every article, every published idea, sorted and findable. They believed that if people could share knowledge freely, they might stop going to war. Hold on to that thought. It returns at the end.

The two men were Paul Otlet and Henri La Fontaine. Both were Belgian lawyers. Both were restless, idealistic, and a little obsessive. In 1895 they founded an International Institute of Bibliography in Brussels. Their tool was humble: the index card. One card for one piece of information. A title, an author, a subject, a reference. Then another card, and another, until the drawers filled entire rooms. Over the decades the collection grew into the millions of cards.

Here is the first surprise. To sort all those cards, Otlet and La Fontaine needed a system. They took the Dewey Decimal Classification, the number system many libraries used, and expanded it. Their version could link subjects together with symbols, almost like a grammar for knowledge. It became the Universal Decimal Classification. If you have ever seen a long string of numbers and colons on the spine of a European library book, you may have met their work without knowing it.

The collection was not just stored. It was used. People could send a question by post, and staff would search the card files and send back an answer. Think about that for a second. A question goes in, the system searches, a result comes back. Long before computers, this was a search service run by hand. Journalists in recent years have called the Mundaneum a paper Google. The comparison is not perfect, but it is not silly either.

Paul Otlet went further than anyone around him. In his writing, especially a large book on documentation published in the 1930s, he imagined people at home consulting knowledge from a distance. He described screens, linked documents, and a network connecting them. He did not have the technology. He had the idea. Historians of information now treat him as one of the early thinkers of what we would later call the internet.

Henri La Fontaine followed a different path through the same dream. He was a senator, a campaigner for women's rights, and a tireless worker for international peace. In 1913 he received the Nobel Peace Prize. For both men, organising knowledge was never a hobby for librarians. It was a political project. A world that could share information, they believed, could build shared institutions. They pictured a kind of world capital of knowledge and cooperation.

That picture once reached the drawing board of a famous architect. In the late 1920s Le Corbusier, the Swiss-French modernist, sketched plans for a world city built around the Mundaneum. There was even a stepped, spiralling museum in the design, a little like a ziggurat, the stepped temple-towers of ancient Mesopotamia. None of it was ever built. Look for those plans and models in the displays. They show how seriously some people once took this paper empire.

The twentieth century was not kind to the project. In Brussels the collection had space in a large government building, but it lost that space in the 1930s. During the Second World War, under German occupation, much of the material was moved, damaged, or lost. Paul Otlet died in 1944, with his dream in pieces. For decades afterwards, what survived sat in storage, half forgotten. The great index of the world became a pile of boxes.

The archive's rescue is the second surprise, and it is why you are standing in Mons and not in Brussels. In the 1990s the surviving collection came here, to this city in the province of Hainaut. It found a home in a former department store from the interwar years, with clean Art Deco lines. Art Deco is the style of the 1920s and 1930s, with crisp geometry and elegant ornament. A building made for selling things now keeps a dream about sharing things.

Today the Mundaneum works both as an archive and as an exhibition space. Researchers come to study the collections, which include far more than the famous cards. There are posters, photographs, pamphlets, and records of the peace, feminist, and anarchist movements of the period. In 2013 the archives were added to UNESCO's Memory of the World register. Interest also came from the technology world, and the internet age has looked back at Otlet with real curiosity.

As you move through the rooms, notice the furniture itself. The long cabinets of small drawers are the heart of the place. Each drawer is shallow, labelled, numbered, and made to be pulled out by hand. Imagine the staff who filed every card by hand, day after day, year after year. Mons itself has a long relationship with culture, and in 2015 it was a European Capital of Culture. The Mundaneum was one of the places that drew new attention then.

Before you leave, stand in front of one of those cabinets and simply look. Millions of small decisions sit inside it: where a fact belongs, what it connects to, how someone might find it later. Every time you type a question into your phone, you are using the same idea, only faster. When you step back outside into the streets of Mons, listen again. The traffic and the voices are louder now. But somewhere behind you, the drawers are still quiet, still waiting for a question.
