The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the gate, the noise of the Seville streets drops away. In its place comes the soft trickle of a fountain, the scrape of shoes on old marble, and the cool air that stone walls hold even in high summer. Look around, and almost every surface carries colour. There are glazed tiles in green, honey and deep blue, plaster carved as fine as lace, and pale statues watching from the corners. Welcome to Casa de Pilatos.

This is one of the great private palaces of Seville, and it is still a family house at heart. It belongs to the ducal house of Medinaceli, one of the oldest noble lines in Spain. The building grew over several generations, so you will not find one single plan here. Instead, you will find a conversation between styles. Each owner added what was fashionable, and nobody tore the earlier work down.

The story begins in the late fifteenth century. A powerful Andalusian couple, Pedro Enríquez and Catalina de Ribera, began buying land in this part of the city. They started a house that would become the family seat. Catalina de Ribera is remembered in Seville as a strong and generous figure in her own right. She is also linked to the founding of a hospital in the city. Her family name, Ribera, appears again and again in the history of this palace.

Now for the name. Why is a Seville palace called the House of Pilate? Their son, Fadrique Enríquez de Ribera, made a long journey to Jerusalem and the Holy Land in the early sixteenth century. When he came home, he started a Way of the Cross. It is a devotional route of prayer stations that ran from this house out to a cross beyond the city walls. Over time, people began to call his home the House of Pilate.

Here we must be careful. Locals say the palace was built as a copy of Pontius Pilate's house in Jerusalem. That is a popular tale, not documented history. Historians see the name as coming from the Way of the Cross, because the route began here, just as the Passion story begins at Pilate's court. So enjoy the legend... but know that the real inspiration was a journey, not a blueprint.

Let us step into the main courtyard. This is the heart of the house. You see light, slender arches resting on white marble columns. Around them, the walls are covered with tiles to shoulder height and above. Higher still is delicate carved plasterwork. This is the Mudéjar style. It is the art of Muslim craftsmen and their heirs working in Christian Spain. It uses geometric patterns, interlacing stars and repeating motifs that seem to have no end.

Now look at the Gothic touches in the balustrades above you. Then look at the Renaissance fountain in the centre. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. These three traditions — Islamic, Gothic and Italian Renaissance — sit side by side in one space, and they do not fight. That mix is the signature of Seville in this period. It was a city between worlds, rich from trade, and open to craftsmen from many backgrounds.

The tiles deserve a slow look. Spanish speakers call them azulejos. Casa de Pilatos holds one of the largest collections of historic tilework in any house in Spain. Many were made in the workshops of Seville, especially across the river in the Triana district, which was known for its potters. Walk close and follow a single pattern with your eyes. Notice how slightly uneven the glaze is. Each tile was shaped, painted and fired by hand.

Now turn to the statues in the corners of the courtyard and the busts set into the walls. These are classical figures, and many are genuine Roman works. A later member of the family, Per Afán de Ribera, served as Viceroy of Naples in the sixteenth century. In Italy, he collected ancient sculpture and shipped much of it back to Seville. The tall goddess figure is Athena, or Pallas, the Greek goddess of wisdom. The busts in the niches show Roman emperors and famous men.

This is the second surprise. For a long time, this house was not only a home but also an early museum of antiquity. Educated visitors came to see Roman art here when such collections were rare in Spain. The Italian influence is everywhere once you notice it. Even the marble gateway on the street side was carved in Genoa, Italy, and brought here by sea. It is one of the earliest Renaissance doorways in Seville.

Upstairs, if you join the guided visit of the upper rooms, look at the ceilings. One room has ceiling paintings by Francisco Pacheco from the early seventeenth century. Pacheco was a respected Seville painter and writer on art. He was also the teacher, and later the father-in-law, of Diego Velázquez, the great painter of the Spanish court. His ceiling shows scenes from classical myth, including the hero Hercules raised up to the heavens.

Before you leave, find the gardens. There is a larger garden and a smaller one, with paths, low hedges, palms and climbing plants against the walls. Here the palace feels quieter and more personal. You can hear birds, water, and sometimes the distant bells of the city. Film crews have also found this place. Scenes for films such as Lawrence of Arabia and Kingdom of Heaven were shot here, because the house can stand in for many lands and centuries.

So take one last moment in the courtyard. Look up at the open square of sky above the arches. Then look down at the worn marble under your feet, polished by more than five hundred years of footsteps. When you walk out through the Genoese gate into the small square outside, turn back once. From the street, the house looks calm and modest. Now you know how much colour, travel and memory it keeps behind that door.
