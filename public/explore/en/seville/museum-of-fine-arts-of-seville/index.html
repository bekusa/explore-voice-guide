The guide

The story

Before you see a single painting, listen to the water. Somewhere inside these walls a fountain runs into a stone basin. The sound carries through quiet arcades, and the air is cooler here than in the street outside. Underfoot, smooth marble gives way to old brick. Overhead, the light comes in soft and filtered, already a little golden. This is the Museum of Fine Arts of Seville. Long before it held art, this building was a place of prayer.

The museum lives inside a former convent. It belonged to the Order of Mercy, a Catholic religious order known as the Mercedarians. They were founded in the Middle Ages with one unusual mission: to ransom Christian captives held across the Mediterranean. Their Seville house grew over the centuries. In the early seventeenth century it was largely rebuilt, and the architect Juan de Oviedo is usually credited with that work. What you walk through today is mostly his convent, softened by four centuries of Andalusian sun.

So how did a convent become a museum? The answer is a political decision in Madrid. In the 1830s, the Spanish government seized large amounts of church property, a process known as the disentailment. Monasteries and convents across the country were closed. Their paintings, altarpieces, and sculptures suddenly had no home. In Seville, much of that art was gathered here. A religious house became a public gallery... and the city's sacred art passed into the hands of its citizens.

Take a moment in the cloisters before you hurry inside. There are three of them, each with its own character. Look at the walls. Panels of glazed ceramic tiles, called azulejos, line many of the passages. Their blues, yellows, and greens are typical of Seville, a city with a long tradition of tile making. Plants and orange trees soften the courtyards. The arches create a slow rhythm of light and shadow as you walk. This rhythm was designed for monks who walked here in silence.

Now climb the main staircase, and look up. The ceiling above the stairs is richly decorated with carved and painted ornament. Many visitors miss it completely because their eyes are already searching for the galleries. Pause on the landing. From here, you can sense the scale of the old convent. It was a wealthy community, and it wanted the people climbing these stairs to feel it.

The heart of the museum is the former convent church. Here the collection reaches its peak, with the work of Bartolomé Esteban Murillo. Murillo was born in Seville in the seventeenth century and spent most of his life here. He painted for the city's churches and religious houses. Standing in this tall, bright space, you see his paintings in conditions close to their original purpose. They hang high, under a painted vault, in a room built for worship. The effect is very different from a plain white gallery wall.

Murillo became famous for his images of the Immaculate Conception. They show the Virgin Mary floating on clouds, often surrounded by small angels. Notice how soft his edges are. His colours seem to dissolve into warm haze rather than sharp outline. Many of the paintings here came from a Capuchin friary in Seville. Look for a small, tender picture of the Virgin and Child. Locals call it the Virgin of the Napkin. According to a popular tale, Murillo painted it on a napkin for a friar who asked for a keepsake. It is a lovely story, but treat it as legend rather than record.

Here is a detail most people walk straight past. In this museum you will find a terracotta sculpture of Saint Jerome by the Italian sculptor Pietro Torrigiano. The saint kneels, thin and ageing, in an act of penance. Torrigiano has a famous place in art history for another reason. As a young man in Florence, he punched the young Michelangelo and broke his nose. Writers of the Renaissance recorded the story. Torrigiano later travelled widely and ended his days in Seville. Tradition says he died in a prison of the Inquisition.

If Murillo is warmth, Francisco de Zurbarán is stillness. Zurbarán worked in Seville in the same century. He painted monks and saints with an almost sculptural clarity. Look for his large painting of the Apotheosis of Saint Thomas Aquinas, where the medieval scholar rises among Church fathers and saints. Then find his scenes painted for Carthusian monks. White habits glow against dark backgrounds. The folds of the cloth look heavy enough to touch. Zurbarán could make a plain white robe feel like the most important object in the world.

The collection also includes Juan de Valdés Leal, a painter with a more dramatic and restless style. His figures move. His brushwork is fast and nervous. You will find Francisco Pacheco too, the painter who trained the young Diego Velázquez and later became his father-in-law. And keep an eye out for a portrait by El Greco, the Greek-born painter who worked in Toledo. It is often identified as a portrait of his son. Together, these rooms give you the Seville School, the city's own way of painting during Spain's Golden Age.

The Golden Age was the period when Spanish art and literature flourished. It coincided with Seville's own great era of wealth. For much of that time, ships returning from the Americas were required to come up the river to this city. Silver, merchants, and money flowed through Seville. That wealth paid for churches, convents, and paintings. In a real sense, the art on these walls is the art of a port city at the centre of an empire.

Upstairs, the story continues into the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The mood changes. You will see Andalusian scenes, portraits of local society, and images of everyday life in Seville. Look for the work of Gonzalo Bilbao, a Sevillian painter who recorded working people, including women in the city's tobacco factory. After so many saints and visions, these rooms feel like stepping back out into the street. The faces are ordinary, and that is their power.

When you leave, stand for a moment in the square outside, the Plaza del Museo. Tall trees give it shade. In the middle rises a statue of Murillo, looking toward the building that holds so much of his work. On Sunday mornings, local painters often set up easels and sell their work here. It is a fitting tradition. The artists of today gather at the door of the artists of the past.

So look back once more at the plain front of the old convent. Behind it, the fountain is still running into its basin. The monks are long gone, and the captives they ransomed are forgotten. But the light still moves across the cloisters, and Murillo's soft clouds still float under the painted vault. As you walk away into the streets of Seville, notice the tiles on the doorways around you. You have just seen where many of them began.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the cloisters first to adjust to the light.
Start with the former church to see Murillo first.
Save the upper floor for a lighter, modern finale.
Visit on Sunday morning to browse the painters' market.
Bring a light layer for the cooler indoor galleries.
