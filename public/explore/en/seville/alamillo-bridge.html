The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you there is the steady hum of tyres on the deck, and above you there is the soft whistle of wind through steel cables. Look up, and the white mast leans back over your head. It does not stand straight like a church tower. It tilts away from the water, as if it is pulling against something. It is. This is the Alamillo Bridge, and almost everything about it depends on that lean.

The bridge was built for the Universal Exposition of 1992, the great world's fair that Seville hosted that year. The fair took place on La Cartuja, the long island of land between two channels of the Guadalquivir River. Before the Expo, this ground sat on the edge of the city and felt cut off from it. The organisers needed new crossings to bring millions of visitors over the water. The Alamillo became the most daring of them.

The designer was Santiago Calatrava, a Spanish architect and engineer from Valencia. In the early 1990s he was still building his reputation. Today his work stands in cities across Europe and the Americas, but this bridge was one of the projects that made his name. Calatrava often designs structures that look like bodies or birds in motion. Here the shape is simpler... a single white stroke against the sky.

Now for the clever part. Most cable-stayed bridges use a tower with cables on both sides. The cables on the back balance the cables on the front, like the two pans of a scale. This bridge has no back cables at all. Instead, the mast itself does the balancing. It leans back at an angle of about fifty-eight degrees, and its own great weight pulls against the weight of the deck. The tower is the counterweight.

So the cables you see are all on one side. They run in pairs from the mast down to the central spine of the deck, like the strings of a harp. The deck spans around two hundred metres across the water, with no supports standing in the river below. Think about that for a moment. The whole road hangs from one leaning arm on the shore — and nothing holds that arm up from behind.

The mast is very tall, well over one hundred metres. It is built from steel and filled with concrete, which gives it the mass it needs. At first glance it looks light, almost delicate, because it is painted white and tapers toward the top. But the lightness is a visual trick. The structure is heavy on purpose. Its weight is the reason the bridge works.

Here is something many visitors never learn. Calatrava's first plan was for two bridges, not one. The idea was a matching pair, one on each side of La Cartuja, with masts leaning toward each other across the island. Together they would have framed the Expo grounds like two hands. In the end only this one was built. When you look at it, you are seeing half of a larger idea.

If you walk across, notice where the walkway sits. Pedestrians do not walk at the edge of the road. The footpath runs along the raised centre of the deck, between the two directions of traffic. The cables come down on either side of you. It is one of the few places where you can walk through the structure of a bridge, rather than simply along it. Cars pass below your feet on both sides.

The bridge takes its name from this northern stretch of the city, an area called the Alamillo. On the far side lies the Alamillo Park, one of the largest green spaces in Seville. It is a place of open lawns, pine trees, and lakes, popular with local families, runners, and cyclists. On a weekend morning you may share the walkway with people in sports clothes heading to the park.

Turn toward the city and look downstream. On a clear day you can pick out the skyline of Seville, with low rooftops and the towers of the old town in the distance. Behind you, on La Cartuja, some Expo pavilions still stand. Many now hold offices, research centres, and a theme park. The island never returned to empty land. The fair changed this part of the city for good.

The bridge has its admirers and its critics. Some engineers point out that a balanced, two-sided design would have been cheaper and simpler. Calatrava chose drama instead. That choice is part of his style, and it divides opinion to this day. Still, the bridge became a symbol of modern Seville, a city of Moorish palaces and Gothic stone that wanted to show it could build for the future too.

The design also had a life after Seville. Years later, Calatrava returned to the idea of a single leaning mast with no back cables. He used it for the Sundial Bridge in the town of Redding, in California. There, the mast also casts a shadow that works as a giant sundial. You can see the family resemblance at once. This bridge in Seville was the first version of that thought.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Watch the light move on the white steel. In the late afternoon the mast turns warm and golden, and the cables catch the sun one by one. Listen again for the wind in the cables and the traffic below. Then look at the angle of the mast one last time... a heavy arm, leaning back, quietly holding the road over the river.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk across on the raised central footpath.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the mast.
Combine your visit with a walk in Alamillo Park.
View the full profile from the riverbank, not the deck.
Bring water and a hat on hot summer days.
