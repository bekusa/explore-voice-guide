The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you hear water. It is everywhere here: running under wooden footbridges, slipping over smooth black basalt, filling the shade with a steady, cool sound. The air feels several degrees colder than the open road behind you. Leaves of laurel and fig crowd the path. The ground underfoot is damp, and the light comes down green through the canopy. Welcome to Tel Dan, at the far northern edge of the Galilee, close to the disputed borderlands that include the Shebaa Farms.

Let us start with that water, because everything here begins with it. The springs at Dan are fed by rain and snowmelt from Mount Hermon, the long mountain ridge you can often see to the northeast. The water sinks into the limestone, travels underground, and comes out here clear and cold. The Dan stream is the largest of the sources that join to form the Jordan River. Most visitors expect the Jordan to start as a trickle in a desert. Instead, it starts here, in something closer to a forest.

That constant supply of water is why people chose this spot thousands of years ago. A reliable spring in a dry region is a reason to build a city, to defend it, and to fight over it. The mound you are about to climb is a tel, a word for an artificial hill formed by layer upon layer of human settlement. Each generation built on the ruins of the one before. So when you walk up the slope, you are walking up through time.

The earliest name for this city comes from ancient texts. It was called Laish. According to the Hebrew Bible, in the Book of Judges, the Israelite tribe of Dan conquered Laish and renamed it after their ancestor. The phrase "from Dan to Beersheba" appears in the Bible as a way to describe the whole land, from its northern edge to its southern edge. You are standing at that northern edge, the first word of the phrase.

Now, the first real surprise. On the southeastern side of the tel stands a gate built of mud brick, dating to the Middle Bronze Age, roughly four thousand years ago. It has a true arch, built of bricks set in a curve. For a long time, many people believed the arch was a Roman invention. This gate is far older than Rome. It is one of the oldest arched gateways known anywhere in the world, and it survived because later builders buried it under earth.

Look closely at the material. These are sun-dried bricks, made from mud and straw. Mud brick is fragile. Rain and wind usually destroy it within a few generations. The gate lasted only because it was covered and forgotten, then uncovered by archaeologists in the twentieth century. Today a protective structure shields it from the weather. Some guides call it Abraham's Gate, linking it to the biblical patriarch, but that name is a popular tradition, not a proven connection.

Excavations at Tel Dan began in the 1960s and continued for decades, led for many years by the Israeli archaeologist Avraham Biran. His teams uncovered city walls, gates, streets, and a large sacred area. The work was slow. A single season might move only a small patch of earth. But the patient digging turned Dan into one of the most important archaeological sites in the region, especially for the period of the Israelite kingdoms.

The second surprise came in 1993. Workers found a fragment of black basalt reused in a later wall near the Israelite gate. It carried an inscription in Aramaic, the language of the neighbouring Aramean kingdoms. More fragments turned up the following year. The text is a victory boast by an Aramean king. And in it appears a phrase read by most scholars as "House of David". It is widely considered the earliest mention of King David's dynasty found outside the Bible.

The original stone is not here. It is kept in the Israel Museum in Jerusalem. But standing near the gate where it was found gives the discovery real weight. A king carved those words to celebrate a war. Later, someone broke the stone and used the pieces as building material, as if it meant nothing. Three thousand years later, it became one of the most discussed inscriptions in biblical archaeology.

Higher on the tel, you will reach the sacred precinct. Archaeologists uncovered a large stone platform here, with the remains of altars and side rooms. Many scholars connect it with the Book of Kings, which says that Jeroboam, king of the northern kingdom of Israel, set up a golden calf at Dan. No golden calf has been found. What remains is the platform itself, its cut stones, and the outline of a place where people came to worship for centuries. A modern metal frame shows the size of an altar.

This region has never been quiet for long. Ancient armies passed through here, and the modern era has also brought conflict. On the tel you may see old military positions and trenches from more recent wars. The surrounding area, including the Shebaa Farms, is contested between neighbouring countries, and tensions have flared in recent years. Access can close at short notice. Before you visit, check current local safety guidance and follow any instructions from park staff.

On your way down, take time with the water again. Part of the trail passes a shallow pool where visitors are sometimes allowed to dip their feet. The water is cold, even on hot summer days. Under the trees you will find old Syrian ash, laurel, and wild fig, plants that need constant moisture. In a dry landscape, this green patch feels almost impossible. Birds call from the branches, and the sound of the stream never stops.

So pause here for a moment. Behind you is a mound that holds four thousand years of gates, altars, and broken inscriptions. In front of you is water that started as snow on a mountain, and that will soon become the Jordan River. Watch the light move on the surface. Listen to the stream. When you walk back toward the road, notice how the air warms with each step, as you leave the shade and the springs of Dan behind.
