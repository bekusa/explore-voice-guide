The guide

The story

Feel the ground under your shoes. It is not quite flat, and it is not quite natural. Every step here lands on dust, broken pottery, and fallen mud brick, pressed together over thousands of years. The wind comes up from the valley below. It carries the smell of dry grass and turned farmland. Above you, the sky is wide and open. You are standing on top of a city... and beneath it, another city, and beneath that one, another.

This is Tel Megiddo. A tel is an artificial hill, built up as people settled on the same spot again and again. Each new town rose on the ruins of the last. Archaeologists have counted around twenty layers of settlement here. Some go back to the Neolithic period, long before writing. Others reach into the Persian era. Standing on this mound, you are on a vertical timeline, and the earliest chapters lie deepest underground.

Look out toward the north and east. That broad, green plain is the Jezreel Valley, the most fertile farmland in the region. Now look southwest, toward the ridges. There is a narrow pass there, which runs through the hills near Wadi Ara. In ancient times, a major trade route ran through it. That route connected Egypt with Syria and Mesopotamia. Whoever held Megiddo controlled the pass. That simple fact explains almost everything that happened here.

The first great event in the written record happened in the fifteenth century before Christ. The Egyptian pharaoh Thutmose III marched north against a coalition of Canaanite rulers. His scribes recorded the campaign on the walls of the temple at Karnak, in Egypt. It is often described as the earliest battle known in real detail. According to the account, Thutmose chose the narrow, risky path through the pass, against his generals' advice. Then he besieged the city for months until it surrendered.

Here is the first surprise. The name of this hill gave the world a word. In Hebrew, Har Megiddo means "the mountain of Megiddo." Over centuries, it became "Armageddon." In the Book of Revelation, the final battle at the end of days is placed here. So when people talk about Armageddon, they are, in a way, pointing at this quiet mound in the countryside. Many visitors stand here and look at the peaceful fields, and the contrast is hard to forget.

The battles did not stop in the ancient world. The Hebrew Bible says that King Josiah of Judah died at Megiddo, facing an Egyptian pharaoh. And in 1918, during the First World War, British forces under General Edmund Allenby defeated Ottoman troops across this region. The campaign became known as the Battle of Megiddo. Afterward, Allenby took the title Viscount Allenby of Megiddo. The same strategic pass mattered to armies more than three thousand years apart.

As you walk the path, watch for the city gates. Megiddo has several of them, from different periods. One of the best known has six chambers, three on each side of the passage. For decades, scholars linked it to King Solomon, because of a description in the Bible. Today, that dating is debated, and some archaeologists place it later. This is part of what makes Megiddo special. The site is not only a ruin. It is an active argument.

Now find the large round stone platform. It is a Canaanite altar from the Early Bronze Age. It was built from fieldstones and has steps leading up one side. Nearby stood temples from the same era. Excavators found animal bones around this area, which suggests offerings were made here. Stand beside it for a moment. This is one of the oldest structures on the whole tel, far older than the gates you passed.

Further along, you will see long rows of stone pillars, some with holes cut through them. The first excavators believed these were stables for chariot horses. The holes, they said, were for tying the animals. Other scholars think these buildings were storehouses or market halls. The debate is still open. Either way, the buildings show a well-organised city with serious resources, during the Iron Age kingdom of Israel.

Then look for a deep, round pit lined with stone, with two staircases spiralling down its sides. It is usually identified as a grain silo from the Iron Age. Two staircases may have allowed people to go down and come up without colliding. It is a small, practical detail. And yet it tells you something about daily life here: food storage, planning, and the fear of hunger during a siege.

The second surprise waits at the end of the visit. Megiddo had a remarkable water system. Engineers cut a vertical shaft deep into the rock, then a long horizontal tunnel to a spring outside the walls. That way, the city could reach water without exposing anyone to an enemy. Visitors can walk down through this system today. The air grows cool and damp as you descend. Many people find it the most memorable part of the site.

Much of what you see was uncovered in the twentieth century. Gottlieb Schumacher dug here in the early 1900s. A large expedition from the University of Chicago worked here in the 1920s and 1930s. Since the 1990s, a team from Tel Aviv University has continued the work. In 2005, Megiddo became a UNESCO World Heritage Site, together with Hazor and Beersheba, as part of the Biblical Tels.

Before you leave, stand at the edge once more and look across the valley. The fields are patterned in green and gold, depending on the season. Tractors move where chariots once moved. Birds circle above the slope. Under your feet, the layers of the tel stay silent and patient. As you walk back down, notice the scattered stones beside the path. Each one was placed here by someone, for a reason, a very long time ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early in the day to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Bring a hat and water; the summit has little shade.
Wear shoes with grip for the water tunnel steps.
Visit the small site museum first to understand the layers.
Finish with the water tunnel, which exits near the car park.
