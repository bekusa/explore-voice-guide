The guide

The story

Take off your shoes and feel the stone under your feet. The courtyard is wide and open, and in the heat of the day the paving holds the warmth of the sun. Around you, arcades run along every side in long, shaded rows. Pigeons lift off the stone and settle again. Somewhere a voice is reciting softly, and the sound carries across the open space. This is the Great Mosque of Kufa, one of the oldest places of Muslim prayer in the world.

To understand this mosque, start with the town around it. Kufa began in the seventh century as an Arab army camp, founded shortly after the Muslim victory over the Persian Sasanian Empire. The Sasanians were the last great Persian dynasty before Islam. Soldiers and their families settled here, close to the Euphrates River. In the beginning the homes were simple, and some early accounts describe them as built from reeds. At the centre of the camp the settlers marked out a place to pray together.

That first mosque was very modest. According to early historians, its limits were set by the throw of an arrow in each direction, and the space was open to the sky. Later, under the Umayyad caliphs, the governor Ziyad ibn Abihi rebuilt it on a grander scale. The Umayyads were the first dynasty to rule the early Islamic empire. The building you see today carries many later rebuildings and restorations, but its core purpose and its place have stayed the same for more than thirteen centuries.

Here is the event that gives this mosque its deepest meaning for many visitors. Ali ibn Abi Talib was the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad, and the fourth caliph. He made Kufa his capital. In the year 661, during the dawn prayer, an attacker struck him with a sword inside this mosque. Ali died of his wound two days later. For Shia Muslims, Ali is the first Imam, and the spot where he fell is a place of deep sorrow and devotion.

Look toward the prayer niche connected with that morning. Pilgrims gather there, touch the surrounding surfaces, and stand quietly in prayer. Some weep. If you come during the month of Ramadan, you may see especially large crowds, because the attack on Ali took place during that holy month. Even on an ordinary afternoon, you will notice that people slow down when they approach this corner. The atmosphere changes. Voices drop. It is a good moment to simply stand back and watch.

Now turn your attention to the golden dome that rises above the complex. Beneath it lies the shrine of Muslim ibn Aqil, a cousin of Imam Husayn, the grandson of the Prophet. In 680, Husayn sent Muslim ahead to Kufa to test the support of its people. The support collapsed, and Muslim was captured and executed. His death came shortly before the tragedy of Karbala, where Husayn himself was killed. Close by is the shrine of Hani ibn Urwa, a local leader who sheltered Muslim and shared his fate.

Here is something many first-time visitors miss. Scattered around the courtyard are small marked stations, called maqamat. Each one is linked by tradition to a prophet or a holy figure who is said to have prayed on that spot. Pilgrims move from one station to the next, praying at each in turn. These associations come from religious tradition rather than archaeology, but they shape how people move through the space. Watch for small groups walking a quiet circuit around the courtyard.

One of these traditions is especially striking. According to Shia lore, the Prophet Noah built his ark on this very ground. A spot in the mosque is linked to the oven, or tannur, from which the waters of the great flood are said to have burst out. This is a sacred story, not a historical record, but it tells you how important this place became in the religious imagination. For believers, Kufa is not only an early Islamic town. It is ground with roots far older than Islam.

Kufa also left its mark on the written word. An early, angular style of Arabic calligraphy takes its name from this town. It is called Kufic script. Its letters are bold and geometric, with straight lines and sharp corners. Kufic was used in early copies of the Quran and in inscriptions on buildings across the Islamic world. As you walk under the arcades, look closely at the decorative writing. You may notice both flowing styles and blockier, more geometric lettering that recalls this old tradition.

Just beyond the mosque walls lie the excavated remains of the Dar al-Imara, the governor's palace. In early Islamic towns, the governor often lived right beside the main mosque, so religious and political power stood side by side. Archaeologists have uncovered the foundations of this palace. Today you can see low walls and the outline of rooms in the earth. It is a quiet, often overlooked spot, and it helps you picture Kufa as a seat of government, not only a place of prayer.

The city of Najaf lies only a short drive away. Najaf holds the shrine of Imam Ali, and it is one of the holiest cities for Shia Muslims. Many pilgrims visit both places on the same journey, moving between the mosque where Ali was struck and the shrine where he is buried. You will often see coaches of pilgrims from Iran, Pakistan, the Gulf, and across Iraq. Listen to the many languages around you. This mosque draws people from far beyond the Euphrates.

A few words about visiting with respect. This is an active place of worship, not a museum. Men and women usually enter through separate entrances, and women are expected to cover their hair and body. A loose, long garment is wise for everyone. Shoes come off before the prayer areas. Ask before you photograph people, especially those in prayer. If you are unsure where you may walk, a quiet question to an attendant is always welcome, and staff are used to visitors.

Before you leave, find a shaded spot under the arcades and pause for a moment. Look out across the open courtyard. The light falls on the stone, the dome shines above the shrine, and pilgrims cross the space at their own slow pace. People have gathered to pray on this ground since the first days of the town. As you walk back toward the gate, listen once more to the soft voices, the footsteps, and the wings of the pigeons rising into the sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear long, loose clothing; women should cover their hair.
Come early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Ask permission before photographing anyone at prayer.
Combine your visit with the shrine of Imam Ali in Najaf.
Expect very large crowds during Ramadan and major pilgrimages.
