The guide

The story

Listen first. Ice cracks somewhere across the water, a deep sound like a door slamming in an empty house. Then comes the slap of small waves against the hull, and a cold wind that carries the smell of salt and wet rock. Grey cloud sits low on the mountains. When it lifts, even for a minute, you see blue ice falling straight into the sea.

This is Alberto de Agostini National Park, at the far southern edge of South America. It covers a large part of the western archipelago of Tierra del Fuego, in Chile's Magallanes region. There are almost no roads here. Most people arrive the way sailors always have, by boat, through channels and fjords that wind between hundreds of islands. Cruise ships from Punta Arenas and Ushuaia pass through it, and so do smaller expedition vessels.

Chile created the park in the 1960s. It takes its name from Alberto Maria de Agostini, an Italian missionary from the Salesian order. He arrived in Patagonia in the early twentieth century and spent decades exploring it. He was a priest, but he was also a mountaineer, a mapmaker, and a photographer. His photographs and films were among the first images many Europeans ever saw of these glaciers.

Here is something most visitors miss. De Agostini carried heavy glass-plate cameras into terrain where even today few people walk. He crossed ice fields, climbed passes, and waited out storms to get a clear frame. His books helped to put these mountains on the map, in a literal sense. Some of the names you hear on this voyage first appeared in his surveys.

The backbone of the park is the Cordillera Darwin, a chain of mountains that rises straight out of the sea. The highest peak is Mount Darwin. During the voyage of the Beagle in the 1830s, Captain Robert FitzRoy named it after his young naturalist, Charles Darwin. Darwin sailed these waters long before he became famous. The cold, the rain, and the people he met here stayed in his memory for the rest of his life.

To the west stands Mount Sarmiento, a sharp double summit that is often hidden in cloud. For centuries sailors used it as a landmark. It is also a hard mountain to climb, with ice on every face and violent weather. De Agostini attempted it himself. In the 1950s, an expedition he organised finally put climbers on the summit. By then, the old priest had dreamed of that mountain for most of his life.

Now, the ice. The Darwin range holds a large ice field, and glaciers flow down from it in every direction. Many of them end directly in the sea. These are called tidewater glaciers. When the front of the ice breaks away, large blocks crash into the water. Sailors call this calving. That deep crack you may hear is the sound of a glacier moving, slowly, toward the ocean.

Along one arm of the Beagle Channel, there is a stretch that people call Glacier Alley. Here, several glaciers come down the mountainside one after another, almost in a line. Many of them carry the names of European countries, such as Holland, Italy, France, and Germany. Explorers from those nations surveyed this coast, and the names stayed. On some ships, the crew serves a small drink from each country as you pass its glacier.

Look closely at the colour of the ice. The deepest blue appears where the ice is oldest and most compressed. Heavy pressure squeezes out the air bubbles. Without the bubbles, the ice absorbs red light and leaves the blue. White ice holds more trapped air. Dark stripes in the glacier are rock and dust that the ice has scraped from the mountains and carried down with it.

Below the ice line, the land changes. Dense forests of southern beech trees cling to the steep slopes. Their branches often grow sideways, bent by the constant wind. Thick moss covers the rocks and fallen trunks, and everything drips. Along the shore, long ribbons of kelp move in the water. This is one of the wettest and windiest places on Earth, and the plants show it in their shape.

People lived here long before any explorer arrived. The Kawésqar and the Yaghan were canoe peoples. For thousands of years, they travelled these channels in bark canoes, fishing and hunting seals and shellfish. Some families kept a small fire burning inside the canoe itself. European sailors saw fires along the coast at night, and that is one reason for the name Tierra del Fuego, which means land of fire.

Keep your eyes on the water and the sky. Black-browed albatrosses glide low over the waves. Cormorants stand on the rocks near the shore. Andean condors sometimes circle above the cliffs. Seals and sea lions rest on the stones, and dolphins may appear beside the bow. Wildlife sightings change from day to day, so treat every sighting as a small gift rather than a promise.

The park is also part of a much larger protected area. The Cape Horn Biosphere Reserve stretches across the southern islands of Chile. Scientists come here to study clean air, cold water, and glaciers that react quickly to a warming climate. Several glaciers in this region have pulled back a long way in recent decades. Bare, polished rock now shows where the ice used to reach.

So take a moment before the boat turns away. Watch the ice front for a minute without your camera. Notice how the cold rises off the water and touches your face. Listen for that deep crack again. The mountains will probably disappear into cloud behind you. But the sound of the ice, and the quiet that follows it, will stay with you for a long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a waterproof jacket, gloves, and a warm hat.
Stand on the open deck when approaching a glacier front.
Keep binoculars ready for albatrosses and seals.
Expect sudden weather changes even in summer.
Use a lens cloth; spray and drizzle cover lenses quickly.
