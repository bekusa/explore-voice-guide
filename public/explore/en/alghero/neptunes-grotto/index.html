The guide

The story

Cool air reaches you first. It comes up from below, damp and faintly mineral, and it carries the smell of salt and wet stone. Somewhere beneath you, the sea moves in and out of the rock with a slow, hollow breath. Above, the sky over Capo Caccia is wide and bright. Below, the cliff falls straight into water so clear it looks green. You are standing at the doorway to Neptune's Grotto, one of the best-known sea caves in the Mediterranean.

Capo Caccia is a limestone promontory on the north-western coast of Sardinia. It sits a short drive west of Alghero, beyond a wide bay and a stretch of scrubby headland. The name means Cape of the Hunt. The cliffs here are pale, cracked and layered. Seabirds wheel along their faces. And inside this pale wall, the sea and rainwater have spent a very long time dissolving the rock into chambers, pools and columns.

How did this happen? Limestone is a soft rock that slowly dissolves in slightly acidic water. Rain seeps through cracks from above. Over immense spans of time, those cracks widen into passages. Then, drop by drop, the water leaves behind tiny deposits of mineral. Hang from the ceiling, and those deposits become stalactites. Rise from the floor, and they become stalagmites. When the two finally meet, they form a single column. The grotto is full of them.

There are two ways to reach the entrance. Many visitors arrive by boat from Alghero's harbour, following the coast past cliffs and small coves. Others come by road and take the long staircase cut into the cliff face. Locals call it the Escala del Cabirol. That is Catalan, and it means the goat's steps. The name fits. The staircase zigzags down the rock like a mountain goat's path, with the open sea on one side the whole way.

That Catalan name deserves a moment. Alghero is unusual in Italy. After the Crown of Aragon took the town in the fourteenth century, Catalan settlers arrived, and their language stayed. Today, some older residents still speak a local form of Catalan, and you will see it on street signs in town. So the steps beneath your feet carry a Spanish-Catalan name, on an Italian island, in a cave named for a Roman sea god.

Here is something many visitors miss. The staircase has more than six hundred steps. It was built in the mid-twentieth century, and before that the cave was much harder to reach. Going down feels easy. Coming back up is a real climb, in sun, with little shade. Take it slowly. Stop at the turns. Look out at the water and at the small island that lies offshore. The pauses are part of the experience.

At the bottom, a walkway leads just above the waterline, and then you step inside. The temperature drops. Sound changes too. Voices soften and echo. The light outside is sharp and white, but within a few metres it turns low and golden, coming from carefully placed lamps. Your eyes need a moment to adjust. Then the shapes appear — columns, curtains of stone, ceilings that drip with pale points.

According to a popular local account, fishermen were the first to know this cave, sheltering their boats in its mouth long before it became a visitor site. That story is easy to believe. From the sea, the entrance is a dark opening at the foot of the cliff. For anyone working these waters, it would have been impossible to miss. Only a small part of the cave system is open to visitors. Much more continues beyond, deeper into the rock.

The heart of the visit is the underground lake. It lies flat and still, and its surface holds the reflections of the columns above. In some places, it is hard to tell where the stone ends and the reflection begins. The water is salty here, because the cave connects to the sea. Notice how quiet the lake is compared with the waves outside. The rock wall keeps the open sea's movement away from this chamber.

Guides often point out formations with names. One great cluster of columns is often compared to a church organ, with tall pipes of stone standing side by side. Other shapes look like draped fabric, or frozen waterfalls. These names are human inventions, of course. The rock does not know what it looks like. But naming the shapes is an old habit in caves everywhere. It helps people remember a place where the usual landmarks of sky and horizon are gone.

One practical surprise: the grotto depends on the sea. When the wind is strong and the waves are high, water can surge into the entrance, and visits may stop for safety. Boats may not sail on rough days. So the same sea that shaped this cave also decides, on any given morning, whether you may enter it. Many visitors check conditions with the boat operators or the site before setting out from Alghero.

As you walk, look closely at the floor and the rails. The path is narrow in places and often wet. Stone that looks dry can be slick. Please do not touch the formations. The oils on human hands can stop a stalactite from growing, and growth here is extremely slow. A formation you could break in a second may have taken thousands of years to form. Looking is enough. The cave rewards patience more than touch.

When you step back out, the brightness will make you squint. Listen again for the sea moving against the base of the cliff. Feel the warm air after the cool chamber. Look up at the pale rock and remember the passages you have just walked through, hidden inside it. As you climb or sail away, watch the entrance shrink into a small dark mark on the cliff — and the stone close quietly around its secrets again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check sea conditions before going; rough waves can stop visits.
Wear shoes with good grip for wet, slippery cave paths.
Bring water and pace yourself for the climb back up.
Consider arriving by boat from Alghero for coastal cliff views.
Bring a light layer; the cave air feels cool inside.
