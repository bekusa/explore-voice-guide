The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the tall Georgia pines, and the needles hiss softly overhead. The ground is sandy and red beneath them, and the air smells of resin and warm earth. Ahead of you sits a small white house with a modest porch. It does not look like a place where history was made. Yet this quiet cottage on the slope of Pine Mountain was the private refuge of an American president. It is also the place where his life ended.

This is the Little White House, near the town of Warm Springs in Meriwether County, Georgia. It belonged to Franklin Delano Roosevelt, the thirty-second president of the United States. He led the country through the Great Depression and most of the Second World War. In Washington he lived in the grand White House. Here, he wanted something different. He wanted somewhere small and private, where he could rest among ordinary people.

The story begins with an illness. In 1921, Roosevelt contracted a paralysing disease, believed at the time to be polio. He lost the use of his legs. He was a young, ambitious politician, and he refused to accept that his career was over. He searched for any treatment that might help. In 1924, he heard about a resort in rural Georgia. Natural mineral springs there flowed from the mountain with a steady warmth, close to ninety degrees Fahrenheit, all year round.

Roosevelt came to see the springs for himself. He found that the warm, buoyant water let him move his legs in a way he could not on land. He never walked unaided again. But the pools gave him strength, and they gave him hope. Here is a surprise many visitors miss. Roosevelt did not simply visit the resort. He bought it. In 1927, he created a foundation to turn the springs into a treatment centre for other people living with polio.

The house in front of you came a few years later. Roosevelt had it built in 1932, while he was still governor of New York. He had not yet become president. That same year, he won the national election, and this cottage soon gained its famous nickname. Look at its scale. It has only a handful of rooms, finished in local pine. There is no grand staircase and no ballroom. For a man who used a wheelchair, a simple single-storey layout made daily life easier.

Walk inside, and the rooms feel lived in rather than staged. Much of the furniture is original to Roosevelt's time. You will see a small kitchen, a living room with a stone fireplace, and the bedrooms of the president and his staff. Notice how close everything is. The rooms open into one another. This was a home for a man and his closest companions, not a seat of government. Visitors often say it feels as if the family stepped out for an afternoon drive.

And driving was important here. Roosevelt could not use foot pedals, so his cars were fitted with hand controls. One of his cars is displayed on the site today. It allowed him to steer himself along the country roads around Pine Mountain. He liked to stop and talk with local farmers. Those conversations shaped his politics. He saw rural poverty up close. He saw families without electricity and land worn out by cotton.

One story is often told about this connection. Roosevelt is said to have been shocked by his electricity bill at Warm Springs. It was far higher than the bill at his family home in New York. According to this popular account, the experience helped convince him that rural power had to become cheaper and more widely available. Later, his government created programmes to bring electricity to the American countryside. Whether or not the bill was the spark, the poverty he saw here clearly stayed with him.

Now we come to the heart of this place. In April 1945, Roosevelt came here to rest. The war in Europe was nearly over, and he was exhausted. On the twelfth of April, he sat in the living room while an artist, Elizabeth Shoumatoff, painted his portrait. He complained of a terrible headache, and he collapsed. He died that afternoon. He was sixty-three years old.

The painting was never completed. Today it is known as the Unfinished Portrait, and it hangs in the museum on the site. Look closely at it when you go inside. The face is carefully painted, but the body and background fade into bare outlines. It is a quiet record of a single interrupted afternoon. Many visitors find this the most moving object in the entire collection.

The news spread quickly. Roosevelt's body was carried by train from Warm Springs to Washington. Crowds lined the tracks along the route. At the departure here, a navy musician named Graham Jackson played his accordion as the procession passed. A photograph of his face, wet with tears, became one of the best-known images of that day. It showed the country's grief in a single human expression.

Roosevelt's work against polio did not end with him. The foundation he started here helped lead to a national charity that later became known as the March of Dimes. Ordinary Americans sent small coins to fund research. That money helped support the development of a polio vaccine in the 1950s. So in a real sense, a path runs from these Georgia pools to the vaccines that protect children around the world today.

Before you leave, take a slow walk along the paths between the buildings. Find the small guard posts where Marines once kept watch. Feel the slope of the land, and listen again to the pines. Look back at the white house from a distance. It still looks modest. A man who could not walk unaided chose this place to recover, to think, and to meet his neighbours. Carry that thought with you as you go.
