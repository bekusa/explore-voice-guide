The guide

The story

Listen first. Water drips from somewhere high above you, and every drop echoes. A paddle dips into dark water with a soft, hollow knock. The air is cool and damp after the heat of the beach, and it smells of wet stone and something sharper, the scent of the bats that live in the dark ahead. Behind you the jungle hums with insects. In front of you a low mouth of limestone opens over a green lagoon, and the river disappears into the mountain.

This is the Puerto Princesa Subterranean River, on the western coast of Palawan in the Philippines. It sits near the small village of Sabang, a long drive north from the city that gives it its name. Most people come by van along winding roads, then cross the bay by outrigger boat, a slim wooden craft with bamboo arms that balance it on the waves. By the time you reach this lagoon, the city already feels very far away.

Here is what makes this place unusual. The river does not simply run through a cave. It runs underground for several kilometres beneath a mountain range, then flows straight out into the sea. Because the cave opens onto the ocean, the tide reaches inside. The lower stretch of the river is brackish, a mix of fresh and salt water. Scientists value this, because a cave river connected so directly to the sea is rare anywhere in the world.

The rock around you is limestone, and the whole landscape is what geologists call karst. Karst is the shape limestone takes when rain and rivers slowly dissolve it over very long periods. The process leaves sharp ridges above ground and hollow chambers below. Look up at the cliffs around the lagoon. Their grey, jagged faces and the trees that cling to them are the visible half of the same story that continues inside the cave.

In 1999, UNESCO added the park to its list of World Heritage Sites. The listing recognised more than the cave. It covered the full sweep of land from the mountain peaks down to the sea, including forest, the river, and the shoreline. Later, in a global public vote, the river was also named one of the New Seven Wonders of Nature. You may see signs for both honours near the landing area.

Now the boat ride. Visitors wear a life vest and a helmet, and a local boatman paddles you in. Engines are not used inside the cave, so the only sounds are water, voices, and wings. One person in the front holds a light and points it where the guide directs. Once the daylight behind you fades, that single beam is the only light you have. It is quietly thrilling, even for people who have done it before.

As your eyes adjust, the formations appear. Stalactites hang from the ceiling, formed drop by drop as mineral-rich water leaves tiny deposits of stone. Stalagmites rise from below in the same slow way. The boatmen have given many of these shapes playful names. Locals point out figures that look like fruit, vegetables, animals, and even religious scenes. Part of the fun is deciding whether you see the same picture they do.

One chamber is often called the Cathedral, and the name makes sense when you reach it. The ceiling rises so high that your light struggles to touch it. Sounds stretch out and linger here. Many guides ask passengers to stay silent for a moment, and in that silence you can hear water dripping all around you in the dark. It is one of the most memorable moments of the whole trip.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Researchers have reported finding fossils in the walls of this cave, including remains of an ancient sea mammal related to the dugong. That tells you something remarkable. The rock around you formed under an ancient sea, long before it was lifted up into a mountain. You are floating through what was once the seabed, inside stone that still holds traces of the creatures that swam there.

You are not alone in here. Thousands of bats roost on the ceiling, and swiftlets, small birds that navigate the dark using echoes, nest in the cave as well. The guides usually share a cheerful warning: keep your mouth closed when you look up. It is a joke, but it is also good advice. The dark marks on the rock below the roosts show exactly why.

Back outside, the forest is just as alive. Long-tailed macaques often wander near the beach and the boardwalk, and large monitor lizards move slowly through the leaf litter. They are wild animals, so keep your bags closed and your snacks out of sight. The park protects a wide range of plants and wildlife, from the coastal mangroves up to the forested slopes of the mountains above the cave.

The tour itself only goes a short way into the river, perhaps a kilometre or so, before the boat turns back. The cave continues far beyond that point, into passages most people will never see. Daily visitor numbers are limited, and a permit is required. That system is part of how the park protects a place this fragile. The quiet you feel inside is, in part, the result of careful management.

So take a moment before you leave. Feel the change as you come back into the warm, bright air. Notice the green colour of the lagoon, the shadows on the cliffs, and the small boats waiting at the shore. Somewhere behind that rock face, the river is still moving through the dark, the bats are still hanging from the ceiling, and the water is still dripping, one slow drop at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Secure your entry permit in advance through an accredited operator.
Keep bags zipped near the beach because macaques grab food.
Keep your mouth closed when looking up at the bats.
Bring a waterproof pouch for your phone on the boat.
Stay silent in the Cathedral chamber to hear the drips.
