The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you hear it. A low, steady chatter rises from the reeds. Wings beat somewhere out of sight. A heavy splash lands close to the boat. The air is warm and smells of mud, river water and wet grass. Under the open sky of northern Senegal, the light is flat and bright on the channels. This is Djoudj, one of the most important wetlands for birds in all of West Africa.

You are in the delta of the Senegal River, north of the old colonial city of Saint-Louis. Here the river slows, spreads and breaks into lakes, creeks and flooded marsh. To the north, across the water, lies Mauritania. To the east and south, the land quickly turns dry. That contrast matters. For a bird crossing the Sahara, this green strip is the first reliable water after a very long flight.

Senegal created the park in 1971. Ten years later, in 1981, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List. The protected area covers roughly 16,000 hectares of lakes, channels, reed beds and seasonal pools. It is also listed under the Ramsar Convention. That is an international treaty, signed by many countries, to protect wetlands of global importance. So this place is valued not only by Senegal, but by the many countries whose birds depend on it.

And those birds come from far away. Every year, as the northern winter begins, huge numbers of migrants arrive from Europe and beyond. Ducks, waders and many smaller birds spend the cold months here. They feed, rest and build up strength for the return journey. Estimates for the total number of birds in the park often run into the millions. Several hundred species have been recorded across the year. Some live here permanently. Others stay only for a season.

The most famous residents are the great white pelicans. Djoudj holds one of the largest breeding colonies of these birds in West Africa. Picture a crowd of heavy white bodies packed together on the ground. Each bird has a long pale bill and a yellow pouch underneath. At close range, the colony is loud, busy and strongly scented. The pelicans often fish together. They swim in a line and drive small fish into the shallows before they scoop them up.

Look beyond the pelicans and the list keeps growing. Greater flamingos stand in the shallow lakes, their pink colour softened by distance. African spoonbills sweep their flat bills from side to side through the water. Herons wait motionless at the edge of the reeds. Cormorants dry their wings on dead branches, holding them open like dark flags. Egrets flash white against the green. Even if you know nothing about birds, the variety of shapes and movements is easy to enjoy.

Here is something many visitors miss. Djoudj is not only about birds. Warthogs trot along the dry banks with their tails held straight up. Jackals sometimes appear at dusk. Monitor lizards move slowly through the grass. In the deeper water, there may be crocodiles, so the guides keep a careful eye on the channels. The wetland supports a whole food chain. The birds sit near the top of that chain, but they are not alone.

The second surprise is how fragile this place has been. The Senegal River has changed a great deal in recent decades. Dams built downstream and upstream altered the flow of water and the balance between fresh water and salt. Then an invasive floating water fern, called giant salvinia, spread across the open water. It formed thick green mats that blocked light and choked the channels. Because of these threats, UNESCO placed Djoudj on its list of World Heritage in Danger more than once.

The response was patient and practical. Park staff and partners cleared channels and managed water levels more carefully. A small weevil, an insect that feeds only on this fern, was introduced to control it. This is called biological control, which means using a natural enemy instead of chemicals. Over time, the open water returned, and the park came off the danger list. When you see clear channels around you today, you are looking at years of quiet work.

Most visitors explore Djoudj by pirogue. A pirogue is a long, narrow wooden boat, the traditional working boat of this coast. Today many carry small motors. The boat moves slowly through the channels, and the guide cuts the engine near the best spots. In that silence, the sounds of the marsh come back. You hear reeds brushing the hull, water dripping from a raised paddle, and the steady murmur of thousands of birds nearby.

The human side of the park is worth noticing too. Many guides and boat drivers come from villages around the delta. They know the birds by sight, often by local names as well as by their French or English ones. Fishing and herding have shaped this landscape for generations. People and birds have shared the river here for a very long time. The park tries to protect the birds while still making room for the communities who live along its edges.

Timing changes everything here. The migrant season usually runs through the cooler months, roughly from November into spring. That is when the numbers are highest and the lakes are most crowded. Early morning brings soft light, cooler air and active birds. By midday, the heat rises and many birds rest in the shade of the reeds. Late afternoon brings softer colour again, and long flights of birds heading back to their roosts across the water.

Before you leave, pause for a moment. Look at the line where the green marsh meets the pale, dry land beyond. That thin edge is the reason all these birds are here. Watch a pelican lift slowly off the water, legs trailing, wings stretched wide. Some of the smaller birds around you may soon fly thousands of kilometres north. For now, they are here, in this same quiet stretch of river, with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit between November and spring for peak migrant numbers.
Book an early morning pirogue trip for cooler air.
Bring binoculars to watch flamingos on distant lakes.
Pack a hat, sun cream and plenty of drinking water.
Wear muted clothing to avoid startling birds near the boat.
