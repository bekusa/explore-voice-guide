The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Above you, traffic runs across steel with a low, steady hum. Below, the river moves slowly towards the sea. Then you hear the sharp, repeated cry of seabirds, louder than you expect in the middle of a city. You are standing beside the Tyne Bridge, the green steel arch that links Newcastle on the north bank with Gateshead on the south. For many people in the North East, this shape simply means home.

Look up at the arch first. It is a single curve of steel, and the road deck hangs beneath it on vertical hangers. The deck does not rest on piers in the river. That was the point. The design left the water clear for the ships that once crowded this stretch of the Tyne. When it opened, it was described as the longest single-span bridge in Britain. It held that title for a period... until bigger engineering came along.

The bridge opened in October 1928. King George the Fifth and Queen Mary came to perform the ceremony. Crowds filled the banks on both sides of the river. The royal car was among the first vehicles to cross. For a city that had built ships, engines and locomotives for the world, this was a public statement. Newcastle could still build something large, modern and confident in the years after the First World War.

The steelwork came from Dorman Long, a firm from Middlesbrough, a little further down the coast. The engineers were the consulting firm Mott, Hay and Anderson. Here is the first surprise. The same steel company went on to build the Sydney Harbour Bridge in Australia, which opened a few years later. The two bridges look very alike. Locals often say the Tyne Bridge was the model for Sydney. Engineers debate how direct that link really was — but the family resemblance is easy to see.

Now turn your attention to the two towers at each end. They look like solid stone gateways. They are faced with granite brought all the way from Cornwall, in the far south-west of England. Their style is calm and classical, a contrast to the bare steel above. But here is the second surprise. These towers are not solid. They were designed as warehouses, with floors stacked inside, several storeys high. Plans for using them in this way were never fully carried out, and much of the space stayed empty.

The towers also held lifts. These connected the high road deck with the Quayside far below, so people and goods could move between the two levels. Think about why that mattered. Newcastle is a steep city. The old town climbs sharply up from the river. Before the bridge, travellers from Gateshead faced a long, slow route. The new crossing put the two towns on one level, high above the water.

Listen again for those birds. They are kittiwakes, small gulls with grey wings and black wingtips. Normally they nest on sea cliffs. Here, every spring, they nest on the ledges of the bridge and nearby buildings along the Quayside. This colony is often described as one of the most inland kittiwake colonies in the world. The birds return each year from the open ocean. You may see netting on some buildings — a sign of the long negotiation between the city and its feathered residents.

The colour is part of the bridge's identity. The deep green paint has become so familiar that locals sometimes call it simply Tyne Bridge green. Repainting a structure of this size is a huge task. Over the decades, rust and weather have worked on the steel. In recent years the bridge has gone through major restoration work. Depending on when you visit, you may see scaffolding or sheeting on parts of the arch. That is the bridge being cared for, not falling apart.

Now look along the river, left and right. The Tyne Bridge is one of several crossings packed into a short stretch of water. Upstream sits the High Level Bridge, a nineteenth-century design by Robert Stephenson that carries trains on top and road traffic below. Close to it is the low Swing Bridge, which can turn sideways to let boats pass. Downstream, the Gateshead Millennium Bridge tilts upwards like a slowly opening eyelid. Together, they show more than a century of engineering ideas side by side.

On the Gateshead side, you will see the curved glass and steel of the Glasshouse music venue, once known as Sage Gateshead. Nearby stands the Baltic, a former flour mill that is now a centre for contemporary art. These buildings appeared long after the bridge. Yet the bridge frames them. Many photographs of this riverside place the green arch in the foreground, with the new buildings behind. Old industry and new culture share the same view.

The bridge is also a stage for big public moments. Each year, thousands of runners cross it during the Great North Run, a half marathon from Newcastle to South Shields on the coast. On some occasions, the Red Arrows, the Royal Air Force display team, have flown low over the arch, trailing coloured smoke. Fireworks have lit it up at celebrations. For people here, crossing this bridge marks arrival, departure and homecoming.

It is worth remembering the people who built it. Construction took several years and involved hundreds of workers. They riveted steel high above the moving water, in wind and North Sea weather, with safety standards far below today's. The two halves of the arch were built out from each bank and met in the middle. That moment of joining was a quiet triumph of measurement. A small error would have meant two halves that did not meet.

Before you go, find a spot on the Quayside where you can see the whole arch at once. Notice how the road seems to float under the curve. Notice the granite towers, grey and still, against the restless river. Watch the kittiwakes leave and return to their ledges. When you walk away, the hum of traffic will fade. But turn around once more. The green arch will still be there, holding the two banks of the Tyne together, as it has for nearly a century.
