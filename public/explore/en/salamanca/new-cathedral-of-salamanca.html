The guide

The story

Lay your palm against the stone here. It feels faintly warm, even on a cool morning, and the surface is soft and slightly grainy, like fine sand pressed together. This is Villamayor sandstone, quarried just outside Salamanca. In the low sun of early morning or late afternoon, it turns a deep honey gold. That colour is why people call this the Golden City. And the building in front of you holds more of that gold than almost anything else in town.

You are standing before the New Cathedral of Salamanca. The word new deserves a smile. Work began in 1513, and the last major pieces were not finished until 1733. That is more than two centuries of construction. Generations of stonemasons were born, trained, worked here and died while the walls slowly rose. Some of the men who carved the final details never saw the drawings their great-grandfathers had followed.

Here is the first surprise. Salamanca already had a cathedral when this one was started. The Old Cathedral, built in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, stands directly beside it. Usually, a city would tear down the old church to make room for the new. Salamanca did not. Instead, the builders placed the new cathedral right against the old one, and kept the older church standing. Today the two share a wall, and you can visit one through the other.

Why build a second cathedral at all? By the early sixteenth century, Salamanca was one of the great university cities of Europe. Its university was among the oldest in Spain, and the city was wealthy, crowded and ambitious. The old Romanesque church, with its thick walls and rounded arches, felt small and dark for a city of that importance. The clergy wanted something taller, brighter and more modern. This cathedral was their answer.

The early design was led by the architects Juan Gil de Hontañón and Juan de Álava, and later Rodrigo Gil de Hontañón carried the work forward. They chose a late form of Gothic, with pointed arches, tall columns and vaults that rise like a stone canopy. But fashions changed during those long two hundred years. So the building you see is a conversation between styles. Gothic bones carry Renaissance decoration, and Baroque touches arrive near the very end.

Look at the main façade now. The surface is covered with carving so dense it seems almost like lace. This style has a Spanish name, Plateresque. It comes from the word for silversmith, because the stonework looks like the fine detail a silversmith hammers into metal. Saints, coats of arms, plants and tiny figures crowd every space. The soft sandstone made this possible. It cuts easily when fresh from the quarry, then hardens as it weathers in the air.

Now for the detail that most visitors come looking for, and many still miss. Walk to the side entrance called the Puerta de Ramos, the Door of Palms. Among the old carvings around the doorway, search for a small figure in a spacesuit. Yes, an astronaut. It was added in 1992, during a restoration of the façade. Restorers have long left a small signature of their own era in the stone, and this team chose a modern symbol.

Nearby, on the same doorway, look for a winged creature that seems to be eating an ice cream cone. It was added during the same restoration work. Locals enjoy watching visitors hunt for these two figures, often with their phones raised and their heads tilted back. Over the years, the astronaut has become one of the most photographed details in the city. It is a reminder that a cathedral is never truly finished. People keep adding to it.

Look up at the bell tower. In 1755, a powerful earthquake struck Lisbon, in neighbouring Portugal. The shock was felt across much of the Iberian Peninsula, and here in Salamanca the tower was damaged. To save it, builders reinforced the base and wrapped the lower section in extra stone. If you study the tower carefully, you can see how the lower part looks heavier and more solid. That extra mass was a response to one terrible morning far away.

Step inside, and the first thing you notice is height. The columns rise in clusters, and at the top they open into ribbed vaults, stone lines that spread across the ceiling like the branches of trees. Light falls through tall windows and settles on the golden stone. The air is cool and still. Footsteps echo softly. Even when the cathedral is busy, the space seems to absorb sound, so voices drop to a murmur without anyone asking.

At the centre of the nave stands the choir, enclosed by carved wooden stalls and heavy stonework. This is where the Baroque period left its strongest mark. Members of the Churriguera family, a dynasty of Spanish architects and sculptors, contributed work to the cathedral in its later phase. Their style was rich and dramatic, full of curves, gilding and movement. It is such a recognisable style that Spanish uses their family name, Churrigueresque, to describe it. Notice how different it feels from the clean Gothic lines above.

If you want to see more, look for the passage into the Old Cathedral. Moving from one building to the other is like walking between two centuries. The older church is lower and darker, with rounded arches and heavier walls. For a wider view, the Ieronimus exhibition lets visitors climb into the towers and walk along upper balconies. From up there, you look down on both cathedrals and across the rooftops of the university quarter. It requires a separate ticket.

Before you leave, walk around the outside to the small square called the Patio Chico, behind the cathedrals. From here you can see both buildings together. The Old Cathedral's tower, with its scaled stone dome, rises beside the taller Gothic lines of the New Cathedral. Then pause in the Plaza de Anaya, the open square beside the main building. Students cross it with backpacks. Bells sound across the rooftops. Take one last look at the golden stone, and notice how the colour has already shifted since you arrived.
