The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your shoes, gravel crunches softly on the path. Somewhere below, the Asahi River moves slowly past the hill. Then you look up, and the castle looks back at you — dark, almost black, with roof edges that catch the light in thin lines of gold. It does not look like the pale castles on most postcards of Japan. It looks like a bird that has landed on the hill and folded its wings.

That is no accident. Locals call this place Ujo, which means Crow Castle. The name comes from the black lacquered wooden boards that cover the outer walls. Many visitors know Himeji Castle, further east, by its other nickname, White Heron Castle. So the region holds a pair of opposites. One castle is a white bird, and the other is a black one. Keep that contrast in mind as you walk closer.

The story of this hill begins in the late sixteenth century, in a time of civil war. A young lord named Ukita Hideie rose to power here. He was a close ally of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the general who brought most of Japan under one ruler. Hideie became one of five senior lords who were chosen to govern after Hideyoshi's death. He built this castle as a statement of that rank, and work on the main keep was completed in the 1590s.

Here is something many visitors walk straight past. Look at the base of the main keep. It is not a neat square or rectangle. The foundation is an uneven, five-sided shape. The builders did not flatten the hill to suit a perfect plan. Instead, they followed the natural shape of the rock beneath them. The result is a tower that leans into its landscape, with walls that meet at unexpected angles. Walk around the base and you will feel the corners shift.

Hideie's fortunes did not last. In 1600, he fought on the losing side at the Battle of Sekigahara, the great clash that decided who would rule Japan for the next two and a half centuries. He lost everything. He was sent into exile on a remote island far to the south of Tokyo, and he lived there for decades. The man who built the Crow Castle never came back to see it again.

Other lords took his place. For most of the long Edo period — the era of peace under the Tokugawa shoguns — the Ikeda family ruled from here. They added to the castle and shaped the town around it. As you walk the grounds, look closely at the stone walls. Some sections use rough, natural stones, piled with great skill but little cutting. Other sections use neater, squared blocks. Those differences show different generations of builders, working on the same hill.

Now turn your eyes to the river. Just across the water lies Korakuen, one of the most admired gardens in Japan. The Ikeda lords created it as a private garden, a place for rest, poetry and tea, and it was completed around 1700. The castle and the garden were planned to belong together. From the garden, the black keep rises above the trees like borrowed scenery. From the castle, the garden spreads out below like a green carpet.

The second surprise is harder to see, because it is about what is missing. The keep in front of you is not the original. In 1945, near the end of the Second World War, an air raid struck Okayama. The old wooden keep burned to the ground. For about twenty years, the hill stood without its crow. Then, in 1966, the city rebuilt the keep in reinforced concrete, following old photographs and records to recreate its outer form.

So what survived? A small number of older structures still stand. The most notable is the Tsukimi Yagura, the Moon-Viewing Turret. A yagura is a defensive tower, usually used for storage or for watching the approaches. This one had a gentler second purpose, as its name suggests. Lords and their guests could sit here and watch the moon rise over the grounds. When you find it, remember that this is genuine Edo-period wood and plaster, not a reconstruction.

Look up again at the rooftops of the main keep. You will see the gold. Ornaments shaped like mythical fish sit on the roof ridges. In Japanese castles, these fish were believed to protect the building from fire — a hope that, sadly, did not save the original keep. The gold against the black walls gives Okayama Castle its sharp, dramatic look. On a clear afternoon, the contrast is strongest, and the gold seems to glow.

Inside the reconstructed keep, the atmosphere changes. Concrete floors and modern stairs replace the old timber. The interior now works as a museum, with displays about the Ukita and Ikeda families, armour, swords and daily life in the castle town. It is worth climbing to the top floor. From the windows, you can see the river curving around the hill, the garden beyond, and the modern city spreading out toward the mountains.

The view also explains why a castle stands here at all. The river worked as a natural moat on one side. The hill gave height and a clear view over the plain. Over time, a town grew at the castle's feet, with merchants, craftsmen and samurai households. That town became the city you see today. In fact, the city takes its name from this low hill, which was called Okayama long before the castle was built.

Before you leave, walk slowly back toward the river. Notice how the castle changes as you move. Up close, it is heavy and dark, all timber, stone and steep roofs. From the bridge, it becomes lighter, a black shape with gold edges above the water. Many people take their best photograph from the far bank, with the river in the foreground and the keep framed by trees.

So stand still for a moment. Listen to the water, and to the gravel under other visitors' feet. The tower in front of you has been built, burned and built again. The stones beneath it carry the work of people who lived more than four hundred years ago. And the crow is still here, on its hill, watching the river go by.

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for the gravel paths and stairs.
