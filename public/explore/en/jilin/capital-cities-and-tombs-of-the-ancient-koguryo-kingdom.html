The guide

The story

Listen first. On a still morning here, you hear wind moving through pine needles and, somewhere below, water running over stones. Under your feet the ground rises in grassy humps and long, low ridges. They look natural at first glance. They are not. Many of these mounds are graves, and many of these ridges are walls. You are standing in a landscape that a vanished kingdom shaped with its own hands.

A quick word on geography, because the name can mislead. This site is not in Jilin City itself. It lies mainly around the small border town of Ji'an, in Jilin Province, with one more component across the provincial line in Huanren, Liaoning Province. Ji'an sits on the Yalu River. On the far bank is North Korea. That border position matters, because the kingdom remembered here reached across both sides of this river.

The kingdom is Koguryo, also written Goguryeo. It is one of the Three Kingdoms of early Korean history, and later the name Korea itself traces back to it. Tradition places its founding in the first century before Christ. For several hundred years, its rulers governed from this mountainous valley. Then, in the year 427, the capital moved south to Pyongyang. The kingdom finally fell in 668, defeated by an alliance of the Korean kingdom of Silla and Tang dynasty China.

UNESCO added these remains to the World Heritage List in 2004. The listing covers three fortified cities and a large group of tombs. Some are thought to belong to kings, and others to noble families. North Korea's Koguryo tombs were inscribed in the same year. Together, the two listings tell one story, now split by a modern border. Who owns that story is still debated between scholars in China and Korea. Here, you can simply look at what the builders left behind.

Start with the stone city of Guonei, sometimes written Gungnae. Its wall remains run through modern Ji'an. You may see sections of squared stone beside apartment blocks and ordinary streets. Most capitals of the ancient world are buried deep beneath their successors. Here, the old line of defence still shows above ground, and daily life simply continues around it. It is one of the quiet surprises of this place.

Guonei was the lowland capital, the place of palaces and markets. But Koguryo kings did not trust flat ground alone. Up in the hills stands Wandu Mountain City, a fortress that used the slopes themselves as defence. Its walls follow the ridgelines in a rough ring around a valley. The idea was simple. In peace, you lived below. In war, you climbed. The pairing of a plain city with a mountain refuge became a pattern in Koguryo planning.

The third city, Wunu Mountain City, lies near Huanren. It sits high on a flat-topped mountain with sheer cliffs on several sides. Many historians link it to the earliest years of the kingdom, possibly to its first capital. If you can make the journey, the climb gives you a clear sense of why this spot was chosen. From the top, the land drops away below, and few attackers could approach unseen.

Now, the tombs. The valley around Ji'an holds thousands of burials, from simple earth mounds to great stone structures. The most famous is known as the Tomb of the General. It is a stepped pyramid of cut granite blocks, rising in seven tiers. Some scholars attribute it to King Jangsu, the ruler who moved the capital south. That attribution is widely repeated, but it is not certain. Walk around its base and notice the huge stones leaning against the sides.

Those leaning stones are a second surprise. They are not decoration. They act as buttresses, set against the lowest tier to stop the structure from spreading outward under its own weight. It is a practical answer to a real engineering problem. Look closely at how tightly the upper blocks fit together. The builders worked without modern machinery, yet the tomb has held its shape for roughly fifteen centuries.

Not far away stands the stele of King Gwanggaeto, a tall block of rough stone, more than six metres high. His son raised it in the early fifth century. Its four faces carry well over a thousand carved Chinese characters. The text records the founding legend of the kingdom and lists Gwanggaeto's military campaigns. Today a protective pavilion surrounds it. For historians of early East Asia, this inscription is one of the most important written sources to survive.

Nearby is a large, collapsed stepped tomb, often linked to Gwanggaeto himself. Archaeologists found inscribed bricks here. The wording is often translated as a wish that the tomb of the great king would stay as secure as a mountain. Time had other plans. Much of the structure has slumped into a broad heap of stone. Standing here, you can compare it with the Tomb of the General and see what centuries of weather do.

Some Koguryo tombs hold painted chambers inside. The murals show hunters on horseback, dancers, household scenes, and guardian creatures of the four directions — a blue dragon, a white tiger, a red bird, and a black tortoise twined with a snake. These paintings are fragile. Air, light, and human breath can damage them, so access is often restricted. Even if you cannot enter, it helps to know that colour may survive in the darkness beneath the mounds around you.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Look at the soft green bumps across the valley floor, the line of hills, and the river moving toward the sea. Each mound is a person, or a family, from a kingdom that ended more than thirteen centuries ago. As you walk back toward town, watch the ground beside the road. A low ridge, a scattered block, a hump of grass... The old capital is still here, just under the surface.

Before you go

Practical tips

Base yourself in Ji'an, not Jilin City, to explore.
Plan a separate trip for Wunu Mountain City near Huanren.
Wear sturdy shoes for the climb at Wandu Mountain City.
Expect some mural tombs to be closed for conservation.
Visit Guonei wall remains on foot through Ji'an town.
