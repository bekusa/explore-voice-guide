The guide

The story

Listen first. Reeds move along the edge of the marsh. Frogs call from the shallow water. Somewhere close, a bird lifts out of the grass. The ground under your feet is low, soft and green. That is unusual in dry Attica, the region around Athens. You are standing in one of the quietest corners of the ancient Greek world. Long ago, this was a place where girls came to grow up.

This is Brauron, the sanctuary of Artemis Brauronia. It lies on the east coast of Attica, near the modern town of Markopoulo, in the gentle farmland called the Mesogaia, or the Midlands. Artemis was the goddess of wild animals, of hunting, and of the edges of life. She watched over birth, childhood, and the moment when a girl became a woman. Here, close to the sea and the wetland, the Athenians gave her one of their most important shrines.

Look toward the long line of columns. This is the stoa, a covered hall open on one side, built in the Doric style. Doric is the plainest of the Greek orders, with sturdy columns and simple, cushion-like tops. Archaeologists date this hall to the later fifth century before Christ. Part of it has been raised again from its original stones. Behind the columns, scholars believe there were rooms with couches. Some think the girls of the sanctuary slept and ate here.

Now for the strangest part of Brauron's story. Girls from Athenian families came here to serve Artemis. They were called arktoi... the little bears. Ancient writers and painted pottery tell us they ran races and danced. In Aristophanes' comedy Lysistrata, a woman remembers that she once "was a bear at Brauron", wearing a saffron-yellow robe. For many families, this service was an important step in a daughter's childhood.

Why bears? Here we move from history into legend. According to one popular tale, a tame bear lived in the sanctuary. A young girl teased it, and the bear scratched her. Her brothers killed the animal. Artemis was angry, and a plague fell on the land. The oracle said that Athenian girls must now "play the bear" for the goddess. Locals and scholars still debate what the ritual truly meant. The mystery is part of its power.

The archaeology gives this story a real shape. Excavators found small clay vessels, called krateriskoi, painted with girls running or dancing. Some girls are shown without clothes, some in short tunics. These pots are rare. They are some of the clearest pictures we have of a ritual for girls in ancient Greece. Many of them are now in the small museum just beside the site.

Here is something most visitors miss. The sanctuary sits right next to a spring. In ancient times, people dropped offerings into the water. Over the centuries, mud covered these gifts. Wet mud keeps away the air, so wood, metal and fabric traces survived far better than usual. The finds include bronze mirrors, jewellery, rings, and small statues. The marsh you hear around you has protected these things for more than two thousand years.

There is another layer of myth here. Greek tradition linked Brauron to Iphigenia, daughter of King Agamemnon. In Euripides' play Iphigenia in Tauris, the goddess Athena declares that Iphigenia will serve as priestess at Brauron, and be buried here. In the same story, Iphigenia and her brother Orestes bring an ancient statue of Artemis home from a distant land. This is legend, not history. But the ancients took it seriously, and a small area near the rock face was honoured as her tomb.

The same play contains a moving detail. It says that the clothes of women who died in childbirth would be dedicated to Iphigenia. Inscriptions from the period list garments that were given to Artemis at Brauron. Women offered them after a safe birth, as a thank-you to the goddess. Picture these robes, folded and stored, each one tied to a real woman's story. It gives this quiet place a very human weight.

Look for the low stone foundations of the temple of Artemis. It stood on a terrace above the stoa, against the rocky slope. Very little of the building rises now. Your eyes will need to rebuild it from the base lines. Nearby, you may also spot the remains of an ancient stone bridge over the stream. It is low, broad, and practical. People crossed here on foot, carrying gifts, much as you walked in today.

And then there is the small white chapel on the hill. It is dedicated to Saint George and dates from the Byzantine era, the long Christian centuries after antiquity. It is a reminder that holy places in Greece often stay holy. The gods change, but people keep returning to the same springs, the same rocks, the same quiet ground. Its simple shape against the sky is a fine counterpoint to the ancient columns below.

The modern rediscovery of Brauron came in the middle of the twentieth century. The Greek archaeologist Ioannis Papadimitriou led major excavations here from 1948. His team uncovered the stoa, the spring, and the treasures in the mud. Without that work, much of what we know about the little bears would still be under the marsh. Today the museum holds statues of children, some holding birds or small animals. Their faces are calm and serious, and very young.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the columns in the soft light, and the reeds beyond them. Listen to the wetland again. Girls once ran races on this ground, in yellow robes, under the eyes of their goddess. Mothers came here with gifts after the most dangerous day of their lives. As you walk back toward the road, notice the hills of the Mesogaia around you... the same low hills they saw on their journey home.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the site museum to see the girl statues and pottery.
Come early morning for cooler air and calmer marsh sounds.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground near the spring.
Bring insect repellent, since the wetland attracts mosquitoes.
Climb toward the chapel for a view over the whole site.
