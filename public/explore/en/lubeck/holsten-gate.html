The guide

The story

Stand on the stone paving for a moment and look up. Dark red brick fills your view, layered with bands of glazed black that catch the light when the sun moves. Two round towers rise on either side of a pointed archway, each one capped with a steep, cone-shaped roof. Gulls call from the river behind you. Bicycles rattle past over the cobbles. This is the Holsten Gate, the old western entrance to Lübeck, and it has stood here for more than five hundred years.

The gate was built in the second half of the fifteenth century and completed in 1478. In those years Lübeck was the leading city of the Hanseatic League. That was a powerful trading network of merchant towns around the Baltic and North Seas. Herring, salt, timber, cloth and grain moved through this harbour. A city this rich needed strong walls, and it wanted every traveller arriving from the west to understand that.

So the gate had two jobs. The first was defence. The second was to make an impression. Look at the side facing away from the old town, toward the west. The walls on this side are very thick, and they have gun ports instead of large windows. Anyone approaching along the road saw a closed, heavy front. The message was simple: this city can protect itself.

Now walk through the arch and turn around to face the city side. It looks quite different. Here there are more windows, with lighter and more open decoration. This side faced the citizens, not the enemy, so the builders could relax a little. Many visitors only photograph one face of the gate. It is worth seeing both, because together they show two ideas of the same city — guarded toward the world, and proud toward its own people.

The building material tells its own story. Northern Germany has very little good building stone, but it has plenty of clay. So the cities of the Baltic coast built their churches, town halls and gates in fired brick. Historians call this style Brick Gothic. It is the northern answer to the stone cathedrals of France. The pointed arches and tall proportions are Gothic, but the colour and texture are entirely local.

Look closely at the bands running across the towers. These are friezes made of terracotta, which is moulded and fired clay. They repeat a pattern of small decorative shapes, almost like embroidery on a dark red coat. Between them, the black glazed bricks form stripes. On a grey northern day the gate looks heavy and serious. When sunlight strikes it, the glaze shines and the whole surface seems to come alive.

Here is a detail many people miss. Stand back and look carefully at the towers. They are not perfectly straight. The ground here is soft and marshy, close to the river, and over the centuries the heavy brick has slowly sunk into it. Parts of the gate lean and tilt. Engineers in later periods worked to stabilise the foundations, but the slight unevenness remains. Once you notice it, it is hard to stop seeing it.

And here is a second surprise. In the nineteenth century, the gate was nearly torn down. Lübeck was growing, a railway arrived nearby, and many people saw the old fortifications as a barrier to traffic and progress. Other parts of the city defences were removed. The Holsten Gate survived only after a fierce public debate. According to the often-told account, the city council voted to keep it by a very narrow margin. Restoration work followed in the decades after.

During that restoration period, an inscription was added to the city side in gold letters. It reads, in Latin, Concordia domi foris pax. In English, that means harmony within, peace outside. It is a short motto, but it sums up what a trading city wanted most. Merchants needed calm streets at home and safe routes abroad. On the field side you will also find the letters S P Q L, a Latin abbreviation for the senate and people of Lübeck.

Today the gate holds a museum about the city and its Hanseatic past. Inside, the rooms are narrower and dimmer than you might expect from the outside. That is a reminder that this was a working fortification, not a palace. The displays deal with trade, ships, and life in the medieval port. If you go in, notice the thickness of the walls around the window openings. You can feel how much brick stands between you and the street.

The gate became a symbol of Lübeck, and later of Germany more widely. For many years it appeared on the old fifty Deutsche Mark banknote. More recently it was shown on a German two euro coin. Many people in Germany recognise its silhouette even if they have never visited the city. It also stands at the edge of Lübeck's old town, which is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site.

Before you leave, look past the gate toward the river Trave. Along the water stand the old salt storehouses, tall brick buildings with stepped gables. Salt was one of the most valuable goods in the Hanseatic trade, because it preserved fish for long journeys and hard winters. Beyond them, the island of the old town begins, with its church spires rising above the rooftops. The gate was the doorway to all of this.

If you walk on into the old town, you will cross the bridge and enter streets of narrow merchant houses. Many of them were built in the same brick tradition as the gate. Lübeck is also known for marzipan, and you will likely smell sugar and almonds from shop windows before long. It is a small, walkable centre, and the gate sets the tone for everything inside it.

For now, take one more look. The bricks under your hand were shaped and fired by people more than five centuries ago. The towers lean a little, the gold letters shine, and the gulls still circle over the harbour. Traders once passed through this arch with carts of salt and cloth. Today it is you passing through. Listen to the bicycles, feel the cobbles under your feet, and carry that picture with you into the old town.
