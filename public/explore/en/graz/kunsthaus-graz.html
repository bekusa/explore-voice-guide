The guide

The story

Stand on the riverbank and look up. A huge blue form sits among the red-tiled roofs, smooth and swollen, like something that landed here overnight. Its skin is made of glassy acrylic panels, and when clouds move overhead, the surface shifts from deep blue to grey to almost black. Small round snouts rise from the top. Behind you, the river Mur runs fast and green. This is the Kunsthaus Graz, the city's house for contemporary art, and locals call it the Friendly Alien.

The nickname fits. From almost any angle, the building looks soft, curious, and slightly out of place. It has no flat walls and no obvious front door. It seems to lean towards the street, as if it wants to see who is passing. Yet it does not feel threatening. Children point at it. Older residents, who once argued about it loudly, now give directions by it. In a city of baroque towers and Renaissance courtyards, this blue shape has become one of the easiest landmarks to find.

The building opened in 2003, the year Graz served as European Capital of Culture. That title is a European Union programme that gives one or two cities each year a spotlight and a budget for cultural projects. Graz used its year to make a bold statement. The city wanted to show that a place known for its old town could also take risks. The Kunsthaus was the boldest of those risks — a building that nobody could mistake for anything else.

Two architects designed it: Peter Cook and Colin Fournier, both based in London. Cook is the more famous name. In the 1960s, he was a founder of Archigram, a group of young British architects who drew walking cities, plug-in towers, and buildings that looked like machines or living creatures. For decades, almost none of those ideas were built. They stayed on paper, in magazines and exhibitions. Here in Graz, one of those paper dreams finally became concrete, steel, and acrylic.

Look at where the building stands. The old town, with its red roofs and church spires, sits on the other side of the river. That historic centre is protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. This bank, the Lend district, was traditionally the working side of the city — a place of traders, craftsmen, small workshops, and travellers arriving from the west. Putting a daring art building here was a deliberate choice. It pulled attention, and people, across the water.

Now look at the left edge of the blue skin. Here is the first surprise many visitors miss. The Alien did not land on an empty site. It wraps around an older building, the so-called Iron House, built in the middle of the nineteenth century. That earlier structure was one of the early buildings in the region to use a cast-iron frame, which was modern technology at the time. So this corner holds two moments of daring, separated by more than a century, joined in a single complex.

Up on the roof, notice the round openings that point in different directions. They are skylights, sometimes called nozzles. Most of them face north. That direction gives steady, soft daylight without harsh sun, which is gentle on artworks. One nozzle is often said to point towards the Schlossberg, the wooded castle hill across the river, with its old clock tower. Whether or not that framing was the main intention, the alignment is real, and it ties the new building to the oldest view in Graz.

After dark, the second surprise appears. Behind the acrylic panels on the side facing the river, there is a grid of circular fluorescent lights. Together they form a huge, low-resolution screen called BIX, created by a Berlin design studio. Each ring works like a single pixel. The images are slow and simple — words, patterns, soft pulses. Artists have used this facade for their own works. On some evenings, the building looks like it is breathing in light across the water.

Inside, the experience changes again. A long moving walkway carries you up from the ground floor into the belly of the building. You rise slowly, almost like entering a cave or a ship. The upper exhibition spaces are dark and open, with few fixed walls, so each show can shape the room in its own way. This makes the Kunsthaus a difficult space for curators — and that difficulty is part of the point. Artists must answer the building, not just hang pictures in it.

The Kunsthaus does not own a permanent collection on display. Instead, it presents changing exhibitions of contemporary art, mostly from the last decades, from Austria and abroad. It is part of the Universalmuseum Joanneum, a large museum group in Styria, the Austrian region around Graz. That group is one of the oldest public museums in Austria. So this newest-looking building belongs to a very old institution, another quiet contrast between surface and history.

At the top of the building, a long glass gallery runs out towards the river. It is called the Needle. From here, the view opens across the Mur to the old town roofs and the Schlossberg. This is a good place to slow down. Inside, the blue skin feels strange and close. Through the glass, the city feels familiar and old. Standing between the two, you can understand what the architects wanted — a conversation, not a replacement.

Not everyone loved the Alien at first. Many people in Graz found it too strange, too loud, too foreign for a careful historic city. Over time, opinion softened. The building now appears on postcards, in school projects, and in almost every travel photo of the riverbank. Just downstream, you can also see the Murinsel, an artificial steel island in the river built for the same culture year. Together, they show how 2003 changed the way Graz looks at itself.

Before you leave, step back to the river path once more. Watch how the light moves across the blue panels as clouds pass. Listen to the trams on the street and the rush of the water below the embankment. Then turn towards the old town and look at the clock tower on its hill. Two very different ideas of a city stand in the same view. As you walk away, notice how quickly the Alien starts to feel like it belongs.

Before you go

Practical tips

Return after dark to see the BIX light facade.
Ride the moving walkway slowly to feel the entry.
Pause in the Needle gallery for old town views.
Photograph from the opposite riverbank for the full shape.
Combine your visit with a walk to the nearby Murinsel.
