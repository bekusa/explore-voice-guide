The guide

The story

Push open the heavy door and the noise of the old town falls away. The air inside is cool and still. It smells faintly of candle wax and old stone. Your eyes need a moment to adjust... and then the gold begins to appear. Altars, frames, and carved figures catch the light from the tall windows. Outside, the walls look plain, almost severe. Inside, the church is full of colour. That contrast is the first story this building tells.

You are standing in the Cathedral of Saint Giles, the main church of Graz. Saint Giles was a hermit from southern France. In the Middle Ages, people prayed to him for help with sickness and hardship. The building did not begin as a cathedral. It began as a court church. It was the private place of worship for a Habsburg ruler who lived next door. The Habsburgs were the family that ruled Austria and much of central Europe for centuries.

That ruler was Frederick the Third. In the fifteenth century, he made Graz one of his favourite residences. He built his castle, the Burg, just beside this church. He wanted a house of God that matched his ambitions. The result was this late Gothic hall. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches and tall windows that reach toward the light. Work ran through the middle decades of the fifteenth century.

Frederick left a signature here, and it is easy to miss. Look for five letters: A, E, I, O, U. They appear on the building, including around the west entrance. Frederick marked many of his buildings and possessions with these vowels. Nobody knows for certain what they mean. Scholars and storytellers have offered dozens of readings. The most popular is a Latin phrase that suggests Austria is destined to rule the world. Frederick never fully explained it himself.

Now walk around to the south side of the church, facing the street. There, on the outer wall, is a large and faded painting. Locals call it the Painting of the Divine Plagues. It was made in the late fifteenth century, and it is usually attributed to an artist known as Thomas of Villach. It shows three disasters that struck this region around 1480. A swarm of locusts. An outbreak of plague. And raids by Ottoman armies from the south-east.

Stand in front of it for a moment. The colours are weak now, and parts are hard to read. But this is a rare thing — a picture painted by people who lived through those events. It is not a memory recorded generations later. It is something close to news. Look for the small view of Graz included in the scene. It is one of the earliest known images of the city. Many visitors walk past this wall without stopping.

Back inside, notice how the space is built. The nave is tall, and the ceiling is covered in a fine web of stone ribs. This is called net vaulting. The ribs cross and divide the ceiling into many small shapes, like a fishing net held overhead. This is the medieval skeleton of the church. Almost everything you see attached to it — the altars, the pulpit, the gilded statues — came much later.

That later layer arrived with the Jesuits. In the late sixteenth century, the church was handed to this Catholic religious order. The Jesuits were teachers and preachers. They were also central to the Counter-Reformation, the Catholic effort to win back people who had turned Protestant. In Graz, that struggle was intense. The Jesuits used art as a tool. They filled the church with Baroque decoration. Baroque is a style full of movement, gold, and drama, designed to move the heart.

The church became a cathedral only in the late eighteenth century. The bishop of this region had his seat at Seckau, a quieter place to the north-west. In the 1780s, under the reforms of Emperor Joseph the Second, the seat moved to Graz. The Jesuits had been dissolved a few years earlier. So the former court church, later the Jesuit church, finally became the cathedral. One building, three different identities, across about three and a half centuries.

Now look toward the front of the church, where the nave meets the choir. On either side of the great arch, you may see two decorated chests. They are often called the bridal chests. They are linked to Paola Gonzaga, a noblewoman from Mantua in Italy, who married into an Austrian noble house in the fifteenth century. They were made in Italy in the early Renaissance. Their panels show scenes inspired by the poet Petrarch. Today they serve as reliquaries — containers for holy relics. A wedding gift became an object of faith.

Take a slow walk along the side chapels. Each altar has its own saints, its own painted story, and its own patrons. Some are dark with age. Others shine where restorers have cleaned them. Listen to the sounds around you. Footsteps carry a long way on the stone floor. Visitors tend to whisper here. On many days, someone will be sitting quietly in a pew. This remains a working church, not only a museum.

When you step outside, look at the buildings that crowd close to the cathedral. Directly beside it stands the Mausoleum of Ferdinand the Second, with its domes and pale façade. Ferdinand was a Habsburg emperor born in Graz, and he chose to be buried here. Just a short walk away is the Burg, Frederick's old castle. Inside it is a famous double spiral staircase in stone, built around 1500. Two stairways wind around each other, separating and meeting again. Together, these buildings form the old centre of Habsburg power in the city.

Before you leave, turn back once more toward the cathedral. Notice how quiet the outside is compared to the gold inside. Frederick's vowels, the faded plagues on the south wall, the Jesuit gold — they all share one roof. Above you, the small streets climb toward the Schlossberg, the wooded hill that rises over the old town. As you walk on, listen for the bells. They have marked the hours of this neighbourhood for a very long time.

What to look for

Gothic net vaulting above the gilded Baroque altars.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk around the south wall before going inside.
Keep voices low; services may be taking place.
Combine your visit with the neighbouring Ferdinand Mausoleum.
Visit the nearby Burg to see the double spiral staircase.
Bring binoculars or zoom to study the faded fresco.
