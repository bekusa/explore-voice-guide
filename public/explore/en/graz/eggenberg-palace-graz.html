The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere across the lawn a peacock gives a sharp, almost human cry. The air smells of cut grass and old trees. Ahead of you, a long pale facade rises from the park, square and calm, with a tower at each corner. Rows of tall windows catch the light. You are on the western edge of Graz, the capital of Styria in southern Austria, standing in front of Eggenberg Palace.

This building did not begin as a palace. An older family seat stood here first, and its medieval core was kept and wrapped inside the new design. The man behind the transformation was Hans Ulrich von Eggenberg. He rose from a Styrian family of merchants and bankers to become one of the most powerful statesmen at the court of Emperor Ferdinand the Second, in the early seventeenth century. Work on the new residence began in the 1620s.

Think about what that rise meant. Hans Ulrich was a trusted adviser to the Habsburgs, the dynasty that ruled much of central Europe. He was raised to the rank of prince. A man of that standing needed a house that spoke clearly about his place in the world. So he did not ask for a simple country seat. He asked for something closer to a model of the universe, built in stone and plaster.

That is the first surprise most visitors miss. The palace is planned as a kind of calendar. It is commonly described this way: four corner towers for the four seasons, three hundred and sixty-five windows on the outside for the days of the year, and twenty-four state rooms on the main floor for the hours of the day and night. You do not need to count every window. Just notice how ordered everything is. Nothing on this facade feels random.

The architect most closely linked to the design is Giovanni Pietro de Pomis, an Italian-trained artist who worked for the Habsburg court in Graz. You can feel that Italian influence in the strict symmetry and the inner courtyards with their stacked arcades. Step into a courtyard if you can. The noise of the park drops away. The arches repeat in steady rhythm, and the light falls in clean rectangles on the stone.

The heart of the cosmic idea waits upstairs, on the main floor. Here a chain of state rooms runs around the building, one opening into the next. The doors line up in a long straight axis, so you can look through room after room at once. This arrangement is called an enfilade — a row of rooms with aligned doorways. Walking through it feels like moving through time, hour by hour.

The largest of these rooms is the Planetary Hall. Its ceiling and walls carry a cycle of paintings by Hans Adam Weissenkircher, a Baroque painter who worked for the family in the late seventeenth century. The pictures show the planets as known at the time, along with the signs of the zodiac and the elements. Baroque, in simple terms, is the dramatic, richly decorated style of that era. Here it serves one idea: the Eggenberg family placed at the centre of an ordered cosmos.

Now the second surprise. In one of the state rooms hangs a painted Japanese screen showing the city of Osaka and its castle in the early seventeenth century. Very few images of Osaka from that period survive, even in Japan. How it travelled to a Styrian palace is part of the long story of European fascination with East Asian art. Today scholars from Japan come here to study it. It is a small object with a very long journey behind it.

It is worth knowing that the rooms you see are not frozen in the 1600s. Later generations of owners redecorated in the eighteenth century, adding wall coverings, stoves, and furnishings in newer fashions. The Eggenberg line itself died out, and the estate passed to other noble families. Each left a layer. So as you walk, you are reading several centuries of taste at once, one room laid over another.

Today the palace belongs to the province of Styria and is run by the Universalmuseum Joanneum, the large regional museum. Several collections live here. There is a coin cabinet, a picture gallery of older European art, and an archaeology museum. One famous piece in that archaeology collection is the Strettweg Cult Wagon, a small bronze vehicle with figures, found in Styria and dating back to the early Iron Age. It is much older than anything else on this site.

In 2010, the palace was added to the UNESCO World Heritage listing for the historic centre of Graz. This was an extension of the existing listing for the old town. The reasoning is simple. The palace and the city centre belong to the same story — the period when Graz was an important Habsburg seat. The old town shows the city. This building shows the ambition of the people who held power there.

Before you leave, give the park some time. It was reshaped many times, from formal Baroque gardens to the looser, landscaped style of later centuries. In recent decades a Planet Garden was created, echoing the theme of the house. Wide lawns open between old trees. Peacocks wander freely, sometimes spreading their tails on the paths. Families picnic on the grass. Runners pass on the outer loop. The palace is a museum, but the park still feels like a neighbourhood space.

So stand still for a moment and look back at the facade. The towers mark the corners. The windows step across in their patient rows. A prince planned this house as a picture of time itself — seasons, days, and hours, all held in one building. Four centuries later, the light still moves across those same windows, one by one, through the day. As you walk out toward the city, listen once more. The gravel. The trees. And somewhere behind you, a peacock calling across the lawn.
