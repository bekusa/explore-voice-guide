The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the concrete. It is grey, rough, and enormous, and it runs away from you in long stepped walls. The sun bounces off it with real force. A light wind crosses the open park around it, carrying the smell of cut grass and warm stone. Somewhere behind you, traffic hums on the avenues of eastern Santo Domingo. Then you look up, and the building just keeps going. This is the Columbus Lighthouse. Locals call it El Faro a Colón.

Before any history, take in the shape. This is not a lighthouse in the usual sense. There is no slender tower and no lamp room at the top. Instead, the building lies low along the ground, like a vast stepped pyramid stretched out lengthwise. From the air, it forms a giant cross. That cross is the whole idea. The monument was designed so that its shape, and the light it throws upward, would make a cross visible from the sky.

The story begins far earlier than the concrete. In the late 1920s, an international design competition invited architects around the world to imagine a memorial to Christopher Columbus. Entries arrived from many countries. The winner was a young British architect named J. L. Gleave. His design was bold and severe, with none of the decoration you might expect for a monument to an explorer. Then the project stalled for decades. Money, politics, and changing governments kept it on paper.

Construction finally began in the 1980s, under President Joaquín Balaguer. He pushed hard to finish it in time for a symbolic date. October 1992 marked five hundred years since Columbus first reached the Americas in 1492. The lighthouse opened for that anniversary. So a design from the age of jazz and early aviation was completed in the age of satellite television. Keep that gap in mind as you walk. It explains why the building feels like it belongs to no single era.

Now for the light itself. Along the roof and edges, the builders installed well over a hundred powerful lamps, pointing straight up. When they are switched on, they project a cross of white beams onto the clouds above the city. On a clear night, people across Santo Domingo can see it. But here is a detail many visitors never hear. The lamps draw so much electricity that turning them on became controversial. In a country with frequent power cuts, many residents resented the cost. Today the lights are switched on only rarely.

That tension was there from the beginning. When the monument opened in 1992, it drew criticism as well as celebration. Some people objected to the cost of the project at a time of hardship. Others objected to the very idea of honouring Columbus. His arrival brought violence, disease, and enslavement to the Taíno, the Indigenous people of this island. The building stands at the centre of that debate. It is a monument, but it is also an argument made of concrete.

Walk toward the heart of the cross, where the two arms meet. Here you find the most surprising thing about this place. Inside sits an ornate mausoleum, decorated in marble and bronze. According to the Dominican Republic, it holds the remains of Christopher Columbus himself. The contrast is striking. Around you, everything is plain and grey. In the centre, there is gleaming stone and carved detail, like a jewel box placed inside a fortress.

But there is a twist, and it is a good one. Seville, in Spain, also claims to have Columbus. His bones have travelled a great deal since his death in 1506. They moved from Spain to Santo Domingo in the sixteenth century, to rest in the city's cathedral. When Spain gave up this part of the island in the 1790s, remains were shipped to Havana, and later to Seville. Then, in 1877, workers in the Santo Domingo cathedral found a lead box with an inscription naming Columbus. Both cities have defended their claim ever since.

Who is right? Nobody can fully say. Researchers tested the bones in Seville in the 2000s and reported that at least some of them likely belong to Columbus. The Dominican side has not allowed the same kind of testing here. One possibility is that both cities are partly right, and the explorer's remains were divided along the way. Whatever you believe, the tomb in front of you was moved here from the old cathedral when the lighthouse opened. Uniformed guards often stand watch beside it.

The long arms of the building hold more than the tomb. Inside are exhibition halls, where many countries from the Americas and beyond have displayed objects about their culture and history. The rooms vary a lot in style and in condition. Some hold costumes, maps, or crafts. Others feel quieter and more sparse. Wander slowly. Part of the pleasure is the mix, and the feeling of a building that tried to gather a whole hemisphere under one roof.

Step back outside and look at the walls again. The stepped sides rise toward the centre like a man-made canyon. Sunlight cuts sharp shadows across each ledge. If you walk along the long axis, the scale changes as you move. Up close, the concrete feels heavy and almost harsh. From a distance, the lines become clean and graphic. Photographers love the hard geometry here, especially in early morning or late afternoon, when the shadows stretch long.

The lighthouse also sits in a particular part of the city. It stands east of the Ozama River, in Santo Domingo Este, inside a broad park. Across the river lies the Colonial Zone, the oldest European-built city in the Americas, with its narrow streets and old stone churches. That is where Columbus's remains rested for centuries. The two sides of the river tell one story in two very different voices. One is intimate and old. The other is monumental and modern.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at one end of the long arm and look down its length. Notice how the walls lead your eye toward the centre, where the tomb waits. Listen to the wind, the birds in the park, the distant traffic. Think about the cross of light that sometimes appears above this spot on dark nights. Whatever you feel about Columbus, this building asks you to feel something. As you walk away, turn back once. Watch how the grey mass settles into the green park, quiet and enormous, holding its argument in place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for softer light and cooler temperatures.
Bring water and sun protection for the open park.
Combine your visit with the Colonial Zone across the river.
Walk the full length of one arm for perspective.
Ask locally whether the roof lights are scheduled to shine.
