The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, gravel crunches softly. Somewhere beyond the hedges, sheep call across the hills. Then the castle appears: a low, broad mass of grey stone, its round towers squatting on the ridge as if they grew from the rock. It does not soar like a fairytale tower. It settles. It looks heavy, patient, and very hard to argue with. That was the point. Chirk Castle was built to watch a border, and seven centuries later it still holds the high ground over the Welsh hills.

You are a short drive from Llangollen here, close to the small town of Chirk and very near the line between Wales and England. That location explains almost everything about this place. In the late thirteenth century, King Edward the First of England was completing his conquest of Wales. He ringed the country with castles. Chirk was part of that wider effort. It was built by one of his loyal lords, Roger Mortimer de Chirk, and work was finished in the early fourteenth century.

Mortimer was a Marcher lord. That term needs a word of explanation. The March was the frontier zone between England and Wales. Lords here held unusual power. They kept their own courts and their own armies, and they answered only loosely to the crown. A Marcher castle was a statement of control over a restless landscape. Look at the thick walls and the heavy drum towers. They were built for defence first and comfort a very distant second.

Now for the first surprise. Unlike most of the great Welsh castles from this period, Chirk never fell into ruin. Many of its sisters became empty shells, open to the sky. This one has been lived in, more or less continuously, for around seven hundred years. So as you walk through, you are seeing medieval stone wrapped around later rooms. Tudor windows, grand eighteenth century interiors, and modern repairs all sit inside the same walls.

The family most tied to this castle arrived at the end of the sixteenth century. Sir Thomas Myddelton was a London merchant, a man who made his fortune in trade. He bought Chirk in 1595 and later served as Lord Mayor of London. His descendants stayed here for roughly four centuries. It is a strange picture, when you think about it. A fortress built by a warrior lord to hold a frontier became the family home of a city businessman and his heirs.

The seventeenth century brought the castle back to war. During the English Civil War, the Myddeltons sided with Parliament, while the castle itself was held for a time by Royalist forces. Loyalties shifted as the conflict went on. Later, the family became involved in a failed rising in 1659, and the castle was attacked and damaged. Look closely at the stonework as you go round. Some sections were rebuilt after that damage, and the repairs show slightly different stone and finish.

Inside, the mood changes completely. The state rooms are warm with timber, plaster, tapestry, and paint. A long gallery stretches out, made for walking indoors on wet Welsh days and for showing off family portraits. Ceilings carry decorative plasterwork. Furniture, clocks, and paintings gathered over generations fill the rooms. It feels less like a museum and more like a house where people actually argued, dined, and wrote letters by the window.

And then, the second surprise. Beneath all that elegance lies the older castle. In the Adam Tower, steps lead down to a dark, chilled space. This is the dungeon. The air feels damp, and daylight barely reaches it. Standing there for a moment, you feel the original purpose of this building. Above you is polished wood and painted plaster. Below, there is only cold stone and silence.

Many visitors remember one thing above all else: the iron gates. They stand at the edge of the estate, a vast screen of wrought iron covered in scrolls, leaves, and heraldic decoration. They were made in the early eighteenth century by the Davies brothers, skilled smiths from nearby Bersham. Up close, the work looks almost like lace. It is hard to believe it was hammered from hot metal by hand.

The gardens are the other great pleasure here. Clipped yew hedges stand in solid green walls and rounded shapes, kept neat by patient gardeners year after year. Walk on and the formal lines soften into lawns, borders, and open views. On a clear day you can look out over several counties. In spring and summer, the beds fill with colour and the air carries the smell of cut grass and flowers.

The landscape holds an even older border than the castle. Offa's Dyke, an earthwork named after an eighth century king of Mercia, crosses this estate. Mercia was an Anglo-Saxon kingdom in what is now central England. The dyke was a long bank and ditch marking a frontier with the Welsh. So two borders meet here, centuries apart. One is made of earth, the other of stone.

Today the castle is cared for by the National Trust, a charity that protects historic places in Britain. It took over the property in the early 1980s. Since then, the estate has become a place for slow wandering. Parkland, old trees, and grazing animals surround the buildings. The nearby Llangollen Canal and its aqueduct at Chirk are worth adding to your day if you want to see another side of this valley.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Look at the towers again from the outside. Notice how the stone changes colour as the clouds move, from pale grey to almost silver. Listen for the wind across the ridge, and for the sheep below. This was once a line between peoples, guarded with walls and soldiers. Now it is a quiet hilltop where visitors walk, talk, and look out over Wales and England at the same time.
