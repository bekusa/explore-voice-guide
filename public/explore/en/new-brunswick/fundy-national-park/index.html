The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below the trees, water is moving. It might be a stream running over mossy stones, or it might be the sea, pulling back across a wide floor of mud and pebbles. Here on the edge of the Bay of Fundy, the air often smells of salt and spruce at the same time. Cool fog can drift in from the water, soften the hills, and leave small beads of moisture on every needle and fern.

Welcome to Fundy National Park, on the southern coast of New Brunswick in eastern Canada. The park sits where two very different worlds meet. On one side are the rolling, forested Caledonia Highlands. On the other is one of the most unusual bodies of water on Earth. Canada established the park in 1948, and it has drawn walkers, campers and curious travellers ever since. You do not need to hike far to feel both worlds at once.

Let's start with the water, because it is the reason most people come. The Bay of Fundy has some of the highest tides in the world. Along this stretch of coast, the difference between low tide and high tide can be more than ten metres. That is roughly the height of a three-storey building. And it happens twice a day, every day. The sea floods in, then it drains away again, and the shoreline you stand on now will look very different in a few hours.

Why are the tides so extreme here? The answer lies in the shape of the bay. It is long and narrow, and it funnels toward its head. Scientists explain that the natural rhythm of water sloshing in the bay closely matches the rhythm of the ocean tides. The two movements reinforce each other — a little like pushing a child on a swing at exactly the right moment. Each push builds on the last, and the water climbs higher and higher.

Here is something many visitors miss. At low tide, you can often walk out across the exposed sea floor at places like Herring Cove or near the village of Alma. You may find periwinkles, barnacles and seaweed clinging to rocks that sat under deep water only hours before. But the tide returns quickly. Always check the tide times before you go, and keep an eye on the water as you explore. The bay is generous, but it does not wait.

Now turn toward the land. Behind the coast, the park climbs into the Caledonia Highlands, covered in what Canadians call the Acadian forest. This is a mixed forest, a blend of northern conifers and broad-leaved hardwoods. You will see red spruce and balsam fir beside maple, birch and beech. In autumn, the hardwoods turn red, orange and gold against the dark green of the conifers. Even in summer, the mix of light and shade feels layered and deep.

This forest has a human story too. Long before it became a park, the area was logged heavily, and small settlements grew around sawmills and shipbuilding. Point Wolfe, in the park's southwest, was once a working lumber community near the mouth of a river. Today you can cross a red covered bridge there and look down into the gorge. Covered bridges are a familiar sight in New Brunswick. The roof protected the wooden deck from rain and snow, so the bridge lasted longer.

The people of this region have worked with the sea for generations. Just outside the park entrance, the village of Alma is a small fishing community. Lobster boats rest in its harbour, and at low tide some of them sit tilted on the mud, waiting for the water to lift them again. It is one of the clearest pictures of the tides you will ever see. If you visit the wharf, notice how tall the pilings are. They have to be.

Walk deeper into the park and you will find water of another kind. Many short trails lead to waterfalls tucked into steep, mossy ravines. Dickson Falls is one of the most visited. A boardwalk and wooden stairs carry you down through a cool, damp hollow, where ferns grow thick and the air feels several degrees cooler. The sound of falling water fills the space. Moss covers fallen logs so completely that they look like soft green sculptures.

The park also protects animals that depend on this meeting of forest and sea. Moose live in the highlands, and early morning or evening is the best time to spot one near wetlands. White-tailed deer, beavers and many songbirds share the forest. The rivers here are also part of an important conservation effort. Inner Bay of Fundy Atlantic salmon became endangered, and the park has worked with partners to help these fish return to their home streams.

Here is a second surprise. Fundy is not only a place for daytime. The park has been recognised as a Dark Sky Preserve, which means it works to limit artificial light. On a clear night, away from towns, the sky fills with stars. You may see the faint band of the Milky Way stretching overhead. The same fog that rolls in by day can lift at night, and the darkness here is real darkness.

The wider landscape matters as well. Fundy National Park lies within the Fundy Biosphere Reserve, a UNESCO designation. A biosphere reserve is a region where people try to balance nature protection with everyday life and work. So the park is not an island. It is connected to the fishing villages, farms and forests around it. The way locals fish, build and travel here is part of the same story as the trees and tides.

Take a moment now, wherever you are standing. If you are near the shore, look at the line of seaweed and wet rock. It marks where the water reached not long ago, and where it will reach again. If you are under the trees, notice the smell of resin and damp earth. When you walk away, listen once more for the water. Here, it is always moving, always on its way in or out.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check daily tide times before walking the sea floor.
Visit the shore at low and high tide.
Look for moose near wetlands at dawn.
Bring layers for sudden coastal fog and cool air.
Stay after dark on clear nights for stargazing.
