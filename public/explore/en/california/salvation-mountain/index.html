The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, on the edge of the Colorado Desert, there is almost no sound. Maybe a little wind moving dust across the road, maybe a truck far away. Then you turn, and a hillside of colour rises out of the beige ground. Red, pink, blue, yellow, green. It looks almost unreal against the pale sky, like a child's painting set down in the middle of the desert.

This is Salvation Mountain, near the small town of Niland in Imperial County, Southern California. It is not a natural mountain painted over. It is a hand-built hill of adobe clay, straw, and paint, shaped over many years by one man. His name was Leonard Knight. At the centre, in large letters, is the message he wanted everyone to see — God Is Love.

Knight was born in the early 1930s and grew up in Vermont. By his own account, he was an ordinary working man who drifted through odd jobs. Then, in middle age, he had a religious experience that changed the direction of his life. He did not join a church or a movement. Instead, he wanted to tell the world one simple idea, in the biggest way he could imagine.

The story he liked to tell begins in the sky. Knight first tried to sew and fly a huge hot-air balloon with that same message, God Is Love. The balloon never really flew. Over the years it rotted and failed. By the mid-1980s he had found his way to this corner of the desert. He planned to stay a short time and leave behind a small monument. He stayed for nearly three decades.

The first version of the mountain did not survive. Knight built it with sand and cement, and it collapsed. Many people would have given up there. He did not. He started again, this time with adobe — a mix of local clay, water, and straw, the same kind of material people have used for building in dry lands for thousands of years. Then he covered it with paint. Layer after layer after layer.

Here is something many visitors miss. The paint is not only decoration. It is part of the structure. The thick coats help seal the clay against the sun and the rare desert rain. Much of the paint was donated. People drove out with cans in their trunks, and Knight used whatever colours arrived. That is one reason the mountain looks the way it does — bright, mixed, a little unplanned.

Now look closely at the surface. You can see flowers, birds, waterfalls, trees, and a river of blue running down the slope. You can see words from the Bible and a short prayer painted across the face. There is a large heart near the centre. Everywhere, the brushwork is simple and direct. Knight had no formal training as an artist. He painted the way someone writes a letter to a friend.

Walk around to the side, and the mountain becomes something else. Knight built a structure he called the museum, a series of rooms and domes made from straw bales, adobe, old tyres, car windows, and branches from desert trees. Inside, the light falls through gaps and openings onto painted walls. It feels like the inside of a shell, or a cave. This part surprises many people who only know the famous front view.

For years, Knight lived here alone, sleeping in an old truck parked beside his work. The truck itself is painted, like everything else he touched. He welcomed visitors, talked with them, and gave small tours. People remember him as cheerful and patient. He asked for nothing except, sometimes, more paint. In 2011 his health declined, and he moved into a care home. He died in 2014.

The mountain did not always have friends in official places. At one point, county officials raised concerns about the site and its paint, and there was serious talk of removing it. Supporters pushed back, and the mountain stayed. Over time, folk art organisations recognised it as an important work of outsider art — art made by people working outside galleries and schools. Its story even reached the United States Congress, where it was praised in the official record.

Look past the mountain, toward the road behind you. Just beyond is Slab City, a community of trailers, camps, and makeshift homes. It grew on the concrete slabs left behind by a former military training base. People come here for many reasons — freedom, low costs, art, or simply to be left alone. Salvation Mountain sits at its gate, like a front door. Many people also recognise this place from the 2007 film Into the Wild.

Beyond Slab City, a few kilometres to the west, lies the Salton Sea, a large and very salty lake in a desert basin. On a clear day you may feel the heavy, slightly mineral air that drifts from that direction. It is a strange, quiet landscape — and it makes the colour of the mountain even stronger. Nothing else here is painted. Nothing else here tries so hard to be seen.

Since Knight's death, volunteers and a nonprofit group have looked after the site. The desert is hard on paint and clay. Sun cracks the surface. Wind wears it down. Keeping the mountain alive means constant repair, the same work Knight did every day. When you see fresh patches of colour, you are looking at that care in action. Please stay on the marked yellow path, so the fragile surface lasts.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the bottom of the slope. Look up at the words, then at the empty desert all around. One man, with clay, straw, and donated paint, made this. As you walk back to the road, notice how quickly the colour drops behind you... and how the quiet of the desert returns.

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strongest desert heat.
Bring plenty of drinking water for the dry desert air.
Stay on the yellow path to protect the painted surface.
Bring a can of paint if you wish to donate.
Wear sun protection; shade near the mountain is limited.
