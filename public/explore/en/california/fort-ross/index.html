The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the bluff, the Pacific breaks against dark rock, and the wind carries salt and the dry scent of grass. Fog often sits on the water here, grey and soft, then lifts to show a long coastline with almost no buildings on it. In front of you, a wall of upright redwood timbers stands against that open sky. It is a strange sight on the California coast. This is a Russian settlement, built far from Russia, at the edge of an empire.

Welcome to Fort Ross. The settlement was founded in 1812 by the Russian-American Company, a trading company that operated under the Russian crown. At that time the company already held colonies in Alaska. Those northern outposts had a serious problem: food. The Alaskan climate made farming very hard, and supply ships from Siberia were slow and unreliable. The company needed a place farther south where it could grow grain and raise cattle. This sheltered stretch of coast became that place.

The man who led the founding was Ivan Kuskov, a senior company official. He arrived with a party of Russians and a much larger group of Native Alaskans, mostly Alutiiq and Unangan hunters who were skilled in kayaks. The name of the settlement comes from an old word for Russia itself. Over time, people simply called it Fort Ross. For nearly three decades, this small enclosure was the southernmost Russian outpost in North America.

But this land was not empty when the Russians arrived. It was, and still is, the homeland of the Kashaya Pomo people. They called this place Métini. The Kashaya had lived along this coast for a very long time, gathering acorns, fishing, and harvesting seaweed and shellfish. Their relationship with the newcomers was complex. There was trade, there was labour, and there were marriages. Today, Kashaya voices are an important part of how the park tells its story.

That mix of people is one of the first surprises of Fort Ross. Inside and around this stockade lived Russians, Finns, Native Alaskans, Kashaya Pomo, and children of mixed heritage, who were often called Creoles in company records. Several languages were spoken here every day. Different foods, different faiths, and different skills met in a few small fields above the ocean. In many ways, it was one of the most diverse communities on the Pacific coast of its time.

Now look at the stockade wall itself. The timbers are redwood, cut from the forests that still rise on the ridges behind you. Redwood resists rot and insects, so it suited a damp, salty coast. At two corners, you will see wooden blockhouses with small openings for cannon and muskets. Most of what you see today is careful reconstruction, built using historical drawings and archaeology. The shape, though, follows the original layout of the fort.

There is one important exception. The long, low house inside the walls is the Rotchev House. It is named after Alexander Rotchev, the last manager of the colony, who lived here with his family. This building is the only original Russian-era structure that still stands at Fort Ross. Its walls are actual timbers from the settlement period. When you step inside, you are standing in a room that Russian colonists built with their own hands, almost two centuries ago.

The chapel, with its small dome and bell tower, is the image most visitors remember. It is a reconstruction too, and it has a difficult history. The great earthquake of 1906 badly damaged the original structure. Later, in 1970, a fire destroyed much of what remained. It was rebuilt again afterward. That repeated loss and repair tells you something: people have cared about this building for a long time, and they kept bringing it back.

Here is a second surprise. The great earthquake of 1906, the one that struck San Francisco, was caused by the San Andreas Fault. That fault runs very close to Fort Ross, just inland from the fort. The quiet valley behind the settlement follows its line. So the colonists built their outpost almost on top of one of the most famous faults on Earth, without any idea it was there.

At first, the company also came for sea otters. Their thick fur was extremely valuable in trade with China. Native Alaskan hunters in skin kayaks pursued the otters along this coast and far beyond. Within a few decades, the otters had been hunted close to disappearing in this region. Farming here also proved disappointing. Fog, rust on the wheat, and pests reduced the harvests. The colony never quite became the food basket the company had hoped for.

Still, the settlement left many quiet firsts. Workers here built ships, ran workshops, and raised orchards. Many historians describe early windmills and shipbuilding at Fort Ross as among the first in California. In 1841, the company decided the colony was no longer worth the cost. It sold the movable property to John Sutter, a Swiss-born settler in the Sacramento Valley. A few years later, gold was found near his lands, and California changed forever.

After the Russians left, the land passed through private owners and ranching families for many years. Eventually it was protected, and today it is Fort Ross State Historic Park. Outside the walls, you can walk to the old orchard on the hillside and to the cemetery on the bluff, marked with Orthodox crosses. Many people never make the short walk to these places. They are worth it. They give the settlement its human scale.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the chapel and face the sea. Notice how small the fort looks against the coastline. Feel the wind, and listen to the waves below the cove where ships once anchored. People from Alaska, Siberia, Finland, and this very coast all stood where you are standing now. As you walk back toward the road, look once more at the grey redwood wall, rising quietly above the grass.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer, since coastal wind and fog are common.
Walk out to the bluff cemetery for the wider coastal view.
Allow extra driving time on the winding Highway 1.
Visit the orchard on the hillside above the fort.
