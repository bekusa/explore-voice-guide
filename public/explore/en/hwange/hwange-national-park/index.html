The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the hum of insects there is a low sound, so deep you feel it in your chest more than you hear it. That is an elephant calling to another elephant, somewhere beyond the trees. The ground here is pale, soft sand, and it shifts under your boots like a beach far from any sea. The air smells of dust, dry grass, and sometimes the sharp green scent of crushed leaves. Welcome to Hwange National Park, in the far west of Zimbabwe.

This is the largest national park in Zimbabwe. It covers roughly 14,600 square kilometres. That is an area bigger than many small countries. The eastern edge sits near the railway line and the town that shares its name. To the west, the park runs to the border with Botswana. There are no fences along much of that western side. Animals walk across the frontier freely, and many of them do exactly that, following water and the seasons.

The sand under your feet is the key to this whole landscape. Much of the park lies on the edge of the Kalahari sands. These deep, pale soils drain rainwater fast. Water sinks away instead of collecting into rivers and lakes. So for long months of the year, this is a thirsty place. In the dry season, the grass turns the colour of straw, and many trees drop their leaves. The light becomes hard and bright, and every animal moves with one question in mind... where is the water?

Here is the first surprise. Many of the waterholes you will see are not natural. Over the decades, park managers drilled boreholes and pumped groundwater into shallow pans. Without that pumped water, far fewer animals could survive the dry months here. Today, much of this work depends on conservation groups and donors, who help keep the pumps running. Some now run on solar panels. When you stop beside a full pan in October, remember that someone worked hard to fill it.

The park began as the Wankie Game Reserve in the late 1920s, under British colonial rule. Its first warden was a young man named Ted Davison. He arrived to a wild and difficult region, with few roads and very little surface water. Much of his early work was simply learning the land. Later, the reserve became a national park. After independence, the old colonial spelling of the name was replaced. Wankie became Hwange, a name linked to a local chief of the Nanzwa people.

Now, the animals. Hwange is known above all for its elephants. The population is very large — often counted in the tens of thousands. In the dry season, you may see family groups arrive at a waterhole together. The matriarch, the oldest female, usually leads. Young calves stay close, often under their mothers' bellies. Watch how the adults drink. They suck water into their trunks and then pour it into their mouths. Watch, too, how they spray mud over their backs to cool down and protect their skin.

Elephants are not alone here. The park holds lions, leopards, cheetahs, and spotted hyenas. Buffalo move in dark, heavy herds. Giraffes stand above the acacia trees, and zebras gather with wildebeest on the open grasslands. Look also for sable antelope. Males are almost black, with long horns that curve back like sabres. Hwange is one of the better places in southern Africa to see them. And keep your eyes open for something rarer still — the painted dog.

Painted dogs, also called African wild dogs, are among the most endangered large carnivores in Africa. Each dog has its own pattern of black, brown, yellow, and white patches. No two coats are the same. They live and hunt in close packs, and they share food with the pups and the sick. Near the village of Dete, close to the park, a conservation centre works to protect them. It also teaches local children about these animals. Many visitors stop there on the way in or out.

Here is the second surprise. Many people around the world first heard of Hwange because of one lion. His name was Cecil. He was a well-known male with a dark mane, and he wore a research collar from scientists at Oxford University. In 2015, he was lured outside the park and killed by a trophy hunter. The story spread across the world in days. It started a global debate about hunting, conservation, and who should benefit from wildlife. Hwange's lions are still studied closely today.

Birds fill the gaps between the large animals. Hundreds of species have been recorded here. In the early morning, listen for the francolins calling loudly from the grass. Look for the bright blue flash of a lilac-breasted roller on a dead branch. Vultures circle high in the afternoon heat. Near the water, you may see storks and hornbills. After the rains begin, migrant birds arrive from other continents. The bush suddenly becomes louder, greener, and much busier.

The vegetation changes as you travel. In some areas, there are open grassy plains, where you can see animals from a long distance. In others, you drive through mopane woodland. Mopane leaves are shaped like a butterfly's wings, folded in pairs. There are also teak forests on the deeper sands, and tall camelthorn trees with thick, twisted trunks. Each habitat holds different animals. A drive of an hour can take you through several of these worlds.

If you are near Main Camp, in the northeast of the park, ask about the raised viewing platforms at some of the waterholes. Nyamandhlovu Pan is one of the best known. Its name means "meat of the elephant" in a local language. From the platform, you can sit quietly and watch the animals come and go below you. There is no engine noise and no rush. You may wait twenty minutes and see nothing... and then a whole herd walks out of the trees.

So take a moment now. Look at the sand at your feet, and the tracks pressed into it. Some are round and wide — elephant. Some are small and sharp — antelope. Listen again for that low rumble beyond the trees. As you move on, notice how the light changes on the grass, and how the animals move toward the water as the day cools. This park was shaped by thirst, and by people who chose to share their water with the wild.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit late in the dry season for crowded waterholes.
Sit quietly at a pan platform for twenty minutes.
Bring binoculars to spot sable and distant predators.
Head out at dawn when animals are most active.
Stop at the painted dog centre near Dete.
