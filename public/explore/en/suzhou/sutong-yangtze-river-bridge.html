The guide

The story

Listen first. Out on the water, a long, low horn sounds from a cargo ship heading downriver. The air smells of wet silt and diesel. Then look up. Two grey towers rise out of the haze, and from each one a fan of steel cables slopes down to a road deck far above the river. The water below is wide and brown, and it moves with a slow, heavy patience. This is the Sutong Yangtze River Bridge. On a misty morning, the far tower can seem to float.

The name tells you what the bridge connects. Su stands for Suzhou, the city famous for its canals and classical gardens. Tong stands for Nantong, on the northern bank. You are on the edge of Suzhou here, close to the city of Changshu. For centuries, this stretch of the Yangtze was a barrier. The river is several kilometres wide at this point, and crossing it meant boarding a ferry and waiting on the weather. The north bank was close on the map, but slow to reach.

The bridge changed that. Construction began in the early 2000s, and the crossing opened to traffic in 2008. Today it carries a major expressway, so it is built for cars and trucks, not for people on foot. Most visitors experience it in one of two ways. Some drive across and feel the long, gentle rise of the deck. Others watch it from the riverbank, the way you may be doing now. Each view gives you a different sense of its scale.

Now for the number that made engineers around the world pay attention. The main span is the distance between the two great towers, and it measures 1,088 metres. That is more than a kilometre of road deck held up by cables alone, with no pier standing in the river between them. When the bridge opened, no cable-stayed bridge anywhere had a longer main span. A few years later, a bridge in the Russian city of Vladivostok passed it, but only by a small margin.

What does cable-stayed mean? It is worth a moment, because it explains what you are looking at. On a suspension bridge, two thick main cables hang in curves between the towers, and the deck hangs from those curves. Here, there is no curved main cable. Instead, each straight cable runs directly from a tower down to the deck. Look at the pattern they make — like the ribs of a giant fan, or the strings of a harp. Every cable carries its own share of the load.

The towers themselves are some of the tallest bridge towers ever built, at around three hundred metres. That is roughly the height of a skyscraper of seventy or eighty floors. They needed that height for a simple reason. The longer the span, the steeper the cables must be to hold the deck, and steep cables need tall towers. If you follow one tower from the water upwards, notice how its legs lean in towards each other and join near the top. This shape, like an upside-down letter Y, adds stiffness against the wind.

Here is a detail most visitors never think about. The ground under this river is not solid rock near the surface. It is deep, soft sediment carried down from the interior of China over thousands of years. So the builders could not simply set the towers on bedrock. They drove huge groups of piles deep into the riverbed to spread the weight. Everything you see above the water depends on structures you will never see, hidden in the mud below the brown current.

The river itself set the rules for the design. This part of the Yangtze is not far from where it meets the East China Sea, and it is one of the busiest shipping routes in the world. Container ships, bulk carriers, and long trains of barges pass under the deck day and night. That is why the deck sits so high above the water, and why the central span had to be so long. The bridge had to stay out of the way of the ships, not the other way around.

Weather was the other great challenge. Summer typhoons push in from the sea. Fog rolls up the estuary in the cooler months, sometimes so thick that the towers disappear. A long, light deck can twist and sway in strong wind, so its shape was tested carefully before construction. The steel deck has a smooth, rounded profile, a little like the underside of a wing. That shape helps the wind flow past it, instead of catching it like a sail.

Now think about what the bridge did to daily life on both banks. Before it opened, a trip from Nantong to Suzhou or Shanghai could take most of a day, depending on the ferry queues. After it opened, the same trip became a drive of an hour or two. Factories, families, and commuters suddenly had a fast link to the Yangtze River Delta, one of the busiest economic regions in China. For Nantong, people often describe it as the moment the city joined its southern neighbours.

While you stand here, take in the light. In the early morning, the haze over the river often turns the towers a soft blue-grey, and the cables almost vanish. Late in the afternoon, the sun drops in the west and catches the steel from the side, so each cable becomes a thin bright line. After dark, lights trace the outline of the towers and the deck. The traffic moves across as a steady stream of small lights, high above the black water.

One more thing to notice. Look carefully at the cables near the deck, and you may see small fittings close to the bottom of each one. These are dampers. In wind and rain, long cables can start to vibrate, a little like a guitar string. The dampers absorb that motion before it grows. It is a small detail on a very large structure, but without careful work like this, a bridge of this size could not stand safely for long.

So stay a moment longer. Watch a cargo ship slide under the deck and notice how small it looks against the span. Listen for the hum of tyres high above you, and the slap of the wake against the bank. When you walk away, turn back once. From a distance, the two towers line up with the river and the sky. They are a reminder that this wide, brown water once divided two cities — and now, every minute, it does not.

What to look for

Rounded, wing-like underside of the steel road deck.
Cargo ships and barge convoys passing under the central span.
Before you go

Practical tips

Watch from the riverbank, since the deck carries only motorway traffic.
Come early morning for soft haze around the towers.
Return after dark to see the towers outlined in lights.
Bring a zoom lens to frame ships beneath the deck.
Check fog forecasts first, as towers can vanish entirely.
