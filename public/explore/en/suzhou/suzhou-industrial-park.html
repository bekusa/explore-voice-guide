The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of Jinji Lake and listen. Water slaps softly against stone embankments. A breeze carries over a wide sheet of grey-green water, and on the far shore glass towers catch the light. Joggers pass behind you. Somewhere a scooter hums by almost silently. This does not feel like the Suzhou of old postcards, with canals and moon gates. It is the other Suzhou, the one that grew up in a single generation.

You are in Suzhou Industrial Park, a planned district east of the historic city. In 1994, the governments of China and Singapore signed an agreement to build it together. The idea was ambitious. Singapore would share its experience in town planning, public administration and attracting foreign investment. China would provide land, labour and a fast-growing market. The site was mostly farmland, fish ponds and small villages crossed by waterways. Within a few decades, it became one of the best-known development zones in the country.

Why Singapore? In the early 1990s, China was opening its economy, and its leaders looked abroad for models. Singapore had turned itself from a trading port into a wealthy, carefully managed city-state. Lee Kuan Yew, Singapore's founding prime minister, championed the project from the Singapore side. The park became a kind of experiment. Could the Singapore approach to planning work at Chinese scale? The answer took years to arrive, and it was not always smooth.

Here is something many visitors do not know. The early years were difficult. Local authorities in Suzhou also ran a competing industrial zone, and the two sides disagreed about priorities. In the early 2000s, the arrangement changed, and the Chinese side took a majority share in the development company. Singapore stayed involved, but in a smaller role. The park you see today carries both influences: Singaporean ideas about zoning and greenery, with Chinese speed and scale.

Now look across the water. You will probably notice one tower before any other. It is the Gate to the East, a huge arch made of two linked towers that join at the top. It was completed in the mid-2010s and quickly became a talking point. Its designers meant it as a gateway, a frame for the city's future. Local residents had a different idea. Many people in Suzhou call it by a joke name, comparing its shape to a giant pair of trousers.

That nickname tells you something real about the place. Suzhou has a long tradition of refined taste, from silk embroidery to classical gardens. People here have strong opinions about beauty. So when a bold modern building rose over the lake, the city answered with humour. Today, the joke and the pride live side by side. You will see the arch on souvenirs and in wedding photos, as well as in friendly teasing.

Turn your attention to the lake itself. Jinji Lake sits at the centre of the park's planning. Instead of placing factories at the heart of the district, the planners put water, parks and public space there. Offices, homes and shopping areas spread around it in rings. A long causeway crosses part of the lake, with trees and benches along its length. It echoes the old garden causeways of the region, but at a far larger size.

Walk along the shore and you will notice how much of the space is designed for people on foot. Wide promenades run beside the water. There are lawns, small plazas and places to sit. In the evening, families come out after dinner. Grandparents push strollers. Teenagers take photos against the skyline. On many evenings, a music fountain runs on the lake, with jets of water moving to recorded music and coloured lights. Crowds gather along the railings to watch.

The second surprise is about what the park actually makes. The name says industrial, so many visitors expect chimneys and warehouses. Those exist, but further out. The park became a base for many foreign and Chinese companies in electronics, precision manufacturing, biomedicine and research. Laboratories and office campuses now sit beside production lines. Much of the wealth that built these towers came from work you cannot see from the lakefront.

The park also took on an unexpected role in education. To the south lies Dushu Lake, another body of water surrounded by university campuses. Several Chinese universities opened branches here. There is also an international university created as a partnership between Chinese and British institutions. Students from many countries live in the area. On weekday afternoons, you may hear several languages in the cafés around the lake, a reminder of how international this district has become.

For shopping and food, the lakefront has large malls and a waterside dining area called Moon Harbour, with restaurants facing the water. The architecture is mostly glass, steel and stone, clean and orderly. Yet small details recall older Suzhou. Look for curved bridges, white walls with dark trim, and planting that softens hard edges. The planners borrowed the language of classical gardens, even when the buildings are thoroughly modern.

It helps to think of the park as one half of a conversation. A short metro ride west takes you back to the old city, with its narrow lanes, canals and walled gardens, some of them centuries old. Suzhou chose not to tear down its historic core to make room for growth. Instead, it built growth beside it. The old city kept its low skyline. The new skyline rose here, around the lake. Both are part of the same city.

If you have time, stay until dusk. The towers light up one by one, and their reflections stretch across the water. The arch glows against the darkening sky. The air cools, and the promenade fills with people walking slowly, talking, eating snacks from paper bags. It is ordinary life, but set in a landscape that did not exist when many of these walkers were children.

Before you move on, take one more look across the water. Notice the joggers, the fishing rods along the edge, the dog walkers and the couples on the benches. Notice how the wind ruffles the surface of the lake. Thirty years ago, much of this ground was fields and fish ponds. Now it is a neighbourhood people call home. As you walk away, listen to the water against the stones behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive before sunset to watch the skyline light up.
Take the metro to link the park with the old city.
Walk the lake causeway for wide views of both shores.
Check locally whether the lake music fountain runs that evening.
Wear comfortable shoes; the lakeside promenades are long.
