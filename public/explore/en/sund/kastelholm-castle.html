The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against the rocks below the walls, and the wind moves through the reeds along the shore. Underfoot, the ground is uneven. Rough granite pushes up through the grass. In front of you, a heavy grey mass of stone and brick rises from the edge of a quiet inlet. This is Kastelholm Castle, in the municipality of Sund, on the main island of Åland. It looks calm today. For several centuries, though, it was the place from which these islands were governed.

A little geography helps. Åland is an archipelago of thousands of islands and skerries between Sweden and Finland. Today it is an autonomous region of Finland, and its people speak Swedish. In the Middle Ages these waters were a busy sea route across the Baltic. Whoever held Åland could watch the ships moving between the two shores. A fortress here was not about defending farmland. It was about control of the sea, of trade, and of tax.

Kastelholm appears in written sources in the late fourteenth century. A document from 1388 mentions the castle in connection with Queen Margaret, the Danish ruler who later united the Nordic crowns. So the castle was already standing then, although nobody knows exactly when the first stones were laid. Historians still discuss its earliest phase. What is clear is that it served the Swedish crown as the seat of royal power on the islands.

Now look at the setting again. The castle was placed on rocky ground beside a narrow, sheltered inlet. Boats could reach it, but they could not approach easily or in large numbers. Marshy land and water protected parts of the site. Builders in the Middle Ages often used the landscape as their first wall, and here you can still see that logic. The stone and the shoreline work together.

As you walk closer, notice the building materials. The lower walls are mostly rough grey granite, the stone that lies everywhere on Åland. Higher up, and around some openings, you can see red brick. Brick was a more refined material in the medieval Baltic, and it allowed cleaner edges and arches. The mix of the two shows a castle that grew and changed over many generations. It was not built in one single campaign.

Inside these walls lived the bailiff, the king's representative on the islands. From here, officials collected taxes from local farmers and fishermen. Those taxes were often paid in goods, not coins. Think of fish, butter, grain, and animal skins. Imagine the storerooms filling up with sacks and barrels, and the smell of salted fish in the cold rooms. For ordinary islanders, this castle meant the crown's demands. It did not mean romance.

Here is a story many visitors do not expect. In the sixteenth century, the deposed Swedish king Erik the Fourteenth was held prisoner here for a period. Erik was a son of Gustav Vasa, the king who built the early modern Swedish state. He lost his throne to his brother, and he spent his final years moved from one fortress to another. For a time, one of those fortresses was Kastelholm, here on this quiet island.

The sixteenth century was the castle's most important era. Sweden was becoming a strong Baltic power, and Kastelholm was strengthened and expanded. The period also brought struggle. In the conflicts of the late sixteenth century, the castle was attacked and changed hands. Power on the mainland was contested, and remote fortresses like this one felt the effects. Some of the damage you see today belongs to that troubled time.

After its peak, Kastelholm slowly lost its importance. Warfare changed, and stone castles of this kind could no longer resist artillery. Administration moved elsewhere. In the eighteenth century the castle suffered serious damage, and it fell into decline. For a long time, parts of it stood as a ruin. Local people reused stones from the walls for their own buildings. This was common practice across Europe, and it explains some of the gaps in the masonry.

In the twentieth century, interest in the castle returned. Archaeologists and restorers worked to stabilise the ruins and to understand the building's history. Some parts were rebuilt, and others were left as broken walls. As you move through the castle, try to notice where new work meets old stone. Modern repairs are often neater, with straighter joints. The older masonry is more irregular, and it shows centuries of weather.

Look up as you go through the rooms. Small, deep window openings tell you that defence mattered more than comfort. Thick walls kept out the cold, but they also kept out most of the light. On a winter day here, daylight is short and the wind from the sea is sharp. Try to imagine the fires, the smoke, and the damp. Life inside a medieval castle on the Baltic was hard, even for the people in charge.

The castle does not stand alone. Close by is an open-air museum with old wooden farm buildings brought here from around Åland. It shows how rural islanders lived. Nearby you will also find Vita Björn, a former prison museum. Together, these places tell a fuller story. You can see the castle of the rulers, the farms of the people who paid them, and the cells where those who broke the law were held.

Before you leave, walk down toward the water and turn around. From the shore, you see the castle the way travellers by boat would once have seen it. Grey stone and red brick rise over the reeds. Listen again to the water and the wind, the same sounds this place has heard for more than six hundred years. Then take one more look at the walls, and the patient work of everyone who built, damaged, and repaired them.
