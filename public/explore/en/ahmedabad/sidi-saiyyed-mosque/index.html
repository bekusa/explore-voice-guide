The guide

The story

Listen first. Car horns, the rattle of autorickshaws, a vendor calling out near the traffic circle. Then you step through the gate, and the noise drops away. Under your feet, the stone of the courtyard is smooth and warm. In front of you stands a small mosque of pale yellow sandstone. It is not tall, and it is not grand. Yet people come from across the world to stand exactly where you are standing now, to look at a single carved window.

This is the Sidi Saiyyed Mosque, in the old heart of Ahmedabad. The city itself was founded in the fifteenth century by Sultan Ahmed Shah, and it grew into the capital of the Gujarat Sultanate. For about a century and a half, sultans and nobles filled Ahmedabad with mosques, tombs, and stepwells. This modest building came at the very end of that story. It is usually dated to 1572 or 1573, which makes it one of the last mosques built before the sultanate fell.

The name comes from its patron, Sidi Saiyyed. He was a Siddi, a member of a community of African origin, often from Abyssinia, the old name for Ethiopia. Many Siddis came to India as soldiers, sailors, or enslaved people, and some rose to real power. Sidi Saiyyed served in the household of a Gujarat noble. Accounts describe him as a man of learning and generosity. Standing here, you are looking at the legacy of an African man in medieval Gujarat — a part of Indian history that many visitors never hear about.

The timing matters. In 1573, the Mughal emperor Akbar marched into Gujarat and took control of the region. The independent sultanate came to an end. Many historians believe this upheaval interrupted work on the mosque. Look closely at the building as a whole. There are no tall minarets, the slender towers you see on many Ahmedabad mosques. Some details feel simpler than you might expect. The mosque may be, in a quiet way, an unfinished sentence.

Now turn to the rear and side walls, where the arched windows are filled with stone screens. In India these pierced screens are called jali, which simply means a net or a lattice. They let light and air into the prayer hall while keeping out the harsh sun. In a hot city like Ahmedabad, this was practical design. But here, the craftsmen turned a practical screen into something much more.

The most famous screen shows trees. Their trunks rise and twist around each other. Branches curl outward in looping spirals, and they end in small leaves and fronds. A palm-like tree appears among them. People often call this panel the Tree of Life. Notice that the whole design is carved from stone slabs, yet the branches look as light as threads. When sunlight comes through from behind, the trees appear in dark outline against a bright background.

Here is a detail that most visitors miss. Count the arched windows. Most of them hold carved screens, many with geometric patterns, some with the tree designs. But one arch, where you might expect another screen, is filled with plain stone masonry. Why? The most common explanation links it to the Mughal conquest — the work simply stopped. Another view is that a screen was planned but never carved. Either way, the blank arch tells you that history can arrive suddenly, even at a building site.

The tree screen became a symbol far beyond these walls. Ahmedabad treats it as an unofficial emblem of the city. You will see its outline on souvenirs, signs, and printed fabric across town. The Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad, one of India's best-known business schools, uses a design based on this lattice in its logo. So a window carved for a small neighbourhood mosque now appears on certificates carried by graduates all over the world.

Think for a moment about the craft. There were no machines here, only chisels, mallets, and patience. The carvers had to plan every curve before they cut, because stone does not forgive mistakes. One wrong strike could crack a branch. The sandstone of Gujarat is relatively soft to work when fresh, and it hardens with time. That is part of why these fine lines have survived for more than four centuries.

The style itself is a blend. Gujarat had long traditions of Hindu and Jain temple carving, with rich floral and vegetal patterns. When the sultans built mosques, they employed local masons who brought those skills with them. Islamic art usually avoids images of people and animals, so the carvers focused on plants and geometry. The result is a distinctly Gujarati style, sometimes called Indo-Islamic, which means a mix of Indian and Islamic building traditions.

Remember that this is a working mosque. People still come here to pray, and the call to prayer still rises over the traffic. Dress modestly, remove your shoes where asked, and keep your voice low. Access to the prayer hall can be restricted, especially for women and non-Muslim visitors. Do not worry if you cannot enter. The famous screens are designed to be seen from both sides, and the view from outside is the one on most photographs.

Around you is the old city of Ahmedabad. In 2017, UNESCO recognised the historic city as a World Heritage Site, the first city in India to receive that status. Nearby are the old Bhadra Fort and the market streets around Lal Darwaja, an old gate whose name means red gate. Narrow lanes run off into the pols, traditional residential neighbourhoods with carved wooden houses. The mosque is a good starting point for a walk through these layers.

Before you leave, stand still and look at the tree screen one more time. Watch how the light changes as clouds pass or the sun moves. A pigeon may land on the ledge. The traffic outside will carry on as it has all day. This window has watched over a busy crossroads for more than four hundred years. As you walk back into the noise, look for its shape on a shop sign or a scarf — you will find it again.

What to look for

Intertwined tree trunks twisting upward in the carved stone screen.
The one arched window filled with plain masonry.
A palm-like tree among the curling branches of the jali.
Geometric lattice patterns in the smaller side window screens.
Absence of tall minarets on the mosque's facade.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in morning light to see the screens backlit clearly.
Dress modestly and carry socks for warm stone floors.
Avoid prayer times, especially Friday midday, to visit respectfully.
View the famous jali from outside if the hall is closed.
Continue on foot toward Bhadra Fort and the old city lanes.
