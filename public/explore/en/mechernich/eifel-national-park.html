The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through beech leaves overhead, a dry, papery rustle. Somewhere below, water runs over stone. Underfoot, the ground is soft with last year's leaves, dark and slightly damp, and the air smells of moss and old wood. This is Eifel National Park, a stretch of forest, water and open upland spread across the low hills a short drive west of Mechernich. It does not announce itself with gates or grand monuments. It simply surrounds you.

The park was founded in 2004, and it is the only national park in the German state of North Rhine-Westphalia. That makes it young by national park standards. Much of what you see is not ancient untouched wilderness. It is land that people farmed, logged, flooded and trained armies on. The idea here is different. The goal is to step back and let nature slowly take over again, at its own pace.

That idea shapes everything around you. In many German forests, foresters plant neat rows of spruce for timber. Here, those conifer stands are gradually being left to change, and native beech is returning. Fallen trees are often left where they lie. To a tidy eye, it can look messy. But a dead trunk on the forest floor is home to beetles, fungi, mosses and the birds that feed on them. Look closely at any rotting log and you will see a small city at work.

The beech forests are the heart of the park. Beech trees have smooth, silver-grey bark, almost like elephant skin, and their canopy is dense. In summer, the light on the forest floor turns green and dim. In autumn, the leaves turn copper and gold, and the whole slope seems to glow. In winter, the bare trunks stand in long grey lines, and you can see far through the woods.

Now, here is something many visitors do not expect. These quiet woods are home to the European wildcat. It looks a little like a heavy, bushy tabby house cat, with a thick tail ringed in black and a blunt dark tip. It is shy and mostly active at dusk, so very few people ever see one. But the park is one of the places in Germany where this rare animal has found a refuge. You may walk past one without knowing it.

Water is the other great character of this landscape. Long reservoirs wind through the valleys, created in the early twentieth century by damming the rivers. The Urft dam, built in the early 1900s, was one of the largest dams in Europe when it was completed. Its reservoir, the Urftsee, lies like a dark ribbon between steep wooded slopes. On still mornings, mist often sits on the water and slowly lifts as the sun climbs.

Above the water, the forest opens onto something quite different — a wide, high plateau of grass and scattered shrubs called the Dreiborn Plateau. The wind is stronger up here, and the sky feels enormous. Red deer graze on the open ground, and in autumn the stags call during the rutting season. That deep, roaring sound carries a long way across the grass. It is one of the most memorable sounds of the Eifel.

This open plateau has a difficult history. For decades after the Second World War, much of this area was a military training ground, used for many years by the Belgian army. Soldiers trained here, and the public could not enter. Because of that, the land was not farmed or built on. In a strange way, the military years protected it. When the soldiers finally left in the mid-2000s, the area became part of the national park.

On the plateau, you can find the remains of Wollseifen, a village that no longer exists. Its residents were told to leave shortly after the war, when the area was taken for military use. The houses were later used for training and then removed. Today, a few traces remain, including the old church, standing alone on the open ground. It is a quiet place. Many visitors stop here and simply stand for a while.

At the edge of the park stands a much heavier reminder of the past — Vogelsang. In the 1930s, the Nazi regime built a large complex here to train future party leaders. The stone buildings are severe and imposing, set high above the Urftsee. After the war, the site became a military camp. Today, it is open to the public as a place of remembrance and learning, with exhibitions about its history and a visitor centre for the national park.

It is worth pausing on that contrast. On one side, a building designed to train people in an ideology of control. On the other, a park whose whole purpose is to give up control and let the land decide. Standing on the terraces at Vogelsang and looking out over the forest and water, many visitors feel that tension very clearly. The view is calm, but the ground beneath it carries a long story.

To explore the park, you do not need special skills. There are many marked trails, from short loops to long-distance routes. A trail called the Wildnis-Trail, meaning the Wilderness Trail, crosses the park over several days. Rangers lead guided walks, and they are often the best way to understand what you are seeing. Some paths are designed to be accessible, so visitors with wheelchairs or pushchairs can also enjoy the forest.

As you walk, use all your senses. Notice the change in temperature when you move from open plateau into deep beech shade. Listen for woodpeckers drumming on dead trunks. Watch the ground for fungi growing in shelves on fallen wood. In spring, look for fresh, bright green leaves unfolding, almost translucent in the sunlight. The park changes from week to week, and no two visits feel the same.

Before you move on, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Look at the nearest tree, the nearest patch of moss, the nearest stretch of water or sky. All of this is slowly becoming wilder, year by year, with very little help from us. In a hundred years, this place will look different again. You are seeing one early chapter of a very long story. Take your time on the way back.

What to look for

The lone church of the abandoned village Wollseifen.
Morning mist lifting off the dark water of the Urftsee.
Severe stone buildings of Vogelsang high above the reservoir.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Vogelsang visitor centre to plan your route.
Join a ranger-led walk to understand the landscape better.
Come in autumn to hear red deer stags calling.
Wear sturdy waterproof shoes; forest paths are often muddy.
Stay on marked trails, especially across the former training area.
