The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the hill and listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, small bells hang from the corners of the eaves, and when the wind comes off the river, they move. The air smells of damp stone, camphor leaves and wet earth. Below, a wide brown river slides toward the sea. Above, a tower of white walls and red woodwork rises in layer after layer, like a stack of folded wings.

This is Liuhe Pagoda, the Pagoda of Six Harmonies. It stands on Yuelun Hill, on the northern bank of the Qiantang River, at the southern edge of Hangzhou. Most visitors to the city spend their days around West Lake. Fewer make the short trip down here to the river. That is a pity, because this tower tells a story about water, fear and faith that the lake cannot tell.

The first pagoda on this spot rose in the year 970. At that time Hangzhou was the capital of Wuyue, a small and prosperous kingdom that ruled this coast for several decades before China was reunited under the Song dynasty. Its rulers were devoted Buddhists. They filled the hills around the city with temples and towers. This one had a very practical purpose. It was built to calm the river.

To understand why, you need to know about the tide. The Qiantang River opens into a wide, funnel-shaped bay. Twice a day the sea pushes in, and the narrowing shape forces the water into a single moving wall. This is the Qiantang tidal bore, one of the strongest in the world. For centuries it smashed boats, flooded fields and drowned people. The tower on the hill was meant as a spiritual guard against that force.

It was also a guide in a more ordinary sense. From the river, the pagoda is visible from a long distance. Boatmen used it as a landmark. Records describe lamps lit in the tower at night, so it worked as a kind of lighthouse for ships moving along this dangerous stretch of water. Faith and navigation lived in the same building... which is a very Chinese solution to a problem.

Now, the name. Six Harmonies is a Buddhist phrase. One common reading links it to the six principles of harmony that monks are asked to keep with one another. Another reading points to the six directions — heaven, earth, north, south, east and west — joined together in balance. Both ideas fit a tower that was meant to bring order to a wild river.

There is also a folk tale, and locals still like to tell it. Legend has it that a boy named Liuhe lived by the river with his family. When the Dragon King of the river sent floods that took his father, the boy climbed the hill and threw stones into the water, day after day, until the dragon begged him to stop. The pagoda, so the story goes, carries his name. It is a legend, not history — but you will hear it here.

The original Wuyue tower did not survive. It was destroyed by war in the early twelfth century. A few decades later, during the Southern Song dynasty, the pagoda was rebuilt. At that time Hangzhou had become the capital of the Song court after the north fell to invaders. The brick core you see today comes from that Southern Song rebuilding. So the inner body of this tower is roughly eight hundred and fifty years old.

Here is the first surprise. Count the eaves from outside, and you will find thirteen layers. Go inside, and you will find only seven real floors. The outer wooden shell was added much later, in the late Qing dynasty, around the end of the nineteenth century. It wraps the old brick tower like a coat. Every second roof outside is, in a sense, decoration. The pagoda looks taller and lighter than its true structure.

The second surprise waits inside. Climb the stairs, and look at the brick walls and the bases of the inner passages. Much of the carved decoration here belongs to the Song period. You can see flowers, plants, birds and other figures cut into the surfaces. Many visitors rush past them on the way to the view. Slow down. These carvings are some of the most direct contact you can have with the craftsmen of the Southern Song capital.

The climb itself is steady, not extreme. The stairs are narrow, and the passages turn as you rise. Small openings frame the river at each level, and the light changes as you go higher. At the upper floors, the wind gets stronger and the bells become louder. On a clear day you can follow the river east toward the sea, and look across to the far bank.

From up here, look for a long bridge crossing the river nearby. That is the Qiantang River Bridge, completed in 1937. It was designed by the engineer Mao Yisheng, and it was among the first major bridges in China built by Chinese engineers. Weeks after it opened, war reached Hangzhou, and the bridge was deliberately damaged to slow the advancing army. It was later repaired. An ancient tower and a modern bridge stand side by side, each with its own story of the river.

Back at the foot of the hill, take time to walk the gardens behind the pagoda. There is a collection of small-scale models of famous Chinese pagodas here, set among the trees. Walking past them gives you a quick lesson in how varied these towers can be — brick, stone, wood, round, square, eight-sided. It also helps you see what makes Liuhe distinctive: its heavy, grounded body, built to stand firm against wind and water.

Before you leave, stop once more and look up. Notice how the red woodwork and white walls stack upward against the sky. Listen again for the bells. Then turn toward the river and watch the water move. For more than a thousand years, people on this hill have looked at the same current and hoped it would stay calm. As you walk away, you are part of that long line of watchers.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine the visit with a riverside walk along the Qiantang.
Wear shoes with grip for the narrow interior stairs.
Come on a clear day for long river views.
Explore the pagoda model garden behind the tower.
Allow extra time to examine carvings on each floor.
