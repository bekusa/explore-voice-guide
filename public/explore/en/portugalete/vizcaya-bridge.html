The guide

The story

Listen first. A low electric hum, a soft clank of steel, and then the slow hiss of water moving below. A metal cabin is gliding sideways through the air, just above the estuary. It carries cars, cyclists and people with shopping bags. Above it, a long iron beam stretches from one tower to the other, and the cabin hangs from that beam on a web of thin cables. Welcome to the Vizcaya Bridge, between Portugalete and Getxo, at the mouth of the river that leads to Bilbao.

This is a transporter bridge, and that idea is the heart of the whole story. A normal bridge carries you on a fixed deck. Here, the deck moves. A trolley runs along rails high up on the top beam, and the hanging gondola travels with it, just above the water. The crossing takes only a few minutes. Then the gondola returns, and the river below stays open for ships of almost any height.

Why build something so unusual? In the late nineteenth century, this estuary was one of the busiest industrial waterways in Spain. Iron ore from the Basque hills went out to the world, and tall-masted ships came in toward the docks and furnaces upriver. A low bridge would have blocked them. A high bridge with long ramps would have needed huge amounts of land on both banks. A ferry was slow and depended on the tide. The engineers needed a fourth answer.

The answer came from a Basque architect, Alberto de Palacio, who had connections with the circle of Gustave Eiffel, the engineer of the tower in Paris. He worked together with a French engineer, Ferdinand Arnodin, who had developed a new kind of twisted steel cable. Their bridge opened in 1893. It was the first transporter bridge of its kind in the world. Many cities copied the idea afterwards, in France, Britain, Germany and the Americas.

Here is something most visitors miss. Many of those later transporter bridges are gone now. Some were demolished, others simply stopped working. This one never really stopped. It is considered the oldest transporter bridge in the world that is still in regular service. In 2006, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List. That is a list of places with outstanding value for all humanity, and the bridge was recognised as a key work of the industrial age.

Look up at the towers. They are lattice structures — open frames of riveted iron, not solid walls. The open design lets the strong sea wind pass through instead of pushing against a flat surface. The top beam runs high above the water, at a height that once allowed sailing ships to pass with their masts upright. Today the gap is used by modern vessels heading to and from the port.

Now follow the cables with your eyes. From the top of each tower, long cables run down at an angle and anchor into the ground behind it. They hold the towers in balance, a bit like the ropes of a tent. Between the towers, more cables hang from the beam and carry the load of the rails. The pattern of lines is very precise. On a clear day, against a blue sky, the whole structure can look as light as a drawing.

The bridge also carries a darker chapter. During the Spanish Civil War, in 1937, retreating forces blew up the top beam to slow the advance of the opposing army. The central span fell into the estuary. For several years, the two banks lost their link. The bridge was rebuilt and put back into service a few years later. If you know this story, the clean lines above you look different. They are a repair, not only an original.

The two banks have quite different characters. Behind you on the Portugalete side, the old town climbs a steep hill. Its narrow streets lead up toward the Basilica of Santa María, a late Gothic church at the top. Across the water lies Las Arenas, a district of Getxo. In the industrial boom, wealthy families built large houses there, close to the sea. For many decades, the gondola connected a working town and a resort of villas.

The bridge has also carried a great deal of ordinary life. Workers going to shifts, children going to school, families going to the beach on summer Sundays. For many local people, this is not a monument at all. It is simply how you cross the river. You may notice that the regulars on the gondola barely look up. They check their phones, hold their bicycles, and chat. The structure above them is just part of the day.

If you have time and a head for heights, there is a second way to experience the bridge. A lift inside one of the towers takes visitors up to a walkway along the top beam. From there, you can walk from one side to the other, high above the estuary. A separate ticket is needed. Up top, the wind is stronger and the metal rings under your feet. Below, you can watch the gondola moving across like a small toy.

The view from the walkway is wide. To one side, the estuary opens into the Bay of Biscay and the Atlantic. To the other side, the river bends inland toward Bilbao, with cranes, docks and the green hills behind them. On the riverbanks you can trace the old industrial landscape that this bridge served. You also see how much of it has changed, with promenades and new buildings where many factories once stood.

Notice the sounds once more before you leave. The bell or signal before the gondola departs. The rumble of a car rolling off the platform. The calls of gulls over the water and the slap of small waves on the stone quay. These sounds have changed little over more than a century of crossings. The machines have been modernised, but the rhythm is the same — load, cross, unload, return.

As you walk away, turn back once and look at the bridge from a distance. From further along the promenade, the towers and the beam form a simple frame over the river. Inside that frame, the gondola keeps moving, back and forth, quietly. It is easy to forget that this was once a radical idea. Take one more look... and then let the bridge get back to its work.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the gondola both ways for two different views.
Book the top walkway separately if you enjoy heights.
Bring a windproof layer; the top beam is very exposed.
Walk up Portugalete's old town after crossing back.
Photograph the bridge from the promenade for full framing.
