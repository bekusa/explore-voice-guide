The guide

The story

Put your hand on the stone. It is warm, even in the shade, and slightly rough, like coarse bread. Above you, arch follows arch in a long, curving line. Swifts cut through the openings. Somewhere below, footsteps echo along a vaulted passage. You are standing beside a building that has been in daily use, in one form or another, for close to two thousand years.

This is the Arles Amphitheatre. Locals simply call it Les Arènes — the arenas. The Romans built it near the end of the first century, around the same years that Rome was finishing its own great Colosseum. That timing matters. Arles wanted the newest and grandest kind of building, and it wanted it fast. This was a city that saw itself as a small Rome on the banks of the Rhône.

That pride had a history. Arles took Julius Caesar's side in his civil war, and Caesar rewarded the city. He settled veterans of his Sixth Legion here and made Arles an official Roman colony. Over the next century and a half, the town gained a forum, a theatre, baths, and finally this arena. Arles sat at a key crossing of the Rhône, so trade, money, and soldiers all passed through. An amphitheatre was a way to show that wealth to everyone.

Look at the shape. The building is an oval, not a circle, and it is very large. Scholars estimate that around twenty thousand spectators could fit inside. Count the arches on one level, if you have patience. There are sixty on the ground floor and sixty more above. The lower arches are framed by flat pillars in a plain style. The upper ones carry rounded columns with simple capitals. Each level feels a little lighter than the one beneath it.

Here is something many visitors miss. The building was once taller. Above the two arcades, there was a third level, a solid attic wall, and it is gone. Stone from it, and from other Roman buildings, was carried off over the centuries for new houses and walls. So the arena you see today is incomplete. Imagine one more band of stone along the top, and you can picture how it looked to a Roman crowd arriving for the games.

And what were those games? Gladiators fought here. There were also hunts with wild animals, and public executions. The crowd sat by rank. The most important citizens took the seats closest to the action, and ordinary people climbed higher. The passages under the seats were designed to move thousands of people in and out quickly. Walk through those vaulted corridors now, and you are following the same routes those crowds used.

Then Rome faded, and the arena changed completely. In the early Middle Ages, it became a fortress. Its thick walls offered protection in a dangerous time. The people of Arles added tall square towers to the top of the structure, and you can still see some of them today. They look strange at first — heavy medieval blocks sitting on elegant Roman arches. But they tell the next chapter of the story very clearly.

This is the second surprise. Over time, a whole neighbourhood grew inside the arena. Houses filled the arches and climbed over the old seating. Historians describe around two hundred homes, along with chapels, packed inside the oval. People were born, worked, and died within these walls. The arena was no longer a stage. It was a village, closed in by Roman stone, with its own small squares and lanes.

That village lasted for centuries. Then, in the early nineteenth century, attitudes to ancient monuments changed. France began to value its Roman past as heritage. In the 1820s, the authorities started to remove the houses and clear the arena. The work took years. When it was finished, the old oval was open again for the first time in many generations. Not long after, the arena returned to its original purpose: public spectacle.

Today, the main spectacle is the bull. Arles sits at the edge of the Camargue, a wide, flat delta of marshes and rice fields where black bulls and white horses are raised. The arena hosts the Spanish-style bullfight, which is controversial. It also hosts the course camarguaise, a local tradition. In it, young men dressed in white run at the bull and try to snatch small ribbons from its horns. The bull is not killed, and the best bulls become local celebrities.

You may also know this place through art. Vincent van Gogh lived in Arles in 1888, and he painted the crowd in this arena. He seemed less interested in the bull than in the people — the hats, the colours, the bodies pressed together in the stands. In 1981, the amphitheatre became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing for the Roman and Romanesque monuments of Arles. Recent restoration work has cleaned much of the stone, so parts of the arena now look pale and bright again.

If you climb to the top of one of the towers, you see why the Romans chose this spot. The roofs of the old town spread out in shades of orange and brown. The Rhône bends past the edge of the city. Very close by stands the Roman theatre, with two columns still standing. From up here, the city's layout becomes easy to read. You can see how much of modern Arles still follows the lines the Romans drew.

Before you leave, walk once around the outside. Notice how the street curves to follow the oval, as if the town itself has grown around the arena. Look at the difference between the clean, restored stone and the darker, older patches. Listen to the cafés, the scooters, the voices on the steps. The crowds have changed many times... but this place still gathers people, just as it was built to do.
