Attabad Lake

The water is a blue you do not expect in a place so high and so dry.

⏱ 5 min

The story

The water is a blue you do not expect in a place so high and so dry. It lies between bare brown mountains, still and bright, the color of turquoise stone. You stand at the shore and the silence is large. This is Attabad Lake. It is beautiful, and it was born from disaster.

The road brings you to the lake suddenly. You come around a bend, and there it is, filling the valley floor with color. After the brown and grey of the mountains, the blue seems to glow. You may find yourself simply stopping to look before you say a word. Many travelers do.

This lake is young. It did not exist before 2010. For as long as anyone could remember, the Hunza River simply ran through this valley. Villages sat along its banks. The Karakoram Highway, the great mountain road to China, followed the water. Then, in a single morning, everything changed.

On the fourth of January, 2010, a huge piece of the mountain broke loose. It crashed down onto the village of Attabad. The fall killed about twenty people. The rock and earth did not stop at the valley floor. They piled across it and blocked the river completely, building a natural dam taller than many buildings. The sound, people say, was heard far down the valley.

With the river blocked, the water had nowhere to go. It began to rise. Day by day, week by week, the new lake grew behind the wall of rubble. People watched their homes disappear beneath it. Fields, orchards, and houses slipped under the surface. Some families carried out what they could and left the rest behind. The water climbed for months before it finally settled.

The rising lake swallowed the road as well. A long stretch of the Karakoram Highway vanished underwater. This was no small loss. That road was the only land link between this region and China. Whole communities upstream were suddenly cut off, reachable only by crossing the new water.

So the people turned to boats. For several years, small wooden boats carried everything across the lake. They carried passengers, food, fuel, and goods. They even carried trucks, loaded plank by plank. The crossing was slow and could be dangerous in wind. But for a time, it was the only way through.

Look across the water and try to picture it. Somewhere beneath your reflection lie old village streets. Walls and walnut trees still stand in the cold, dark depths. The families who lived there were moved to new settlements. They lost the ground their grandparents had farmed. Beneath the calm surface, a whole village still waits. The lake is lovely, but it covers a great deal of loss.

A lasting answer came from engineering. Workers cut a series of tunnels through the mountain to carry the highway above the lake. These are the Attabad tunnels. They opened in 2015. Driving through them, you pass from daylight into long electric-lit corridors of rock and out again above the bright water. The land route to China was whole again.

With the road restored, something unexpected happened. Travelers began to come, not despite the lake, but because of it. The strange blue water drew them. Small hotels and tea stops opened along the shore. What was once a place of loss has slowly become a place people choose to visit. The boats that once carried trucks now carry visitors who come only to look and to ride.

The color is the thing people remember. It shifts through the day, from pale green to deep blue, fed by the fine rock dust that mountain rivers carry. On a still morning, the peaks lie mirrored on the surface. Bring a camera, but know that no picture quite holds the real shade of it. The water is very cold all year, far too cold for a long swim.

You can ride out onto the lake in a boat. The engine hums, the shore slides away, and the cliffs rise on either side. Children from the shore villages sometimes wave as you pass. Out in the middle, the quiet returns. It is hard to hold two ideas at once here: that this calm beauty and that old catastrophe are the very same place.

Stand a while longer before you leave. Watch the light move on the water. A lake like this teaches something simple and hard. The mountains are not finished. They still move, still fall, still reshape the valley. What looks permanent is only resting. The blue water keeps that lesson, quietly, in plain sight.

Key facts

Formed in January 2010 by a massive landslide.
The landslide killed about twenty people.
It submerged villages and the Karakoram Highway.
Boats crossed the lake for about five years.
The Attabad tunnels opened in 2015.

What to look for

The turquoise color shifting from green to blue.
Bare brown mountains rising straight from the water.
Driftwood and pale rock along the shoreline.
Peaks mirrored on the still morning surface.

Practical tips

Ride a boat out onto the blue water.
Bring a warm layer for the cold breeze.
Stop at a lakeside cafe for the view.
Allow extra driving time on the mountain roads.
