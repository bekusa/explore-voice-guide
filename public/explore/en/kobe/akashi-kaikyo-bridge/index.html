The guide

The story

Stand on the shore at Maiko and listen for a moment. Waves slap against the rocks. A low, steady hum comes from far above, where cars and trucks cross in a constant stream. Salt hangs in the air, and gulls turn in the wind off the strait. Then look up. Two pale towers rise from the water, and between them hangs a long road, carried on cables that seem far too thin for the job.

This is the Akashi Kaikyo Bridge. It connects the city of Kobe, on Japan's main island of Honshu, to Awaji Island across the Akashi Strait. The water below is a busy shipping lane. Large vessels pass under the deck every day, and the currents here run fast and strong. For centuries, the only way across was by boat. That fact sits at the centre of this bridge's story.

Ferries once carried people and goods between Kobe and Awaji. The crossing was short, but the sea here is not always gentle. Storms, fog and strong tides made the route risky. Ferry accidents in the wider Seto Inland Sea region during the twentieth century turned public opinion. People began to ask a serious question. Could Japan build a fixed crossing over water this deep, this wide, and this rough?

The answer took decades of planning. Engineers studied the seabed, the wind and the tides. They knew that typhoons pass through this region. They also knew that Japan sits on active fault lines. Any bridge here had to bend without breaking. Construction finally began in the late 1980s. The bridge opened to traffic in 1998, after roughly ten years of work at sea.

Now look at the gap between the two main towers. That central span is just under two kilometres long, at 1,991 metres. When the bridge opened, no suspension bridge anywhere had a longer central span. It held that world record for more than two decades. In 2022, a new bridge across the Dardanelles in Turkey finally passed it by a small margin. Even so, this bridge remains one of the longest suspension spans on Earth.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The bridge grew longer while it was still being built. In January 1995, the Great Hanshin earthquake struck this region. It devastated large parts of Kobe and took thousands of lives. At that moment, the two main towers were already standing, but the deck was not yet in place. The ground movement pushed the towers further apart. The span became about one metre longer than the original design.

The engineers did not tear anything down. They adjusted their plans and carried on. For many people in Kobe, the finished bridge carries a quiet meaning. It opened only a few years after the earthquake, while the city was still rebuilding. It became a sign that the region could recover and connect again. When you see it from the shore, you are looking at both an engineering project and a piece of local memory.

Now turn your eyes to the towers themselves. Each one rises close to three hundred metres above the water. That is taller than most skyscrapers in Japan. Inside them, engineers placed heavy swinging weights called tuned mass dampers. When strong winds push the tower one way, these weights move the other way and calm the motion. It is a simple idea. Think of a hand steadying a glass on a moving train.

The main cables are the real heroes of a suspension bridge. Two thick cables run over the tops of the towers and anchor deep into massive concrete blocks on each shore. Each cable is made from tens of thousands of fine steel wires, bundled tightly together. From those cables, vertical hangers drop down and hold up the road deck. The deck is a deep steel truss, a lattice of triangles. Wind can pass through it instead of pushing against a solid wall.

The bridge carries a six-lane expressway, and only road traffic uses it. There is no railway line on the deck. It forms part of a longer route that continues across Awaji Island and then over another bridge at Naruto to the island of Shikoku. So this crossing links two of Japan's main islands by road. Trucks, buses and private cars use it every day. Many travellers cross without stopping to think about what holds them up.

Here is the second surprise. You can walk inside the bridge structure. On the Kobe side, a facility called the Maiko Marine Promenade lets visitors enter a walkway built within the deck framework. It sits high above the sea, below the traffic lanes. Some sections of floor are glass, so you can look straight down at the water. Guided tours that climb to the top of a main tower also run at certain times. These require an advance reservation.

After dark, the bridge changes character. Lights run along the main cables, and they form a curving line across the strait. Locals sometimes call it the Pearl Bridge, because the lights look like a string of pearls hanging over the water. The colours and patterns change on different evenings. From Maiko Park, many people sit quietly on the benches and watch the lights reflect on the moving sea. Ships pass below, their own small lights sliding through the dark.

Before you leave, take one more look. Notice how the road rises gently toward the centre of the span, then falls away toward Awaji. Watch a ship pass under the deck and judge its size against the towers. Listen again to that steady hum of traffic overhead. Each vehicle is crossing water that once required a ferry and good weather. As you walk away along the shore, keep glancing back. The bridge looks different from every angle, and it stays in view for a long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Maiko Park on the Kobe shore for the full view.
Reserve tower climb tours well ahead of your visit.
Stay until dusk to watch the cable lights switch on.
Bring a jacket, as wind off the strait gets strong.
Use a wide-angle lens to fit the whole span in frame.
