The story

The gravel crunches beneath your feet — not ordinary gravel, but millions of small white stones that shift and settle with each step, a sound designed to announce your arrival. You are walking through one of the largest forested areas in central Tokyo, a man-made woodland that feels ancient but is barely a century old.

This is Meiji Shrine, dedicated to Emperor Meiji and Empress Shoken, the rulers who steered Japan from feudal isolation into the modern world. When the emperor died in 1912, the government decided his memorial should not be a tomb or monument, but a living forest. They planted over one hundred thousand trees, donated from across Japan, on land that had been an imperial garden. The forest you see today — dense, layered, almost wild — was entirely planned by botanists who studied which species would thrive together over centuries.

The main pathway curves gently, deliberately hiding the shrine buildings until the last moment. This is not accident. Shinto design uses the approach to shift your state of mind, moving you from the noise of the city into a space of stillness. Notice the massive torii gates — the first one, near the entrance, stands over twelve metres tall and is built from cypress wood over fifteen hundred years old, harvested from a forest in Taiwan.

As you near the main hall, you will see barrels of sake stacked along the path, offerings from brewers across Japan. Opposite them, barrels of French wine — a nod to Emperor Meiji, who helped introduce Western customs to Japan and was known to enjoy Burgundy. The pairing is unusual, almost playful, a reminder that this shrine honours a man who lived between two worlds.

The main worship hall, rebuilt after wartime bombing, is constructed in the nagare-zukuri style — a design where the roof sweeps down protectively over the front porch. The wood is dark, unvarnished cypress. The copper roof has weathered to a soft green. There is almost no decoration. Shinto architecture relies on material and proportion rather than ornament, and the effect here is one of quiet strength.

If you visit on a weekend, you might see a wedding procession. Brides wear white silk kimonos and tall lacquered headdresses. Priests in white robes lead the party across the courtyard, moving slowly, soundlessly, while tourists step aside and bow. These ceremonies happen dozens of times each week — Meiji Shrine is one of the most popular wedding venues in Japan.

The shrine grounds also hold a garden, tucked behind the main hall, that Emperor Meiji designed for his wife. It opens in June when the irises bloom — over fifteen hundred plants in shades of purple, white, and deep blue. The garden is built around a spring-fed pond, and the paths wind so that each turn reveals a new composition of water, flowers, and stone.

On New Year's Day, over three million people visit Meiji Shrine to make their first prayers of the year, the largest such gathering in Japan. The forest absorbs the crowds. Even on busy days, if you step off the main path into the trees, the city noise fades within a few metres. Crows call from the canopy. The air smells of damp wood and moss.

Before you leave, watch how people approach the main hall. They toss a coin into the offering box, bow twice, clap twice, then bow once more. The claps are sharp, precise — a way of calling the attention of the kami, the spirits. You do not need to participate, but noticing the rhythm, the shared choreography, gives you a sense of how many thousands have stood in this same spot, performing the same gestures, year after year.

The forest will outlive the buildings. That was the plan from the beginning. The botanists who planted these trees in 1920 knew they were designing for the twenty-second century, for a time when the wood and copper would weather and be replaced, but the forest would still be here, dense and green, in the heart of Tokyo.
