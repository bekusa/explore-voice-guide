The guide

The story

Feel the air change as you move toward the tunnel mouth. Outside, the fields of the Pas-de-Calais roll away under an open sky, and you can smell cut grass and damp earth. A few steps inside, the warmth drops away. The air turns cool and still. Water beads on pale walls. Your footsteps echo back from concrete and chalk, and the sounds of the countryside fade behind you.

You are standing inside a hill near the village of Landrethun-le-Nord, a few kilometres inland from the Channel coast. From the outside, this place looks like ordinary farmland. Hedgerows, a quiet road, a gentle rise in the ground. That was the point. Under this hill, during the Second World War, Nazi Germany built one of the strangest weapons ever attempted. It was not a rocket and it was not a bomber. It was a gun.

The Germans called it the Hochdruckpumpe, which means high-pressure pump. The name was a disguise. History remembers it as the V-3, the third of Hitler's so-called vengeance weapons. The V-1 was a flying bomb, and the V-2 was a ballistic rocket. The V-3 was meant to be a supergun, built to fire shells at London, well over a hundred kilometres away across the sea.

Here is the clever and slightly mad idea behind it. A normal cannon has one explosive charge at the base of the barrel. The V-3 had many. Small side chambers branched off along the length of a very long barrel, like ribs off a spine. As the shell raced past each chamber, another charge fired behind it and pushed it faster. The goal was speed without one enormous blast that would destroy the gun itself.

The concept came from an engineer named August Coenders, who worked for the Röchling steel company. Engineers tested prototypes on the Baltic coast, at Misdroy, and the trials did not go smoothly. Shells were unstable in flight. Barrels failed. Even so, the project moved ahead. The plan was ambitious — batteries of long barrels, sunk deep into the ground and fixed at a steep angle, all pointing toward one target.

That is the first thing many visitors miss. These guns could not aim. They could not turn left or right. The barrels were set into inclined shafts cut through the chalk, permanently pointed at London. This was not a weapon for battlefields. It was a weapon built to hit a single, very large city again and again, day and night, with a steady stream of shells.

Why here? Look at the ground around you. The hill is made of chalk, which is soft enough to tunnel but firm enough to hold its shape. The site sits close to the narrowest part of the Channel, near the shortest line to London. Work began in 1943. Workers dug galleries and a railway tunnel deep below the surface, and many of them were forced labourers, brought here against their will.

The Allies were watching. Aerial photographs showed strange construction in the fields, and intelligence officers did not fully understand what they were seeing. They understood enough to fear it. Heavy bombing raids struck the site in 1944. In July of that year, the Royal Air Force's 617 Squadron — the unit famous for the Dambusters raid — attacked with Tallboy bombs, enormous bombs designed to penetrate deep before exploding.

Here is the second surprise, and it reaches across the Atlantic. In August 1944, the United States launched a secret operation using old bombers packed with explosives, flown by remote control after the crew bailed out. One of these missions was aimed at Mimoyecques. The plane exploded in the air over England before the crew could jump. One of the pilots was Joseph Kennedy Junior, the elder brother of the future American president John F. Kennedy.

The V-3 never fired a single shell at London from this hill. The bombing damaged the tunnels and shafts, and the technical problems had never been solved. In September 1944, Canadian troops advanced through the area and the site fell into Allied hands. Later, British engineers studied the complex and then blew up parts of it, so that it could never be used again.

As you walk through the galleries today, look closely at the walls. In some places the concrete is smooth and deliberate. In others it is cracked, twisted, or broken open, and you can see raw chalk behind it. Those scars come from the bombs and the demolitions. The tunnels carry the marks of the war in their surfaces. You are walking through damage, not just through architecture.

The site is now a museum. Displays, models, and photographs explain how the gun was meant to work and what happened to it. Take your time with the cutaway models. They help you picture the long barrels running down into the darkness beneath your feet. It is hard to grasp the scale from the corridor alone. The real structure goes much deeper than the path you can follow.

It is worth thinking about what this place might have meant. London had already suffered the Blitz, and later the flying bombs and rockets. A gun that could fire without pause might have made life in the city even harder. Instead, the weapon failed, and this hill became a record of an idea that went too far — and of the people who worked, fought, and died to build or stop it.

When you step back outside, pause at the entrance for a moment. Feel the warmth return to your skin and the light open up again. Look across the fields toward the coast, toward the direction the barrels once pointed. The land is quiet now. Birds move through the hedges, and tractors work the soil. Under your feet, the cold tunnels remain, holding their long and unfinished story.
