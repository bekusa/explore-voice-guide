The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The lanes here are paved with small river stones, set close together in patterns that run underfoot like stitching. The walls on either side are white, washed again and again with lime. Above them, a narrow strip of very blue sky. Somewhere close, water drips into a basin behind an iron gate. And in spring, the air carries the sharp, sweet smell of orange blossom.

You are standing in the old heart of Córdoba, on the north bank of the Guadalquivir river. Before we walk, one piece of geography. Madinat al-Zahra, the ruined palace city of the caliphs, lies a few kilometres west of here, at the foot of the Sierra Morena hills. The two places belong to the same story. This guide covers the old town. The palace city deserves a visit of its own.

That story has many layers, and Córdoba wears them openly. It was an important Roman city, and the philosopher Seneca was born here. Later it became the capital of Muslim al-Andalus. In 929, its ruler Abd al-Rahman the Third declared himself caliph. A caliph claimed leadership of the whole Muslim community, not only a kingdom. For a time, Córdoba was one of the largest and most learned cities in Europe.

The centre of it all is the great building locals call the Mezquita, the Mosque-Cathedral. Work began in the late eighth century under Abd al-Rahman the First. Later rulers extended it again and again over roughly two centuries. From outside, you mostly see high, plain walls with carved gateways set into them. The walls give very little away. The surprise is waiting inside.

First, though, the courtyard. This is the Patio of the Orange Trees, planted in neat rows and crossed by small irrigation channels. In the past, worshippers washed here before prayer. Today it is open, shaded and calm, with the tall bell tower rising above the gate. Here is a detail most visitors miss. That tower was built around the old minaret, the tower used to call people to prayer. The older structure still survives inside it.

Step into the prayer hall and let your eyes adjust. Columns stretch away in every direction, more like a forest than a room. Above them, double tiers of horseshoe arches carry alternating stripes of red brick and pale stone. Look closely at the columns themselves. They do not all match. Many were taken from older Roman and Visigothic buildings, so the colours, heights and capitals change as you walk.

At the far wall is the mihrab, the niche that shows the direction of Mecca. Its decoration dates to the tenth century, under the caliph al-Hakam the Second. Gold and coloured glass mosaics cover the dome and the arch around it. Byzantine craftsmen, sent from Constantinople, are generally credited with the technique. Stand quietly for a moment. The light here is low and warm, and the gold catches it slowly.

Now the second surprise. In 1236, Ferdinand the Third of Castile took the city, and the mosque became a church. For centuries the building changed little. Then, in the sixteenth century, builders opened its middle and raised a full Renaissance cathedral inside it. Walk toward the centre and the low arches suddenly give way to a tall, bright nave. Even at the time, the project was controversial. A story often told says the emperor Charles the Fifth regretted approving it.

Leave by the gates on the west side and you enter the Judería, the old Jewish quarter. The lanes narrow and bend, built for shade rather than for traffic. Here you will find a small synagogue from the early fourteenth century, one of very few medieval synagogues left in Spain. Its walls carry carved plaster and Hebrew inscriptions. Nearby, a seated bronze statue honours Maimonides. He was a great Jewish philosopher and physician, born in Córdoba in the twelfth century.

Keep wandering and look through any open doorway. Córdoba is known for its patios, the inner courtyards of family houses. Pots of geraniums hang in rows on the white walls, and wells and fountains sit in the centre. Each May, residents open their patios to visitors and compete for prizes. This tradition is recognised by UNESCO as intangible heritage. Just off the main lanes, the tiny Calleja de las Flores, the Lane of Flowers, frames the cathedral tower between its walls.

Walk downhill toward the river and the town opens out. The Roman Bridge crosses the Guadalquivir on a long line of stone arches. Its foundations are Roman, but it has been repaired and rebuilt many times over the centuries. At the city end stands a Renaissance gateway. At the far end rises the Calahorra Tower, a medieval fortified gate. Below, the river runs wide and slow, with reeds, old mill buildings and birds wading in the shallows.

A little upstream is the Alcázar of the Christian Monarchs, a fortress-palace with terraced gardens. Long pools run between hedges, cypress trees and fountains. It is said that Ferdinand and Isabella received Christopher Columbus here while he argued for his voyage west. In 1984, UNESCO listed the Mosque-Cathedral as World Heritage. Ten years later, in 1994, the listing grew to cover this whole historic centre. Madinat al-Zahra joined the list separately in 2018.

In the late afternoon, the stone of the Mezquita turns honey-coloured, and the bridge glows above the water. The lanes empty slightly as tour groups leave. You hear more ordinary sounds then: a radio in a kitchen, a scooter, swifts screaming above the rooftops. This is still a living neighbourhood. People hang laundry, sweep doorsteps and argue gently in the street.

Before you move on, look again at what is right in front of you. Notice the river stones under your shoes, and the lime on the walls, rubbed thin where shoulders brush past. Notice one more doorway, and one more patio. Romans, caliphs, rabbis and kings all walked these streets. Their buildings sit side by side here, often inside one another. Take your time on the way out. In Córdoba, the best details are usually just around the next corner.

What to look for

Old minaret enclosed within the cathedral bell tower.
Hebrew inscriptions carved into the synagogue's plaster walls.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Mosque-Cathedral early to beat the tour groups.
Wear shoes with grip for the river-stone pavements.
Plan a separate trip to Madinat al-Zahra west of town.
Come in May to see private patios open to visitors.
Cross the Roman Bridge at dusk for views back.
