The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. It is pale, dry and gritty, a mix of sand and broken limestone that crunches under your shoes. Around you, low rounded hills rise from the plain like the backs of sleeping animals. Some are no taller than a person. Others climb well above your head. They look natural at first glance... but every one of them was built by hand, and every one of them is a grave.

These are the Dilmun Burial Mounds of Bahrain. Here around Riffa and across the northern half of the island, people raised these tombs over several centuries. Most were built roughly between 2200 and 1750 BCE, in the Bronze Age. That makes them older than the pyramids of Mexico and older than classical Greece. In 2019, UNESCO added the mounds to its World Heritage List. The listing recognised them as one of the largest ancient cemeteries anywhere on Earth.

So who were the people who built them? They belonged to a civilisation called Dilmun. Bahrain was its heart, and its reach extended to the nearby Arabian coast. Dilmun sat in the middle of one of the great trade routes of the ancient world. To the north lay Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, in modern Iraq. To the east lay the cities of the Indus Valley, in modern Pakistan and India. Ships passed through these waters carrying goods between them.

Copper was the great prize. It came from a land the Mesopotamians called Magan, generally identified with modern Oman. Merchants of Dilmun handled that trade and grew wealthy from it. Timber, stone, beads and pearls moved through the island too. Bahrain had something else that sailors valued — fresh water. Springs rose on the island and even from the seabed offshore. In a hot, salty gulf, that sweet water made Dilmun a natural stopping place.

Here is the first surprise. Dilmun appears in some of the oldest written literature in the world. Sumerian texts, written on clay tablets in Mesopotamia, describe Dilmun as a pure and holy land. In one myth, it is a place where sickness and old age do not exist. Some writers have connected these stories with the idea of a lost paradise. That link is a modern interpretation, not proof. But it shows how the island lived in the imagination of its neighbours, thousands of years ago.

Now look closely at the shape of a single mound. Under the earth and gravel, most hide a stone chamber in the centre. The builders laid out the chamber with limestone blocks and roofed it with large flat slabs. Then they piled earth and rubble over the top to form the rounded hill. Many mounds also had a low ring wall around the base, holding the shape in place. Time and weather have softened them, but that basic plan holds true across thousands of tombs.

The numbers are hard to picture. Researchers estimate that tens of thousands of mounds once covered the island. Some estimates go as high as seventy-five thousand. Today, about eleven thousand survive inside the protected World Heritage area, spread across many separate fields. Modern roads, farms and housing have replaced a great many of the others. As you stand here, you are looking at only a part of what once stood — a landscape of the dead that once stretched almost from one coast to the other.

The second surprise is in who was buried here. These were not only tombs for kings. Archaeologists found that men, women and children of many ranks lay in these graves. Most mounds held one person, sometimes with a few belongings. Excavations recovered pottery, copper objects, ivory, beads and food offerings. Animal bones suggest that families placed meals beside the dead. Some graves also contained small round stamp seals carved from stone. Merchants used these seals to mark goods and agreements, and they became a signature object of Dilmun.

Not every tomb was modest. Near the village of A'ali, a short drive north of here, stand the so-called royal mounds. They are far larger than the ordinary graves. Some have two-storey chambers inside, and wide ring walls of carefully cut stone. Archaeologists believe they belonged to the rulers of Dilmun or to its highest families. When you see them, notice how they tower over the village houses. Even today they change the skyline.

Much of what we know comes from twentieth-century excavations. A Danish archaeological expedition began work in Bahrain in the 1950s. Its team dug into mounds, settlements and temples across the island, and their findings helped bring Dilmun back into world history. Sadly, the archaeologists were rarely first inside. Many chambers had been robbed long before — in some cases, probably in ancient times. Thieves knew where the treasures lay, and they searched for metal and jewellery.

The mounds are not frozen in the past. At A'ali, local potters have worked beside the tombs for generations. In the past, some built their kilns into the sides of the mounds themselves. The old hills became part of daily village life. Today the government protects the mound fields more strictly, and visitors are asked not to climb or dig. But the closeness remains. Houses, roads and palm groves sit right next to graves that are more than four thousand years old.

Take a moment to listen. You will likely hear traffic in the distance, a bird, perhaps the call to prayer drifting from a mosque. In the middle of the day, the light here is hard and white, and the mounds throw almost no shadow. Early in the morning or late in the afternoon, the low sun gives each hill a dark side and a bright side. Then the landscape suddenly shows its depth and shape, row after row.

As you walk away, look back once more. Each low hill marks one life — a trader, a mother, a child, a ruler. Four thousand years ago, someone stood on this same gravel and said goodbye. The ships, the copper and the clay tablets are long gone. But the graves remain where their families left them, quiet under the sky of Bahrain.

Look closer

What to look for

Low ring walls of stone around some mound bases.
Exposed limestone slabs where a chamber roof has collapsed.
Hollows in mound tops left by ancient tomb robbers.
Rows of mounds stretching toward modern houses and roads.
Pale gravel and broken limestone covering the mound surfaces.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon for softer light.
Carry water and a hat; there is little shade.
Stay on paths and avoid climbing the mounds.
Combine your visit with the royal mounds at A'ali.
Visit Bahrain National Museum to see objects from the graves.
