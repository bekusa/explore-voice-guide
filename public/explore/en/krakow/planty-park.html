The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches softly under your shoes. A bicycle bell rings somewhere behind you, and the leaves above move in a long green tunnel that seems to go on forever. On one side, trams rattle past. On the other, church towers rise over old rooftops. You are standing in Planty, the ring of trees and paths that wraps around the Old Town of Kraków, and you are walking where a wall once stood.

That is the first surprise of this place. Planty is not just a park that happens to sit beside the old city. It follows the exact line of Kraków's medieval fortifications. For centuries, thick defensive walls, towers, and a moat circled the town. Gates opened onto the roads that led to the rest of Poland and beyond. If you walked this loop five hundred years ago, you would have been outside the city, looking up at stone.

By the early nineteenth century, those walls had lost their purpose. Cannons and modern warfare had made them almost useless, and much of the stonework was crumbling. At that time, Kraków was under Austrian control, and the authorities decided to tear most of the fortifications down. Brick by brick, tower by tower, the medieval ring disappeared. The moat was filled in. The land was flattened.

The name of the park comes from that moment. In Polish, the word planty is connected to levelling the ground — making it flat. So the name does not describe flowers or trees at all. It describes the work of erasing a wall. Every time a local says, let's meet on the Planty, they are, without thinking about it, naming an act of demolition.

Not everything was lost. A small group of citizens argued that part of the old defences should stay. One of them, a professor named Feliks Radwański, is remembered for helping to save the stretch around Florian Gate. Thanks to that effort, the northern edge of the Old Town still keeps its gate, a short run of wall, and several defensive towers. Those few hundred metres give you a sense of what once surrounded the entire city.

If you head toward that northern section, look for the tall gate tower with its steep roof. Beside it, the old wall still carries small towers once looked after by different craft guilds. Locals often hang paintings along this wall for sale. Just beyond the gate stands the Barbican, a round brick fortress with turrets, built to guard the entrance. It sits right at the edge of the park, like a full stop at the end of a sentence.

After the walls came down, the empty strip needed a new life. In the first half of the nineteenth century, it was planted as a green belt. A local official named Florian Straszewski is closely linked to this work and is often called the father of the Planty. Near the southern part of the ring, you can find a small obelisk that honours him. Many people walk past it every day without noticing whose memory it holds.

The park is not one long garden. It is divided into many smaller sections, around thirty of them, and each one has its own character. Some are open lawns with benches in the sun. Others are shaded and quiet, with old trees closing overhead. Some feel formal, with neat flower beds. As you walk the full loop of roughly four kilometres, the mood changes again and again, almost like turning the pages of a book.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Because Planty follows the old wall, it also shows you the shape of medieval Kraków from the outside. The loop is not a perfect circle. It bends and stretches. At its southern end, it narrows toward Wawel Hill, where the royal castle and the cathedral stand above the river. The park, in a sense, is a map you can walk.

Along the way, look out for statues and memorials hidden among the trees. Some honour scholars and writers. Others show figures from Polish literature, placed here as quiet reminders of the city's long love of books and learning. Near the old university buildings on the western side, you may find a statue of Nicolaus Copernicus, the astronomer who studied in Kraków. It is a fitting place for him, among students hurrying between lectures.

That human rhythm is the real heart of Planty. In the morning, you will see people walking dogs and commuters cutting across the paths on their way to work. At midday, students sit on benches with coffee and notes. In the evening, couples stroll slowly, and older residents meet friends for a chat. For many people in Kraków, this is not a tourist sight. It is simply the way home.

The seasons change the park completely. In spring, the chestnut trees bloom and the lawns turn bright green. Summer brings deep shade, which feels welcome on hot days. In autumn, the paths fill with yellow and orange leaves, and chestnuts drop onto the gravel with a small, hard sound. In winter, the bare branches let you see the old buildings behind them, and a light snow makes the whole ring very quiet.

Before you move on, take one more moment where you are. Look at the line between the trees and the buildings beside you. Imagine, just for a second, a wall of stone in that same place — tall, dark, and closed. Now it is open air, birdsong, and people passing by. As you walk away, listen for the gravel under your feet, and remember that every step is following a border the city chose to turn into a garden.
