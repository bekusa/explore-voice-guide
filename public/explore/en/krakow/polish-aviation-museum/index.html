The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across wide, flat ground. Somewhere a gravel path crunches under someone's shoes. Above the grass, the low sky opens in every direction, without the hills and church towers you saw in the old town. This openness is not an accident. You are standing on one of the oldest airfields in Europe. For more than a century, people came to this flat stretch of land east of central Kraków for one reason: to leave the ground.

The airfield at Rakowice and Czyżyny began before the First World War. At that time, Kraków belonged to the Austro-Hungarian Empire, and military planners wanted a place for balloons and early aircraft. After Poland regained independence in 1918, the field became an important base for Polish aviation. Pilots trained here. Aircraft were repaired here. Through the twentieth century, the runway carried military and civilian traffic. When flying operations finally moved elsewhere, the land kept its memory... and the museum grew on top of it.

The museum was founded in the 1960s. For decades, its collection lived in the old hangars and out in the open air. Many of those interwar buildings still stand on the site today. Look at their brickwork and their wide doors, made for wood-and-fabric biplanes. The hangars are part of the exhibit as much as the aircraft inside them. They show how small and light the early machines were, compared with the jets that came later.

Then there is the newer main building, opened in 2010. It is hard to miss. The design came from a German architecture studio, and the idea was simple and clever. Seen from above, the building forms the shape of a three-bladed propeller. From the ground you cannot see the whole shape. Instead, you see sharp concrete edges, large panes of glass, and long angled walls. Inside, natural light falls across the aircraft hanging overhead.

Here is the first surprise most visitors miss. Some of the most valuable aircraft in this museum did not come from Poland at all. They came from Berlin. Before the Second World War, Germany built a large national aviation collection there. It held rare aircraft from the earliest years of flight and from the First World War. During the war, Allied bombing threatened Berlin. So the Germans moved many pieces east, to quiet rural areas, for safekeeping.

After 1945, the borders of Europe changed. Those hiding places ended up inside Poland. The aircraft were found, and many of them eventually came to Kraków. Historians and collectors around the world know this story well. Several of these machines are extremely rare today. Some are the last survivors of their type anywhere. For aviation historians, this transfer is one of the strangest twists of the war. The bombing aimed to destroy a collection, and in the end it helped preserve part of it.

Walk among these early machines and look closely. Notice the wooden frames, the stretched fabric, the thin wires holding the wings in place. These were the aircraft of the 1910s, when flying was new and dangerous. The pilots sat in open cockpits, exposed to cold and wind. It is easy to forget how fragile they were. Up close, you can see stitching, faded paint, and patched surfaces. Each one shows the hands of the people who built it.

The second surprise belongs to Poland itself. In September 1939, Polish fighter pilots defended the country against the German invasion. Their main fighter was the PZL P.11, a Polish design with a distinctive gull-shaped wing. The wing bends upward near the body of the aircraft, which gave the pilot a better view. Many were built, but almost all were lost. The example kept here is widely described as the only surviving one in the world.

Stand in front of it for a moment. It is a small aircraft, with a single engine and an open feel. By 1939, it was already older than the German planes it faced. Polish pilots still flew it with great courage. Many of those same pilots later escaped to France and Britain. There, they flew with the Allied air forces. Their story is still very important to Polish people, and you will often see families explaining it to their children here.

Outside, the scale changes completely. Rows of jets stand on the concrete and grass. Many come from the Cold War, when Poland was part of the Warsaw Pact, the Soviet-led military alliance in Eastern Europe. You will see Soviet-designed MiG fighters, helicopters, and transport aircraft. Their metal skins carry rivets, warning stencils, and weathered paint. Walking between them, you can feel how much aviation grew in just a few decades — from wood and fabric to supersonic steel.

The museum is not only about aircraft. It also holds a large collection of aircraft engines. Some are small rotary engines from the earliest years. Others are big piston engines with many cylinders arranged in a circle. Later come the jet engines, long and tube-shaped. Engines are often the most overlooked part of any aviation museum. But here, they show the real story of progress. Each step in engine power made it possible to fly higher, faster, and further.

Look up and around as you walk. Beyond the aircraft, you can see the edges of modern Kraków. The city has grown around the old airfield. Shopping centres, housing blocks, and wide roads now surround it. Not far to the east lies Nowa Huta, the planned industrial district built after the Second World War. The museum area also hosts outdoor events, and locals often come for air shows held on the old field. On those days, engines fill the sky again.

Before you leave, turn around once more. Look at the open ground where the runway once ran. Look at the propeller-shaped building, the old brick hangars, the rows of silent jets. Each layer comes from a different chapter of the twentieth century. Listen again for the wind across the grass. For a hundred years, this was a place of departure. Today, the machines stay on the ground... but the open sky above you is still the same.

What to look for

The upward-bent gull wing on the PZL P.11.
Stretched fabric and wire bracing on early aircraft.
Rotary engines with cylinders arranged in a circle.
Original interwar brick hangars with wide doors.
Stencilled warning markings on Cold War jet fuselages.
Before you go

Practical tips

Allow extra time for the large open-air aircraft area.
Wear comfortable shoes for walking across wide grounds.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed outdoor field.
Check for air show dates if you enjoy live flying.
Visit the old hangars after the main building.
