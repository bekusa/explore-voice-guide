The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. The path here is not flat. It curls, slowly and steadily, around a great cone of grass. Each step lifts you a little higher above Kraków. The wind grows stronger as you climb, and the sounds of the city grow quieter. By the time you reach the top, the traffic below is only a soft hum. This hill was not shaped by nature. It was raised by human hands, basket by basket, as an act of memory.

This is Kościuszko's Mound. It stands on a rise in the west of Kraków, in the green district of Zwierzyniec. Locals often call this hill after Saint Bronisława, a holy woman who, according to tradition, lived here as a hermit in the Middle Ages. The mound was built in the early 1820s. It honours one man — Tadeusz Kościuszko, a soldier, an engineer, and for many Poles the clearest symbol of the fight for freedom.

So who was he? Kościuszko was born in the eighteenth century into a minor noble family, in lands that are today part of Belarus. He trained as a military engineer. Then he did something unusual for a Polish officer of his time. He sailed to America and joined the War of Independence. There he designed fortifications, including defences at Saratoga and at West Point on the Hudson River. Americans still remember him, and his statues stand in several cities across the United States.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Kościuszko is a hero on two continents, but his greatest moment came here, in Kraków. In 1794, Poland was being carved up by its powerful neighbours — Russia, Prussia, and Austria. Kościuszko returned and stood in the Main Market Square, in the heart of the old town. There he swore a public oath to lead a national uprising. A plaque in the square still marks the place where he made that promise.

Weeks later came the battle that made him a legend. Near the village of Racławice, his small army faced Russian troops. Many of his soldiers were peasants. They carried war scythes — ordinary farm blades fixed upright on long poles. Their charge helped win the day. Kościuszko then wore a simple peasant coat as a sign of respect. That image, the noble general in a farmer's coat, became part of Polish identity. The uprising itself failed, and Poland disappeared from the map for more than a century.

Kościuszko died in Switzerland in 1817. His body was brought back and laid in the royal crypts of Wawel Cathedral, beside Polish kings. But the people of Kraków wanted something more — a monument that anyone could touch and climb. They chose a very old form. Kraków already had two ancient mounds, linked in legend to the city's founder Krakus and to his daughter Wanda. Nobody knows for certain who built those, or when. A new mound would join that ancient family.

And here is the second surprise. This mound was built by volunteers. People from every social class came to carry earth up the hill — nobles, students, craftsmen, women, and children. Work went on for about three years. Soil from battlefields where Kościuszko had fought was placed inside, including earth from Racławice. In later years, soil from many other places was added, among them earth sent from America. So the grass under your shoes rests on ground gathered from far beyond this hill.

Now look down at the wide brick walls around the base. They are not part of the original memorial. In the middle of the nineteenth century, Kraków came under Austrian rule. The Austrian army saw this hill as a perfect military position. They built a large fortress around the mound, with thick red brick walls, gun positions, and deep ditches. It became one part of a ring of forts around the city. There is something strange in that. A monument to Polish freedom became wrapped inside a foreign citadel.

The Austrians also had to remove an older chapel to build their fort. In its place they built a new one, the neo-Gothic chapel of Blessed Bronisława, near the entrance. Neo-Gothic means a nineteenth-century style that copies medieval churches, with pointed arches and narrow windows. Today the old fortress buildings hold a small museum about Kościuszko and his times. You can see the brick and the vaulted rooms from inside, which helps you feel the scale of the military works.

The mound has not always been safe. Rain is its quiet enemy. In the late 1990s, very heavy rains soaked the earth and damaged the slopes. The structure was in real danger. A long restoration followed, with new drainage and reinforcement, so the shape you see today is the result of careful modern engineering. It seems fitting. A memorial to an engineer needed engineers to save it.

When you reach the summit, pause and turn slowly in a full circle. To the east lies the old city. Look for the hill of Wawel, with the castle and the cathedral where Kościuszko rests. The towers of Saint Mary's Basilica rise from the Market Square, where he took his oath. The Vistula River bends through the city below. On very clear days, far to the south, you may see the line of the Tatra Mountains on the horizon.

Closer by, look at the stone at the very top. It is a large granite boulder carrying Kościuszko's name. It is simple and heavy, with no statue and no grand decoration. The landscape around it does the work. From here you can also see the green spaces of western Kraków, the woods of Wolski Forest, and, on the far side of the city, the other mounds that share this tradition of raising earth to remember.

Before you start back down, take one more moment up here. Feel the wind on your face, the uneven grass, the open sky. Thousands of people once climbed this same spiral carrying soil, not to see a view, but to leave something behind. As you walk down the path, notice how the city slowly rises around you again. You are walking on their work, on the memory they built with their own hands.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come on a clear morning for the widest views.
Wear sturdy shoes for the spiral path to the summit.
Bring a layer, since the summit is often windy.
Visit the museum inside the old fort before climbing.
Pair the visit with a walk through Wolski Forest.
