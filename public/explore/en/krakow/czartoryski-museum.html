The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on Saint John's Street and listen. Footsteps echo off old stone. A tram bell rings somewhere beyond the city walls. The street is narrow, and the light falls in thin strips between the townhouses. Just ahead, a covered passage crosses above Pijarska Street like a small bridge between buildings. That passage is your first clue. This museum is not one building but several, joined over time to hold one family's long effort to save a nation's memory.

The story begins far from Kraków, at the turn of the nineteenth century. Poland had just disappeared from the map. Its neighbours, Russia, Prussia and Austria, had divided its lands between them in the partitions of the late eighteenth century. In that moment of loss, one woman decided that if the country could not survive as a state, it could survive as a collection. Her name was Princess Izabela Czartoryska.

At the family estate in Puławy, in eastern Poland, Izabela built a small classical temple in her park. It opened in 1801 as the Temple of the Sibyl, a building in the style of an ancient Roman shrine. Above its entrance she placed a motto: the past to the future. Inside, she gathered royal relics, old weapons and national keepsakes. She wanted Poles to see and touch their history. Many historians consider it the first museum in Poland.

Izabela collected with feeling as much as with judgement. Alongside serious treasures, she gathered romantic curiosities, some with doubtful origins. Visitors in her day could see objects said to be linked to great figures of European literature and history. Not every label would satisfy a modern curator. But that was never quite the point. Her museum was about memory, and about the emotional power of objects to connect people with the past.

Then history intervened again. After the failed November Uprising against Russian rule, in 1830 and 1831, the Czartoryski family was forced into exile. The Puławy estate was confiscated. Much of the collection was hidden, packed and moved in secret. A large part travelled to Paris, where the family lived at the Hôtel Lambert, a grand mansion on an island in the Seine. For decades, the treasures of a vanished country lived in a foreign capital.

It was Izabela's grandson, Prince Władysław Czartoryski, who brought the collection home, or close to it. In the eighteen-seventies he chose Kraków. At the time, the city lay in the Austrian part of the partitioned lands, which allowed more freedom for Polish culture than other regions. He rebuilt and joined several older properties here, near the northern edge of the Old Town. The museum opened to the public in the late eighteen-seventies.

Now, look around you. This corner of the city sits right beside the surviving medieval defences. The Florian Gate and its brick walls stand only a short walk away. Part of the museum complex uses the former city arsenal, a place once tied to the defence of Kraków. There is something fitting about that. A building made to protect the city became a building made to protect its memory.

Now to the painting most people come to see. The Lady with an Ermine is one of only a small handful of surviving portraits by Leonardo da Vinci. It was painted around 1490 in Milan. The young woman is usually identified as Cecilia Gallerani, who lived at the court of Ludovico Sforza, the ruler of Milan. Her head turns sharply, as if someone has just spoken to her from outside the frame.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The ermine itself carries meaning. The animal was a symbol of purity, and it was also linked to Ludovico Sforza himself. Some scholars also see a play on words, because the Greek word for a weasel-like animal sounds close to Gallerani. Look at her hand, too. The long fingers, the tendons, the light on the knuckles... Leonardo studied anatomy closely, and you can see it here.

The second surprise is how close this painting came to being lost. Prince Adam Jerzy Czartoryski, Izabela's son, bought it in Italy around 1800. During the Second World War, the Nazis seized it. It was taken to Germany and later returned to occupied Kraków, where it hung for a time in the office of the German governor at Wawel Castle. After the war it was recovered and came home. Many other works from this collection did not.

The most painful absence is a Portrait of a Young Man by Raphael. It was taken during the war and has never been found. It remains one of the most searched-for missing paintings in the world. In a museum full of treasures, a missing picture still shapes the story. When you walk through these rooms, you are seeing a collection that survived exile, war and theft... but not without losses.

There is still much more to see. Rembrandt's Landscape with the Good Samaritan is here, a small and moody scene in dark browns and gold. You will also find ancient objects, decorative arts, armour and textiles. In 2016 the Polish state bought the collection from the family foundation, and it is now part of the National Museum in Kraków. After a long renovation, the museum reopened in 2019, with modern galleries inside the old walls.

Before you leave, pause again outside. Look up at the passage over Pijarska Street, and at the worn stone of the doorways. Think of those crates moving in secret across Europe, two centuries ago. Izabela's motto was simple: the past to the future. Standing here on this quiet street, with the old walls nearby, you are the future she had in mind.
