The guide

The story

Start with the ground beneath you. Szeroka Street is wide, almost a square, and its cobbles are uneven underfoot. At its southern end stands a low, heavy building of pale stone and brick, with a crenellated top that looks more like a fortress than a house of prayer. This is the Old Synagogue, and it is generally considered the oldest surviving synagogue building in Poland.

You are standing in Kazimierz, a district that began life as a separate town. King Casimir the Great founded it in the fourteenth century, just south of medieval Kraków, and it carries his name. For centuries a river channel and a defensive wall separated the two towns. Today the boundary is gone, and a short walk brings you from the Main Market Square to these quieter streets.

Jewish life in Kraków is far older than this building. But Kazimierz became its heart at the end of the fifteenth century. In the 1490s, after tensions and a serious fire in Kraków, the Jewish community moved here. A Jewish quarter grew in the northeastern part of Kazimierz, and over time it developed its own synagogues, schools, cemeteries and markets. The Old Synagogue was the community's first great house of prayer.

The earliest structure here dates to the fifteenth century and was built in the Gothic style. Then, in the sixteenth century, a fire badly damaged it. The community rebuilt it in the Renaissance manner, with the help of an Italian architect working in Kraków. That mix is still visible today. The bones are Gothic, and the outer dress is Renaissance... a building that carries two eras at once.

Look up at the roofline. The tall parapet with its notched edge is called an attic, a Renaissance feature common in Polish towns. It hides the roof behind a decorative wall. Notice too the thick buttresses that brace the walls. This building stood close to the town's defensive line, and its solid shape suggests it was also meant to offer protection. A house of prayer, built like a small stronghold.

Now step inside, and notice something that surprises many visitors. The main prayer hall sits lower than the street outside. You walk down to enter it. One explanation often given is a line from the Psalms — "Out of the depths I call to you" — so that worshippers literally pray from a lower place. It is a tradition seen in several old synagogues. Whatever the reason, the effect is quiet and enclosed.

The hall itself is divided into two aisles. A pair of slender columns runs down the middle, and from them Gothic rib vaults spread across the ceiling like the frame of a tent. Rib vaulting means stone ribs carry the weight of the ceiling. The space feels both tall and intimate. In daylight, soft light falls through high windows onto the pale walls and the stone floor.

In the centre stands the bimah. This is the raised platform where the Torah scroll is read aloud during services. Here it is enclosed by a delicate wrought-iron canopy, a fine piece of Renaissance metalwork. Against the eastern wall, facing Jerusalem, you will find the Aron Kodesh. This is the holy ark, a carved stone niche where the Torah scrolls were kept. Its stonework is another Renaissance detail worth a slow look.

Traditional synagogues seated men and women separately. As the community grew, women's galleries were added to this building in the seventeenth century, attached along its sides. From there, women could follow the prayers without entering the main hall. As you walk around the outside, you can see how these later additions changed the building's footprint. The synagogue was never frozen. It grew with the people who used it.

Here is a moment many visitors do not expect. In 1794, during an uprising against the foreign powers dividing Poland, the national hero Tadeusz Kościuszko is said to have spoken in this synagogue. He called on the Jewish community to join the fight for Polish independence. It is a reminder that Kazimierz was never cut off from the wider story of the country. Its people stood inside the nation's struggles, not outside them.

The twentieth century brought the darkest chapter. During the Second World War, the German occupiers devastated Jewish Kraków. The community of Kazimierz was forced into a ghetto across the river, and most of its members were murdered in the Holocaust. This synagogue was looted and badly damaged, and its interior was stripped. For a time the building was used as a warehouse. Its sacred objects were scattered or destroyed.

After the war, the building was restored in the 1950s. It did not return to use as an active synagogue. Instead, it became part of the Museum of Kraków, the city's historical museum. Today it holds an exhibition on the history and culture of Kraków's Jews. You can see ritual objects, textiles, documents and photographs, and learn about holidays, family life and daily customs. The building itself is the largest object in the collection.

When you step back outside, take in the whole of Szeroka Street. At its far end stands the smaller Remuh Synagogue, with its old cemetery behind it. Around you are former Jewish houses, now cafés, restaurants and small shops. In recent decades Kazimierz has come back to life, with a summer festival of Jewish culture that fills this street with music. Old stories and new life sit side by side here.

Before you move on, pause by the wall of the Old Synagogue. Put your hand on the stone if you wish. It has stood through fire, rebuilding, war and silence, and it is still here. Listen to the sounds of the street — footsteps on cobbles, voices from the cafés, perhaps a tram bell in the distance. Then walk slowly. Many of the doorways around you have their own stories, too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early in the day for a quieter prayer hall.
Walk the full length of Szeroka Street afterwards.
Pair your visit with the nearby Remuh Synagogue and cemetery.
Step back across the street to photograph the whole facade.
Allow time to read the exhibition panels on daily customs.
