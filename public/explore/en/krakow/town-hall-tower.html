The guide

The story

Stand close to the base of the tower and put your hand on the stone. It is cool, even on a warm afternoon. Above you, red brick climbs into the sky in a long, narrow shaft. Around you, Kraków's Main Market Square moves at its own pace. Pigeons scatter, horse hooves knock on the cobbles, and café chairs scrape against stone. This tower stands alone in the square, and that is the first clue to its story.

This is the Town Hall Tower. But look around. Where is the town hall? For centuries, a large Gothic town hall stood on this spot. It was the seat of the city council, the place where merchants, magistrates and guilds argued over the life of the city. The tower was only one part of a much larger building. Today, the tower is all that remains.

The town hall was pulled down in the early nineteenth century, around 1820. By then the old building was in poor condition, and city authorities wanted to tidy the square. Many buildings in the Market Square disappeared in this period. The tower was spared. Some accounts say that public opinion helped to save it. Whatever the reason, the tower survived, and it now stands like a single tall piece left on a chessboard.

The origins of the tower go back to the medieval period, when Kraków was the royal capital of Poland. Its lower parts are Gothic. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches, tall proportions and strong vertical lines. You can see this style in the narrow windows and the way the tower pulls the eye upward. Over the centuries, fires and repairs changed the top. The spire you see today is a later addition, but the body of the tower is old.

Now here is something many visitors miss. Step back a little and look carefully at the tower's edges against the sky. The tower leans. It is not dramatic like the famous tower in Pisa, but it is real. The lean is usually measured at a little over half a metre. According to the usual account, a powerful storm in 1703 pushed the tower out of line. Since then, it has never stood quite straight again.

The tower rises around seventy metres above the square. That makes it one of the tallest structures in the Old Town. From the cobbles, its height is hard to judge, because the square is so wide. The tower looks slim rather than massive. Brick and stone work together here. Pale stone frames the corners and details, while the warm red brick fills the long walls. In late afternoon light, that brick turns almost copper.

Before you climb, look at the entrance. Two stone lions guard the doorway. They are a favourite spot for photographs, and children often want to touch their paws. The lions give the tower a ceremonial feeling, as if you are entering an official building. In a way, you are. This tower was once the front door to the city's government, and the lions keep that memory alive.

The tower is now part of the Museum of Kraków, and you can go inside. The interior is not grand. It is a series of small rooms connected by steep and narrow stairs. Some sections feel tight, and the steps are worn by many feet. Inside, you will find exhibits about the town hall and the history of the city's government. Take your time on the stairs. The climb is part of the experience.

Here is a second surprise. Below your feet, the tower has cellars. In earlier centuries, the space beneath the town hall served darker purposes. Historical accounts describe a prison and a place where the accused were questioned, sometimes with torture. Justice in medieval cities was public and harsh. Later, these same cellars became a place for drinking beer and socialising. Today, the underground rooms hold a theatre stage. Comedy and drama now fill a space that once held prisoners.

As you climb higher, the windows begin to frame the square. Look out, and the city spreads below you. To one side, the long arcades of the Cloth Hall run through the middle of the square. Cloth merchants once traded here, and today stalls sell amber, wood carvings and souvenirs. Further away, the two uneven towers of Saint Mary's Basilica rise above the rooftops. From up here, you understand how the square was designed as the heart of the medieval city.

On the hour, listen. From Saint Mary's, a trumpeter plays a short tune called the Hejnał. The melody stops suddenly, in the middle of a note. Legend has it that a trumpeter was struck by an arrow while warning the city of an attack, and the tune breaks off to honour him. This is a tale, not a documented event, but locals still pause when they hear it. From the Town Hall Tower, the sound carries clearly across the square.

Back on the ground, notice the large bronze head lying on its side near the tower. It is a sculpture by the artist Igor Mitoraj, and its eyes are wrapped in a cloth band. The head is hollow, and children often climb inside to look out through the openings. The modern sculpture and the medieval tower make a strange pair. One is broken and resting, the other upright and leaning. Together, they show how old and new live side by side in Kraków.

Before you walk away, look up one last time. Find the slight tilt against the clouds. Listen to the hooves, the voices and the bells. For centuries, this tower watched merchants, councils and crowds fill this square. Its building is gone, but the tower still marks the place where the city once governed itself. As you cross the cobbles, notice how often your eyes return to it. That is what a tower is for.
