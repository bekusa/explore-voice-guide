The guide

The story

Stand in the central passage for a moment and listen. Footsteps echo under the long vaulted roof, and voices bounce between rows of wooden stalls. Somewhere a vendor folds a lace tablecloth, and the light comes in from both ends of the hall, pale and soft. Under your feet, the floor is worn smooth by centuries of shoppers. This is the Cloth Hall, called Sukiennice in Polish, and it sits in the very middle of Kraków's Main Market Square.

The name tells you its first job. Sukno is the Polish word for cloth, and for hundreds of years this building was a place to sell it. Merchants came from across Europe and the East. Fine fabrics arrived from Flanders and other western cities, while local traders sold lead, wax, and salt. Kraków sat on important trade routes, and this hall was where much of that wealth changed hands.

The building did not begin as the grand structure you see today. At first, it was little more than two rows of stalls with a covered lane between them, so merchants could trade out of the rain. Over time, the stalls were joined under one roof. By the fourteenth century, a long Gothic market hall stood here. Its basic shape, a narrow building running down the length of the square, still follows that early plan.

Then came fire. In the middle of the sixteenth century, a blaze badly damaged the hall. Kraków was then the royal capital of Poland, and the city chose to rebuild in the new Renaissance style, which looked back to the forms of ancient Rome and was arriving from Italy. Italian craftsmen worked on the reconstruction. They gave the roofline the decorated parapet you can see when you step outside and look up.

That parapet is worth a closer look. Along the top of the walls runs a screen of stone, called an attic, that hides the roof behind it. It is decorated with carved faces, known as mascarons. Some look calm, some look grumpy, and some look almost comic. Most visitors walk straight past them. Take a minute to find a few, especially on the shorter ends of the building.

Now here is something many people do not realise. The arcades along the long sides of the hall, those open stone arches where people sit and shelter from the sun, are not medieval at all. They were added during a major restoration in the second half of the nineteenth century. The architects gave the old hall a neo-Gothic look, meaning a romantic revival of medieval style. So the building you see is a layered thing: a Gothic core, a Renaissance crown, and a nineteenth-century skin.

Why restore it then? In the nineteenth century, Poland did not exist as an independent state. Its lands were divided between neighbouring empires. Kraków, under Austrian rule, had more freedom than other Polish cities, and it became a centre of Polish culture and memory. Restoring the Cloth Hall was a statement. It said that this place, and the nation's history, still mattered.

That idea leads upstairs. On the upper floor is a gallery of nineteenth-century Polish art, run by the National Museum in Kraków. In fact, the museum itself was founded in this building in 1879. The story is often told around one painter, Henryk Siemiradzki, who gave his huge canvas Nero's Torches to the new collection. That gift helped give the museum its start, and the painting still hangs here.

The gallery is a good place to understand how Poles saw themselves during those divided years. You will find large historical paintings, portraits, and landscapes of the Polish countryside. Jan Matejko, the most famous painter of Polish history, is represented here. His scenes of kings and battles were meant to remind people of a proud past. The rooms are quieter than the market below, with tall windows and gold frames catching the light.

Back on the ground floor, look for a large knife hanging in the passage. It comes with a dark story, and it is folklore, not documented history. Legend has it that two brothers built the two towers of Saint Mary's Basilica, just across the square. One brother, jealous that the other's tower was taller, killed him with this knife. Locals say the killer then threw himself from his own tower. Another, more practical explanation is that the knife was a warning to thieves in the market, a sign of the punishment that waited for them.

The square around you is one of the largest medieval town squares in Europe. Its grid of streets was laid out in the thirteenth century, when Kraków was given its town charter. Beneath your feet there is more history. Excavations under the square uncovered old paving, foundations, and traces of earlier market life. Today an underground museum shows some of those finds, with its entrance close to the hall.

The Cloth Hall is still a market. The stalls inside sell amber jewellery, carved wooden boxes, chess sets, embroidered textiles, and painted eggs. Some of it is made for tourists, and some is fine craft work. Prices and quality vary, so it is worth comparing a few stalls before you buy. On the outside, cafés spill into the arcades, and in the warm months tables fill the square.

When you are ready to leave, walk to one end of the hall and look back down its length. Notice the pale stone, the rhythm of the arches, and the carved faces along the top. Every hour, a trumpet call rings out from the tower of Saint Mary's. If you hear it now, stop and look up. People have traded, argued, and met friends on this exact spot for more than seven hundred years... and today, for a few minutes, you have been part of that story too.
