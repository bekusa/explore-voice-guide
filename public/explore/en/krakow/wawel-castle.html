The guide

The story

Feel the slope under your feet as the path climbs. The cobbles are smooth and uneven, polished by centuries of boots. Below you, the Vistula River bends slowly around the hill. Above you, brick walls and pale stone towers rise against the sky. Somewhere nearby, a church bell may be ringing over the rooftops of Kraków. This is Wawel Hill — a limestone outcrop that has drawn people to its summit for well over a thousand years.

Why here? Look at the river first. The Vistula once spread into marshes and side channels around this rock. A raised hill beside a major river offered water, trade, and a clear view of anyone approaching. Archaeologists have found traces of settlement on the hill that go back to early medieval times and beyond. Long before there was a Poland in the modern sense, someone held this rock. In time, it became the seat of Polish rulers.

For several centuries, Wawel was the centre of the Polish state. Kings lived in the castle. They were crowned in the cathedral beside it, and most of them were buried there too. Life and death, power and faith, all shared one small hilltop. That closeness is part of what makes this place feel so dense. You can walk from a royal bedroom to a royal tomb in a few minutes.

Now step into the great courtyard of the castle. This is the moment many visitors stop and simply look up. Three storeys of arcades wrap around the space, open galleries supported by slender columns. It feels more like Florence than central Europe — and that is no accident. In the early sixteenth century, King Sigismund the Old, of the Jagiellonian dynasty, rebuilt the medieval castle in the new Renaissance style from Italy.

The Renaissance, in short, was the movement that revived the order and proportion of ancient Rome. Sigismund had an Italian wife, Queen Bona Sforza, and Italian architects worked on the rebuilding. Notice the top gallery. Its columns are noticeably taller and thinner than the ones below. Some historians suggest the builders adjusted them so the tall upper level would not feel heavy above the courtyard. Stand in a corner and follow one line of arches all the way around.

Inside, the royal apartments hold one of the castle's great treasures — a collection of huge woven tapestries. They were made in Flanders, in what is now Belgium, for King Sigismund Augustus in the sixteenth century. Many show scenes from the Bible, landscapes, and animals in astonishing detail. The collection was once far larger. Over the centuries, wars and foreign rule carried the tapestries away, and their journey back to Wawel is a long story of loss and return.

Here is a detail most people walk straight past. In one of the state rooms, often called the Envoys' Hall, the coffered wooden ceiling is decorated with carved human heads. Each face looks down with its own expression. Only a fraction of the original heads survive. Legend has it that one carved woman has a band over her mouth because she spoke out of turn during a royal judgement. Locals still tell the story, but treat it as folklore, not record.

At the end of the sixteenth century, the royal court moved its main seat to Warsaw. Kraków remained the city of coronations and royal burials, but daily power left the hill. Later, after Poland was partitioned by its neighbours in the late eighteenth century, the castle fell into foreign hands. In the nineteenth century, the Austrian army used Wawel as barracks and a military hospital. Soldiers lived in rooms once filled with kings and envoys.

The Austrian garrison left in the early twentieth century, and Poles began a long restoration. Much of what you see today is the result of that careful work. During the Second World War, the castle was taken over again, this time as the seat of the German occupation government. Some treasures, including many of the tapestries, were sent abroad for safety at the start of the war. They spent years in exile before they finally came home.

Now turn toward Wawel Cathedral. Its outline looks like a collection of different buildings joined together — and it is. Chapels in different styles were added over many centuries. Look for the one with a golden dome. That is the Sigismund Chapel, built in the early sixteenth century and often praised as one of the finest Renaissance works north of the Alps. In the sunlight, the gilded dome glows against the darker stone around it.

At the cathedral entrance, look up near the door. A few large, old bones hang there on a chain. Locals call them the dragon's bones. In fact, they are prehistoric animal bones. According to one popular tale, the world will end on the day they fall. Inside, the crypts hold kings, national heroes, and the great Romantic poets Adam Mickiewicz and Juliusz Słowacki. Up in the tower hangs the Sigismund Bell, cast in 1520. It rings only on rare and important occasions.

That brings us to the dragon. Every child in Kraków knows the legend of the Wawel Dragon, a beast that lived in a cave beneath the hill and ate the local sheep. In the best-known version, a clever young shoemaker stuffed a sheep with sulphur. The dragon swallowed it, felt a terrible fire inside, and drank from the Vistula until it burst. You can walk down into the cave, called the Dragon's Den, and come out beside the river. There, a bronze dragon statue by Bronisław Chromy breathes real fire every few minutes.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the hill and look out. The river runs below. The old town spreads to the north, with church towers rising above red roofs. In 1978, Kraków's historic centre, including Wawel, joined the first group of places on the UNESCO World Heritage List. As you walk back down, feel the cobbles again under your feet. Kings, soldiers, poets, and pilgrims took this same path. Today, it is your turn.
