The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, a prayer wheel is turning, its metal drum giving a low, steady creak. A pilgrim's palms slap softly on a wooden board as she stretches flat on the ground, rises, and stretches out again. The air is thin and dry, with a faint smell of juniper smoke and melted butter. You are standing at Kumbum Monastery, in the hills southwest of Xining, on the high northeastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau.

The name tells you where to begin. Kumbum is Tibetan, and it means roughly "one hundred thousand images", which refers to images of the Buddha. In Chinese the monastery is called Ta'er Si, which means "the Pagoda Temple". Both names point to the same story. This is the place where a boy named Tsongkhapa was born in the fourteenth century. He grew up to become one of the most influential teachers in Tibetan Buddhism.

Tsongkhapa founded the Gelug school. Many visitors know it as the "Yellow Hat" tradition, after the yellow ceremonial hats its monks wear. The Gelug school stresses careful study, logic, and monastic discipline. Over the following centuries it became the dominant school in Tibet. The line of the Dalai Lamas belongs to it. So this quiet valley is, in a real sense, the starting point of a tradition that shaped the history of the whole plateau.

Now for the legend at the heart of this place. Locals say that when Tsongkhapa was born, drops of blood fell to the ground here. A tree is said to have grown from that spot. According to the tale, its leaves carried markings that looked like images of the Buddha, or sacred syllables. That is the story behind the name, one hundred thousand images. It is a legend, not documented history, but it has drawn pilgrims for centuries.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the 1840s, the French missionary Évariste Huc travelled through this region and stayed near the monastery. He later wrote about the famous tree and its leaves. He admitted he could not explain the markings he saw. A Catholic priest, far from home, puzzling over a Buddhist relic... it is a small, very human moment in the long record of this place.

The story of the buildings begins simply. Tradition holds that Tsongkhapa's mother first raised a small stupa on the site of his birth. A stupa is a dome-shaped Buddhist monument that holds relics or sacred objects. Over time, halls grew around it. By the late sixteenth century, Kumbum had become a full monastery. It kept growing for hundreds of years, and it became one of the great centres of learning in the Amdo region, the northeastern part of the Tibetan world.

As you walk in, you will likely pass a row of eight white stupas standing side by side. Pilgrims often stop here to touch them and to walk around them. Notice the direction they move. They always walk clockwise, keeping the sacred object on their right. If you join the flow of people, do the same. It is a simple gesture of respect, and nobody will mind a visitor who follows quietly.

The most important building is the Great Hall of Golden Tiles. Its roof is covered in gilded tiles, and on a clear day the gold shines hard against the deep blue sky. Inside stands the large stupa that marks Tsongkhapa's birthplace. The hall is often dim and crowded with pilgrims. Lamps burn, cloth banners hang from the ceiling, and the floor in front of the shrine is worn smooth by people who have bowed there.

The second surprise is softer, and much colder. Kumbum is famous for its sculptures made of yak butter. Monks mix butter with colour and shape it into flowers, gods, palaces, and entire scenes from Buddhist stories. The work is done with cold hands, so the butter does not melt. These sculptures are traditionally shown during the Butter Lamp Festival, around the fifteenth day of the first month of the lunar year. Some are displayed in a dedicated hall all year.

Butter sculpture is one of what the monastery calls its three arts. The other two are wall painting and a raised textile craft. In this textile work, layers of silk are stuffed and stitched so that figures stand out from the surface, almost like low relief carving. Look closely and you can see the padding under the cloth. Faces, robes, and animals have real depth. In the half-light of the halls, they can seem to step forward from the wall.

Kumbum is not a museum. It is a working monastery, and monks still live and study here. You may see young monks in deep red robes crossing a courtyard, or carrying thermoses of tea. In some courtyards, monks practise debate, a traditional method of study. One monk asks a question, then claps his hands loudly to challenge his partner. It looks dramatic, but it is a serious way to test understanding of Buddhist philosophy.

The setting matters too. The monastery spreads across the slopes of a small valley, with buildings climbing the hillside. Some walls are whitewashed, others are a dark red, and many roofs carry gilded ornaments. Outside the gates, the town has stalls selling prayer beads, incense, and scarves called khata. A khata is a white or coloured silk scarf that people offer as a sign of respect. You will see many of them tied to railings and draped over shrines.

Before you leave, stand still for one moment. Feel the dry wind on your face, and the hard sunlight of high altitude. Listen again for the creak of the prayer wheels and the soft slap of hands on wood. Six centuries after a boy was born in this valley, people still come here to bow, to walk, to turn the wheels. As you walk out, notice which way you turn... and perhaps let it be clockwise.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk clockwise around every stupa, shrine, and prayer wheel.
Ask before photographing monks or the interior of halls.
Bring sunscreen and water for the strong high-altitude sun.
Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered inside halls.
Check whether butter sculpture hall needs a separate ticket.
