The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Just behind you, water laps against stone. That is the Hofvijver, the old court pond, and its surface catches the light of the sky above The Hague. Ahead of you stands a compact, square house of pale stone and warm brick. It is quiet and well proportioned. It looks almost modest. Few visitors guess at first that this calm façade holds some of the most loved paintings in the world.

This is the Mauritshuis, which means Maurits House. It was built in the seventeenth century for Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen, a cousin of the princes of Orange. In the 1630s and 1640s he served as governor of the Dutch colony in northeastern Brazil. While he was across the ocean, this house rose here in The Hague. It was ready for him when he came home, a statement of status beside the centre of Dutch power.

The design is linked with Jacob van Campen, working with the builder Pieter Post. Van Campen is also known for the old town hall on the Dam in Amsterdam, which is now a royal palace. Here they helped shape a style called Dutch Classicism. In simple terms, it borrowed the order and symmetry of ancient Rome and Renaissance Italy. Then it translated those ideas into Dutch brick and stone. Look at the tall pilasters, the flat columns set into the walls. Look also at the triangular pediment that crowns the front.

Now a harder part of the story. Locals have long called this building the Sugar Palace. The name points to the wealth behind it. Johan Maurits governed a colony built on sugar plantations, and those plantations ran on the labour of enslaved Africans. For a long time this history stayed in the background. In recent years the museum has spoken about it more openly. So the elegant house in front of you carries traces of that colonial past in its very walls.

The house did not stay a private home for long after its owner's death. In 1704 a fire destroyed much of the original interior. The rooms you walk through today were rebuilt in the eighteenth century, with painted ceilings, silk on the walls, and grand fireplaces. That is a detail many visitors miss. The paintings are mostly from the Dutch Golden Age, but the rooms around them come from a later period. Two centuries meet in every gallery.

The collection itself began with the art of the Dutch stadtholders, the princes of Orange. Prince William the Fifth gathered many paintings in the eighteenth century. Then history intervened. When French revolutionary armies took the Netherlands in the 1790s, much of the collection was carried off to Paris. After Napoleon's defeat, many works came back. In 1822 this house opened as the Royal Picture Gallery. It has served as a museum ever since.

And now, the painting that draws people from every continent. Girl with a Pearl Earring, by Johannes Vermeer of Delft. She turns toward you over her shoulder, lips parted, as if you have just said her name. She wears a blue and yellow turban. Her earring is a single bright drop of light. Here is a surprise. She is not a portrait of a known person. The Dutch call this kind of painting a tronie, a study of a face, a character, or an expression.

Stand close and look at the pearl. Researchers who studied the canvas found that it is made of only a few strokes of paint. There is no hook, no outline, nothing that holds it to her ear. Your eye completes the shape. Step back again, and it becomes a perfect jewel. That small trick says a lot about Vermeer. He painted what light does, not only what objects are. The dark background makes her face seem to come forward into the room.

In the same museum hangs another Vermeer, the View of Delft. It shows his home town across the water, under a wide and changing sky. Clouds cast shadows over some roofs while others shine in sunlight. Many writers and painters have admired this canvas. If you spend time with it, notice how the light falls in bands across the city. It is a portrait of a real place on one particular day... and the day has lasted for centuries.

Not far away, look for a very small painting. It is easy to walk past. It shows a goldfinch chained to a perch on a pale wall. It was painted by Carel Fabritius, a gifted pupil of Rembrandt, in 1654. That same year, a huge gunpowder explosion tore through Delft. Fabritius died in it, still a young man. Only a handful of his works survive. This little bird is the most famous of them, and it later inspired a well-known novel.

Rembrandt himself is here too. His Anatomy Lesson of Doctor Nicolaes Tulp, painted in 1632, shows a group of Amsterdam surgeons around a body. The doctor lifts the muscles of the arm with a pair of forceps. The men lean in. Some look at the arm, some look out at us. Rembrandt was only in his twenties, and this commission helped make his name. Look also for one of his self-portraits from the last year of his life, in 1669. It shows an older man, tired and honest.

The Mauritshuis surprises with its scale. These are rooms, not vast halls. You can stand an arm's length from a masterpiece without a crowd of a hundred people. Watch for the paintings of Jan Steen, full of noisy households and small moral jokes. Look for The Bull by Paulus Potter, a life-size young bull in a Dutch meadow. Even the flies on its skin are painted. For a long time the museum felt too small, so it was expanded and reopened in 2014.

That expansion is easy to miss from the outside. The main entrance now leads down into an underground foyer, bright and modern. A passage below the street connects the old house to a neighbouring building across the road. This new space holds the shop, the café, and rooms for temporary exhibitions. The trick keeps the historic house almost untouched above ground. The square front you saw at the start still looks much as it did when Johan Maurits came home.

When you step outside again, pause by the water. To your right are the old government buildings of the Binnenhof, where the Dutch state has met for centuries. Behind you, a quiet house holds a girl in a blue turban and a small bird on a chain. The light on the pond is the same kind of light that Vermeer studied so closely. Look at it for a moment before you walk on. It is a good way to carry the paintings with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see the Vermeer room before crowds build.
Book a timed entry slot online before you visit.
Use the underground foyer for lockers, the café and the shop.
Walk along the Hofvijver pond for the classic façade view.
Combine your visit with a walk around the nearby Binnenhof.
