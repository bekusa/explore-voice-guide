The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere close by, a tiny train hums along its track. A church bell rings, smaller and higher than any bell you have heard. Water splashes through a lock gate no taller than your knee. The gravel crunches under your shoes, and suddenly you feel very large. You have just become a giant in the Netherlands. Welcome to Madurodam, the miniature country on the edge of The Hague.

Madurodam opened in 1952. The idea was simple and charming. Build the Netherlands in small scale, so a visitor can walk across the whole country in an afternoon. Most of the buildings here follow a scale of one to twenty-five. That means a house that is twenty-five metres tall in real life stands about one metre tall here. Look down at your feet, then look up at the nearest canal house. You are roughly the height of a five-storey building.

The name is the first surprise. Madurodam does not mean a dam on a river called Maduro. It carries the name of a real young man, George Maduro. He was a law student from Curaçao, an island in the Caribbean that was then part of the Dutch kingdom. He came from a Jewish family. When Germany invaded the Netherlands in May 1940, Maduro fought as a young officer in the battle around The Hague.

After the Dutch surrender, Maduro joined the resistance. He helped Allied airmen escape and worked against the occupation. He was arrested, and he died in the Dachau concentration camp in 1945, shortly before the end of the war. He was still in his twenties. After his death, the Netherlands honoured him with its highest military order for his courage in 1940. So this playful little country carries a serious memory at its heart.

His parents chose a gentle way to remember him. They gave money to help create this park, and it took his name. The park was also linked to charity from the start. Its earnings have long supported good causes, especially causes for children and young people. Many Dutch visitors know the miniatures well. Fewer of them know the story of the man behind the name. Now you are one of those who do.

There is another detail that delights people. When the park opened, it needed a mayor, because every Dutch town has one. The first mayor of Madurodam was a young Princess Beatrix. She later became Queen of the Netherlands. A miniature city with a royal child as its mayor... it fits the spirit of the place perfectly. Over the years, young people have kept a symbolic role in how the park presents itself.

Now let your eyes travel across the landscape. The park is not a random collection of models. It is a portrait of the country. You will find old city centres with stepped gables and narrow canals. You will find windmills, farms, bridges and churches. You will also find modern things — highways, container cranes, an airport, a busy port. The Netherlands is shown as it lives and works, not only as a postcard.

Look closely at one building and compare it with the real one in your memory. The model makers work with great care. Brick patterns, window frames, roof tiles and shop signs are all reduced in size. The colours of the bricks change from region to region, just as they do in real Dutch towns. Some facades show small signs of weather and age. That patina is deliberate. It makes the tiny streets feel lived in.

Water is everywhere here, and that is honest. Much of the Netherlands lies at or below sea level. The Dutch have spent centuries building dikes, pumps and locks to keep the sea out. In Madurodam you can see that struggle in small form. Ships move through canals and harbours. Watch a lock open and close. You may also find the great storm barriers and dams that protect the southwest of the country from the North Sea.

The second surprise is how much here moves. Trains run on schedules. Boats glide. Planes taxi at the miniature airport. In recent years the park has added more interactive stations. At many of them, you can press a button or turn a wheel and make something happen. Children love this, but adults often push the buttons first. It turns the visit from looking into playing.

The gardens deserve your attention too. Small trees and shrubs are pruned to fit the scale of the buildings. Lawns become fields. Flower beds become whole landscapes. The gardeners here face a strange challenge. Living plants keep growing, but the buildings never do. So the green world of Madurodam needs constant trimming to stay in proportion. Next time you pass a tiny tree, imagine the patience behind it.

Think, too, about where you are standing in the real city. Madurodam sits in the Scheveningen area of The Hague, between the city centre and the sea. The Hague is the seat of the Dutch government and home to international courts. Real parliament buildings stand only a short tram ride away. Here you can look down on a small version of the political heart of the country, and then go and visit the full-size one.

Before you leave, find a quiet corner and crouch down. Bring your eyes to street level, the height of a tiny resident. The view changes completely. The canal houses rise above you. The church tower points into the sky. The model becomes a real street again, for just a moment. This is the oldest trick of miniature worlds — they work best when you meet them on their own scale.

As you walk back towards the exit, the little bells and trains will keep going behind you. Notice the full-size trees above the park, and the real sky that covers both the giant and the small. Somewhere in this miniature country, a name on the gate remembers a brave young man. Carry him with you as you step back into the life-size Netherlands.
