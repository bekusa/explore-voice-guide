The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water moves quietly in the long pond beside you, and the sound of traffic grows faint. Look at the brick. It is not one flat colour. It is a warm mix of yellow, ochre and soft brown, laid in neat, patient rows. The whole building seems to sit low and calm against the sky, and its reflection lies on the surface of the water. Before you walk inside, take a breath here. This approach is part of the design. It slows you down.

You are standing at the Kunstmuseum Den Haag, the main art museum of The Hague. For most of its life it carried another name: the Gemeentemuseum, which simply means the municipal museum. In 2019 it took its current name, which translates as the Art Museum of The Hague. Many locals still use the old name out of habit. If you hear someone say Gemeentemuseum, they mean this place. The building itself has not changed its character. Only the sign on the front is new.

The architect was Hendrik Petrus Berlage, one of the most important Dutch architects of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In Amsterdam, he is known for the old stock exchange building, a landmark of plain brick and honest construction. This museum was his last major work. Here is the first surprise: Berlage died in 1934, and he never saw the museum open its doors. It opened the following year, in 1935. In a sense, you are walking through his final idea.

Notice the long covered walkway that leads you toward the entrance. It runs beside the water like a quiet corridor. Berlage wanted visitors to leave the street behind gradually, not all at once. The walkway works like a pause between the city and the art. As you move along it, the light changes. Shadows from the piers fall across the floor in a steady rhythm. Your footsteps start to match that rhythm, almost without your noticing.

Berlage built this museum on a strict grid. Many of the measurements repeat across the building, from the size of the bricks to the proportions of the rooms. This gives the whole place a feeling of order. Nothing seems random. Look closely at how the brick volumes step up and down, and at how the windows line up. Berlage believed a building should show how it is made. There is no fake marble here and no decoration glued on for effect. The materials stay visible.

Now step inside, into the central hall. Many visitors stop here without quite knowing why. The answer is colour. The walls carry glazed tiles and bricks in soft yellows, greens, pinks and blues. The tiles catch the light and give the space a gentle glow. This is not a cold white gallery. It feels closer to a warm room in a large house. Take a moment to look up, and then to look down at the floor patterns beneath your feet.

The galleries upstairs rely heavily on daylight. Berlage designed many rooms to receive light from above, so the paintings are lit evenly and softly. On a cloudy Dutch afternoon, the light inside shifts slowly as clouds pass over the roof. A painting can look slightly different from one minute to the next. This is worth noticing. The museum does not only show art. It also shows how changing natural light falls on a surface.

And now, the reason many people travel here. The museum holds the largest collection of works by Piet Mondrian in the world. Mondrian was a Dutch painter. Today, most people know him for grids of black lines and blocks of red, yellow and blue. But here is the second surprise. This collection lets you follow his entire journey. His early works show Dutch landscapes, farms, windmills and trees along the water. They look nothing like the famous grids.

Walk through the Mondrian rooms in order, if you can. You will see the trees slowly lose their leaves and become lines. The landscapes flatten. The colours narrow. Step by step, the painter removes everything he considers unnecessary. By the end, only line, plane and pure colour remain. Mondrian was part of a Dutch movement called De Stijl, which means simply the style. Its artists searched for a visual language of balance and harmony that anyone could understand.

The high point is a painting called Victory Boogie Woogie. Mondrian made it in New York, near the end of his life, and he never finished it. The name comes from boogie-woogie, a fast, energetic style of piano jazz that he loved. Instead of long black lines, the canvas is full of small blocks of colour that seem to flicker and move. Look closely and you can still see traces of tape and paper on the surface. The museum acquired this work in 1998.

Mondrian is not the only story here. The museum also collects work from the Hague School, a group of nineteenth-century painters who worked in and around this city. They painted grey skies, dunes, beaches and fishing boats along the North Sea coast, just a short distance from where you stand. You will also find modern European art, a large collection of Delftware, which is the blue-and-white earthenware made in the nearby city of Delft, and a fashion collection that appears in changing exhibitions.

The museum is part of a small cultural cluster. Right next door, the Fotomuseum Den Haag shows photography, and KM21 focuses on contemporary art. Exhibitions in all three change through the year, so the experience is different each time you come. Outside, the surrounding streets are calm and green. Embassies and wide residential avenues sit nearby. The sea is not far away. This part of The Hague feels spacious and unhurried, very different from the busy government district in the centre.

As you leave, walk back along the water and turn around once. Look at the building again, now that you have seen what it holds. The brick, the grid, the careful lines... perhaps they look a little more like a Mondrian now. Berlage and Mondrian were very different men, but both believed in order, honesty and balance. Listen again to the water in the pond. Watch the reflection of the brick on its surface. Then let the city come back to you slowly.

Key facts

Designed by Hendrik Petrus Berlage, his final major building.
Opened in 1935, one year after Berlage's death.
Renamed from Gemeentemuseum to Kunstmuseum in 2019.
Holds the world's largest collection of Piet Mondrian works.
Unfinished Victory Boogie Woogie was acquired in 1998.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Mondrian rooms in order to follow his evolution.
Come on a cloudy day to watch the soft daylight shift.
Check the current programme at the neighbouring Fotomuseum and KM21.
Allow time for the walkway and pond before entering.
Combine your visit with a trip to the nearby coast.
