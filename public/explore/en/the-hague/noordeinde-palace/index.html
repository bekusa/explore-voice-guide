The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind the traffic and the bicycle bells of the street called Noordeinde, there is a softer sound. It is gravel, raked smooth in front of a pale palace. The building is not enormous. It does not tower over the street. It sits back behind iron railings, white and symmetrical, with tall windows and a calm front. If you stand still long enough, you may hear the clip of hooves from the royal stables nearby.

This is Noordeinde Palace, and it is not a museum. It is a working palace. The King of the Netherlands has his office here. He does not live here; his home is another palace, Huis ten Bosch, on the edge of a wood across town. But this is where much of the daily business of the Dutch monarchy happens. Meetings, audiences, state visits, and the signing of papers all take place behind those windows.

The story of the building starts with something much humbler. Long before it was a palace, a medieval farmhouse stood on this spot, at the northern end of the old village of The Hague. The name Noordeinde simply means north end. Over the centuries the farm grew into a large house. The site was outside the busy centre then, among fields and country estates. Today the city has wrapped itself completely around it.

The palace became connected to the House of Orange in the early seventeenth century. The States of Holland gave the house to Louise de Coligny, the widow of William of Orange. William had led the Dutch revolt against Spanish rule, and he was murdered in Delft in 1584. Louise was French, a daughter of a Protestant leader who also died by violence. Giving her this house was a gesture of gratitude toward the family that had led the young republic.

Her stepson, Frederick Henry, the Prince of Orange, turned the house into something grander in the mid seventeenth century. He liked the elegant, balanced style of classical architecture that was popular in the Dutch Golden Age. The central block and the two wings that reach toward the street date from this era of expansion. Look at the clear, regular rhythm of the façade. It is restrained rather than showy, and that restraint is very Dutch.

Here is a turn in the story that many visitors do not expect. For part of the eighteenth century, this palace did not belong to the Dutch at all. When King William the Third of England, who was also a Prince of Orange, died without children in 1702, his inheritance was disputed. Through this tangle, the house passed to the Prussian royal family. For decades, a German king owned a palace in the heart of The Hague.

The House of Orange eventually regained the property. After the French occupation ended in 1813, the new Dutch kingdom began, and King William the First used Noordeinde as a royal residence. Through the nineteenth century, monarchs lived and worked here, and the building was enlarged and adapted. It became the formal centre of royal life in the city that holds the government, even though Amsterdam is the official capital of the Netherlands.

The twentieth century brought damage and repair. In 1948 a fire badly damaged part of the palace. Restoration followed, and later decades brought more careful renovation. The palace you see today is the result of many layers of work. Some walls are very old, some details are modern repairs, and together they make one calm face toward the street. It is a building that has been patched and loved rather than frozen.

Now turn to the square in front of the gates. A bronze equestrian statue of William of Orange stands here, the same man whose widow first received this house. He is often called William the Silent. The nickname is said to come from his careful habit of keeping his thoughts to himself, though historians debate its exact origin. He is shown on horseback, facing the palace of the family he founded.

Once a year, this quiet street becomes the centre of national attention. On Prince's Day, the third Tuesday of September, the King travels from this palace in a horse-drawn coach to the Binnenhof. There, he reads the speech that opens the parliamentary year. Crowds line the route with orange hats and flags. Afterwards, the royal family often appears on the palace balcony and waves to the people gathered below.

There is another ceremony that surprises many visitors. When a new foreign ambassador arrives in the Netherlands, he or she presents credentials to the King here. The ambassador is often collected in a state carriage and driven through the streets to the palace. If you are lucky, you may see a coach and horses with coachmen in livery rolling along an ordinary shopping street, between cafés and parked bicycles. It is a strange and lovely sight.

Behind the palace lies a secret that is not really secret. The palace garden is a public park. Walk around to reach it, and you will find lawns, old trees, and quiet paths where locals walk their dogs and read on benches. The royal stables stand close by. The Royal House Archives are also kept on these grounds. So the history of the family is stored only a few steps from the trees where people eat their lunch.

Before you leave, look up at the roof. When the King is in the country, his royal standard often flies from the palace. Then look along the street itself. Noordeinde is lined with galleries, antique dealers, and small boutiques. Ordinary life runs right up to the palace railings. This is perhaps the most Dutch detail of all: a monarchy that works quietly in an office, on a street where anyone can stroll past and look in.
