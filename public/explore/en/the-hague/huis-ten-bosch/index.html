The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the trees of the Haagse Bos, the city noise fades into birdsong, the crunch of gravel and the soft drip of water from old beech leaves. The air smells of damp earth and moss. Somewhere beyond these trunks, behind fences and careful guards, stands one of the most private houses in the Netherlands. You will not walk through its doors today. But you are standing in the woods that gave it its name.

Huis ten Bosch means, quite simply, the House in the Woods. It is a royal palace, and today it is the home of the Dutch royal family. That is the first surprise for many visitors. In The Hague, the king does not live in a distant country estate. He lives here, inside a forest that sits only a short walk from offices, tram lines and busy streets. The city grew up around the trees, and the trees stayed.

The woods themselves are older than the palace by many centuries. The Haagse Bos is a remnant of the forests that once covered much of this coastal region. Over time, the land was protected, and later rulers kept it as open ground rather than letting builders take it. So as you walk these paths, you are moving through one of the oldest surviving woodlands in the country. The palace was placed here deliberately, as a retreat from the formal life of the court.

The story of the house begins in the seventeenth century, with a woman named Amalia van Solms. She was the wife of Frederik Hendrik, Prince of Orange and stadtholder, which means the leading official of the Dutch Republic. Building work started in the 1640s. Amalia wanted a summer residence, a quieter place close to the city but apart from it. The design came from the architect Pieter Post, working with ideas from Jacob van Campen, one of the great Dutch builders of that age.

The style was Dutch classicism. That means clean lines, balanced proportions and an elegant use of brick and stone, inspired by ancient Roman and Italian buildings but adapted to Dutch taste. Instead of heavy decoration, the beauty came from order and symmetry. At the heart of the house, the architects placed a tall central hall crowned by a dome. That hall would become the most famous room in the palace, and one of the most remarkable painted interiors in northern Europe.

Frederik Hendrik died in 1647, while the house was still young. Amalia turned her grief into a monument. She commissioned a group of leading painters to cover the central hall with scenes celebrating her husband's life and achievements. This room is known as the Oranjezaal, the Orange Hall, named after the House of Orange. Its walls rise in layer after layer of canvas, filled with allegorical figures, triumphant processions and portraits, climbing all the way up to the dome.

Among the artists was Jacob Jordaens, a master from Antwerp, whose large painting of the prince's triumph dominates one wall. Several Dutch painters worked alongside him. Together they created a single, unified space where architecture and painting meet. Here is a detail many visitors miss: the paintings were made to be seen as one complete scheme. You were meant to stand in the centre, turn slowly, and feel surrounded by the memory of one man. It is a seventeenth-century love letter, painted on a grand scale.

The house did not stay frozen in that moment. In the eighteenth century, the palace was enlarged. Two wings were added on either side, and the French-born architect Daniel Marot shaped much of this later work. He brought a richer, more decorative style to the interiors and the front of the building. So the palace you might glimpse today is really two eras joined together. A compact seventeenth-century core sits at the centre, with grander eighteenth-century wings stretching out to the sides.

The building also played a part in world history, and this is the second surprise. In 1899, the First Hague Peace Conference met at Huis ten Bosch. Delegates from many nations gathered under that painted dome to discuss the rules of war and the peaceful settlement of disputes between countries. It was an early step in a long journey. In a way, The Hague's later reputation as a city of international law and justice has roots in that meeting, held in a summer house in the woods.

The twentieth century was harder on the palace. Like much of the Netherlands, it suffered during the Second World War, and it needed serious restoration afterwards. Repair work continued over the following decades. Later, the palace became the home of Queen Beatrix, who lived here for many years during her reign and after it. More recently, the house went through another long renovation. When it was complete, King Willem-Alexander and Queen Máxima moved in with their family, and the woods once again sheltered a royal household.

There is one more curious detail. Far away in Japan, near the city of Nagasaki, there is a large theme park also called Huis Ten Bosch. It recreates a Dutch town, with canals, windmills and a copy of this palace. It reflects a long connection between the Netherlands and Japan, going back to Dutch traders in the seventeenth century. So a building that most people in The Hague only glimpse through trees has a full-size double on the other side of the world, open for anyone to visit.

Because this is a working royal residence, the palace is generally closed to the public, and security is careful. The best way to experience it is the way you are doing now, through the woods themselves. Walk the paths, follow the ponds and streams, and watch how the light changes under the canopy. Joggers, dog walkers and families with prams share these trails every day. It is a royal landscape, but it feels very local, very ordinary, and very Dutch.

Before you move on, pause and look around you. Notice the tall beeches and oaks, the dark water reflecting the branches, the pale sky above. Somewhere beyond them, under a painted dome, a widow's tribute to her husband still fills the walls of a quiet hall. As you walk back toward the city, listen to the sounds return: the trams, the bicycles, the voices. The House in the Woods stays behind you, out of sight... exactly as it was always meant to be.
