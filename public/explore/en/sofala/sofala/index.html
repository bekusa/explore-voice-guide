The guide

The story

Listen first. A magpie calls from a gum tree. A loose sheet of corrugated iron ticks as it warms in the sun. Somewhere below the street, the Turon River moves over its stones. Underfoot, the ground is dry and gritty, the same pale gravel that drew thousands of hopeful people here more than a century and a half ago. Welcome to Sofala, a small village in the hills of the New South Wales Central Tablelands, and one of the oldest surviving gold towns in Australia.

The story begins with the river. In 1851, news spread that gold had been found in the Turon. It was the early heat of the Australian gold rushes, and the valley filled quickly. Diggers came on foot, on horseback, and by bullock dray. They set up tents and bark huts along the banks. For a few remarkable years, this narrow valley held a population far larger than the quiet village you see today.

Now look at the name. Sofala does not come from the local landscape. It is generally linked to Sofala in Mozambique, an old trading port on the coast of East Africa. That port was known for centuries for the gold that passed through it, and some European writers once connected it with the legendary land of Ophir, the source of King Solomon's gold. Naming a rough diggings camp after a fabled city of gold was a hopeful choice... and perhaps a little bit of a joke.

Walk slowly along the main street. The buildings sit close together, and many lean a little, as if they are tired. You will see weatherboard walls, timber posts, and deep verandahs that throw shade across the footpath. Corrugated iron covers many roofs and walls. Some sheets are silver and new. Others have turned a deep rust red. That patchwork of old and new metal is one of the clearest records of how this village has been patched and repaired over many decades.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The village grew without a formal grand plan. The street follows the shape of the land and the river, not a neat grid. That is why the buildings step up and down with the slope, and why rooflines rarely match. Stand at one end and look along the street. The uneven line of verandahs and gables tells you that people built what they needed, when they could afford it.

The gold rush did not last forever. As the easy surface gold ran out, many diggers moved on to newer fields. Mining continued in various forms, but the crowds thinned. Over time, Sofala became a small rural settlement. Yet the buildings stayed. Because the village never grew into a large town, much of its old fabric was not replaced. In a strange way, quiet decline protected it.

This is where art enters the story. In 1947, the painter Russell Drysdale made a painting called simply Sofala. It shows an empty, sun-bleached street, with timber buildings and long shadows under a heavy sky. The painting won the Wynne Prize, an Australian award for landscape art. For many Australians, that image became a picture of the lonely country town. Standing here, you can compare his dry, still vision with the street in front of you.

Film-makers noticed the place too. Sofala was used as a location for The Cars That Ate Paris, an early feature film by the director Peter Weir, released in the 1970s. The film is a dark comedy about a strange country town. The setting needed to look isolated and a little uneasy. Sofala's old street, with its weathered walls and empty spaces, gave the camera exactly that mood.

Now turn toward the river. The Turon runs below the village, through a valley of rock, sand, and casuarina trees. Casuarinas are thin, needle-leaved trees, and when wind passes through them they make a soft hissing sound. On the banks, look for heaps of stones and disturbed gravel. Some of these marks may come from old mining work. Others come from modern visitors trying their luck with a gold pan.

Yes, people still pan here. Swirling water and gravel in a shallow dish, looking for a yellow flake, is a simple pleasure. It also connects you directly with the people who stood in this same river long ago. Most visitors find only sand and a few dark grains of heavy mineral. But the slow, patient work tells you a great deal about the hard, uncertain life of a digger. Check the local rules before you begin, because fossicking areas and permits can change.

Think about the sounds this valley once held. Picks striking rock. Cradles rocking back and forth to wash gravel. Shouts, arguments, singing, and the noise of hotels and stores serving a crowd of strangers. People came from Britain, Ireland, China, Europe, and other parts of the colonies. Today the valley is much quieter. That contrast is part of the experience here. You are standing in a place that was once crowded, now holding its breath.

The village is still lived in. This is not a museum behind a fence. People run small shops, cafes, and the old pub, and some houses are private homes. So walk with respect. Admire the verandahs and gardens from the street, and keep gates and private yards for their owners. A short conversation with a local can often teach you more than any sign. Many people here know stories about their own buildings.

Before you leave, pause in the shade of a verandah. Feel how much cooler it is under that iron roof. Notice the colour of the light on the old timber, and the dust on the road. Listen again for the river below. The gold that drew the crowds is mostly gone. What remains is this narrow street, these patched walls, and the quiet valley. As you walk away, look back once. You may see the same still street that Drysdale saw.
