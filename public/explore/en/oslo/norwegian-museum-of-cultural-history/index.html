The guide

The story

Start with the smell. Old tar, sun-warmed pine, and a faint trace of woodsmoke drift across the paths. Underfoot, gravel crunches, then gives way to soft grass. Above you, roofs are covered not in tiles but in living turf, with tufts of wildflowers growing on top. You are standing in a gathering of real Norwegian buildings, carried here from valleys, fjords and city streets across the country. This is the Norwegian Museum of Cultural History, known locally as Norsk Folkemuseum.

The idea behind this place is simple, and it was radical in its time. Instead of putting objects in glass cases, the museum moved whole buildings. Farmhouses, storehouses, barns, a church, even city apartments were taken apart, numbered, transported, and rebuilt here on the Bygdøy peninsula, just west of central Oslo. The result is an open-air museum. You do not look at Norwegian history from the outside. You walk through its doorways, duck under its low beams, and smell its timber.

The museum was founded in 1894 by a young collector named Hans Aall. Norway at that time was in a union with Sweden, and many Norwegians felt a strong need to define what made their country distinct. Rural life seemed to hold the answer. Farming traditions, local building styles, folk costumes and carved furniture were disappearing as the country modernised. Aall and his supporters wanted to save this everyday heritage before it was lost — not the treasures of kings, but the homes of ordinary people.

Here is the first surprise. Royalty was involved after all. Before Aall's museum existed, King Oscar the Second had already gathered a small collection of old buildings here on Bygdøy. It opened to the public in the early 1880s, and it is often described as the first open-air museum in the world. In the early twentieth century, the king's collection became part of the Folkemuseum. So the ground beneath you holds one of the earliest experiments in this kind of museum anywhere.

The jewel of that royal collection is the Gol Stave Church. A stave church is a medieval wooden church built on tall vertical posts, called staves, rather than on stone walls. This one came from the village of Gol in the Hallingdal valley and dates from around the year 1200. Look up at its stacked roofs, layered like a dark wooden pagoda. Near the top, carved dragon heads stretch outward from the gables — a reminder that Christian builders in Norway still used older Norse decoration.

Walk closer and notice the colour of the wood. It is almost black. Centuries of tar have been brushed onto the timber to protect it from rain and snow. On a warm day, you can still smell it. Step inside if you can, and let your eyes adjust. The interior is dim, lit only by small openings. Medieval worshippers stood here in near darkness. Much of the church was restored when it was rebuilt, so look for the contrast between older and newer timber.

From the church, the paths lead out into the rural sections. The buildings are grouped by region, so you can move from one part of Norway to another in a few minutes' walk. Look for the small storehouses raised on short wooden posts. Farmers built them this way to keep food dry and to stop mice from climbing in. Some have flat stones set on top of the posts, an extra barrier against rodents. It is clever, practical engineering in plain wood.

Now look at the walls of the older farmhouses. Many are made of horizontal logs, notched together at the corners with no nails. Inside, rooms often centre on an open hearth or a corner fireplace. Families cooked, ate, worked and slept in the same smoky space through long winters. In some houses you will see furniture and walls covered in rose painting, a Norwegian folk art of flowing flowers and scrolls in deep reds, blues and greens. It brought colour into dark interiors.

Here is the second surprise, and it is one many visitors miss. The museum does not stop in the countryside or in the distant past. There is also an Old Town section, with townhouses, shops and streets taken from Norwegian towns, including Oslo itself. One apartment building came from an ordinary Oslo street. Its flats are furnished to show how people lived in different decades — from the nineteenth century right up to recent times.

Walking up that staircase is a strange experience. You open one door and find a cramped working-class home with simple furniture. Another door shows a living room from the middle of the twentieth century. One apartment shows the home of a Pakistani-Norwegian family from the early 2000s. This choice is deliberate. The museum treats immigration and modern city life as part of Norwegian culture, not something separate from it. Cultural history, here, includes the present.

The indoor exhibition halls are worth your time too. They hold collections of folk costumes, known as bunad, with embroidery that changes from district to district. There are displays of folk art, church art, toys, and everyday tools. There is also an exhibition about the Sámi, the Indigenous people of northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia. It is a good place to come in from the rain and see the small, handmade objects that the open-air buildings once held.

In the warmer months, the site feels lived-in. Staff in historical dress often demonstrate traditional crafts, cooking and farm work, and animals may graze near the farm buildings. Children run between the houses and peer into dark lofts. Out of season, the paths are quieter. Snow on the turf roofs makes the old farms look exactly as they were built to look — low, heavy shelters against a long northern winter. Both seasons show a different side of the place.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment wherever you are. Listen to the wind in the birch trees and the creak of old timber. Every building around you once stood somewhere else, in a valley or a city street, and people lived real lives inside it. As you walk back toward the gate, look again at those turf roofs and tarred walls. You are walking through Norway's past, rebuilt one log at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for gravel paths and uneven ground.
Visit in summer to see live craft demonstrations.
Use the indoor exhibition halls on rainy days.
Allow at least half a day to explore properly.
Reach Bygdøy by bus, or by ferry in summer.
