The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Trams hum along the streets nearby, and footsteps cross the open ground of Tullinløkka. In front of you is a stately nineteenth-century building, solid and symmetrical. For more than a century, Norwegians came here to meet their own country in paint. They saw mountains, fjords and farmhouses, and then something stranger: a figure on a bridge, holding its face in its hands.

This is the National Gallery of Norway, known in Norwegian as Nasjonalgalleriet. Its story begins with an idea about national identity. In the early nineteenth century, Norway had only recently loosened its long union with Denmark. It was now joined to Sweden under a shared king. Educated Norwegians wanted institutions that belonged to them alone. A university, a parliament, and a national collection of art were all part of that dream.

The gallery was founded in 1837, after the Norwegian parliament agreed to support it. One of its strongest champions was the painter Johan Christian Dahl. He was Norwegian by birth but spent most of his career in Dresden, in Germany. Dahl argued that a young nation needed a place to keep and show its art. He also helped send paintings home to build the first collection. For years, those works moved between borrowed rooms.

The building you see came later, in the 1880s, and it grew in stages over the following decades. Its style is classical and calm. It has regular windows, careful proportions and a sense of order. The architecture was meant to signal seriousness. Art, in this view, deserved the same dignity as law or learning. That is no accident of location. The university buildings and the city's main avenue are only a short walk away.

Inside, the gallery told a particular story about Norway. Landscape painters of the mid-nineteenth century turned steep valleys and silver fjords into national symbols. One much-loved work, Bridal Procession on the Hardangerfjord, came from a collaboration. Adolph Tidemand painted the people, and Hans Gude painted the water and mountains. A wedding party rows across shining water. For many Norwegians, that canvas came to stand for an idea of home.

Then came Edvard Munch, and the story changed. Munch painted fear, jealousy, illness and longing. His works were not comfortable, and early critics often attacked them. Over time, the National Gallery gathered some of his most important pieces. Visitors could stand before The Sick Child, before Madonna, and before the 1893 version of The Scream. The Scream is painted on cardboard, with swirling orange sky and an empty, howling face.

Here is a story many visitors never hear. In February 1994, the eyes of the world were on Norway. The Winter Olympics were opening in Lillehammer. On that same morning, thieves climbed a ladder to a window of this building. They broke in and took The Scream from the wall. The whole theft lasted only a few minutes. According to widely reported accounts, they left behind a mocking note about the gallery's weak security.

The painting was missing for several months. A police operation, with help from British detectives, finally recovered it later that same year. The work had survived in good condition. The theft changed how Norwegian museums thought about protection. It also turned an already famous image into something closer to a legend. People who had never thought about Munch now knew the face on the bridge.

Now for the second surprise, and it matters if you are standing outside these walls today. The famous paintings are no longer here. In 2003, the National Gallery merged with several other institutions to form the National Museum of Art, Architecture and Design. The old gallery closed its doors in 2019. Its collection moved to a large new National Museum building near the harbour, which opened in 2022.

So this building now holds memory more than masterpieces. For generations, school groups filed through these rooms. Students sat sketching in front of the landscapes. Couples met under the high ceilings, and tourists queued for a glimpse of Munch. Discussions about the building's future use have continued for years. Whatever comes next, the structure itself still carries the shape of that long public life.

Take a moment to look at the facade as architecture rather than as a container. Notice the rhythm of the windows and the weight of the stonework at ground level. Look at how the building sits back from the open square. Nineteenth-century museums were often designed like temples or palaces. The aim was to slow people down before they entered, so that they arrived ready to look carefully.

If you want to see the paintings themselves, the walk to the new National Museum is not long. Head south toward the waterfront, near City Hall and the harbour. There, Bridal Procession on the Hardangerfjord hangs again, and Dahl's mountain views are back on display. The Munch room still draws a crowd around The Scream. The works are the same; only the walls around them have changed.

Before you move on, look around this quiet corner of Oslo once more. The university, the old streets and the open square all date from the same hopeful century. Back then, a small nation was deciding what it wanted to be. This building was one of its answers. As you walk away, listen again to the trams and the footsteps. They are the same sounds that generations of gallery visitors heard on this spot.
