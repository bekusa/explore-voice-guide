The guide

The story

Listen first. On Bygdøy, the peninsula west of central Oslo, the city noise drops away. You hear gulls, wind in the birch trees, and gravel under your shoes. Ahead stands a pale, plastered building with steep roofs. It looks more like a quiet chapel than a museum. Inside, for most of a century, three ships slept in cool, still air. They are wooden vessels more than a thousand years old, and each one was buried in the earth before it reached this place.

First, a practical word. In 2021 the museum closed for a large rebuilding project. The plan is to reopen it as the Museum of the Viking Age, with a new building joined to the old one. The ships did not leave during the work. They are too fragile to move safely, so the builders built around them. Depending on when you visit, the doors may still be closed. Check the current status before you plan your day.

Now, the ships themselves. None of these vessels sank or rotted on a beach. Each one was dragged ashore, set in a trench, and covered with a large mound of earth and turf. They were graves. Powerful people of the Viking Age were sometimes buried with a ship, as if the dead needed a vessel for the journey ahead. Because the ships were sealed in clay and turf, the wood survived. That is the main reason this collection exists at all.

The most famous of the three is the Oseberg ship. It came from a burial mound on a farm in Vestfold, the region on the western side of the Oslofjord. Archaeologists excavated it in 1904. Tree-ring dating of the grave timbers places the burial in 834. Inside the burial chamber lay the remains of two women. Nobody knows their names. Many theories exist about who they were, and some people link them to a queen. That idea remains speculation, not proven history.

What makes Oseberg unusual is its beauty. The ship is long and low, more than twenty metres from end to end. Its curved bow and stern are carved with bands of interlaced animals. These creatures grip and twist around each other in an endless pattern. At the very tip, the stem ends in a tight spiral, like the coiled body of a serpent. Look closely at the carving if you can see it. This was a ship built to be seen and admired.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Oseberg ship was probably not a strong ocean-going vessel. Its hull is light and quite shallow. Many experts think it suited calm coastal waters better than the open North Sea. In other words, it may have been a ship for display and prestige. It was a floating statement of wealth, used close to home, and then given to the grave.

The grave goods tell the same story. Excavators found a carved wooden cart, several sledges, beds, chests, kitchen tools, and textiles. They also found the remains of animals, including horses, buried with the women. The cart and sledges carry rich carving, including human faces and animal heads. Together they show something written sources rarely describe: everyday objects of the ninth century, made by skilled hands and decorated with great care.

The second vessel is the Gokstad ship. It was dug out of a mound near Sandefjord, also in Vestfold, in 1880. This grave belonged to a man, buried around the year 900. Unlike Oseberg, Gokstad is a sturdy, broad ship with a strong keel. Along the upper edge of its hull you may notice round holes for oars. It was built for real sailing, and it shows how far Viking ships could travel.

Now the second surprise. In 1893, Norwegians built a full-size copy of the Gokstad ship and named it Viking. A crew sailed it across the Atlantic to the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. The crossing proved that a ship of this design could handle the open ocean. It was an experiment in history, carried out with rope, sail, and nerve. That replica voyage helped change how people saw Viking seafaring.

The third ship is the Tune ship, found in Østfold, east of the fjord, in 1867. It was the first of the three to be discovered. Much less of it survives, so it is often the quietest part of the collection. But it matters. It reminds us that the other two are rare lucky survivals. Most ships of the period are lost forever, and even the Tune ship is only a fragment.

The building around them also has its own story. The architect Arnstein Arneberg designed it in the shape of a cross, with one long hall for each ship. The first hall opened in the 1920s, and the rest followed over the next decades. The white walls and high, simple rooms were a deliberate choice. They let the dark wood stand out and gave the ships the feeling of a sacred space rather than a showroom.

Preservation is the hidden drama here. In the early twentieth century, conservators treated some wooden objects with alum, a mineral salt, to stabilise them. At the time this seemed a good method. Over the decades, however, the alum has made some pieces brittle from the inside. Today scientists work to understand and protect them. This is one reason the new museum focuses so much on climate control, steady temperature, and a calm environment.

Before you leave, pause outside for a moment. Look at the roofline against the sky, and at the trees that frame the building. Somewhere behind these walls are timbers cut by people who lived more than a thousand years ago. They shaped the planks with axes, carved serpents into the bow, and then buried their work with their dead. As you walk back toward the water, listen again for the gulls and the wind. The fjord those ships once knew is only a short walk away.
