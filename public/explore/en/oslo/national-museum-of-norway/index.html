The guide

The story

Stand for a moment and let your eyes settle on the stone. The walls around you are dark grey slate, cut from Norwegian quarries. Up close, the surface is not flat at all. It ripples with fine layers and small flecks that catch the light. On a wet Oslo day the slate turns almost black. On a bright one it shows soft tones of blue and green. Above it, at the very top of the building, sits a long pale box that seems to float. That is where we will end. But first, let us go inside.

You are standing at the National Museum of Norway, which opened in June 2022. It is the largest art museum in the Nordic countries. The building sits on the waterfront, between the old Western Railway Station and the brick towers of Oslo City Hall. The location was once full of tracks and freight. Today it is one of the calmest corners of the harbour. The ferries to the islands leave just a few minutes' walk away, and you can often hear their horns.

Here is the first surprise. This museum is not one institution with a long, single history. It is four museums joined into one. In 2003, the Norwegian state merged the National Gallery, the Museum of Contemporary Art, the Museum of Decorative Arts and Design, and the National Museum of Architecture. For almost twenty years, these collections still lived in separate buildings around the city. This new home was built to bring them together under a single roof.

The architects were the German firm Kleihues plus Schuwerk, led by Klaus Schuwerk. Their idea was simple and quite strict. They wanted a building that would last for centuries, not decades. So they chose heavy, honest materials: slate, oak, bronze, and marble. There are very few decorative tricks here. The rooms are calm and well proportioned. The building does not try to compete with the art. It gives the art space.

As you walk through the galleries, notice how the story moves. The collection runs roughly from the ancient world and the Middle Ages through to today. Paintings sit near furniture. Silver sits near fashion. A chair might share a room with a landscape painted in the same decade. This mixing is deliberate. The curators want you to see that a period's paintings, clothes, and objects all came from the same world, and the same hands.

One of the oldest treasures here is the Baldishol tapestry. It was found in a church in eastern Norway and dates to the Middle Ages, probably around the late twelfth or early thirteenth century. It is one of the oldest surviving tapestries in Europe. Look closely and you will find a figure representing a month of the year, a knight on horseback, and soft, faded reds and blues. Remember that someone wove every thread of it by hand, more than eight hundred years ago.

Norway's own painters fill many rooms. Johan Christian Dahl, in the nineteenth century, taught Norwegians to see their mountains and fjords as worthy of great art. Later, Harald Sohlberg painted Winter Night in the Mountains, a deep blue scene of snowy peaks under a cold sky. Many Norwegians feel that this painting shows something essential about their country. Stand in front of it for a minute. Notice how the blue slowly becomes darker the longer you look.

And then, of course, there is Edvard Munch. The museum has a dedicated room for his work, and it is usually the busiest place in the building. Here you can see The Scream, painted in 1893. You may also see works such as Madonna and The Dance of Life. Munch painted love, jealousy, illness, and fear with a directness that still feels new. His colours are not pretty. They are loud, and they are meant to be.

Now for the second surprise. In the upper left corner of The Scream, there is a tiny line of pencil writing. It says, in Norwegian, that the painting could only have been painted by a madman. For years, people wondered if a angry visitor had written it as an insult. In 2021, the museum's researchers studied the handwriting and concluded that Munch wrote it himself. Very few visitors ever notice it. Look closely, if the crowd allows.

This painting has also had a dramatic life. In 1994, on the same day the Winter Olympics opened in Lillehammer, thieves broke into the old National Gallery and stole The Scream. They left a mocking note behind. The painting was recovered a few months later, undamaged enough to be restored. Today it hangs here under careful protection. It is a reminder that famous art is not only admired. Sometimes it is also hunted.

Do not rush past the design and craft galleries. They hold furniture, ceramics, glass, and clothing from many centuries. You will find the clean lines of twentieth century Scandinavian design, which shaped how much of the world furnishes a home today. There are also contemporary works, some large and some strange, that ask you to slow down. If Munch's room is loud, these rooms are often quiet. That contrast is worth enjoying.

Finally, go up to the Light Hall, the pale box you saw from outside. Its walls are made of thin panels of glass and marble that let daylight through. After dark, the panels glow from within. Inside, the hall is a vast, open space used for changing exhibitions. It feels very different from the galleries below. There are no walls to guide you, only light. Nearby, a rooftop terrace looks out over City Hall, the fortress, and the fjord.

When you step back outside, look again at the slate under your hand. It came from the same mountains that Dahl and Sohlberg painted. That is perhaps the quiet idea behind this building. Norway's stone holds Norway's art. As you walk toward the harbour, listen for the ferries, and watch the light move on the water. It is the same light many of these painters spent their lives trying to catch.

Deep winter blues in Sohlberg's mountain night painting.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Munch's room early to avoid the thickest crowds.
Save the rooftop terrace for the end of your visit.
Return after dark to see the Light Hall glow.
Combine your visit with a walk to nearby City Hall.
Wear comfortable shoes; the galleries cover a large area.
