The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the open square and listen. Gulls call over the harbour. Ferry engines rumble at the pier, and the wind off the fjord carries a sharp smell of salt. Behind you rise the two brick towers of Oslo City Hall. In front of you is a pale, elegant building that looks a little too grand for a museum. There is a good reason for that. It was never meant to be one.

This is the Nobel Peace Center. Before it held stories of peace, it held trains. The building was once Oslo's western railway station, known locally as Vestbanen. It dates from the nineteenth century, when trains left from here for the towns along the western side of the fjord. Travellers once waited inside with luggage and tickets in their hands. Today, visitors come here to meet the people who have tried to end wars.

The station closed in the late twentieth century, after rail traffic moved to the city's new central station. For years the old building waited for a new purpose. That purpose came in 2005, when the Nobel Peace Center opened its doors. Its location is no accident. Every December, the Nobel Peace Prize is awarded in the City Hall just across the square. The ceremony and the museum face each other across a few steps of open pavement.

So why is a prize named after a Swede awarded in Norway? This question surprises many visitors. Alfred Nobel was a Swedish chemist and industrialist. He made his fortune from explosives, including dynamite. In his will, he left most of his wealth to fund annual prizes. Physics, chemistry, medicine and literature would be judged in Sweden. But the prize for peace, he wrote, should be given by a committee chosen by the Norwegian parliament.

Nobel never explained this choice in writing, and historians still discuss it. At the time, Sweden and Norway were joined in a union under one king. Some people suggest Nobel admired Norway's parliament and its interest in peaceful settlement of disputes. Others say he simply wanted to share the honour between the two countries. Nobody knows for sure. What we do know is the result: one of the world's best-known prizes has its home here, beside this harbour.

The first Peace Prize was awarded in 1901. It was shared by two men. One was Henri Dunant, the Swiss founder of the Red Cross. The other was Frédéric Passy, a French campaigner for international arbitration, which means settling conflicts through negotiation instead of war. Since then, the prize has gone to presidents and protesters, to doctors and diplomats, and to organisations as well as individuals. In some years, especially during the world wars, no prize was given at all.

As you walk inside, notice how the old station has been reshaped. The exhibition design was created by the architect David Adjaye, who was born in Tanzania to Ghanaian parents and later worked from London. He used strong colour, careful lighting and changing levels of darkness and brightness. Rooms move from shadow into light. The effect is deliberate. The building asks you to slow down, to look closely, and to feel the weight of the stories it holds.

One of the most memorable spaces is a dark room filled with small glowing screens. They rise on thin stems, like a field of light. Each screen is connected to a laureate, someone who has received the prize. As you lean close, a face and a short story appear. Some names you will know at once. Others may be new to you. Here, famous leaders stand beside lesser-known activists, and the room treats them all with the same quiet attention.

The centre is not a frozen museum. Every year, after a new laureate is announced, the main exhibition changes to tell that person's or that organisation's story. This means the building looks different from one year to the next. A visitor who came five years ago would find new rooms, new photographs and new voices today. The centre also hosts debates, talks and photography exhibitions about conflict, human rights and democracy. Peace here is treated as unfinished work, not as a closed chapter.

Some of the strongest moments in the prize's history are about absence. In 2010, the prize went to the Chinese writer Liu Xiaobo, who was in prison at the time. Neither he nor his family could travel to Oslo. At the ceremony in City Hall, his diploma and medal were placed on an empty chair. That image travelled around the world. It is a reminder that this prize has often honoured people who paid a high price for speaking out.

The medal itself has a Norwegian story too. The design of the Peace Prize medal is credited to Gustav Vigeland, the sculptor whose figures fill the large park in the west of the city. So when you see a laureate holding that small golden disc, you are looking at the work of an Oslo artist. It is a quiet link between this museum and the rest of the city around you.

When you step back outside, take a moment to look at the square from a new angle. To one side is City Hall, where the ceremony takes place each year. Beyond it are the harbour, the ferries and the fortress walls of Akershus on the point. In early December, this area fills with people and lights for the prize celebrations. On an ordinary afternoon, it is simply a place where locals walk, cycle and drink coffee by the water.

Before you go, look once more at the old station front. Picture the steam, the whistles, the crowds of travellers leaving for the fjord. Then picture the faces you just saw inside, glowing in the dark. This building has always been about departures and journeys. Today the journeys are different... harder, longer, and often still unfinished. As you walk away along the harbour, listen again to the gulls, and to the quiet ordinary sounds of a city at peace.

Practical tips

Allow extra time in the dark room of glowing screens.

Walk the harbour promenade toward Akershus Fortress afterwards.
