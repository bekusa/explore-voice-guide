The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The pine forest around you is never quite silent. Wind moves high in the crowns, needles drop onto soft ground, and somewhere a woodpecker taps at bark. Then the trees open, and a long white house appears among them. It does not stand above the forest. It seems to sit inside it, as if the woods agreed to make room.

This is Villa Mairea, in Noormarkku, a short drive north of central Pori. Noormarkku was its own municipality for a long time and later became part of Pori. The house was completed in 1939. It was designed by the Finnish architect Alvar Aalto, working closely with his wife Aino, who was an architect in her own right. Many historians count it among the most important private houses of the twentieth century.

The story begins with the clients, Harry and Maire Gullichsen. Maire came from the Ahlström family, whose company had deep roots here in Noormarkku. This was an industrial village built around ironworks and sawmills, and the family homes stood close to the work. Harry led the family company. The house carries Maire's name, and that choice says a lot. This was not only a family residence. It was also her project.

Maire Gullichsen was a painter and a serious collector of modern art. She was also a cultural activist. In 1935 she joined the Aaltos and the critic Nils-Gustav Hahl to found Artek, the company that still produces Aalto furniture today. So the people who built this house were friends and partners first. They shared one idea: modern design should be warm, humane, and part of everyday life.

Here is the first surprise. The Gullichsens gave Aalto unusual freedom. They encouraged him to treat the house as an experiment, a place to test ideas he could not yet try in public buildings. The design changed many times on paper before work began. What you see is the result of that freedom. It is a house where Aalto worked out, in wood and stone, what a modern Finnish home could feel like.

Look at the overall shape. The house forms an L around a garden courtyard. The two wings open towards each other, and the courtyard sits sheltered between them, away from the road. On the outer side, the building feels calm and closed. On the courtyard side, it opens up. Aalto often thought about buildings in this way — a protective face to the world, and a softer face for the people inside.

Now look at the entrance canopy. It does not have neat, straight columns. It rests on a cluster of slim posts, some of them tied together, set at slightly uneven angles. The roof above them has a soft, irregular curve. Many visitors walk straight past this without noticing. But Aalto meant it as a small echo of the forest you just walked through — trunks, branches, and a canopy of leaves.

That forest idea continues inside. The main living space on the ground floor is open and flowing. Rooms are separated by changes in floor material, by furniture, and by light, rather than by solid walls. The structural columns are black steel, but many are wrapped in rattan or bound with cord. When you touch them, they feel warm rather than cold. This is the second surprise. Even the steel was dressed to feel natural to the hand.

Find the main staircase. It is screened by a row of vertical wooden poles, placed at irregular intervals. As you move past them, the view through them shifts, much like walking among tree trunks. Light falls between the poles and lands in stripes on the floor. It is one of the quietest details in the house, and one of the most loved by architects who come here.

The house was also built to hold art. Maire's collection was meant to live in these rooms, not in a vault. Aalto planned walls, light, and space so that paintings could be seen in daylight, as part of family life. Upstairs there was a studio for Maire herself. The art on display can change, so ask your guide what is hanging today and who painted it.

Step out into the courtyard if your tour allows. There is a swimming pool with a free, curving outline, something like a kidney or a lake shore. Beside it stands a sauna with a turf roof and dark timber walls. These two elements feel very different. The pool is modern and playful. The sauna points to old Finnish rural building. Aalto placed them side by side on purpose, linking the new with the very old.

That mix is really the heart of Villa Mairea. White rendered walls stand beside dark wood. Industrial steel sits next to rattan and stone. The house uses the language of international modernism, yet it never forgets that it stands in a Finnish forest. Critics have often described it as a bridge between the machine age and the natural world. You can judge that for yourself as you move through the rooms.

A practical note. Villa Mairea is still a private home, cared for by the family's foundation. It is not an ordinary museum, and visits happen on guided tours that need to be booked ahead. Please treat it like someone's house, because it is. Rules about photography inside can vary, so ask your guide before you lift your camera. And take time afterwards to walk the old village of Noormarkku nearby.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Look back at the house through the trunks of the pines. Notice how the white walls catch the light, and how the dark timber seems to sink into the shade. As you walk away, listen again to the wind in the trees. Aalto built this house to belong to that sound. Perhaps you will hear it a little differently now.
