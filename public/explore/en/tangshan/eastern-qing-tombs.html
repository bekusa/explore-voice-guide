The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through old pine trees, and somewhere a bird calls across a wide, quiet valley. Under your feet the stone is pale and smooth, worn by centuries of footsteps. Ahead, yellow glazed roofs sit low against a line of green hills. This is a place built for silence, and even today, with visitors all around, it keeps a little of that hush.

You are standing at the Eastern Qing Tombs, in Zunhua, a county-level city under the administration of Tangshan in Hebei Province. Beijing lies a little over a hundred kilometres to the west. Here, the Qing dynasty buried its rulers. The Qing were the last imperial dynasty of China, founded by the Manchus from the northeast, and they ruled from the mid seventeenth century until 1912. This valley became their largest burial ground, and today it is the largest surviving imperial mausoleum complex in China.

The numbers help you picture the scale. Five emperors rest here, along with empresses, imperial consorts, princesses and other members of the court. Each emperor has his own tomb, with gates, halls, bridges and a burial mound. Together they spread across a wide basin framed by mountains. In 2000, the site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, as part of the Imperial Tombs of the Ming and Qing Dynasties.

Why here? Locals tell a story about the Shunzhi Emperor, the first Qing emperor to rule from Beijing. According to the legend, he was hunting in these hills when he stopped and admired the view. He took off the jade ring he wore for archery and threw it. Wherever it landed, he said, would be his resting place. It is a lovely tale, but treat it as folklore, not as recorded history.

The real reasoning was feng shui, the Chinese tradition of placing buildings in harmony with land and water. A good imperial grave needed mountains behind it for protection and open land with water in front. Look north and you will see Changrui Mountain, which shelters the valley like the back of a great chair. Lower hills stand on either side. The whole landscape was chosen and read as carefully as any building plan.

Shunzhi's tomb, called Xiaoling, sits at the centre of the complex, and the tombs of later rulers spread out around it. Leading toward it is the Spirit Way, a long ceremonial road. Stone figures line both sides — lions, elephants, camels, horses, and tall officials in court dress. They stand in pairs, facing each other across the road, as an honour guard for the dead emperor. Walk slowly here. The faces change from figure to figure.

Here is something many visitors miss. The Spirit Way does not run in a perfectly straight line. It bends gently, following the shape of the land instead of cutting through it. Builders of this period believed a road should move with the hills and streams. So as you walk, the tomb ahead shifts slightly in your view, and the approach feels less like a parade ground and more like a slow journey.

The most famous emperor here is Qianlong, who ruled for about six decades in the eighteenth century, one of the longest reigns in Chinese history. His tomb, Yuling, is known for its underground palace. You can walk down into it. The walls and vaulted ceilings are covered in carved Buddhist images, along with inscriptions in Tibetan and Sanskrit scripts. Standing among them, deep under the earth, is a cool and strangely calm experience.

Then there is Empress Dowager Cixi, one of the most powerful women in Chinese history. For decades in the late nineteenth century, she controlled the court from behind the throne. Her tomb, Dingdongling, shares its site with the tomb of another empress. Late in her life, Cixi ordered parts of her own tomb to be taken down and rebuilt, more richly than before. The interiors were finished with gold, and you can still see the warm shine on the woodwork.

Now look carefully at the white marble in front of Cixi's main hall. The carving shows a phoenix and a dragon. In imperial China, the dragon stood for the emperor and the phoenix for the empress. Usually the dragon held the higher position. Here, the phoenix flies above, and the dragon sits below. Many people read this as a quiet statement in stone about who truly held power in her lifetime.

The tombs also carry a darker chapter. In 1928, during years of civil war, soldiers under the warlord Sun Dianying broke into the tombs of Qianlong and Cixi. They used explosives to reach the burial chambers and carried away jewels, gold and other treasures. The event shocked the last emperor, Puyi, who was living in exile at the time. Much of what was stolen was never recovered.

The Qing did not bury every ruler here. Another group of imperial tombs, the Western Qing Tombs, lies southwest of Beijing. One later emperor chose that site, and after him, emperors were buried in either the eastern or western cemetery. So this valley is only half of the dynasty's story. Even so, it holds the founder in Beijing, the great Kangxi Emperor, Qianlong, and the women who shaped the court's final years.

As you move between the tombs, notice the colour pattern that repeats everywhere. Red walls. Yellow tiles. Yellow was the colour reserved for the emperor, and red stood for good fortune and dignity. The repetition creates a calm rhythm across the valley. Distances between the tombs are real, so many visitors choose a few of the main sites instead of trying to see everything in one day.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and turn back toward the hills. The roofs sit low among the trees, exactly where they were placed to sit, with the mountain rising behind them. The pines still move in the wind. The stone figures still face each other across the road. Whatever you heard today, the simplest thing here is the land itself — chosen, long ago, as a quiet place to rest.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes; the tombs are spread far apart.
Prioritise Yuling and Dingdongling if your time is short.
Bring a light layer for the cool underground palace.
Arrive early to walk the Spirit Way in quiet.
Plan a full day if travelling from Beijing.
