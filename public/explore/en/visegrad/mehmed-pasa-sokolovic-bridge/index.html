The guide

The story

Put your hand on the parapet. The stone is pale and smooth, worn soft by centuries of elbows and palms. Below you, the Drina moves past the piers with a low, steady sound. On a clear day the water is a deep, cold green. It is a colour that seems almost too strong to be real. Close by, the voices of people crossing mix with the call of birds over the river.

You are standing on one of the great works of Ottoman engineering in the Balkans. The bridge was completed in 1577, and it still carries people across the river today. Eleven stone arches run from bank to bank, low near the shores and higher toward the middle. Together they stretch a little under two hundred metres. From the riverbank, the arches look light, almost delicate, for something made of solid rock.

The story of this bridge begins with a boy from the hills nearby. He was born in a village called Sokolovići, not far from here. As a child, he was taken from his family through the devshirme. This was the Ottoman system of collecting Christian boys from the provinces to train them for the army and the government. Many such boys never saw home again. This one rose further than almost anyone.

He became Mehmed Paša Sokolović, Grand Vizier of the Ottoman Empire. The Grand Vizier was the sultan's chief minister, the most powerful official in the state. He served three sultans in turn. Late in life, he paid for a bridge across the river of his childhood. Some historians see it as a gift to his homeland. Others see it as a statement of power. It may well have been both at once.

To design it, he turned to Mimar Sinan. Sinan was the chief architect of the empire, and he built the great mosques of Istanbul. By the time of this project, he was already an old man with a long career behind him. His mark is in the proportions — the way each arch flows into the next, and the way the roadway rises gently over the river. Nothing here feels heavy, even though everything here is heavy.

Now walk toward the centre of the bridge. Here the deck widens into a terrace on one side. Locals call this place the kapija, which simply means the gate. There are stone seats here, and a raised platform facing out over the water. For generations, this was the town's meeting room. People came here to talk, to trade news, to court, to argue, and to watch the river. If you sit down, you join a very old habit.

If the bridge feels familiar, it may be because of a book. The writer Ivo Andrić grew up in Višegrad. In 1945 he published a novel called The Bridge on the Drina. It follows this town across roughly four centuries. Each chapter shows the bridge as empires rise and fall around it. In 1961, Andrić received the Nobel Prize in Literature. Readers around the world came to know this structure before they ever saw it.

Andrić also wrote down some of the local legends. In one tale, a spirit of the river destroyed the building work each night. According to the legend, the builders could only finish after two infant twins were sealed inside the bridge. Locals once told of a white stain on the stone, said to be the mother's milk. This is folklore, not history. But such stories show how people tried to explain a structure that seemed almost impossible.

The bridge has survived a great deal of real violence. During the First World War and again during the Second World War, some of its arches were blown up. Each time, they were rebuilt. Look closely at the stonework, and you may notice small differences in tone and texture between sections. The stone shows centuries of repair. What you see today is the original design, carefully kept alive.

There is also a harder memory here, and it is a recent one. During the war in Bosnia in 1992, the bridge was a place where many civilians from the town were killed. Their bodies were thrown into the river. For many families, this is not a story from a book. Visitors often pause quietly at this point. It is right to remember that this stone carries grief as well as beauty.

The river itself has changed too. A hydroelectric dam was built downstream in the late twentieth century, and it raised the level of the water. Over time, this put strain on the old foundations. In 2007, UNESCO added the bridge to its World Heritage List. A major restoration followed and was completed in the early 2010s. Engineers strengthened the piers and cleaned the stone, so the bridge could face the river for a long time to come.

From the riverbank, you can see the whole design at once. The arches reflect in the water, and in still weather each arch and its reflection form an almost perfect circle. Not far away, the smaller Rzav river joins the Drina. Behind you, the town climbs the hillsides. On the far side, a newer quarter called Andrićgrad was built in the 2010s, named for the writer. Old and new sit very close together here.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the middle of the bridge. Feel the slight rise of the roadway under your feet. Listen to the water against the piers below. People have stood in this same place for more than four hundred years — soldiers, merchants, children, and lovers. Now it is your turn. As you walk back to the shore, look once more over your shoulder. The arches will still be there, low and pale, crossing the green water.
