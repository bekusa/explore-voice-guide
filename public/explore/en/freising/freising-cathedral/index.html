The guide

The story

Climb the last stretch of the cathedral hill and the noise of the town falls away behind you. The cobbles tilt under your feet. Somewhere above, a bell marks the hour, and the sound rolls out across the rooftops toward the Isar valley. In front of you rise two pale towers, plain and solid, the kind of towers that look as if they were built to last. This is Freising Cathedral, dedicated to the Virgin Mary and to Saint Corbinian, and it has crowned this hill for many centuries.

The hill itself is called the Domberg, which simply means cathedral mountain. It is not high, but it is steep enough to matter. In the early Middle Ages, high ground meant safety and authority. A bishop who lived up here could look down over the river, the farmland, and the roads. For more than a thousand years, this small hill was the spiritual centre of a large part of Bavaria. Today it feels quiet, almost like a village of its own above the town.

The story of this place begins with one man. Saint Corbinian was a missionary from the Frankish lands to the west, and he came to Freising in the early eighth century, around the year 724. He founded a religious community here, and from that seed grew the diocese of Freising. Corbinian is still everywhere on this hill. His name is on the cathedral. His relics rest in its crypt. And his most famous companion... was a bear.

Legend has it that Corbinian was travelling over the Alps to Rome when a bear attacked and killed his packhorse. The saint did not run. Instead, according to the tale, he scolded the bear and ordered it to carry the luggage the rest of the way. The bear obeyed, walked all the way to Rome, and was then set free. It is a folk story, not history. But Freising took it to heart — the bear with its pack is on the town's coat of arms to this day.

That bear travelled even further than Rome. A young priest named Joseph Ratzinger was ordained in this cathedral in 1951. He later became Archbishop of Munich and Freising, and in 2005 he was elected Pope Benedict the Sixteenth. When he chose his papal coat of arms, he placed Corbinian's bear on it, loaded with its pack. So a medieval legend from this hill ended up on a pope's shield. Few visitors who come here know that connection.

Now look at the building as a whole. Underneath everything, this is a Romanesque church. Romanesque is the style of Europe around the eleventh and twelfth centuries — thick walls, round arches, heavy columns, a sense of weight and shelter. An earlier cathedral here burned in a great fire in 1159, and the church you see was rebuilt after that disaster. The outline of that medieval basilica, with its long nave and side aisles, is still the frame of the building today.

Step inside, though, and the first impression is very different. The interior is a burst of eighteenth-century Baroque — white stucco, gold, soft pink and pale green, and painted ceilings full of figures and clouds. In the 1720s, Freising prepared to celebrate one thousand years since Corbinian's arrival. For that anniversary, the bishop hired the Asam brothers, Cosmas Damian and Egid Quirin, two of the most gifted Bavarian artists of their age. One was mainly a painter, the other mainly a sculptor and stucco artist.

Take a moment in the nave and let your eyes travel upward. The Asam brothers did not tear down the old church. They dressed it. The Romanesque pillars and arches are still there, but now they carry curling stucco, gilded frames and ceiling paintings that tell the life of Saint Corbinian. It is like a medieval body wearing festive eighteenth-century clothes. Once you know this, you start to see both layers at the same time — the heavy old structure, and the light decoration floating over it.

Now for the part many visitors walk straight past. Go down the steps into the crypt. The air is cooler here, and the light is low. The crypt is a forest of short stone columns under a low vaulted ceiling, and it belongs to the medieval church, not the Baroque one. Look closely at the capitals, the carved tops of the columns. They are not identical. Each one has its own pattern, as if the stonemasons were allowed to play.

Among these columns stands one that people travel specifically to see. It is known as the Beast Column. Its surface is covered with tangled creatures — dragon-like monsters, writhing beasts, and human figures caught among them, including a figure that looks like a knight with a sword. Scholars still argue about what it means. Is it a battle between good and evil? A warning about sin? A story whose key has been lost? Nobody can say for sure, and that mystery is part of its power.

Also in the crypt is the shrine that holds the relics of Saint Corbinian. This is the heart of the whole hill, the reason the cathedral exists at all. Even if you are not religious, it is worth standing here quietly for a moment. Above your head is the bright, celebratory church of the Baroque age. Down here, it is stone, shadow and silence — much closer to how the medieval pilgrims would have felt this place.

Back outside, walk along the edge of the Domberg. Freising was the seat of its own prince-bishops for centuries, and the hill around the cathedral holds buildings connected to that long church history. In 1821, the seat of the archbishop moved to Munich, and the diocese took the name Munich and Freising, which it still carries. Across the valley you can see another hill, Weihenstephan, home to a monastery brewery with a very long history. Freising has always been a town of hills, faith and beer.

Before you leave, stop at a spot where you can see the two towers against the sky. Listen for the bells, the footsteps on the cobbles, the voices drifting up from the town below. Think of the long line of people who have climbed this same slope — missionaries, bishops, stonemasons, painters, a future pope. Each of them left a small mark. As you walk back down the hill, look behind you once more... and imagine a bear, patiently carrying its load.

Before you go

Practical tips

Go down to the crypt; many visitors miss it entirely.
Walk the Domberg's edge for views over the Isar valley.
Keep quiet during services; the cathedral is an active church.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep cobbled climb up.
Combine your visit with nearby Weihenstephan hill across the valley.
