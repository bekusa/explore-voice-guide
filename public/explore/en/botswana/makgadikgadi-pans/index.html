Makgadikgadi Pans

The story

White ground runs flat to the horizon in every direction. No tree, no bird, no shadow. The air above the surface trembles with heat, and the silence presses on your ears. Then your eyes adjust, and the emptiness begins to look like space itself. This is the Makgadikgadi, a place where the earth is made of salt.

The Makgadikgadi Pans form one of the largest salt-pan networks on Earth, covering roughly 12,000 square kilometers of northern Botswana. Two great pans dominate: Sua in the east and Ntwetwe in the west, with dozens of smaller pans scattered among grass plains around them.

All of this was once the floor of a lake. Ancient Lake Makgadikgadi covered a huge part of northern Botswana; at its greatest it was larger than Switzerland. Rivers shifted, the climate dried, and over thousands of years the lake shrank and finally died. Old wave-cut shorelines still mark distant ridges. The salt underfoot is the lake's ghost.

The story connects to the Okavango Delta, a day's drive west. The same restless faults that trap the delta's water once fed this lake. Even now a thin thread survives: in strong flood years, overflow from the delta creeps down the Boteti River toward the pans, a faint memory of the old system.

In the dry season, the pans are another planet. The crust cracks into pale polygons and crunches underfoot like frost. Mirages float silver on the horizon, and distances lose their meaning. Guides drive quad bikes far out onto Ntwetwe Pan, where you can stop, switch off the engine, and hear your own heartbeat.

Then the rains come, usually between November and March, and the transformation is complete. Shallow water spreads across the salt, algae and tiny crustaceans bloom, and the dead floor turns into a living lake a few centimeters deep. The sky doubles itself in the water, and the horizon disappears entirely.

With the water come the flamingos. Greater and lesser flamingos arrive at Sua Pan in enormous numbers, and it ranks among the most important flamingo breeding grounds in southern Africa. In good years the colonies stretch pink into the distance. Bring binoculars; the birds keep far from firm ground. Their arrival is sudden, loud, and never guaranteed; the pans reward luck.

The grass plains around the pans stage another spectacle. After the rains, zebra and wildebeest move from the rivers to the fresh grazing in one of the longest land migrations in Africa. Makgadikgadi Pans National Park protects the western section, where the Boteti River draws the thirsty herds in dry months, along with lions that follow them.

On the southwestern edge of Sua Pan rises Kubu Island, a low granite outcrop crowned with baobab trees. The name is usually translated as hippopotamus, a memory of wetter times. Stone tools and old stone circles show that people used this island long before roads existed. It is a national monument, and camping under its baobabs is an experience people plan whole trips around.

The baobabs of this region were the lighthouses of the nineteenth century. Travelers and traders crossing the thirstland steered by them. Chapman's Baobab, one of the largest trees ever recorded in Africa, served as a landmark, and stories say passing travelers left letters in its trunk. After standing for centuries, it collapsed in 2016. Its fallen trunks still lie where they dropped.

Smaller lives thrive here too. Habituated meerkat colonies live on the grassy pan edges near Gweta; on cool mornings they stand upright to warm their bellies in the sun, and sometimes use a seated visitor as a lookout post. Brown hyenas, aardvarks, and springhares move through the dark hours.

Treat the pans with respect. Never drive on a wet pan surface; the crust hides soft mud that can swallow a vehicle to the axles. Travel with a guide or a second vehicle, carry extra water and fuel, and tell someone your route. From Maun, the national park gate lies about two hours east, but the deep pans ask for local knowledge. Fuel up in Gweta or Nata before you commit to the tracks.

At sunset the salt turns rose, then violet, then grey. Stars come out in numbers that city eyes do not believe. You are standing on the floor of a vanished lake, on salt left by water that no living thing remembers. The silence here is not empty. It is the sound of deep time, resting.

Key facts

Salt pans cover roughly 12,000 square kilometers
Remnant of an ancient lake larger than Switzerland
Chapman's Baobab, a nineteenth-century landmark, collapsed in 2016
Sua Pan hosts major flamingo breeding colonies after rain
Kubu Island is a protected national monument

What to look for

Heat mirages floating above the white salt crust
Ancient baobab trees on Kubu Island's granite slopes
Meerkat colonies standing guard on grassy pan edges
Zebra herds along the Boteti River in dry months

Practical tips

Never drive on wet pan surfaces; vehicles sink fast
Travel with a guide or a second vehicle
Carry extra water, fuel, and a GPS device
Visit December to March to see flamingos and migration
