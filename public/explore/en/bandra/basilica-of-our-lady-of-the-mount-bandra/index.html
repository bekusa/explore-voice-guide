The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the sea wind moves through the palms. Nearer, a vendor calls out over a tray of wax candles. Somewhere a bell rings, and a family climbs the last steps beside you, shoes in hand. The air smells of salt, melting wax, and marigolds. You are standing on a low hill in Bandra, a seaside suburb of Mumbai, at the Basilica of Our Lady of the Mount. Locals simply call it Mount Mary.

Look up at the facade. The church is built in the Gothic Revival style, a nineteenth-century European fashion that copied the pointed arches and tall towers of medieval cathedrals. Here that style meets the light of the Arabian Sea. Twin towers frame the entrance, and the stone looks pale against a monsoon sky or a hard blue afternoon. The building you see today dates from the early twentieth century. The story of this hill is much older.

That story begins with the Portuguese. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, Bandra lay inside Portugal's territory on this coast. Portuguese Jesuits built a chapel on this hilltop in the seventeenth century, and the current basilica replaced it around 1904. In between, the site passed through war, raids, decay, and rebuilding. This hill has seen several churches. The faith of the people who climb it has lasted longer than any one of them.

At the centre of everything is a statue of Mary holding the infant Jesus. Here, history and legend start to mix, so it is worth separating them. Tradition says Portuguese priests brought the statue from Portugal. According to a well-known local tale, raiders attacked the chapel around 1700 and cut off the statue's right hand. The damaged figure was restored, and the hand became part of the story people tell about it.

The second legend is the one Bandra loves most. Locals say a fisherman from the Koli community, the original fishing people of these islands, dreamed that he would find the statue in the sea. Soon after, the story goes, he found it floating in the water. It was carried back up the hill and placed in a chapel again. You do not have to believe every detail. Notice how the tale joins the Portuguese church to the much older fishing life of this coast.

Now step inside, if the doors are open. The air drops a few degrees, and the noise of the street fades. Your eyes need a moment to adjust. Look toward the main altar, where the statue of Mary stands. Then let your gaze move along the walls. The murals and paintings show scenes from the life of Mary. The pointed arches carry your eye upward, which is exactly what their medieval models were designed to do.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. Look at who is praying around you. You may see Catholic families, Hindu women with bangles and vermilion in their hair parting, Muslim visitors, and Parsi grandparents. Mount Mary has long drawn people of many faiths. In a city as mixed as Mumbai, this church is one of the places where those lines soften. People come with a request, and they come back to say thank you.

That brings us to the wax. On the steps and the hill road outside, stalls sell candles shaped like human body parts. You will see wax hands, legs, eyes, hearts, and even small houses, cars, and babies. This is the second surprise. Each shape is a prayer. A person with a bad knee lights a wax leg. A couple hoping for a child lights a wax baby. A family saving for a home lights a tiny wax house.

The church gained a special status in 1954. The Pope declared it a minor basilica, a title the Catholic Church gives to churches of particular importance for pilgrimage and history. The title did not change the stones. It did confirm what the people of Bombay, as the city was then called, already knew. For generations, this hill had been one of the most important places of Marian devotion on India's western coast.

If you visit in September, you will meet a very different Mount Mary. The Bandra Fair, also called the Bandra Feast, begins on the Sunday after the feast of the Nativity of Mary, which falls on the eighth of September. For about a week, huge crowds climb the hill. Stalls fill the lanes with sweets, rosaries, toys, and snacks. Pilgrims walk from across the city, and some come from much farther away.

Outside the fair, the hill feels calmer, and that is when you can notice the setting. Walk to the edge of the grounds and look west. The Arabian Sea spreads out below, often grey-green and hazy with heat. On a clear evening, the sun goes down over the water. Old Bandra lies around you, with village lanes, cottages with tiled roofs and wooden balconies, and newer apartment towers rising behind them.

That contrast tells the story of Bandra itself. This was once a cluster of separate villages, many of them Catholic, linked by fields and fishing shores. The city grew around them and then over them. Yet the old names, the wayside crosses, and the small chapels survive in the lanes below. Mount Mary sits at the top of all this, a landmark that sailors could see and that residents still use to give directions.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the top of the steps. Feel the warm stone under your feet. Hear the candles hiss in the stand, and watch someone choose a small wax figure with great care. Every person on this hill is carrying something private up the slope. As you walk back down toward the sea, notice the drips of old wax on the steps. Each one marks a hope that someone brought here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a weekday morning for a quieter church interior.
Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered inside.
Expect very large crowds during the September Bandra Fair.
Walk the old village lanes below after your visit.
Stay quiet near the altar while people pray.
