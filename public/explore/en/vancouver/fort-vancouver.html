The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere ahead a hammer rings on iron. The tall timber walls of the stockade rise around you, grey and weathered, with sharpened tips against the sky. On a damp morning the air smells of woodsmoke and wet grass. Beyond the walls, the Columbia River moves slowly toward the Pacific. This is Fort Vancouver, in Vancouver, Washington, just across the river from Portland. Once it was the busiest outpost in the entire Pacific Northwest.

The fort began as a business, not a military base. It belonged to the Hudson's Bay Company, a British fur-trading enterprise that operated across much of North America. In the 1820s the company moved its regional headquarters here, to the north bank of the Columbia. From this spot, managers ran a vast network of trading posts. Furs, mostly beaver pelts, came in from across the region. Ships carried them out to markets in London and beyond.

The man most closely linked to this place is Doctor John McLoughlin. He was the Chief Factor, which means the senior company officer in charge of the whole district. McLoughlin was tall, with a mane of white hair, and Indigenous people in the area knew him by a name meaning the White-Headed Eagle. He ran the fort for about two decades. Later, he became known for helping American settlers who arrived exhausted at the end of the Oregon Trail.

Look for the large white house with a wide front porch. This is the Chief Factor's House, the home of McLoughlin and his family. Notice the two curving staircases at the front. Inside, officers and important guests ate together at a long dining table, and strict rules of rank decided who sat where. Here is one detail many visitors miss. The building in front of you is not original. It is a careful reconstruction.

In fact, almost everything inside these walls is a rebuild. After the Hudson's Bay Company left in 1860, the old fort fell into ruin. A fire destroyed what remained a few years later. For nearly a century the site was mostly open ground. Then, in the mid-twentieth century, archaeologists began to dig. They found foundations, post holes, and millions of artefacts. Those discoveries guided the National Park Service as it rebuilt the stockade and its buildings in their original positions.

That archaeology produced some small surprises. Researchers recovered broken blue-and-white dishes from English potteries, glass trade beads, clay smoking pipes, buttons, and tools. Each object helps tell who lived here and how they lived. Some of these finds are kept in a large curated collection on site. When you walk on the paths, remember that the ground beneath you held this record for more than a hundred years before anyone dug it up.

Now follow the sound of the hammer. The blacksmith shop was one of the most important workshops in the fort. Smiths made axes, traps, hinges, nails, and repairs for almost everything. On many days, costumed volunteers or staff work at the forge and explain the craft. Watch the colour of the metal as it heats. It goes from dull red to bright orange. The smith reads that colour to know when to strike.

Nearby are the fur store, the kitchen, and a reconstructed trade shop. The trade shop is where Indigenous people and company workers exchanged goods. Blankets, kettles, beads, and cloth were traded for furs and food. This exchange was the economic heart of the fort. Inside, shelves are stocked with reproductions of the kinds of goods that once passed across the counter. Take a moment to look at the woollen blankets and their coloured stripes.

The people who lived around this fort came from remarkably far away. Outside the stockade walls stood a village of workers and their families. They included French-Canadians, Scots, English, Indigenous people from many nations, people of mixed heritage, and a large number of Hawaiians, who sailed here on company ships. Many languages were spoken in the village. Today the village area is mostly open land, with a few marked sites, so you have to imagine the houses and gardens.

The fort also fed itself. The company planted orchards and large farm fields, and kept cattle and other livestock. Just outside the walls, a reconstructed garden grows vegetables, herbs, and flowers of the kind grown in the nineteenth century. In summer it is full of colour and insects. Some people say one of the oldest apple trees in the region is connected to this early orchard, and a historic tree is still cared for near the river.

In 1846, a treaty between Britain and the United States set the border further north. Fort Vancouver was now on American soil. The United States Army soon built a base on the hill above the fort, called Vancouver Barracks. A young officer named Ulysses S. Grant served here in the early 1850s, long before he led Union armies in the Civil War and became president. The company stayed for some years, but its time here was ending.

Walk up the slope and you reach Officers' Row, a line of large Victorian houses built for army officers. Their porches face a long green lawn. In the other direction, a curving pedestrian land bridge, designed by the artist Maya Lin, carries you over the highway toward the river. It is planted with native grasses. Nearby is Pearson Field, one of the oldest operating airfields in the United States. In 1937, a Soviet crew landed there after flying nonstop over the North Pole.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the stockade gate and look toward the river. Fur traders, Hawaiian sailors, Indigenous families, soldiers, and weary settlers all passed through this ground. The walls are new timber, but the land is the same, and so is the light on the water. Listen again for the gravel under your feet and the distant hammer. Then, as you walk away, notice how quietly the city now surrounds this place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the blacksmith shop to catch a live forge demonstration.
Walk up the slope afterward to explore Officers' Row.
Cross the Maya Lin land bridge toward the Columbia River.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and open grassy ground.
Check ranger program schedules at the visitor center on arrival.
