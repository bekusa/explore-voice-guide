The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the foot of the stone steps. The granite is cool and worn smooth by a century of shoes. Behind you, traffic hums along the street. Ahead of you, tall classical columns hold up a pale facade, and above them a dome rises against the sky. It looks like a building made for judges and lawyers. That is because, for most of its life, it was.

This is the Vancouver Art Gallery. The building began as the provincial courthouse for British Columbia, completed in 1911. Its architect was Francis Rattenbury, a confident designer who shaped much of the province's public face. He also designed the Parliament Buildings in Victoria. Here he chose a neoclassical style, meaning a style borrowed from ancient Greek and Roman temples. Columns, symmetry, a central dome. The message was simple: law and order had arrived on the edge of the Pacific.

Think about what Vancouver was at that time. The city was barely a generation old. Much of the land around you had been dense coastal forest only decades earlier. This land is also the traditional territory of the Musqueam, Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh peoples, who lived here long before any courthouse. A stone temple of justice in the middle of a young timber town was a statement. It said that this place intended to last.

Now for the first surprise. The art gallery itself is older than its life in this building. It was founded in 1931, during the hard years of the Great Depression. A group of citizens raised money to start a public collection. For decades the gallery lived in a much more modest home a few streets away. Only in 1983 did it move into the old courthouse, after the courts relocated to a new complex just to the south.

The man who made that move possible was Arthur Erickson, one of Canada's best-known modern architects. Erickson designed the new law courts and the public square that connects them to this building. He also led the conversion of the old courthouse into galleries. He kept the historic shell and reworked the interior for art. So when you walk inside, you are moving between two eras. Edwardian stone outside, late twentieth-century gallery spaces within.

If you step inside and look up, you will find the rotunda beneath the dome. It is a round, open space at the heart of the building. Light falls from above. Sounds carry in an unusual way here — footsteps and voices soften and drift upward. Pause here for a minute before you head into the exhibition rooms. It is one of the few places where the courthouse character still feels fully intact.

The second surprise is about one artist in particular. The gallery holds the largest collection of work by Emily Carr anywhere. Carr was born in Victoria in the nineteenth century and became one of the most important painters in Canadian history. She painted the deep green forests of the Pacific coast. She also painted the villages and carved poles of Indigenous communities along the coast. For much of her life, she worked with very little recognition.

When you stand in front of a Carr canvas, watch how the brushwork moves. Her later forests are not still. The trees swirl upward in rhythmic strokes of green, grey and deep blue. The sky often seems to bend around them. Many visitors expect quiet landscapes and find something closer to motion. If you have walked in the rainforest outside the city, you may recognise the feeling — the scale, the damp, the sense of being small.

The gallery is also linked to a later chapter in art history. From the 1970s onward, a group of Vancouver artists became known internationally for large photographic works. Critics sometimes call them the Vancouver School. Names such as Jeff Wall, Ian Wallace and Rodney Graham are often connected to this movement. Their pictures look closely at ordinary city life, staged with great care. Exhibitions change often, so what you see on the walls depends on the season.

That changing programme is part of the experience here. The gallery does not only show its own collection. It brings in touring exhibitions, contemporary installations, and work by Indigenous artists from the coast and beyond. One visit might pair a quiet room of historic paintings with a loud, bright, contemporary space next door. Give yourself time to move between them slowly. The contrast is often the most interesting part.

When you come back outside, walk around to the Georgia Street side. This side faces a broad plaza with a fountain at its centre. Two stone lions sit beside the steps, keeping watch. For decades, this plaza has worked as the city's public front porch. People gather here for rallies, protests, celebrations and street performances. On a dry afternoon, the steps fill with office workers eating lunch in the sun.

The Robson Street side feels different. Erickson's square spreads out in terraces, low planters and wide stairs. Water and greenery soften the concrete. In winter, part of the square below street level has been used as a public skating rink. So this single building faces two very different public spaces: a classical plaza on one side and a modern civic garden on the other. Walk between them and notice how the mood changes.

For years, the city has also discussed plans for a new purpose-built home for the gallery a short distance away. Those plans have moved slowly, and the future of this building as a gallery is part of a long civic conversation. For now, art still hangs inside the old courthouse. That is worth remembering as you stand here. You are seeing the gallery in a home it was never designed for, and that it has made its own.

Before you walk on, look once more at the columns and the dome. Listen to the fountain, the buses, the voices on the steps. A court once decided the fates of people inside these walls. Now people come here to look, to sit, and to argue about paintings instead. As you head off into the city, notice the trees on the mountains to the north. Emily Carr painted forests very much like them.
