The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under your feet, gravel crunches. Around you, high walls of pale limestone hold back the noise of the city. Somewhere a gull calls, and somewhere else a bell from the cathedral drifts across the square. You are standing inside a fortress that has watched over this hilltop for more than nine centuries. Its stones are older than almost every building you can see from here.

The story begins a little before the castle. Long before the Normans, the Romans chose this same ridge for a legionary fortress. Later it grew into a walled town called Lindum Colonia. The ridge rises steeply above the River Witham, and from the top you can see for miles across flat Lincolnshire country. Any army that held this hill could watch the roads, the river and the land below. That simple fact shaped everything that came after.

In 1068, just two years after his victory at Hastings, William the Conqueror ordered a castle here. It was part of his plan to control a newly conquered kingdom. The Normans built it inside the old Roman defences, and they did not build gently. Records from the period describe many houses being cleared to make room. For the people of Lincoln, the castle was not a symbol of romance. It was a sign that a foreign king now ruled them.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. Look around the enclosure and count the mounds. There are two. Most Norman castles have a single earth mound, called a motte, with a tower on top. Lincoln Castle has two, which is very rare in England. On one stands the Lucy Tower, a many-sided stone keep. On the other stands the Observatory Tower, with a later turret added in the nineteenth century so that a resident could watch the stars.

The castle did not stay quiet for long. In 1141, during a bitter civil war between King Stephen and his cousin, the Empress Matilda, a great battle took place right here. Stephen fought on foot outside the walls. He was captured and taken away as a prisoner. A king of England, defeated in the shadow of these walls. It is one of the few times in English history that a reigning monarch was taken in battle.

The second battle came in 1217, and it has an unusual hero. During a war between the English crown and rebel barons supported by a French prince, the town fell to the rebels. The castle did not. It was held by Nicola de la Haye, a noblewoman who served as its castellan, meaning its keeper. She was already an older woman, and she held out under siege until a royal army arrived to break it. Her defence helped secure the throne for the young King Henry the Third.

That war connects to the treasure kept inside these walls. In a purpose-built underground vault, the castle displays one of only four surviving original copies of Magna Carta from 1215. Magna Carta, Latin for Great Charter, was an agreement forced on King John by his barons. It set out the idea that even a king is bound by law. Beside it lies a copy of the Charter of the Forest from 1217, a less famous document that protected the rights of ordinary people to use common land.

It is worth pausing over that small sheet of parchment. The ink is brown now and the Latin is written in a tight, careful hand. It does not look powerful. Yet ideas from it travelled far beyond England. They influenced later documents in many countries, including ideas about fair trial and the limits of power. Lincoln's copy has stayed in the care of the city for most of its history, and it has rarely left.

Now turn from the charter to something much harsher. For centuries, Lincoln Castle was a place of law and punishment. Courts sat here, and prisoners were held here. A Georgian prison was built inside the walls in the late eighteenth century. Then, in the Victorian era, a larger prison was added, designed around a strict idea called the separate system. Prisoners were kept apart almost all the time. They were not supposed to speak to one another, or even see each other's faces.

The prison chapel shows this idea more clearly than anything else. Instead of open benches, it is filled with tall wooden boxes. Each prisoner sat in his own narrow stall, able to see the preacher but nobody else. Climb in and the walls close around your shoulders. It is a quiet, uncomfortable room. Many visitors say it stays with them longer than any tower or battle story.

Here is the second surprise. Law is still practised here today. The building near the castle walls is a working Crown Court, where serious criminal cases are heard. So this site has served justice, in one form or another, for close to a thousand years. Elsewhere, the walls carry darker memories too. Public executions once took place on the roof of Cobb Hall, a tower at one corner, in full view of watching crowds below.

For the best view of all, take the medieval wall walk. A path runs right around the top of the curtain wall, so you can circle the whole castle at height. From one side you look down on the rooftops of the old town. From another, the cathedral fills the sky, its three towers rising just across the square. Walking the full loop takes time, and some sections involve steps, but every corner gives you a different view of the city.

When you leave by the gate, stop in the open square outside. Behind you are the walls of the king's castle. In front of you, through the old Exchequer Gate, stands the cathedral of the church. For centuries, these two powers faced each other across this small space. Feel the worn stones under your feet, listen to the voices of people passing, and take one last look back at the walls before you walk on.
