The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the mountain and tilt your head back. The limestone rises almost straight up in pale grey folds, warm where the sun reaches it and cool and blue in the shadowed cracks. Somewhere near the base, water runs from a spring into a still pool. Then your eye catches it. High on the cliff there is a smooth, square panel, and inside it small human figures stand in a line. It is easy to miss at first. That was partly the point.

This is the Behistun inscription, also written Bisotun, carved on Mount Behistun in Iran's Kermanshah Province. It is one of the most important texts of the ancient world. Not because of its beauty alone, but because of what it unlocked. For a long time, scholars could look at wedge-shaped cuneiform writing and understand nothing. This cliff gave them a key. In that sense, it played a role for ancient Mesopotamia like the Rosetta Stone played for Egypt.

The man behind it was Darius the First, often called Darius the Great, king of the Persian Achaemenid Empire. The Achaemenids were the dynasty that built the first great Persian empire, stretching from Egypt to the edges of India. Darius came to power in 522 BC, in a moment of chaos and disputed succession. Revolts broke out across his lands. The inscription, carved in the years that followed, is his version of how he won.

Look at the relief above the text. The tallest figure is Darius himself. Under his foot lies a man on his back, arms raised. According to Darius, this was Gaumata, a priest who had seized the throne by pretending to be the dead brother of the previous king. Before Darius stand nine men in a row. Their necks are tied together with a single rope, and their hands are bound behind them. Each one represents a rebel leader Darius says he defeated.

Above them all floats a winged symbol, a figure inside a ring with outstretched wings. It is usually understood as Ahura Mazda, the supreme god in Darius's faith, or as a sign of divine favour. The message is simple and direct. The god stands with the king, and the king stands on his enemies. Even from this distance, without reading a single word, you can understand the story. That was a clever choice in a world where most people could not read.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look at the end of the line of captives. The last figure wears a tall, pointed hat. He is a Scythian chief, from the horse-riding peoples of the northern steppe. He was not part of the original plan. Scholars believe he was added later, after another campaign, and carving him in meant cutting into part of the existing text. Even a king's official history, it seems, needed an update.

Now to the writing itself. The same account appears in three languages. One is Old Persian, the language of Darius's own people. One is Elamite, an older language of southwestern Iran used in royal administration. The third is Babylonian, a form of Akkadian, the old prestige language of Mesopotamia. All three use cuneiform script, but each works differently. Some scholars think the Old Persian script may have been developed around this time, possibly for monuments like this one.

Why carve it here, of all places? The mountain stands beside one of the great roads of the ancient world. This route linked the plains of Mesopotamia with the Iranian plateau, and later it became part of the long trade network we now call the Silk Road. Armies, merchants, and pilgrims passed this cliff for thousands of years. The spring at its foot made it a natural stopping point. Travellers would rest, drink, and look up.

But they could not read it. The panel sits high above the ground, far too high for any passer-by to make out the words. Darius seems to have wanted it untouchable. A popular account says the workers removed the path below once the carving was finished, so no one could climb up and deface it. Whether or not that is exactly true, the height protected it for many centuries. To spread his message, Darius also sent copies across the empire. A version in Aramaic was later found on papyrus in Egypt.

Over time, people forgot who made it. Some ancient Greek writers linked the cliff to Semiramis, a legendary queen of Assyria. Local stories gave it other meanings. Then, in the nineteenth century, a British officer named Henry Rawlinson took on the job of copying it. He climbed the cliff on ladders and ropes, often in dangerous positions. For the hardest section, the Babylonian text, he had help from a local Kurdish boy who swung across the rock face to make paper impressions.

That work changed everything. With the Old Persian text deciphered, scholars could compare it line by line with the other two versions. Slowly, Babylonian and Elamite opened up. And once Babylonian could be read, a huge library of clay tablets from Mesopotamia began to speak. Laws, letters, myths, astronomy, and accounts of daily trade all became readable. Much of what we know about the ancient Near East today traces back, in part, to this cliff.

The inscription is not alone here. As you walk the site, you will find carvings from many later periods. There is a reclining figure of Heracles, the Greek hero, from the Seleucid era, when Greek-speaking kings ruled this region after Alexander the Great. There are Parthian reliefs, and a huge, smoothed section of rock linked in local legend to Farhad, the lovesick sculptor of the Persian tale of Farhad and Shirin. The whole mountain is a record of who passed by. In 2006, the site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Before you leave, look up one more time. Notice how small the figures are against the mountain, and how much of the cliff stays bare and wild. Listen for the water from the spring and the wind on the rock. Darius wanted his words to last forever, and in a way they did... though not in the way he imagined. As you walk away, the panel will shrink back into the stone, and the mountain will look like a mountain again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring binoculars to see the relief figures clearly.
Visit in the morning when light falls on the cliff.
Walk the full site to see the later carvings.
Wear sturdy shoes for the rocky, uneven paths.
Rest by the spring pool at the mountain's base.
