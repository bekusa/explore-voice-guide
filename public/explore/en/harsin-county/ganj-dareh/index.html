The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your shoes. It is soft, a little uneven, made of pale earth that crumbles easily. Around you, the Zagros Mountains rise in long folds of rock and grass. The wind moves through the valley without much to stop it. This low mound does not look like much at first. But you are standing on one of the most important places in the story of how people first learned to live with animals.

The name Ganj Dareh comes from Persian. Ganj means treasure, and dareh means valley. So this is the Treasure Valley. There is no gold here, and there never was. The treasure is something much quieter. It is a layered record of a village from about ten thousand years ago, buried in the mud of its own houses. For archaeologists, that kind of record is worth more than any coin.

The site lies in Harsin County, in Kermanshah Province, in western Iran. This is high country, with cold winters and dry, warm summers. Springs and streams cut through the valleys. Wild goats and sheep once grazed these slopes in large numbers. Oak woodland covered parts of the hills. For people who lived by hunting and gathering, this was a generous landscape. It was also a landscape where something new was about to begin.

The mound was excavated in the 1960s and 1970s by a team led by the Canadian archaeologist Philip Smith. His team dug down through several layers of occupation. At the bottom, they found traces of a simple early camp. Above it, they found something more permanent — small rooms built of mud brick, packed close together. These were some of the earliest mud-brick buildings known in this part of the world.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. At least one phase of the village appears to have ended in fire. That sounds like a disaster, and for the people living here, it probably was. But for archaeologists, the fire was a gift. Heat baked the mud walls hard, a little like pottery. Structures that would normally melt back into the earth survived. Some of the small rooms may have been used for storage, holding food through the long mountain winters.

The people of the village also worked with clay in other ways. Excavators found small clay objects, including figurines and simple shaped tokens. Their exact meaning is still debated. Some may have been toys, some may have had a ritual role, and some may have helped people keep track of goods. We do not know for certain. What is clear is that clay was part of daily life here, long before pottery became common.

Now for the discovery that made this mound famous. Thousands of animal bones came out of the excavation, most of them from goats. Decades later, the zooarchaeologists Melinda Zeder and Brian Hesse studied these bones closely. Zooarchaeologists are scientists who study animal remains from ancient sites. They looked carefully at the age and sex of the animals when they died. The pattern they found was not what you would expect from hunting.

Hunters usually take the biggest, strongest animals they can find. But at this village, many young males were killed, while females were kept alive to older ages. That is exactly what a herder does. You keep the females, because they give birth and produce milk. You eat most of the young males. The bones showed people managing goats, not simply chasing them. It is some of the earliest clear evidence of goat herding anywhere.

And here is the second surprise. The goats still looked wild. Their bones had not yet changed size or shape the way domestic animals later did. So the human behaviour changed first, and the animal bodies changed later. Without the careful study of age and sex, this early stage of domestication would have stayed invisible. The goats that now live in farms across the world have a part of their story right here.

The people themselves have told a story too. In the 2010s, scientists recovered ancient DNA from human remains found at the site. The results showed that these early Zagros villagers were genetically quite distinct from the early farmers of Anatolia, in what is now Turkey. That suggests farming and herding did not spread from one single group. Different communities, in different regions, took part in this great change in their own ways.

It is worth thinking about what daily life might have looked like here. Smoke from cooking fires. The sound of goats on the slopes. People shaping clay, storing food, burying their dead close to home. They did not know they were at the start of anything. They were solving practical problems — how to have meat and milk through a hard winter, how to stay in one place. Those small choices added up to a new way of living.

Today the site is quiet and rural. Do not expect a museum building or a gift shop on the mound. Much of what was excavated is now held in collections and described in scientific reports, not displayed in the field. What you can see is the shape of the land itself. Look at how the mound sits in the valley, close to water and grazing. That position tells you why people chose to stay.

If you have time, the city of Kermanshah is within reach, and its museums and other ancient sites help place this village in a longer timeline. This region holds many layers of the human past, from early villages like this one to the empires that came much later. Ganj Dareh sits near the very beginning of that long sequence. It is a good place to remember how far back the story goes.

Before you leave, look once more at the hills around you. Imagine them with oak trees, and with goats moving in small, watched groups. Then look down at the pale earth under your feet. Somewhere in that soil, people once decided to keep an animal instead of hunting it. As you walk back toward the road, listen to the wind in the valley. It is the same wind they heard.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for loose, uneven earth on the mound.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is scarce.
Visit Kermanshah's museums to see the wider regional context.
Go with a local driver who knows the rural roads.
Treat the mound gently; avoid digging or removing anything.
