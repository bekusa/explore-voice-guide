The guide

The story

Breathe in first. The air here smells of pine resin and damp sandstone. Under your shoes, the ground is soft with needles and pale grit. Somewhere in the trees a woodpecker knocks, then stops. The forest is very quiet, and that quiet sets the mood for one of the strangest castles in Central Europe. Houska rises from a rocky ridge above the woods, with pale walls and steep roofs. From a distance it looks calm. Its reputation is anything but calm.

You are standing in the Czech countryside, north of Prague, in a landscape called Kokořínsko. It is a protected area of sandstone rocks, deep ravines and pine forest. Over thousands of years, wind and water cut the soft stone into towers, ledges and narrow valleys. Small villages sit in the clearings. Farm tracks wind between the trees. It is a gentle, rural place. That makes the castle's dark legends feel even more surprising when you first hear them.

The castle dates to the second half of the thirteenth century. It is usually linked to the reign of King Ottokar the Second of Bohemia. He belonged to the Přemyslid dynasty, the family that ruled the Czech lands for centuries. His era was a time of ambitious building, new towns and royal power. Houska belongs to the early Gothic period. Gothic is the medieval style known for pointed arches and tall, vertical forms. Here that style appears in a compact, solid form, set firmly on the rock.

Now comes the first puzzle — and many visitors miss it. Castles usually exist for a clear reason. They guard a river crossing, a trade road, a mine or a town. Houska seems to guard very little. It stands deep in the forest, away from major routes. People who study castles often comment on this. Why build a strong stone fortress in such a quiet spot? There are practical answers, such as royal administration of the surrounding land. But the question has also fed a very different kind of story.

That story is the legend of the pit. Locals say that before the castle existed, there was a deep crack in the rock here. According to the tale, it had no visible bottom. Strange creatures were said to climb out of it at night. People called it a gateway to hell. In this telling, the castle was not built to keep enemies out. It was built to keep something in. This is folklore, not documented history. Still, it is the reason many visitors drive down these forest roads.

The legend has a famous sequel. According to one popular tale, condemned prisoners were offered a pardon. The condition was simple — they must agree to be lowered into the pit on a rope and report what they saw. The first man went down. Moments later, he began to scream. When the others pulled him up, the story says, his hair had turned white. He looked decades older. He never explained what he had seen. No records confirm any of this. It survives as a story told by firelight.

The legend gives special meaning to the castle chapel. Tradition holds that the chapel stands directly above the pit, sealing it under stone and prayer. Whatever you believe, the chapel is the oldest and most atmospheric part of the castle. It is an early Gothic space with thick walls and a quiet, enclosed feeling. Light enters slowly through the windows. Sounds change when you step inside. Footsteps become softer, and voices drop on their own. It is a room that makes people speak quietly.

Look closely at the walls, because this is the second surprise. The chapel holds medieval wall paintings, painted in a time when few people could read. Images taught the stories of faith. Among the expected religious scenes, there are some unusual figures. One is often described as a strange half-human creature drawing a bow. Visitors like to point out that the archer seems to shoot with the left hand. Scholars debate the meaning of such figures. For legend lovers, they simply add to the mystery.

The castle did not stay frozen in the Middle Ages. In the sixteenth century it was rebuilt in the Renaissance style. The Renaissance was a period that brought calmer, more balanced architecture to Europe, inspired by classical forms. You can still see the two eras side by side. Solid Gothic walls carry later gables, windows and residential rooms. The building slowly changed from a fortress into a comfortable noble home. Over the centuries, many different families owned it, and each one left small changes behind.

The twentieth century added a new layer of rumour. During the Second World War, the Czech lands were under German occupation. Locals say that German forces used the castle and took an interest in its legends. Some stories claim secret occult experiments took place here. There is no solid evidence for these claims. They are best treated as modern folklore, built on top of older folklore. Even so, they show how a quiet building can collect stories, one generation after another.

Today the interiors are open to visitors, usually as part of a guided tour. The rooms are furnished, so you get a sense of how people lived here, not only how they fought or prayed. Floors creak. Stone stairs are worn smooth in the middle. Windows frame the forest in small, bright rectangles. Guides often mix the history with the legends, and they are usually clear about which is which. Ask questions. The best stories often come out in conversation, not from the script.

Before you leave, give the landscape some time. Walk a little way into the woods around the castle. The sandstone here has many shapes — smooth walls, overhangs and small caves. In damp weather the rock turns darker and the moss looks almost green-gold. On clear days, sunlight falls through the pines in long stripes. It is easy to imagine how medieval villagers, walking home at dusk, might see shadows move between these rocks and begin to tell stories.

So stand still for a moment, here, in front of the walls. Notice the pale stone and the steep roofs. Notice the silence of the forest around you. Whether there is a pit under the chapel or simply solid rock, this place has held people's imagination for centuries. As you walk away, listen to the trees and the crunch of needles under your feet. And perhaps, just once, look back over your shoulder at Houska.
