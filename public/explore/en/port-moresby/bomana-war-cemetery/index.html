Bomana War Cemetery

The first thing you notice is the quiet.

⏱ 6 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

The first thing you notice is the quiet. Rows of white headstones rest on a wide green lawn. The grass is cut low and even, and it is watered until it stays bright in the heat. Birdsong carries across the open ground. Somewhere a groundskeeper moves slowly between the rows. You slow your own steps without deciding to. This is the Port Moresby war cemetery at Bomana, the largest of its kind in Papua New Guinea.

The cemetery sits about nineteen kilometers northeast of central Port Moresby. The drive takes you out of the busy city and toward low, dry hills. When you arrive, the land opens into wide, level terraces. The headstones stand in long, straight lines that follow the gentle slope. From the gate you cannot quite see the end of them. Each single stone marks a person who did not come home.

Bomana holds the graves of 3,826 Commonwealth servicemen. Of these, 699 could not be identified. Their headstones carry no name, only a few quiet words and a date. More than six hundred Indian soldiers are also buried here, far from the land of their birth. They came from across the world to fight in a war that had reached these distant shores.

Most of the men buried here died during the Second World War. Many of them fell in the Kokoda campaign. That campaign was fought along a narrow mountain track that crosses the rugged interior to the north. Australian and Japanese forces struggled through mud, heavy rain, and steep, root-tangled ground. The fighting was close and hard. The harsh conditions claimed many lives on their own, apart from the battles.

The Commonwealth War Graves Commission built this cemetery, and it still cares for it today. The Commission tends the graves of service members in many countries around the world. Its work follows one simple and steady idea. Each person is to be remembered by name. Where the name has been lost, the person is still given a marked and tended place of rest, equal to all the others around it.

The cemetery was designed by an architect named A.G. Robertson. He shaped the land into level terraces that climb gently upward toward the memorial. The headstones here are cut from white marble. Bomana is the only war cemetery in Papua New Guinea to use marble of this kind. The pale stone holds the light through the day, and it stays cool to the touch even when the sun is high overhead.

At the heart of the grounds stands the Stone of Remembrance. It is a large, plain block of pale stone, simple in shape and free of ornament. You will find one like it in many Commonwealth cemeteries across the world. It carries a single short line of carved text. The words ask that these men be remembered for all time, and the plainness of the stone makes the request feel calm and sure.

The cemetery was formally unveiled on 19 October 1953. The ceremony was led by Field Marshal Slim, who was then the Governor-General of Australia. Families, officials, and former soldiers gathered together on the lawn that day. Years had already passed since the fighting ended. Yet for the parents, widows, and old comrades who stood among the stones, the grief was still very close to the surface.

Behind the graves stands the Port Moresby Memorial. It honors almost 750 men who died in this region and have no known grave at all. Their bodies were never found, or never named. The memorial gives them a place to be mourned. Visitors can walk to the panels and read the long lists of names carved into the stone, each name a person held within this single act of memory.

Walk slowly along the rows and read a few of the headstones as you pass. Some give an age beneath the name. Many of the men were very young, still in their late teens or early twenties. Some stones carry a short message chosen by a family far away, a line from a hymn or a few plain words of love. These small inscriptions turn numbers back into people.

The grounds are kept with great care all through the year. Gardeners trim the grass and tend the low plantings set between the stones. Nothing is left ragged or forgotten. The order is deliberate, and it speaks of a respect that has not faded with the passing decades. Even the deep silence of the place feels looked after, held gently in the open air.

It is a long way from the towns of Australia, India, and Britain to this green slope above Port Moresby. The men who rest here never made the journey home again. Instead, the land they fought over has kept them, and it has been shaped into a place of peace around them. The cemetery turns a hard and painful history into something a visitor can stand within and hold quietly.

Before you leave, stop for a moment near the entrance. Look back across the white rows on the green slope, all of them facing the same way. Each stone is one life, one story cut short. Together they hold a piece of history that asks only to be remembered. Take that quiet with you as you go, and carry it gently.

Key facts

Largest war cemetery in Papua New Guinea.
Contains 3,826 Commonwealth burials, 699 of them unidentified.
More than 600 Indian soldiers are buried here.
Designed by architect A.G. Robertson after the war.
Unveiled by Field Marshal Slim on 19 October 1953.

What to look for

White marble headstones in long, even rows.
The large Stone of Remembrance among the graves.
Names carved on the Port Moresby Memorial panels.
Personal messages chosen by families on some stones.

Practical tips

Drive about nineteen kilometers northeast from central Port Moresby.
Walk the rows slowly and read a few inscriptions.
Bring water and sun protection for the open ground.
Keep your voice low out of respect.
