The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the great square and feel the cobbles under your feet. They are wide, uneven, worn smooth by centuries of carriage wheels and footsteps. Ahead of you, a long sandstone front stretches out in pale gold and cream. On a bright morning the stone looks warm, almost like bread crust. On a grey day it turns soft and silvery. Either way, the building fills your whole field of view. That is exactly what its builders intended.

This is the Würzburg Residence, the palace of the prince-bishops. For centuries, Würzburg was ruled by men who held two kinds of power at once. They were bishops of the Church, and they were also secular princes with land, taxes, and armies. Earlier bishops lived up on the hill across the river, in the fortress of Marienberg. In the early eighteenth century, a new prince-bishop from the powerful Schönborn family decided that a fortress was no longer enough. He wanted a palace in the town, built to match the courts of Europe.

Construction began in 1720. The man who shaped the project was Balthasar Neumann, a young engineer and artillery officer who became one of the great architects of the German Baroque. Baroque, in simple terms, is a style of drama and movement: curving forms, rich decoration, and spaces designed to surprise you as you walk through them. Neumann did not work alone. Leading architects from Paris and Vienna sent plans and opinions. But the finished building carries his name above all others.

The work took decades. The main structure was largely complete by the 1740s, but the decoration of the interiors continued for many years after that. Prince-bishops came and went. Some spent lavishly, some cut back. Painters, stucco workers, cabinet makers, and gilders arrived from across Europe. When you walk through the rooms today, you are seeing the combined effort of a whole generation of craftsmen, not the vision of a single moment.

Now, step inside, and the first thing you meet is the staircase. This is the heart of the building. The steps rise slowly, then split and turn back on themselves, so you climb towards a ceiling you cannot fully see at first. Notice how the light changes as you rise. The space opens above you gradually, almost like a curtain lifting. Neumann designed this climb as a piece of theatre.

Here is the first surprise. Above the staircase there is a single vast vault of stone, with no columns holding it up from below. In the eighteenth century, this was a bold piece of engineering. A popular story says that rival architects warned it would collapse, and that Neumann offered to have cannons fired underneath it to prove its strength. Whether or not that tale is true, the vault has stood for well over two centuries.

And that vault carries a painting. In the early 1750s, the Venetian master Giovanni Battista Tiepolo came to Würzburg with his sons and covered the ceiling with one of the largest frescoes ever painted. A fresco is painted onto fresh, wet plaster, so the artist must work fast, section by section, before it dries. At the centre, the sun god Apollo rises in a bright sky. Around the edges, the four continents known to Europeans at the time appear as crowded, colourful scenes.

Take your time with each side. America is shown as a woman riding a crocodile. Africa sits on a camel, Asia on an elephant. Europe, on the side facing the hall beyond, is full of music, art, and courtly figures. And here is the second surprise. Tiepolo painted real people into the crowd. Look for Balthasar Neumann himself, dressed in uniform near a cannon. Tiepolo is said to have included his own face too, along with his son.

From the staircase, the route leads into the White Hall, where the colour suddenly drains away. The walls are covered in pale stucco, white and soft grey, with almost no painting. After the explosion of colour on the stairs, it feels quiet, like a pause between two loud passages of music. That contrast is deliberate. It prepares your eyes for the next room.

The next room is the Imperial Hall, and the colour returns in full. Marble, gold, mirrors, and more frescoes by Tiepolo fill the space. The paintings here show scenes from medieval history linked to Würzburg, including the wedding of the emperor Frederick Barbarossa, which took place in this city in the twelfth century. Notice how painted figures and real stucco seem to blend. A curtain may be carved, or it may be painted. Sometimes it is hard to tell where one ends and the other begins.

This survival was not guaranteed. In March 1945, near the end of the Second World War, a bombing raid destroyed most of Würzburg. Fire swept through the Residence and many rooms were lost. Yet the great vault over the staircase held, and with it, Tiepolo’s fresco. Afterwards, an American officer working to protect cultural heritage helped arrange a temporary roof, so rain would not ruin what remained. Many of the rooms you see today were carefully rebuilt over the following decades.

In 1981, the palace, together with its gardens and the square in front, was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list. Before you leave, find the Court Church, tucked into one corner of the building, with its twisting columns and gold. Then walk out into the Court Garden behind the palace. The terraces, clipped trees, and stone figures follow the line of the old town fortifications, which gives the garden its unusual, angled shape.

When you step back out onto the square, turn around once more. Look at that long sandstone front again, now that you know what is inside. Somewhere behind those windows, a stone vault is still holding a painted sky above a staircase. Listen to the sound of the cobbles under your shoes, the voices of other visitors, perhaps a bell from the town. The prince-bishops are long gone. But the building they wanted the world to notice is still here, and today you noticed it.
