The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere below, the River Main slides past the old town of Würzburg. The wind up here carries the faint, green smell of vines. Rows of grapes climb the slope beneath the walls, rising almost to the stone itself. You have climbed to a place that watched over this city for centuries. From these walls, the rulers of Würzburg looked down on everyone below.

Welcome to Marienberg Fortress. It sits on a high hill on the western bank of the Main, directly across the river from the old town. People used this hilltop long before any castle stood here. The height gave a clear view of the river valley, and the river was a road for trade. Whoever held this hill could see who was coming. That simple fact explains almost everything that happened here.

Here is the first surprise. Inside these walls stands one of the oldest church buildings in Germany. It is the round Church of Saint Mary, the Marienkirche. Tradition places its origins in the early eighth century, when Christianity was still new in this region. The fortress takes its name from it. Marienberg simply means "Mary's Hill". So before this was a place of power, it was a place of prayer. The soldiers and bishops came later.

For about five hundred years, this hill was the home of the Prince-Bishops of Würzburg. That title needs a short explanation. A prince-bishop was a churchman and a ruler at the same time. He led the faithful, but he also collected taxes, made laws, and commanded troops. The bishops moved up here in the thirteenth century. From this hilltop, they ruled a large territory in the region called Franconia.

Notice how the fortress feels like several buildings fused together. That is exactly what it is. Each generation of bishops added walls, towers, gates and living quarters. Medieval stonework sits beside Renaissance windows. Later military walls wrap around everything like a thick coat. Look at the stone colours as you walk. The pale and reddish sandstone changes from section to section. Those changes show different centuries of building, one layer pressed against the next.

In 1525, the fortress faced its most famous test. This was the time of the German Peasants' War. Across the region, farmers and townspeople rose up against heavy taxes and the power of lords and bishops. A large peasant army marched to Würzburg and laid siege to this hill. They attacked the walls, but the walls held. The rising was crushed, and the punishments that followed were harsh. The fortress remained a symbol of the bishops' authority.

This is the second surprise. One man connected to that uprising is now among Würzburg's most admired figures. Tilman Riemenschneider was a master woodcarver and sculptor. He was also a member of the city council. When the council sided with the rebels, he was arrested. Historians agree he was imprisoned, and he was likely tortured. Locals say his hands were broken so he could never carve again... but that part is legend, not documented fact. His sculptures survive, and many are treasured today.

A century later, the walls failed. During the Thirty Years' War, a long and brutal conflict between Catholic and Protestant powers, Swedish troops arrived. In 1631, the army of the Swedish king Gustavus Adolphus captured the fortress. The bishops lost treasures and books, which were carried away as war booty. After that shock, the defences were rebuilt and greatly expanded. The heavy bastions you see today come largely from this later period.

Walk to the old well house in the inner courtyard. It is a small round building with columns, almost like a little temple. Beneath it, a deep shaft drops through the rock. In a siege, water meant survival. A castle without water could hold out only a few days. This well allowed defenders to stay behind their walls for much longer. Stand close and imagine the effort it took to dig this shaft by hand.

Near the church rises the round keep, the oldest tower of the fortress. A keep was the last place of refuge. If attackers broke through every other wall, the defenders climbed up here. Its thick walls and small openings were built for defence, not for comfort. Compare it with the graceful windows of the later residential wings. You can see the castle slowly shifting from a war machine into a palace.

In the early eighteenth century, the bishops finally left the hill. They moved down into the city, to a grand new palace called the Würzburg Residence. A hilltop fortress was cold, windy and old-fashioned. A modern ruler wanted ballrooms and gardens. The fortress stayed in use as a military site. In March 1945, near the end of the Second World War, bombing destroyed much of Würzburg. The fortress was damaged as well and later carefully restored.

Now find the Princes' Garden, a terraced garden on the edge of the walls. This is where the view opens up. Below you, the Old Main Bridge crosses the river, lined with statues of saints. Behind it, church towers rise from the rooftops of the old town. On a clear day, you can trace the river as it bends through the valley. This was the view of power. It is now the view everyone comes for.

Take a last look across the water before you leave. The city you see was largely rebuilt after the war, stone by stone. The fortress behind you has survived sieges, plunder and fire. As you walk back down through the vineyards, listen again for the gravel under your feet. Notice how the old town slowly rises to meet you. And when you reach the bridge, turn around. The fortress will be there on its hill, watching, as it always has.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep climb through vineyards.
Visit the Princes' Garden for the best city view.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the town.
Take the riverside path back to cross the Old Main Bridge.
Bring water on hot days; the climb is exposed.
