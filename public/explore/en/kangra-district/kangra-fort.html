The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your feet. It is rough, uneven, and worn smooth in places by centuries of sandals and boots. A warm wind moves up from the river valley below. On a clear day, the snow line of the Dhauladhar range sits along the horizon like a pale stripe of chalk. You are standing on a narrow ridge, and the ground falls away steeply on either side.

This is Kangra Fort, in the hills of Himachal Pradesh in northern India. The ridge was not chosen by accident. Two rivers meet below it, the Banganga and the Manjhi, and their gorges form natural moats. An army coming from almost any direction would face cliffs before it ever reached a wall. The builders only needed to close the gaps the land left open.

The fort is closely linked to the Katoch dynasty, one of the oldest royal lines in the Himalayan foothills. The family traces its roots to the ancient kingdom of Trigarta, a name that appears in the Mahabharata, the great Sanskrit epic of India. How far back the stones themselves go is debated. What is clear is that this fortress was already old and already famous by the time written records begin to describe it.

And it was famous for a reason that may surprise you... treasure. Temples inside the walls collected offerings for generations. Gold, silver, and jewels piled up in a place that seemed impossible to attack. That reputation travelled far beyond these hills. In the early eleventh century, the raider Mahmud of Ghazni came from what is now Afghanistan. Chroniclers of the time wrote about the enormous wealth he carried away from Kangra.

The fort changed hands many times after that. Rulers in the plains of India saw it as a key to the hills. Whoever held Kangra held the road into the valleys behind it. Several sultans tried their luck. Some succeeded briefly. The Katoch kings often returned once the outsiders lost interest or strength. It was a long rhythm of loss and recovery.

The most determined attempt came from the Mughal Empire, the dynasty that built the Taj Mahal. In the early seventeenth century, under the emperor Jahangir, Mughal forces laid a long siege here. It lasted many months. The fort finally fell around 1620, and Jahangir himself is said to have visited afterward. A mosque was built inside the walls, and one of the gates still carries his name — the Jahangiri Darwaza.

As you climb, notice how the path works. It does not run straight. It turns, narrows, and passes through a series of gates, one after another. Their names are a timeline in stone: the Ahani Darwaza, the Amiri Darwaza, the Jahangiri Darwaza, and higher up, the Darshani Darwaza. Each turn forced attackers to slow down. Each gate was a place where defenders could rain arrows and stones from above.

The Katoch family did return. In the late eighteenth century, Raja Sansar Chand Katoch took the fort back from the weakening Mughals, around 1789. His court became a centre of art. Under his support, painters developed the Kangra style of miniature painting — small, detailed works with soft colours, flowing lines, and scenes of the god Krishna among green hills. Many of those hills look very much like the view around you.

But Sansar Chand's power did not last. Rival hill states and Gorkha armies from Nepal pressed in. To push back the Gorkhas, he turned to Maharaja Ranjit Singh, ruler of the Sikh Empire in Punjab. The price of that help was the fort itself. Kangra passed to the Sikhs in the early nineteenth century. After the Anglo-Sikh wars of the 1840s, it passed again — this time to the British, who kept a garrison here.

Then came the event that shaped what you see today. In 1905, a powerful earthquake struck the Kangra valley. Thousands of people died across the region. The fort's palaces, halls, and upper buildings cracked and collapsed. The British garrison left soon afterward. Much of the broken masonry around you dates from that morning. The ruin is not only the work of centuries. It is also the work of a few terrible minutes.

Near the top, look for the temples that survived inside the walls. There are shrines to Lakshmi Narayan and to the goddess Ambika Devi. There is also a Jain temple with an image of Adinath, the first of the Jain spiritual teachers. Hindu, Jain, and Muslim worship all left their mark on this one ridge. Some visitors still come here to pray, not only to look.

Before you leave, consider a stop at the Maharaja Sansar Chand Museum near the entrance. It is run by the Katoch royal family, who still have close ties to this place. Inside, you can see objects connected to their history, from weapons to costumes to paintings. It is unusual to visit a fortress and find the descendants of its builders still telling its story.

Now take a moment at the highest point you can reach. Listen. You may hear the rivers far below, birds in the trees on the slopes, and the low voices of other visitors. Look out at the valley, the town of Kangra, and the long line of mountains. The walls under your hand have seen raiders, emperors, painters, soldiers, and an earthquake. Today they hold something quieter — the view. As you walk back down through the gates, count them again, and imagine climbing them under fire.

What to look for

Cracked masonry and collapsed walls from the 1905 earthquake.
The Jain temple's image of Adinath near the summit.
Snow-capped Dhauladhar peaks along the northern horizon.
Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, uneven stone paths.
Carry water, since the climb is exposed and tiring.
Come early morning for clearer views of the Dhauladhar.
Visit the Sansar Chand Museum before or after the climb.
Dress modestly if you plan to enter the temples.
