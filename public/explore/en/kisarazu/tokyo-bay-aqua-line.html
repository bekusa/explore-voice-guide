The guide

The story

Listen first. The tyres hum on smooth asphalt, the wind pushes against the side of the car, and on both sides there is nothing but water. Grey-green water, wrinkled by the breeze, stretching away to a hazy line of cranes and towers. You are crossing the mouth of Tokyo Bay on a road that sits only a short distance above the waves. Few roads in the world give this feeling. It is part bridge, part tunnel, and part island.

This is the Tokyo Bay Aqua-Line. It links the city of Kawasaki, on the western shore of the bay, with Kisarazu, here on the eastern shore in Chiba Prefecture. The whole crossing runs about 15 kilometres. It opened in December 1997, after many years of planning and construction. Before it existed, people driving from Kanagawa to this side of the bay had to curve all the way around through central Tokyo. That trip could take hours in traffic. The Aqua-Line turned it into a short, direct drive across the water.

Here is the first surprise. Most of the crossing is not a bridge at all. Roughly two thirds of the route runs inside a tunnel under the seabed. That undersea section is close to ten kilometres long. When it opened, it was among the longest undersea road tunnels anywhere in the world. Drivers who start on the Kawasaki side go underground almost immediately. They see daylight again only in the middle of the bay, where the tunnel rises to meet the bridge.

Why build a tunnel instead of one long bridge? The western half of the bay carries some of the busiest shipping lanes in Japan. Huge container ships and tankers move in and out of the ports of Tokyo, Yokohama and Kawasaki every day. A bridge there would block them or would need to be enormously tall. So the engineers sent the road under the shipping lanes. On the quieter eastern side, near Kisarazu, the water is shallower, and a low bridge made more sense.

The tunnel was dug with giant shield tunnelling machines. These are cylinders of steel with rotating cutting heads at the front, which grind through the soft ground beneath the bay. Several machines worked from different points and met deep under the water. The ground here is soft and full of water, which made the work slow and careful. Japan is also a country of earthquakes, so the whole structure had to be designed to move and survive strong shaking.

At the point where tunnel becomes bridge, there is an artificial island called Umihotaru. The name means sea firefly, a small glowing creature of Japanese coastal waters. Umihotaru is a rest area, but it does not feel like an ordinary motorway stop. The building is long and shaped a little like a large ship anchored in the bay. It has several floors of parking, shops and restaurants, and open decks where you can stand in the wind and look out across the water.

From the upper decks of Umihotaru, the view changes with the weather. On a clear day, the skyline of Tokyo and Yokohama lines the horizon. Some people say that on the clearest winter mornings, Mount Fuji appears far to the west. Ships slide past in slow lines. Planes descend toward Haneda Airport on the far shore. At night, the bridge lights run toward Kisarazu like a string of beads laid on dark water.

Look out toward the Kawasaki side and you may notice a strange structure standing alone in the middle of the bay. It is a ventilation tower on its own small artificial island, known as the Tower of Wind. Its job is simple and essential. A tunnel this long fills with exhaust fumes from cars. The tower pulls stale air out and pushes fresh air in. Its shape is designed to work with the strong winds that blow across the bay.

Now turn east, toward Kisarazu. The bridge section stretches across the water for several kilometres, close to the surface. Driving it feels a little like skimming over the sea. The road rises gently in one place to let smaller boats pass beneath. Then it comes down to land at the Kisarazu shore. For this city, the opening of the Aqua-Line changed everything. A place that once felt far from Tokyo suddenly sat at the end of a direct road.

And that is the second surprise. In its early years, the Aqua-Line carried fewer cars than its planners had hoped, partly because the tolls were high. Later, the toll for ordinary cars was reduced with support from Chiba Prefecture. Traffic grew. Highway buses began to run often between Kisarazu and stations in Tokyo, Kawasaki and Yokohama, and to Haneda Airport. Many people now live on this side of the bay and commute across the water every day.

The area around the Kisarazu end has grown with the road. A large outlet shopping centre stands near the interchange and draws visitors from across the bay on weekends. But Kisarazu also keeps an older relationship with the sea. The shallow tidal flats along this coast are some of the largest remaining in Tokyo Bay. In spring and early summer, families come at low tide to dig for clams in the wet sand, a seasonal tradition in this part of Chiba.

Those tidal flats are also important for wildlife. Migrating shorebirds stop here to feed on the mud. Herons stand in the shallows. So the Aqua-Line lands beside two very different worlds. One is the world of engineering, traffic and commuters. The other is the slow world of tides, sand and birds. Both are part of what makes this side of the bay feel distinct from the dense city across the water.

If you are standing near the shore at Kisarazu now, look back along the line of the bridge. Follow it out across the water until it ends at the small shape of Umihotaru. Beyond that, the road goes down under the sea, out of sight, all the way to the other side. Listen to the wind and the water. Watch the ships moving slowly in the distance. Then, as you turn away, notice how close Tokyo suddenly feels.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stop at Umihotaru and climb to the upper open-air decks.
Come on a clear winter morning for the longest views.
Take a highway bus from Tokyo if you have no car.
Visit Kisarazu tidal flats at low tide in spring.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed decks.
