The guide

The story

In the old quarter of Oran, the streets are narrow and the walls are high, so you hear the mosque before you see it. The call to prayer rolls over the rooftops five times a day, as it has for more than two centuries. Then you turn a corner, and there it is: an octagonal minaret dressed in glazed tiles, catching the light above the worn facades of Sidi El Houari.

This is the Hassan Pasha Mosque, known locally as the Pasha Mosque. It was built in 1797, in the first years after three centuries of Spanish rule ended in Oran. That timing is the key to everything about this building. It is not only a place of prayer. It is a victory monument.

Spain had held Oran, with one interruption, since 1509. The city lived as a fortress of the Spanish crown, with churches, garrisons, and governors. In 1790 an earthquake ruined much of the city, and in 1792 Spain withdrew. Bey Mohamed el-Kebir, the Ottoman governor of western Oran, entered Oran and made it his capital.

The new rulers marked the change in stone. Mohamed el-Kebir ordered this mosque built in 1797 and dedicated it in honor of Hassan Pasha, the Dey of Algiers, the highest Ottoman authority in the land. A grand mosque in the heart of the old city announced, to anyone who passed, that Oran had returned to Muslim rule.

Mohamed el-Kebir is a name you will meet across western Oran. He moved his capital from Mascara to Oran, resettled the emptied city, and built mosques, palaces, and baths. Chroniclers describe him as a builder as much as a soldier. This mosque was among the proudest of his works, raised in the years before his death.

Tradition adds a sharper detail. Accounts from the period say the construction was paid for with money raised from the ransom of Spanish captives. Historians still discuss the details, so hold the story lightly. True or embroidered, it shows how completely this building was understood as a monument to victory.

Look up at the minaret. Most minarets in the Maghreb are square towers, plain and strong. This one is octagonal, slender, and covered with ceramic tiles. That shape comes from Istanbul, not from North Africa. It is an Ottoman signature, placed here deliberately, the way a ruler signs a document. Count the eight sides as you walk around the base.

Step through the doorway and the street noise thins. Light falls into the courtyard and slides along the arcades. Pigeons mutter on the roof tiles. Under your feet lies stone worn smooth by two centuries of passing feet. Places of prayer age differently from fortresses. Use makes them softer, not harder.

The mosque follows Ottoman taste inside as well. A courtyard opens to the sky. Arcades rest on stone columns. The prayer hall is measured and calm, with arches repeating in quiet rhythm and a mihrab niche showing the direction of Mecca. Decoration stays disciplined: carved plaster, tile, and light, rather than gold.

The building has watched the city change hands again. French forces took Oran in 1831, and colonial rule lasted 130 years. The old quarter changed around the mosque: streets renamed, houses rebuilt, communities moved. Through it all, the tiled minaret kept its place on the skyline, and prayer returned, generation after generation, to the hall beneath it.

Oran has classified the mosque as a protected monument, and restoration work has come in waves. Time, salt air, and earthquakes are patient enemies of old buildings, and caretakers here fight them with lime, tile, and slow money. If you find scaffolding on your visit, read it as care, not neglect.

Remember that this is an active house of prayer, not a museum. Visitors are generally welcome outside prayer times. Dress with covered shoulders and knees, speak quietly, and remove your shoes where the carpets begin. If a caretaker offers to show you the courtyard, accept. A few words and a smile open many doors here.

Give the neighborhood a little of your time too. Sidi El Houari is the oldest part of Oran, layered with Spanish walls, Ottoman fountains, and French balconies. Around the mosque you will find workshops, bread ovens, and children playing in lanes where soldiers of three empires once marched.

Before you go, stand where the street frames the minaret and watch the tiles change color with the light. This tower was raised to announce a victory. Two centuries later, the empires are gone, and what remains is quieter and larger: a place where a city keeps meeting itself, five times a day.
