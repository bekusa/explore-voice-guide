The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the voices and the strollers, there is a dry rustle of grass on the hillside. Somewhere below, a hoof strikes hard earth. The air out here smells of sun-warmed dust and sage, nothing like the sea breeze along the coast. You are in the San Pasqual Valley, inland from San Diego, and the land opens wide in front of you. These are the brown hills of Southern California, but the animals on them came from much farther away.

This is the San Diego Zoo Safari Park. It opened in 1972 under a different name, the San Diego Wild Animal Park. The new name arrived in 2010. The idea behind it was simple and, at the time, quite unusual. Instead of keeping large animals in small enclosures, the planners wanted to give herds real space. They wanted giraffes, antelope and rhinos to move in groups across open ground, the way they would in the wild.

The scale is the first surprise for most visitors. The park covers roughly 1,800 acres, and a large part of that land is set aside as open habitat. Much of it is not built up with paths at all. That is why the experience here feels so different from a city zoo. You often look across long distances rather than through a fence. The animals may be close, or they may be small shapes on a far ridge, grazing in the heat.

The park is run by San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance, the nonprofit organisation that also runs the San Diego Zoo in Balboa Park. The two places work as partners, but they have different jobs. The city zoo sits in a compact canyon, full of shade and tropical planting. The Safari Park has room. That room makes it possible to keep breeding herds of large animals, which need space, quiet and stable social groups to raise their young.

Perhaps the most important story here belongs to a bird. The California condor is the largest flying land bird in North America. By the 1980s, only a handful survived in the wild. In a difficult and controversial decision, the last wild condors were captured and brought into breeding programmes. The Safari Park was one of the key places for that work. Birds bred in captivity were later released back into the wild, and today condors fly again over parts of California and the American Southwest.

That is the first thing many visitors miss. This park is not only a place to look at animals. It is also a working conservation centre. On the grounds stands the Beckman Center for Conservation Research. It holds the Frozen Zoo, a collection of living cells from thousands of animals, kept at extremely low temperatures. Scientists began this collection in the 1970s. At the time, nobody knew exactly how it would be used. They simply believed it would matter one day.

That belief has started to pay off. The northern white rhino is now functionally extinct. Only two females survive, and both live in Kenya. Cells from northern white rhinos are stored in the Frozen Zoo. At the Safari Park, researchers work with their close relatives, southern white rhinos, as possible future surrogate mothers. In 2019, a southern white rhino here gave birth to a calf after artificial insemination. It was a small step, but an important one, toward a very long goal.

Now look around at the landscape itself. The valley floor is warm and dry for much of the year, and the sky is often a hard, clear blue. Inland San Diego County gets much hotter than the coast. On summer afternoons, many animals rest in the shade and move very little. In the early morning, and again late in the day, the herds become more active. The low light also turns the grass gold and makes the hills look softer.

Most visitors first see the great open plains from the Africa Tram. It is a slow, guided ride along the edge of the large field habitats. You may see giraffes moving with their unusual rolling walk. You may see rhinos lying down like grey boulders, or antelope in loose groups. A guide talks you through what is in front of you. The animals share this space with each other, which is rare in most zoos and much closer to life on a real savanna.

On foot, the park is organised around different regions of the world. There are trails for Asia, Australia and Africa, each with its own planting and atmosphere. There are lions, tigers, gorillas and elephants, and there is a large aviary where birds fly freely around you. Expect hills. The paths climb and fall with the valley, and the walking adds up quickly. Comfortable shoes and water make a real difference here, especially when the sun is high.

Here is the second surprise. This valley was already part of history long before the park arrived. Close by is the site of the Battle of San Pasqual, fought in 1846 during the Mexican-American War. Californio horsemen, who were Mexican settlers of California, fought United States troops in this area. Long before that, the valley was home to the Kumeyaay, the Indigenous people of this region. Their connection to this land is far older than any modern boundary or name.

The park also changes with the season and the hour. In spring, after the winter rains, the hills can turn green for a short time, and wildflowers appear along the slopes. Through the summer, the green fades to tan and gold. You will notice how the sounds change during the day. In the morning there are bird calls everywhere. In the heat of the afternoon, the park becomes quieter, and you mostly hear people, water features and the wind in the grass.

Some experiences here go deeper than a normal visit. Small group tours in open vehicles drive out into the field habitats. On some of them, you can feed a giraffe or stand closer to a rhino. These tours need a separate booking. Even without one, there are good places to stop and watch. Find a viewing point over the plains, sit for a few minutes, and let the herd behaviour play out in front of you. Patience is often rewarded here.

Before you move on, take one more look across the valley. Notice the distance, the open sky, and the slow movement of animals that have room to walk. Many of them belong to species that are struggling in the wild. Some exist today partly because of work done on these hills. As you walk on, listen again for that dry rustle of grass, and for the sound of hooves somewhere far below.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early, when animals are most active and air is cool.
Wear comfortable shoes for steep, hilly walking paths.
Carry water and sun protection on hot inland days.
Ride the Africa Tram first for an overview of the plains.
Book field tours in advance if you want close encounters.
