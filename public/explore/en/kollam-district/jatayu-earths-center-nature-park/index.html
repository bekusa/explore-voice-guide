The guide

The story

Feel the rock under your feet first. It is warm, grey granite, smooth in some places and rough in others, and it rises out of green Kerala countryside like the back of a sleeping animal. A breeze moves across the top. Below you, rubber trees, coconut palms and tiled rooftops spread out in every direction. Then you turn, and you see the bird.

You are standing on Jatayu Para, a great granite hill in the Chadayamangalam area of Kollam district, in southern Kerala. Para means rock in Malayalam, the language of this state. And lying across the summit is a sculpture of Jatayu, a figure from the Ramayana, one of the two great epic poems of India. Its wings stretch across the stone. Its head lifts toward the sky. It is so large that people often describe it as the largest bird sculpture in the world.

To understand why it is here, you need the story. In the Ramayana, the prince Rama is exiled to the forest with his wife Sita and his brother Lakshmana. The demon king Ravana kidnaps Sita and carries her away through the sky toward his kingdom of Lanka. Jatayu is an old and noble vulture king. He sees the abduction, and he does not hesitate. He flies up to fight Ravana in the air.

It is not a fair fight. Jatayu is old, and Ravana is powerful. In the epic, Ravana cuts off one of Jatayu's wings, and the great bird falls to the earth, mortally wounded. Later, Rama and Lakshmana find him while searching for Sita. With his last strength, Jatayu tells them which way Ravana went. Then he dies, and Rama himself performs his funeral rites... an honour usually given only to family.

Many places across India claim a link to this moment. Here, local tradition is very clear about it. Locals say that this is the rock where Jatayu fell. Some people also say that the name Chadayamangalam comes from Jatayu's name. That is a popular belief rather than settled history, but you will hear it often in the area. Either way, the story gives this hill its meaning, and the sculpture gives that story a body.

The sculpture is the vision of Rajiv Anchal, a filmmaker and artist from Kerala. He spent years on the project. The park was developed together with Kerala's tourism authorities, and it opened to visitors in the late 2010s. Look at the pose. The bird is not shown in triumph. It lies on its side, wounded, one wing spread and its talons gripping the edge of the rock. This is Jatayu in his final moment — still fierce, still defiant.

Here is something many visitors miss. The figure is not only a sculpture to look at. It is a building. The body of the bird is hollow, and the interior is designed as a space for visitors, with exhibits and audiovisual displays about the legend. So the structure works as a landmark from far away, and as a gallery from close up. If the interior is open on your visit, step inside and look at how the shape of the body curves around you.

Now look at the surface. The sculpture is made of concrete, finished in a dark, grainy texture that sits close to the colour of the granite below it. Up close you can follow the long lines of feathers along the wing. The head carries a sharp, hooked beak, and the eye has a hard, focused stare. Walk slowly around it. From each side, the bird looks different — sometimes calm, sometimes in pain, sometimes ready to strike.

Getting up here is part of the experience. Most visitors ride the cable car from the base of the hill. The cabins rise slowly over boulders and forest, and the plains open up beneath you. The ride is short but very steep, and it shows you the true size of this rock. For those who prefer to walk, there are paths as well, but the climb is demanding in the Kerala heat.

The rock is not only about the sculpture. The park also offers adventure activities on another part of the hill, and there is a small cave area linked to traditional wellness practices. On the rock itself, look for the old shrine dedicated to Rama. Pilgrims came to this hill long before any cable car existed. You may also hear about a small depression in the stone that holds water. Legend has it that this water never dries, and some say it marks the place where Jatayu's beak struck the rock as he fell.

Take a moment for the view. This is one of the highest points for some distance around. On a clear day, the land rolls away in layers of green, with the Western Ghats, the long mountain range of southwestern India, sometimes visible as a blue line far to the east. In the monsoon season, clouds can drift right across the summit. In the dry months, the light is sharp and the stone gets hot by midday.

Listen, too. Up here the sounds of the villages below become soft and distant. You might hear a temple bell, a motorbike on a far road, or birds in the trees on the slopes. Kites and other birds of prey sometimes circle above the rock. Watching a real bird turn slowly over a giant stone one is a quiet surprise, and it brings the old story a little closer.

The deeper idea here is about loyalty. Jatayu had no duty to fight Ravana. Sita was not his family. He simply saw a wrong and tried to stop it, knowing he would probably lose. In Kerala, the sculpture is often linked to the protection of women and to the courage to act. That is why many people come here not only as tourists, but with real feeling for the story.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Feel the granite under your feet again, the same rock that pilgrims have walked for generations. Look at the wing laid across the stone, and then at the open sky the bird was trying to defend. As you ride down, watch the figure grow smaller behind you — until it looks, once again, like part of the hill itself.
