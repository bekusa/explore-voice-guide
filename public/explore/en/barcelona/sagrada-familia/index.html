The story

The light here moves like water. Step inside and tilt your head back — columns branch toward the ceiling like a forest canopy, and the stained glass floods the stone in shifting pools of emerald, sapphire, amber. This is not a church frozen in the Middle Ages. This is a building still under construction, still arguing with gravity, still unfinished after more than a century.

Antoni Gaudí began work on the Sagrada Familia in 1883, when he was thirty-one years old. He inherited the project from another architect, scrapped the original neo-Gothic plans, and spent the next forty-three years of his life here — sleeping in his workshop, sketching on site, refining every curve and surface. By the time a tram struck and killed him in 1926, he had completed only one facade and a single tower. He knew he would never see it finished. When asked about the glacial pace, he is said to have replied that his client — God — was not in a hurry.

Gaudí drew his forms from nature, not from pattern books. The columns inside are modeled on trees, their geometry borrowed from eucalyptus and sequoia trunks to distribute weight efficiently. The ceiling vaults mimic the branching of leaves. He tested his structures with hanging chain models, letting gravity reveal the ideal shape, then flipping the design upright. Walk the perimeter and you will see that no two facades are alike — the Nativity side is lush and organic, encrusted with stone flora and fauna, while the Passion facade is stark, angular, almost brutal.

The building site has never closed. Cranes still swing overhead. Stonemasons still carve capitals in workshops open to the street. Gaudí left behind drawings, plaster models, and written notes, but many were destroyed during the Spanish Civil War when anarchists set fire to his studio in 1936. What survived was fragmentary — enough to guide the work, not enough to dictate every detail. Architects and engineers have been interpreting his vision ever since, using computer modeling and modern materials Gaudí never imagined.

The towers — eighteen in total when complete — represent the twelve apostles, the four evangelists, the Virgin Mary, and Jesus Christ. The central Jesus tower, still under construction, will rise to one hundred seventy-two and a half metres, making it the tallest church structure in the world. Gaudí insisted it remain one metre shorter than Montjuïc, the hill overlooking Barcelona — his work, he said, should not surpass God's creation.

The Nativity facade, the only section Gaudí saw substantially completed, faces northeast to catch the morning light. Look closely at the stone and you will find casts taken from real plants, real animals, even real people — local workers and families posed for the angels and shepherds. Gaudí believed that copying nature directly was not laziness but honesty. Somewhere in the carved chaos, a stone tortoise supports a column, a symbol of patience and endurance.

The interior feels less like a cathedral and more like standing beneath an enormous living organism. The columns change color as you move through the nave — white stone near the altar, red stone along the aisles — and the stained glass windows are calibrated to the sun's path, warming the space in gold and orange at sunset, cooling it in blue and green at dawn. Gaudí wanted the light to transform throughout the day, to make the building feel alive and responsive.

Construction is now projected to finish around 2026, the centenary of Gaudí's death, though delays and funding gaps have pushed that date before. The work is funded entirely by donations and entrance fees — no government money, no corporate sponsorship. You are standing inside a building paid for by visitors like yourself, coin by coin, decade by decade.

The Passion facade, completed in the late twentieth century by sculptor Josep Maria Subirachs, provoked fierce debate. Its gaunt, angular figures and stark geometry feel almost alien next to Gaudí's exuberant Nativity side. Subirachs worked from Gaudí's notes but interpreted them freely, and many critics argued he betrayed the original vision. Others say he honored it — Gaudí wanted each facade to tell a different emotional story, and the Passion is meant to feel severe, stripped, unforgiving.

Walk around to the apse and you will find the schools Gaudí built for the workers' children — a small, undulating brick structure with a wavy roof, now used as an exhibition space. It is easy to miss, dwarfed by the towers overhead, but it shows Gaudí's obsession with curves. He believed straight lines belonged to men, curved lines to God.

When you step back outside, notice how the building changes depending on where you stand. From Plaça de Gaudí, across the pond, the Nativity facade reflects in the water and the towers seem to float. From carrer de Sardenya, the cranes and scaffolding dominate, and the church looks like what it is — a work in progress, unfinished, still becoming. Gaudí once said that the Sagrada Familia would be a Bible in stone, readable by anyone. Whether he succeeded is something you decide with your own eyes, standing here in the light.
