The guide

The story

Stand on the wide pavement of the Gran Via and look up. Two tall domes rise above the traffic, shaped almost like eggs balanced on brick towers. They are covered in small glazed tiles, white and deep blue, and on a clear day they catch the sun like porcelain. Cars and buses pass in a steady hum. Pigeons settle on the ledges. This is La Monumental, the last great bullring of Barcelona... and today, a building waiting for its next life.

You are in the Eixample, the grid district that grew beyond the old city walls in the nineteenth century. The streets here run straight and wide, with cut corners at each crossing. La Monumental sits at the meeting of the Gran Via de les Corts Catalanes and Carrer de la Marina. The Sagrada Família is only a short walk away. Many visitors pass this corner on their way to Gaudí's basilica and barely turn their heads.

The ring opened in 1914, under a different name — El Sport. Barcelona already had other bullrings, and the city's appetite for spectacle was large. Within a couple of years, the building was enlarged and redesigned. The architects gave it the look you see now. The new, bigger arena took the name La Monumental, and it soon became the main stage for bullfighting in the city.

Look closely at the style. The architects worked in what is called Neo-Mudéjar. That term needs a short explanation. Mudéjar art was made in medieval Spain by Muslim craftsmen living under Christian rule. It used brick, tile, and geometric patterns. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Spanish architects revived that look for bullrings, train stations, and markets. Here they mixed it with Byzantine touches, which explains the rounded domes.

Now follow the walls with your eyes. The red brick forms arches, bands, and small decorative panels. The ceramic work adds colour where you might expect plain stone. Notice how the domes are not attached to a church or a palace. They crown the entrance towers of a sports arena. That is the first surprise of this building. A place made for crowds, noise, and sand received the kind of ornament usually saved for sacred spaces.

For most of the twentieth century, this ring filled on summer afternoons. Thousands of people climbed the steep stone tiers. Vendors sold cushions, because the seats were hard. Shade and sun divided the crowd, and the shaded seats cost more. Bullfighting was never as deeply rooted in Catalonia as in Andalusia or Madrid. Even so, Barcelona drew famous matadors, and La Monumental was where they came.

Here is the second surprise. On 3 July 1965, the Beatles played in this arena. It was part of their only tour of Spain. The band stood on a stage set up on the sand, where bulls normally ran. Accounts from the time suggest the ring was not full, partly because of ticket prices and the cautious mood of Spain under Franco. Still, for one night, the bullring became a rock venue.

That night was not unusual for the building. Over the decades, La Monumental hosted concerts, circuses, boxing, and political gatherings. A ring is simply a round space with seats on every side. That shape works for many kinds of show. So when you picture the history of this place, do not picture only the bull and the cape. Picture lights, music, acrobats, and crowds cheering for very different reasons.

The story changed in 2010. The Parliament of Catalonia voted to ban bullfighting in the region, after a citizens' petition brought the issue to the chamber. The debate was intense, and it touched questions of animal welfare, tradition, and Catalan identity. The ban came into force at the start of 2012. The final corrida in this arena took place in September 2011. Many people in the stands wept. Others outside celebrated.

Later, Spain's Constitutional Court ruled that the Catalan ban went beyond the region's powers. On paper, that decision reopened the door. In practice, the gates of La Monumental stayed closed to bullfighting. Public opinion in Barcelona had moved on. So the building now stands in a strange position. It is protected as architecture, it is too large to ignore, and it no longer has the purpose it was built for.

Compare this with the city's other old bullring, Las Arenas, at Plaça d'Espanya. That ring closed decades earlier and was turned into a shopping and leisure centre, keeping its historic facade. Over the years, people have proposed many futures for La Monumental too — cultural spaces, sports venues, and other projects. None has fully taken shape. For now, the domes watch the Gran Via, and the arena behind them waits.

Take a slow walk around the outside if you have time. The ring is a complete circle, so the facade continues around the block. You will see gates, ticket windows, and old painted lettering. Some details are faded, and some tiles are missing. That wear is honest. It shows a building that is still standing but no longer busy. The metro station nearby on line two carries the name Monumental, a small sign of how deeply the ring marked this neighbourhood.

Before you leave, step back across the street and look once more at the two domes against the sky. Listen to the traffic, the scooters, the voices from the cafés on the corner. A century ago, this same corner filled with crowds walking toward the gates on a summer afternoon. Today you stand here in a quieter moment of the building's life. Whatever comes next for La Monumental, its blue and white tiles will keep catching the light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with a walk to the nearby Sagrada Família.
Take metro line two to Monumental station.
Cross the Gran Via for a full view of both domes.
Walk the full circle to see the whole facade.
Visit in late afternoon when sunlight warms the brick.
