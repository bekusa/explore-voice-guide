The guide

The story

A white building rests on the green slope of Montjuïc. Its walls curve gently, and rounded skylights rise from the roof like small white towers. Pines and olive trees grow around the terraces. Even the entrance feels informal, like arriving at a friend's summer house. This is the Fundació Joan Miró, the museum Joan Miró created for his own city.

Miró was born in Barcelona in 1893, in a narrow street near the old center. His father was a goldsmith and watchmaker. He trained here and showed his first paintings here, with little success at first. Later he lived in Paris and finally settled in Mallorca. But he never cut the thread to Barcelona. In the late 1960s he decided to give the city a foundation.

He wanted more than a museum of his own work. The foundation would also study modern art and support young artists. For the building, he turned to his close friend Josep Lluís Sert, a Catalan architect who had spent long years in exile and led the architecture school at Harvard University.

Sert designed a white, open building in a Mediterranean spirit. It opened to the public in June 1975. Notice how it treats sunlight. Curved skylights catch the bright southern day and turn it soft. White concrete, pale floors, and sea light: the palette is simple. The galleries feel clear and calm, never dark, never harsh.

Courtyards break the building into pieces. Between rooms you step past patios, sculptures, and framed views of sky. Architecture critics count this among Sert's best works. Miró and Sert understood each other. Both loved simple forms, strong color, and the light of the Mediterranean.

The collection is vast. The foundation keeps thousands of works: paintings, sculptures, textiles, ceramics, and a huge archive of drawings. Most came from Miró himself, from his wife Pilar Juncosa, and from close friends. Almost nothing was bought; nearly everything was given. Together these works follow his whole path, from careful early landscapes to the bold late canvases.

Miró's language looks simple and stays mysterious. A red disc. A black star. A thin line that turns into a bird. He returned again and again to the same signs: night skies, women, birds, ladders reaching upward. You will find dream paintings from his Paris years and fierce, dark works from wartime. Children understand these paintings at once. Scholars spend whole careers on them. Both are right.

One work fills an entire wall. It is the Foundation Tapestry, woven in wool in 1979. Miró made it together with the textile artist Josep Royo. Its reds and yellows glow like fire. Stand well back and let the colors do their work.

Another treasure is not by Miró at all. Behind glass flows the Mercury Fountain by the American sculptor Alexander Calder. Instead of water, it pumps real liquid metal. Calder built it in 1937 for the pavilion of the Spanish Republic at the Paris world fair, during the Civil War. It honored the mercury miners of Almadén. Picasso's Guernica hung in that same pavilion. Today the fountain sits sealed, because mercury vapor is toxic. Watch the silver stream move, slow and heavy, like something from a dream.

Climb to the roof terrace before you leave. Bright sculptures stand against the sky in yellow, red, and blue. Look down into the courtyards, where more works hide among the plants. Beyond the railing the city falls away toward the sea. It is one of the friendliest views in Barcelona.

The foundation also keeps Miró's promise to the young. A space called Espai 13 shows experimental work by new artists through the year. Miró wanted a living place, not a monument to himself. Temporary exhibitions bring other great names, and concerts and family workshops fill the calendar.

Getting here is part of the pleasure. The funicular from Paral·lel metro station climbs the hill in about two minutes, and the museum stands a short walk from the top station. The Laribal gardens next door offer shaded paths and fountains for after your visit. Allow about two hours inside.

Miró is often quoted as saying he wanted to assassinate painting. He meant the tired, polite painting of the past. In its place he left signs as old as cave marks and as fresh as a child's chalk on a pavement. On this bright hillside, inside this white house, his rebellion feels like joy.
