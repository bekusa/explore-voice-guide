The guide

The story

Start with the climb. The stone steps rise in a long, steady line up the hill, and with each step the wind off the Atlantic grows stronger. It carries salt and a little dust from the city below. Above you, a figure in dark bronze gets larger and larger, until it fills most of the sky. Then you stop at the top, catch your breath, and turn around. Dakar spreads out at your feet, and the ocean surrounds it on three sides.

This is the African Renaissance Monument. It stands on one of two volcanic hills on the western edge of Dakar. Locals call them Les Mamelles, a French name that means the breasts, after their rounded shape. These hills are among the few high points on the Cap-Vert peninsula. The peninsula is the westernmost tip of mainland Africa. So in a sense, the statue stands at the very edge of the continent, looking out toward the Americas.

Look up at the figures themselves. A muscular man strides forward. On his raised arm he holds a small child, and the child points out toward the sea. Behind the man, a woman leans into his side, her hair and her cloth blown back as if by a strong wind. The three figures form one family. Together they climb out of what looks like a mountain or a volcano. The message is simple. Africa rises from the earth and turns toward the future.

The scale is hard to take in from below. The statue rises about 49 metres, taller than the Statue of Liberty without its pedestal. It is often described as the tallest statue in Africa. On a clear day, ships at sea and planes landing at the old airport nearby could see it from far away. From the city streets, you catch glimpses of it between buildings. It appears suddenly at the end of a road, then disappears again.

The monument opened in April 2010. That timing was deliberate. In 1960, Senegal gained independence from France, and 2010 marked fifty years of self-rule. The inauguration was a large state event, and leaders from many African countries attended. For the government of the day, the statue was meant to say that the continent had entered a new era. The phrase African Renaissance, meaning an African rebirth, was popular among African political leaders at the time.

The man behind the project was Abdoulaye Wade, the president of Senegal at that time. He championed the idea from the start and spoke about it often. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The statue was not cast in Senegal, or even in Africa. It was built by a state-owned art company from North Korea. That company had built large monuments in several African countries. If the style reminds you of socialist-era statues from Eastern Europe or Asia, that is the reason.

Look closely at the bodies. The muscles are heavy and smooth. The poses are heroic and full of forward movement. The faces look toward the horizon with calm determination. This style is often called socialist realism. It developed in the Soviet Union in the twentieth century to show ideal workers and heroic citizens. Here the same visual language tells a story about Africa. Some people love the boldness of it. Others feel the style comes from somewhere else entirely.

In fact, the statue caused real controversy from the very beginning. Critics questioned the cost, which was reported in the tens of millions of dollars, at a time when many Senegalese families faced hard daily lives. Some protesters took to the streets. President Wade also said that he should receive a share of the tourism income, because the idea was his. That claim angered many people. For some Dakar residents, the monument still carries the memory of those arguments.

There was a second debate, about the figures themselves. Senegal is a mostly Muslim country. Some religious leaders objected to a large human statue, and to the woman's clothing, which leaves her legs and shoulders bare. Others said the figures did not look much like Senegalese people. These debates matter. They show that a monument is never only bronze and concrete. It is also a public question about who decides how a nation sees itself.

Now for the second surprise. The statue is hollow, and you can go inside. The base holds exhibition rooms, and a lift carries visitors up through the body of the man. At the top, a small viewing room sits inside his head. Through the openings there, you look out across Dakar from high above the hill. It is a strange feeling. You are standing inside the statue, seeing the city through its eyes.

From the top, or even from the terrace at the base, the view is wide. To the north, you see the coastline curve away. To the south lies the dense centre of Dakar, with its rooftops, minarets and construction cranes. On the second hill nearby stands the Mamelles lighthouse, a small white tower that has guided ships along this coast for a long time. Out at sea, fishing boats move slowly across the water.

Back on the ground, notice the life around the monument. On weekends, families come to walk the steps and take photographs. Young people sit on the stairs in the late afternoon and watch the sun move toward the ocean. Vendors sometimes sell water and snacks near the bottom. The neighbourhoods around the hills, like Ouakam, are busy with traffic, car horns and the call to prayer. The monument stands above all of it, silent and dark against the sky.

Before you leave, take one last look at the child's hand. It points west, toward the open Atlantic. For centuries, that ocean carried people away from Africa, often by force. Today the gesture points the other way in meaning. It asks the viewer to look forward rather than back. Whatever you think of the statue, it gives you a reason to pause here, at the edge of a continent, and watch the light change over the water.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the steps in late afternoon for softer, cooler light.
Bring water; the hilltop stairs have little shade.
Ask at the base about the lift to the viewing room.
Hold onto hats; Atlantic winds blow hard at the top.
