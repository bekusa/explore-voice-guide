The guide

The story

Glass towers rise above a lagoon. From across the water, the skyline looks like a wall of light. Ferries and water buses cross toward it through the morning haze. Car horns echo between the towers. This is the Plateau, the business heart of Abidjan, and for decades people have called it the Manhattan of West Africa.

The Plateau sits on a low rise of land between the Ébrié Lagoon and the rest of the city. The streets follow a grid. Banks, ministries, insurance companies, and law offices fill the towers. On weekday mornings, thousands of workers pour in across the bridges. In the evening, the flow reverses. The district is small enough to walk end to end in under an hour.

Abidjan became the capital of the French colony in 1934, and the Plateau became its seat of power. Administrators built offices on this high ground above the water. After independence in 1960, the new state kept the same center. Ministries replaced colonial bureaus, floor by floor.

Then came the boom. In the 1960s and 1970s, Côte d'Ivoire grew rich on cocoa and coffee. People spoke of an Ivorian miracle. Money flowed into the Plateau, and the towers began to climb. Hotels, cinemas, and company headquarters followed the banks. For a time, this was the showcase of West African success.

One building from that era still surprises visitors. La Pyramide is a huge stepped mass of concrete, wider at the top than at the base. The Italian architect Rinaldo Olivieri designed it, and it was completed in 1973. He wanted a modern building that worked like an African market, with trade below and life above. The building later stood empty for years, but it remains a landmark of bold design.

A few streets away stands St. Paul's Cathedral. Another Italian architect, Aldo Spirito, designed it. Pope John Paul II laid its first stone in 1980 and consecrated the finished cathedral in 1985. The bell tower takes the shape of a giant figure leaning back, holding cables that sweep down to the roof. Some see a cross. Some see a fisherman casting a net. Inside, colored glass fills the walls with light.

Power lives here too. The Presidential Palace faces the lagoon behind high walls and guards. Government ministries line the nearby avenues. In 1983, the official capital moved to Yamoussoukro, the home town of the first president, Félix Houphouët-Boigny. His name is everywhere in this country: a bridge, a stadium, and the airport all carry it. But in practice, government business never left the Plateau.

Walk the streets and read the layers. Some buildings show the clean lines of 1960s modernism. Some show colonial arcades and deep shutters. Newer towers add blue glass and steel. Street vendors set up at their feet, selling phone cards, fruit, grilled plantain, and newspapers. Suits and market baskets share the same sidewalks.

The Plateau is also where Ivorian business speaks to the world. The regional stock exchange for eight West African countries has its seat here. Cocoa export houses, shipping agents, and telecom headquarters cluster within a few blocks. Côte d'Ivoire grows about forty percent of the world's cocoa, and much of that trade is arranged behind these windows.

Look toward the water. Two bridges tie the Plateau to the southern communes: the Houphouët-Boigny Bridge, opened in 1957, and the Charles de Gaulle Bridge, opened in 1967. Farther east, the long Henri Konan Bédié Bridge, opened in 2014, links other districts across the lagoon. Every morning the bridges carry the Plateau's workforce in. Every evening they carry it home.

Timing changes everything here. On a weekday at noon, the Plateau runs at full speed, and lunch counters fill with office workers. Friday afternoons have their own mood, loose and loud before the weekend. On Sunday, the same streets fall almost silent. The towers stand empty, and you can hear birds over the lagoon. Visit twice, if you can, and you will see two different cities.

The district rewards looking up. Count the satellite dishes on the tower roofs. Find the older facades hiding between the glass giants. Notice how the light changes on the lagoon in the late afternoon, when the sun drops behind the skyline and the water turns to metal.

Cities put their hopes into architecture. The Plateau holds the hopes of a young nation that wanted to rise fast, and for a time did. Booms passed, crises came, and the towers stayed. Stand at the water's edge, look up once more, and imagine the next chapter being drawn above the lagoon.
