The guide

The story

Listen first. On Kimolos, the loudest sound is often the wind. It moves across low stone walls, dry thyme and pale rock, and then it drops away. In that quiet you may hear a goat bell, a fishing boat's engine in the harbour, or the scrape of a chair outside a café. The light here is white and strong. It bounces off chalky ground and whitewashed houses, so even the shadows look soft.

Kimolos is a small island in the Cyclades, the ring of islands in the central Aegean Sea. It sits very close to its larger neighbour, Milos, and the crossing between them is short. Many visitors see Kimolos only as a smudge on the horizon from Milos. That is part of its character. It is quiet, and fewer than a thousand people live here all year. Life still follows the rhythm of fishing, farming and the ferry timetable.

The island's story begins in the ground beneath your feet. Kimolos is volcanic, like Milos, and its rocks are full of soft, pale minerals. In the ancient world, the island was known for a special white clay. Greek and Roman writers called it Kimolian earth. People used it to clean cloth, to whiten fabric and for medicine. Here is the first surprise: a mineral named cimolite takes its name from this small island.

That link between the island and its earth never really ended. Mining still takes place on Kimolos today, mostly for industrial clays such as bentonite. You may notice quarry areas, pale scars on the hillsides, or trucks on the roads. Some visitors find this unexpected on a quiet Greek island. But it is an honest part of local life. The same soft rock that cleaned ancient robes still gives work to people here now.

The ferries arrive at Psathi, the small harbour. From there, a road climbs to the main village, known simply as Chorio, which means the village. Walk into its lanes and the noise of engines fades. The streets are narrow and paved with stone, and the edges of the steps are often painted white. Doors and window frames come in blue, green and deep red. Bougainvillea hangs over walls. Cats sleep in doorways and do not move for anyone.

At the heart of Chorio is the Kastro, the old fortified settlement. Look closely at how it was built. There is no separate castle wall. Instead, the outer houses stand side by side, and their back walls form the defence. The windows on the outside were small and high. This design is common in the Cyclades and dates from the centuries of piracy, when villages had to protect themselves. Today, parts of the Kastro are in ruins, and parts are being carefully restored.

This was not always a peaceful sea. For centuries, pirates and rival powers moved through these waters. Under Western rule, sailors knew the island by another name, Argentiera. The name is often linked to the silver colour of its rocks, though explanations vary. Locals also tell a legend that the island takes its modern name from an early settler called Kimolos. It is a story passed down, not a documented fact, but it shows how strongly people here connect place and identity.

Now head down toward the coast, and you will find another side of the island. Along certain bays, fishermen built small houses directly at the water's edge. They are called syrmata. The ground floor held the boat, and in winter the family hauled it up inside, away from the storms. The families lived above. In places like Goupa and Rema, these houses sit in the rock itself, with brightly painted doors almost touching the sea.

Stand in front of these fishermen's houses and look at the rock around them. It is soft, layered, and full of curves made by wind and water. Erosion is the real artist on Kimolos. Inland, on the western side of the island, it has shaped a famous rock called Skiadi. It looks like a giant stone mushroom, with a wide top on a narrow stem. Reaching it takes a walk on rough paths, but many people say the walk is part of the reward.

The beaches here are another surprise. Because of the pale minerals, some bays have sand that looks almost white, and the water above it shines a bright turquoise. Prassa, in the northeast, is one of the best known. Elsewhere, the coast is quiet and rocky, with small coves you may have to yourself. Near the beach called Ellinika, there are remains of an ancient settlement. Over time, parts of it sank below the sea, as the land slowly moved.

Look out across the water from the eastern coast, and you may see a low, empty island. This is Polyaigos. Nobody lives there now, and it is a protected natural area. Its sea caves and clear waters are important for the Mediterranean monk seal, one of the rarest seals in the world. Boat trips sometimes visit its coves in summer. If you go, keep your distance from any wildlife, and enjoy the silence.

Back in Chorio, food tells you a lot about the island. Small tavernas serve local cheese, fresh fish, and bread baked in the island style. Many dishes use simple ingredients from dry soil: capers, tomatoes, chickpeas and wild greens. In the evening, the village squares fill with families and friends. Children play in the lanes. Older people sit outside and talk. Nobody seems to hurry, and after a few hours, you probably will not either.

There is one more detail worth noticing. Kimolos has many small chapels, scattered across the hills and along the coast. Many belong to families, and each one has a feast day. On those days, people gather for a service, followed by food and sometimes music. These festivals, called panigyria, keep the community together. If you see a chapel decorated with flags, or hear bells in the afternoon, you may be close to one.

So take a moment where you are. Feel the dry, warm air. Look at the pale rock, the white walls, and the blue sea between here and Milos. This island has always lived from its earth and its water. As you walk away, notice the small things: a painted step, a boat pulled up on the sand, a chapel bell. They are the real story of Kimolos.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the short ferry hop from Pollonia on Milos.
Wear sturdy shoes for the rocky path to Skiadi.
Carry water and sun protection on inland walks.
Book summer boat trips to Polyaigos in advance.
Visit Chorio in the evening when the squares fill.
