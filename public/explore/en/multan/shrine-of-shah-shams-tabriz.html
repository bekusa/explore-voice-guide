The guide

The story

They say Multan owes its famous heat to the man buried here. On high ground above a vanished riverbed stands a walled courtyard, and above it a dome sheathed in green glazed tile. Inside rests Shah Shams, a wanderer from Persia who reached Multan in the thirteenth century and never left. At dusk, the shrine glows like a lamp.

His full name was Shams-ud-Din, and he came from Sabzwar in Persia, so many call him Shams Sabzwari. He arrived as a preacher and missionary, one of many holy men drawn to Multan in that century. He taught, gathered followers, and earned a reputation for miracles. He died here around 1276, an old man, loved across the city.

Multan in the thirteenth century was full of such arrivals. Mongol armies had burned the great cities of Persia and Central Asia, and scholars, craftsmen, and mystics fled east to safety. Multan received them by the caravan-load. The city became a refuge of learning, and shrines like this one mark the places where those long journeys finally ended.

The name over the gate says Shah Shams Tabriz, and that name carries a famous confusion. Shams of Tabriz was the wandering teacher who transformed Rumi, the most beloved poet of the Persian language. Many in Multan believe this is that very man. Most historians say the two were different people who shared a name and a century. The pilgrims are untroubled either way.

Then there is the legend of the sun. The story goes that the saint asked for meat, and the city refused him fire to cook it. So Shams called the sun down closer and roasted his meal in its rays. Since that day, people say, Multan burns hotter than any other city of Punjab. It is a legend, told with a smile, and locals repeat it every summer.

The shrine's recorded story is simpler. Accounts say his grandson raised the first tomb here in 1330. The building you see today dates mostly from 1718, when a descendant rebuilt it after long years of flood and wear. New tiles and repairs have been added many times since. Shrines in Multan are never really finished. They are kept alive, like fires that must not go out.

The tomb is a square chamber wrapped in Multan's signature crafts. Glazed tiles band the walls in blue, white, and green. Carved wooden doors open into a cool inner room where the cenotaph lies under embroidered cloth and rose petals. Outside, a whitewashed courtyard holds old graves, shade trees, and generations of sparrows.

The shrine stands on what was once the high bank of the Ravi River. The water left long ago, but the raised ground remains. From the courtyard edge you can read the old geography: the fort mound rising nearby, and the flat land below where the river used to run. Boats once moored below; today rickshaws hum where water flowed.

Devotion here crosses boundaries. Sunni and Shia Muslims pray side by side. Ismaili tradition honors him as Pir Shams and preserves hymns in his name. Hindu and Sikh families with old Multan roots have long visited too. One grave, many doors. That has always been the Multani way: many communities, one city of saints.

Thursday evening is the shrine's heartbeat. Drummers settle into the courtyard, and qawwali singers begin after sunset, repeating verses of longing until they shine. Pilgrims tie threads at the screens, light oil lamps, and share cooked rice. If you come then, stand quietly at the edge and simply listen.

The largest crowds come for the urs, the yearly festival of the saint's death anniversary. For several days the lanes fill with stalls, drummers, and pilgrims from far villages. A death anniversary may sound sad. Here it is celebrated like a wedding, because Sufis describe death as a union with the Beloved.

On ordinary days, small rituals carry the shrine along. A caretaker sweeps the floor with a straw broom. Women whisper wishes at the lattice. A man pours water from a clay pot for thirsty visitors. The air holds rosewater, incense, and warm dust, the shared perfume of every shrine in Punjab.

Leaving, you pass once more under the dome's shadow. Whether or not this Shams was Rumi's Shams no longer seems important. A city has kept a stranger's grave for 750 years and still brings it flowers every day. The sun overhead feels a little personal here. Perhaps that is the real point of the story.
