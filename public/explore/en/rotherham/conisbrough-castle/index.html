The guide

The story

Pause at the foot of the slope and look up. The great round tower is pale, almost cream, against the Yorkshire sky. Its stone is magnesian limestone, a soft local rock that catches light in a way darker stone never does. On a bright morning the tower seems to glow. On a grey one it looks like bone. Underfoot, the grass of the old earthworks is uneven and springy, shaped by people who dug these banks almost a thousand years ago.

You are standing above the valley of the River Don, in the small town of Conisbrough, a short journey from Rotherham and Doncaster. The position explains everything. From this hill, anyone in charge could watch the river, the road, and the farmland around it. The name itself is very old. It comes from an Old English phrase usually translated as the king's fortification, so this ridge carried a royal meaning even before the Normans arrived.

After the Norman Conquest of 1066, these lands went to William de Warenne, one of William the Conqueror's most trusted followers. The Warenne family became earls of Surrey and built up enormous estates across England. Here, their first castle was most likely a fortress of earth and timber. Banks, ditches, and wooden walls came first — stone came later. Much of what you see around you follows the outline of that early design.

The tower in front of you belongs to the next chapter. In the later twelfth century the Warenne heiress, Isabel, married Hamelin, an illegitimate half-brother of King Henry the Second. Hamelin took the Warenne name and the Warenne wealth. Historians generally credit him with building the great keep. A keep is the strongest tower of a castle — the last refuge, and also the lord's statement of power.

Now look closely at its shape, because this is the first surprise. Most English keeps of that age were square. This one is round, and around it stand six huge buttresses, like solid ribs pressed against a drum. Round walls are harder to undermine at the corners, because there are no corners. The buttresses add strength and height. Together they give the tower a profile that is unlike almost any other in England.

Walk slowly around the base and notice the finish of the stone. The blocks are cut and fitted with great care. This was expensive work, done by skilled masons, and it was meant to be seen. Hamelin was close to the royal family, and his castle needed to say so. A visitor riding up the valley would see this pale tower long before reaching the gate.

The entrance does not sit at ground level. Instead, you reach the keep at an upper floor, up a flight of steps. That was a deliberate defence. An enemy could not simply break down a door at the foot of the wall. Inside, the floors are stacked one above another, and a narrow stair climbs within the thickness of the wall itself. You can feel that thickness as you pass through the doorways.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it. High up in the keep, built into one of those massive buttresses, is a small chapel. It is a private space for the lord and his household, with carved stonework and a vaulted ceiling. Look for the decoration around its arches and windows. Prayer, in other words, happened inside the defences, tucked into the very structure that held the tower up.

The interior you walk through today is partly a modern reconstruction. In the early twenty-first century, English Heritage, which looks after the site, put new floors and a new roof back into the keep. Before that, the tower had stood open to the sky for centuries. The new timber lets you move from level to level and imagine the rooms as living spaces rather than an empty shell.

Step back out into the open area below the keep. This is the inner bailey, the enclosed courtyard of the castle. A stone curtain wall once ran right around it, with small towers along its length. Parts of that wall have fallen down the slope over time, and the gaps show you how steep the hillside really is. Inside the bailey there were halls, kitchens, and service buildings, now marked mostly by low foundations.

The castle later passed out of Warenne hands and into the wider royal family. In the fourteenth century it was linked with Edmund of Langley, a son of King Edward the Third and the first Duke of York. His son, Richard of Conisburgh, took his name from this place and is believed to have been born here. Richard's own son became Duke of York, and that line led to two later kings — Edward the Fourth and Richard the Third.

The castle also has a second life in fiction. In 1819, the novelist Sir Walter Scott published Ivanhoe, a romantic tale set in medieval England. Scott used Coningsburgh, as he spelled it, as the home of the Saxon noble Athelstane. The novel has little to do with real history. Still, it helped bring visitors to this hillside in the nineteenth century, and the romance of the tower has never quite faded.

Before you leave, find a spot on the bank where you can see the keep and the valley together. Listen for a moment — the wind on the hill, traffic far below, perhaps rooks calling from the trees. Look at how the light moves across those six buttresses. Generations have stood roughly where you are standing, judging this tower by the same pale stone. As you walk down the slope, turn back once more. The tower still keeps watch over the Don.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, uneven earthwork slopes.
Climb to the upper floors for views over the Don valley.
Visit on a bright day to see the limestone glow.
Allow time to walk the full circuit of the bailey.
Check English Heritage booking information before you travel.
