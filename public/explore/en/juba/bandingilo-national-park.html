The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through tall grass, and the grass answers with a long, papery hiss. The ground underfoot is pale and cracked in the dry months, then dark and soft after the rains. The light here is wide and flat at midday, and gold at the edges of the day. You are east of the White Nile, not far from Juba. Yet the city already feels far behind you. Welcome to Bandingilo National Park, one of the great open grasslands of South Sudan.

You may also see the name written as Badingilo. Both spellings appear on maps and in conservation reports. The park was declared in the early 1990s, during the long years of civil war. At that time, almost nobody outside the region knew what lived here. War closed the roads and kept researchers away. For a long time, this place existed mostly on paper. The landscape itself kept going, of course. The grass grew, the rains came, and the animals kept moving.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. This park is part of one of the largest wildlife migrations on Earth. Most people picture the Serengeti when they hear the word migration. But in this corner of East Africa, huge herds of antelope move across the land every year. They follow the rains and the fresh grass. Their route links Bandingilo with Boma National Park to the east and with the great Sudd wetlands to the north. The whole system is often called the Boma-Badingilo landscape.

The star of that movement is the white-eared kob. It is a medium-sized antelope with a warm brown coat. Look at the head if you see one. The ears and a patch around the eyes are white, and the males carry curved, ridged horns. The kob are found in big numbers mainly in South Sudan and nearby parts of Ethiopia. They live in herds that can stretch across the horizon. When they move together, the dust rises in a low brown cloud.

Two other antelope travel with them. The first is the tiang, a tall, long-faced antelope with a glossy coat and dark patches on the legs. It is a local form of the topi, which some visitors know from Kenya. The second is the Mongalla gazelle, small and quick, with a dark stripe along each side. The gazelle takes its name from Mongalla, a town on the Nile south of here. Together these three species give the grassland its constant motion.

Now for the second surprise. After the civil war, scientists finally returned with small aircraft to count the herds. The first surveys in the late 2000s showed that the migration had survived decades of conflict. Later aerial surveys across the wider landscape, carried out with the conservation group African Parks, counted antelope in the millions. Those results drew attention from conservationists around the world. Few people expected such numbers in a country so often described only through war.

Since the early 2020s, the national wildlife authorities have worked with African Parks to manage Bandingilo and Boma together. The goal is to protect the migration as one connected system, not as two separate parks. Rangers patrol on foot and by vehicle. Poaching for bushmeat is a real pressure here, and so is the spread of farms and cattle. Protection in this region depends on local communities as much as on rangers. Many families in the area have lived beside these herds for generations.

The people around the park are mostly pastoralists and farmers. Cattle hold deep importance in many South Sudanese cultures. A herd can mark wealth, marriage, and social standing. So the same grass that feeds the kob also feeds the cows. In the dry season, you may meet herders with long-horned cattle moving toward water. This shared use of land is old. Conservation here means finding a balance between wild herds and the people who also need this ground.

Look around you at the land itself. Much of Bandingilo is flat savanna grassland, broken by scattered trees and low patches of woodland. Some trees are thorny acacias, with small leaves and sharp spines. In the wet season, wide areas flood and turn into shallow marsh. The grass shoots up fast and green. In the dry months, the colours shift to straw, grey, and red earth. The same view can look like two different countries, depending on the month you stand here.

Birds give the park much of its sound. In the wetter areas, watch for storks and herons walking slowly through the shallows. Raptors often circle high overhead, riding the warm air above the plains. Vultures may gather where an animal has died. Smaller birds move through the grass and the thorn trees. If you stop the vehicle and turn off the engine, the bird calls become clear. It is a good moment to simply sit and listen for a minute.

Visiting Bandingilo is not like visiting a busy safari park. There are no paved loops and no crowds of minibuses. Roads are rough, and many become impassable in the rainy season. Most visitors come with an authorised guide or an organised trip from Juba. Permits and local permission are part of the process. This effort is part of the experience. When you do reach the open plains, you will likely have the view almost entirely to yourself.

Think, too, about what the park means for South Sudan today. The country became independent in 2011, the youngest nation in the world at the time. It has faced conflict and hardship since then. Wildlife has become one of the few stories of hope that reach the outside world. Some people see the migration as a future source of tourism and income. Others value it simply as a living part of the nation's landscape and identity.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the horizon and count the shapes you can see. Some may be trees, some may be cattle, and some may be antelope. Notice the colour of the grass and the feel of the wind on your skin. Listen again for that dry hiss in the grass. The herds will keep moving long after you drive back toward Juba. For now, you are standing in the middle of their road.

Practical tips

Arrange permits and local permission before setting out.

Carry plenty of water, sun protection, and spare fuel.
