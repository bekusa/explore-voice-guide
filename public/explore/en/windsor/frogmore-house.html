The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel shifts under your feet, and somewhere across the water a moorhen calls. The air here smells of cut grass and damp leaves. Ahead of you, a long white house sits low and calm beside a curving lake. The roads and crowds of Windsor feel much further away than they are. This is Frogmore House, and for more than two centuries it has been one of the quietest corners of royal life in England.

You are standing in the Home Park, the private parkland that stretches south of Windsor Castle. The castle's towers are not far away, yet the mood here is completely different. The castle was built for defence and display. Frogmore was built for rest. It is a place where kings and queens came to read, paint, walk and grieve, away from the formal rooms of court. It is a quiet house, and it is worth exploring slowly.

The name itself raises a smile. According to a popular local explanation, it comes from the frogs that once filled this low, marshy ground beside the Thames. Nobody can prove that story, but stand by the lake on a spring evening and you may decide it is believable. The land here is flat and green, with water never far away. That damp, gentle landscape shaped everything that came later.

The first house on this site dates from the late seventeenth century, during the 1680s. It was a modest country residence, built in the restrained, symmetrical style of that period. Royal ownership came later. The house passed through several hands before the most important figure in its story arrived. She was a queen with a deep love of plants, books and the quiet pleasures of the countryside.

That queen was Charlotte, the wife of King George the Third. She acquired Frogmore in the 1790s. She wanted a retreat — somewhere she and her daughters could escape the strain of court. The king's long periods of illness made life at Windsor difficult. Here, Charlotte could follow her own interests. She enlarged the house with the help of the architect James Wyatt, and gave it the long, elegant shape you see today.

Charlotte's real passion was botany, the scientific study of plants. She collected specimens, studied flowers and encouraged artists to paint them. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Inside the house, one room is decorated with large painted flowers by Mary Moser. She was one of only two women among the founding members of the Royal Academy of Arts in London. Her garlands make the room feel like a garden brought indoors.

Now turn towards the grounds, because the gardens are the true heart of Frogmore. Charlotte had the landscape reshaped in the informal English style. Straight lines gave way to winding paths, open lawns and the serpentine lake. The idea was to make nature look natural, even though every curve was carefully planned. Walk a few steps along the water and watch how the views open and close behind the trees.

Scattered through the gardens are small buildings called follies. A folly is a decorative structure built mainly for its look rather than for practical use. One of them is a Gothic ruin, built to look old and crumbling from the start. Romantic taste in that era loved the feeling of melancholy, and a fake ruin provided it. There is also a delicate, Indian-style kiosk, a reminder of how far royal tastes travelled.

In the nineteenth century, Frogmore became the home of the Duchess of Kent, the mother of Queen Victoria. Mother and daughter had a difficult relationship in Victoria's early years, but they later grew close. The duchess died here in 1861. Her death hit Victoria hard. Only months later, Victoria lost her husband, Prince Albert. That year of grief changed Frogmore forever.

Here is the second surprise. Frogmore is not only a place of rest for the living. Within these grounds stands the Royal Mausoleum, where Queen Victoria and Prince Albert are buried together. Victoria planned it after Albert's death, and it takes its style from the art of the Italian Renaissance. Nearby, the duchess has her own smaller mausoleum. The garden became a landscape of memory, as well as a place for walking and quiet thought.

Close by lies the Royal Burial Ground, a peaceful plot used for members of the royal family. Among those buried there are King Edward the Eighth, who gave up the throne in the 1930s, and Wallis Simpson, the American woman he married. Their story divided a nation. Today, their graves rest quietly under the trees, far from the arguments of their time.

Frogmore has not stopped being part of royal life. Close to the house stands Frogmore Cottage, a smaller residence that became widely known in recent years. In 2018, Frogmore House itself hosted the evening reception after the wedding of Prince Harry and Meghan Markle. The house is still a working royal property. That is why it opens to the public only on a small number of selected days each year.

So take a moment where you are. Look at the pale front of the house and the low line of its windows. Notice how still the lake is, and how the trees frame each view like a painting. Charlotte wanted a place where the noise of the world faded. As you walk back towards the castle, listen to the gravel again... and see if some of that stillness walks with you.
