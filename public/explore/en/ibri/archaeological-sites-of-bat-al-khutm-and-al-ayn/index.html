The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the wind moves across bare limestone with a dry, papery sound. The ground crunches underfoot, loose and pale, scattered with flat stones that someone, long ago, chose and carried. On the ridges around you, small rounded shapes sit against the sky. They look almost like natural rock piles at first. Look again. Their curves are too regular. These are tombs, built by people who lived in this valley around five thousand years ago.

You are standing in one of the oldest built landscapes in the Arabian Peninsula. The sites of Bat, Al-Khutm and Al-Ayn lie in the interior of Oman, in the Dhahirah region near the town of Ibri. In 1988, UNESCO added them to the World Heritage List. Together they form one of the most complete groups of Bronze Age settlements and burial grounds known from the third millennium before Christ. That means these stones were set in place around the same era as the early pyramids of Egypt.

Start with the tombs, because they are the first thing your eye catches. Archaeologists often call them beehive tombs. The name comes from their shape — a rounded dome of stacked, unworked or lightly shaped stones. Many stand along the tops of ridges and slopes. Placing them on high ground made them visible from far away. For the people of the valley below, the dead were not hidden. They were part of the skyline, seen every day from the fields and the paths.

The oldest of these tombs belong to what scholars call the Hafit period. The name comes from Jebel Hafit, a mountain on the border with the United Arab Emirates, where similar tombs were first studied. Hafit tombs are generally small, with a single chamber inside. Later tombs, from a phase called the Umm an-Nar period, tend to be larger and more carefully built. Some have neat outer walls of dressed stone. Walking among them, you can see changing ideas about death and craft.

Now look down toward the valley floor at Bat. Here the story changes from the dead to the living. Scattered among the date palms and fields are the remains of large round stone towers. Their exact purpose is still debated. Some researchers suggest they were defensive strongholds. Others think they may have been linked to water, storage, or the display of status. Several were built around wells. The honest answer is that nobody knows for certain, and that uncertainty is part of what makes them fascinating.

Here is something many visitors miss. The modern village of Bat is still farmed today. Date palms grow close to stones that were laid thousands of years ago. Water still reaches the fields through a falaj — a traditional Omani irrigation channel that carries water by gravity from a source to the gardens. Falaj systems are much younger than the Bronze Age towers. Still, the scene shows how long people have chosen this same patch of land for one simple reason: water and soil, together, in a dry country.

Serious excavation here began in the 1970s, when a Danish archaeological team led by Karen Frifelt worked at Bat. Later, other international teams continued the research, including archaeologists from American universities working with Omani authorities. Their work helped show that Bat was not an isolated village. It was part of a wider network of settlements across what is now Oman and the United Arab Emirates. Each season of digging has added detail, but large parts of the site remain unexcavated even now.

That wider network matters. Ancient texts from Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, mention a land called Magan that supplied copper and stone. Many scholars believe Magan included parts of modern Oman. The mountains here are rich in copper, and Bronze Age smelting sites have been found across the region. The people who built these towers and tombs may have been connected, directly or through trade, to the great cities of the Tigris and Euphrates. Picture copper moving from these hills toward distant river ports.

About two kilometres west of Bat lies Al-Khutm. It is a smaller site, centred on the remains of another stone tower. It is quieter and easier to take in at a single glance. Standing beside it, you can compare its construction with the towers at Bat. Notice how the builders fitted large blocks without mortar, relying on weight and careful placement. Al-Khutm helps show that these towers were not a one-off. They were a repeated idea, built again and again across the valley.

Farther away, roughly twenty kilometres or so to the east of Bat, is Al-Ayn. Many travellers find this the most beautiful of the three. A line of beehive tombs runs along a ridge. Behind them rises Jebel Misht, a sharp limestone mountain whose jagged crest looks like the teeth of a comb. At sunset, the tombs stand dark against the cliff while the rock turns orange and pink. It is a view that has changed very little since the tombs were built.

It is worth slowing down at any of these places. There are no grand halls or carved inscriptions here. The power of the sites is quieter. It comes from scale, from repetition, and from patience. Hundreds of tombs, built stone by stone, by hand, in a harsh climate. Try to imagine the work involved — gathering the stones, carrying them uphill, fitting each one so the dome would hold. Many of these tombs have stood through five thousand summers.

A note of care as you explore. The tombs are fragile, even if they look solid. Stones that have balanced for millennia can shift under a single foot. Stay on the paths where they exist, and please do not climb on the structures or move any stones. The heat can be intense for much of the year, and shade is scarce on the ridges. Water, a hat, and sturdy shoes make the walk far more pleasant, especially on the rocky slopes.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look along the ridge. Follow the row of rounded shapes with your eyes, one after another, until they fade into the hills. Somewhere below you, the palms move in the breeze, and water runs in its channel toward the fields. The tombs are silent, but the valley around them is still lived in, still farmed, still home. As you walk back, listen again for the wind over the stones.
