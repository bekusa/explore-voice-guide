The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the traffic on the bridge and the low hum of the river, there is a quieter world here. Behind these pale stone walls, people are working with wet clay, fine brushes, and furnaces, much as their predecessors did more than two centuries ago. Welcome to the Manufacture nationale de Sèvres, the French national porcelain works. It sits just across the Seine from Boulogne-Billancourt, on the edge of the wooded Parc de Saint-Cloud.

First, a small surprise about the name. This place carries the name of Sèvres, but its story did not begin here. The workshop started in the 1740s in the royal château of Vincennes, on the opposite side of Paris. At that time, Europe was obsessed with porcelain. Chinese and Japanese pieces were treasured, and the German factory at Meissen had already learned the secret. France wanted its own answer, and the king's circle was willing to pay for it.

In 1756 the enterprise moved here, to Sèvres. The location made sense. It lay on the road between Paris and Versailles, close to the royal court and its wealthy buyers. The river also brought wood and materials by boat. One name appears again and again in this chapter of the story. Madame de Pompadour, the influential companion of King Louis the Fifteenth, was a passionate supporter of the factory. Soon after the move, the king himself took ownership, and Sèvres became a royal manufacture.

In those early decades, the factory made what is called soft-paste porcelain. This is a material made without kaolin, the special white clay used in Asia. Soft-paste is difficult to fire and easy to lose in the kiln. But it accepts colour beautifully. The glaze melts into the painted surface, and the colours appear deep and soft. That quality made the reputation of Sèvres across Europe's courts.

Those colours are worth knowing by name. There is the famous Sèvres blue, a strong and rich blue that collectors still recognise at a glance. There is a delicate pink, often called rose Pompadour, after the factory's great patron. There are also turquoise and apple greens, framed by thick gilding. When you visit the museum next door, look for these ground colours. Painted flowers, birds, and small landscapes often appear inside white panels, surrounded by blue or pink and edged with gold.

Then came a turning point. In the second half of the eighteenth century, deposits of kaolin were found in France, near Limoges. With this white clay, Sèvres could finally produce hard-paste porcelain, the same type as in China. Hard-paste is stronger and whiter, and it rings when you tap it. From then on, the factory worked with both materials. Limoges, meanwhile, grew into a great porcelain centre in its own right.

The factory survived the French Revolution, which is remarkable for an institution so closely tied to the king. It then served each new regime in turn. Napoleon used Sèvres porcelain as a diplomatic tool, sending grand services and vases as gifts. A long-serving director in the early nineteenth century, the scientist Alexandre Brongniart, brought a new rigour to the work. He studied ceramics from all over the world and began the collection that became the museum you can visit today.

Now look at the buildings in front of you. They are not the original eighteenth-century workshops. The factory moved into these purpose-built premises in the eighteen-seventies, at the foot of the park. The long, orderly façades were designed for production and for display at the same time. Studios, storerooms, and kilns are arranged around courtyards. It feels a little like a college campus, with trees, gravel paths, and quiet footsteps.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it. Sèvres is not only a museum. It is still a working factory, owned by the French state. Craftspeople here continue to throw, mould, paint, and gild porcelain largely by hand. Many techniques pass from one generation of artisans to the next. Some pieces go to official residences and embassies, or become gifts from the French Republic to foreign leaders. Others are sold, and some are created with contemporary artists and designers.

The factory also keeps an enormous archive of plaster moulds and models, built up over centuries. In principle, a shape designed for a royal table can still be cast again today. This continuity is part of what makes the place unusual. The historic wood-fired kilns are also protected as heritage, tall brick structures that once needed great quantities of fuel and careful watching through long firings. Workshop visits are only possible on certain guided tours, so check in advance if you hope to see the studios.

The museum itself, the national ceramics museum, is worth your time. It does not show only Sèvres. It displays pottery and porcelain from many cultures and periods, from ancient vessels to Islamic tiles, Chinese porcelain, Italian maiolica, which is a type of painted, tin-glazed earthenware, and modern work. Walking through it, you can follow how one material travelled around the world. Then you can see how the craftspeople here learned from all of it.

Watch for one more effect in the galleries. Sèvres became known for biscuit figures, which are porcelain sculptures left unglazed and unpainted. Their matte white surface looks almost like marble. Biscuit is unforgiving, because any small flaw is visible. Under good light, these figures look soft and close to living skin. Compare them with the shining glazed vases nearby, and you can see two very different ideas of beauty from the same clay.

Before you leave, take a slow look around you. The Seine runs past just beyond the road, and the trees of Saint-Cloud rise behind the roofs. Across the bridge, the modern towers of Boulogne-Billancourt stand against the sky. Somewhere inside these walls, a brush is perhaps adding a thin line of gold to a white plate. As you walk back toward the river, remember that quiet work. It has continued here, in one form or another, for a very long time.
