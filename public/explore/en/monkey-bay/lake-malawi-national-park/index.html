The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves fold onto the sand, soft and steady, more like a sea than a lake. The water is so clear near the shore that you can see the pale granite boulders below the surface. Out on the bay, a dugout canoe moves slowly, one paddler, no engine. Somewhere above the trees, a fish eagle gives its high, ringing call. This is Lake Malawi National Park, at the southern end of one of the great lakes of Africa.

The park sits on and around the Nankumba Peninsula, a finger of hills and woodland that pushes north into the lake, not far from Monkey Bay. It covers dry land, a stretch of the lake itself, and a scatter of rocky islands offshore. Malawi created the park in 1980. Just a few years later, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage list. It was the first site on that list protected mainly for freshwater life.

So what is being protected here? The answer is easy to miss from the beach. It is the fish. Lake Malawi holds hundreds of species of cichlids, a family of small, brightly coloured fish. Scientists believe most of them live nowhere else on Earth. Many of them evolved right here, in this one body of water. Biologists often compare these fish to the finches of the Galápagos, which helped Charles Darwin develop his ideas about evolution.

Put on a mask and float for a few minutes near the rocks, and you will see why. Locals call many of the rock-dwelling cichlids mbuna. They come in electric blue, yellow, orange, and striped patterns. Some species scrape algae from the stones. Others pick at tiny insects or guard a small patch of rock with surprising aggression. Each rocky outcrop can hold its own local forms. A fish from one island may look different from its cousin on the next.

Here is a second detail that surprises many visitors. Many cichlids in this lake are mouthbrooders. The mother carries her eggs, and later her young fry, inside her mouth to protect them. When danger comes close, the tiny fish swim back into her mouth for safety. If you are patient and very still in the water, you may notice a parent fish hovering with a cloud of young around her head.

Now look up from the water to the shore. This part of the coast is called Cape Maclear. The name has a story. The Scottish explorer David Livingstone travelled on the lake in the mid-nineteenth century. He named this cape after his friend Thomas Maclear, an astronomer who worked in South Africa. A popular tale also says that Livingstone called the lake the lake of stars, after the lamps of night fishermen on the water.

Livingstone's visits led to something more lasting. In the 1870s, Scottish missionaries founded a mission here at Cape Maclear and named it Livingstonia. Life was hard. Malaria and other diseases took a heavy toll, and the mission soon moved further north to a healthier site. A few graves of those early missionaries remain near the shore. They are quiet and easy to walk past. They show how difficult this coast was for newcomers from cold, northern countries.

The park is not empty wilderness. People live here, and they have lived here for generations. Villages such as Chembe sit inside the park area, and fishing remains the centre of daily life. Early in the morning, you may see men pulling nets onto the beach. Later in the day, look for wooden racks covered in tiny silver fish, drying in the sun. These small fish, called usipa, are an everyday food across much of Malawi.

The land behind the beaches deserves attention too. The hills are covered in dry woodland, with huge rounded boulders and old baobab trees. A baobab is the wide, thick-trunked tree that stores water in its wood. In the dry season, many baobabs lose their leaves and their bare branches look like roots reaching into the sky. Among these trees, you may see monkeys, birds of prey, and lizards warming themselves on the warm rock.

Out on the water, the islands of the park sit like low green humps. Thumbi West Island lies close to Cape Maclear and is a common destination for snorkelling trips. Further out are other islands, including Mumbo and Domwe. Their rocky shores drop into deep, clear water, which makes them good places to see the colourful fish. Boat trips usually leave from the main beach, and local guides know where the fish gather.

One of the best sights here costs nothing but patience. Watch for the African fish eagle. It has a white head, a dark brown body, and chestnut wings. Local boatmen sometimes throw a fish onto the water to call one down. The eagle drops from a tree, stretches its yellow feet forward, and snatches the fish from the surface in one smooth movement. Then it beats its wings and returns to its perch.

A practical word about the water. Like many freshwater lakes in tropical Africa, parts of Lake Malawi can carry bilharzia, a parasite that lives in shallow, still water near reeds and villages. Many visitors still swim and snorkel, especially in deeper water away from the shore. It is wise to ask local guides about the safest spots. It is also wise to speak to a doctor about testing after your trip.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the edge of the water. Notice the colour of the lake, which changes from pale green near the sand to deep blue further out. Notice the canoes, the drying racks, the children playing in the shallows. Somewhere under the surface, small bright fish are guarding their rocks, as they have done for a very long time. As you walk away, listen once more for the fish eagle's call.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring your own mask and snorkel for the clearest views.
Snorkel near rocky shores where the cichlids gather.
Ask local guides which swimming spots carry lower bilharzia risk.
Book an island boat trip from the main Cape Maclear beach.
Come early morning to watch fishermen pull in their nets.
