The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The river Scheldt is wide here, and the wind comes off the water with a faint smell of salt, diesel, and wet stone. Gulls call above the quay. Under your feet, the ground has been paved, raised, and paved again over many centuries. In front of you stands a small, stubborn castle of grey stone. It looks almost too small for the city behind it. That contrast is the first thing to notice about Het Steen.

The name simply means 'The Stone'. For a long time, a stone building in this low, muddy river landscape was rare enough to deserve that plain title. Most houses were wood and thatch. A building of solid masonry meant power, permanence, and protection. Het Steen is widely described as the oldest building in Antwerp. What you see today, though, is only a fragment of something much larger.

Picture this riverbank around a thousand years ago. There was no grand port yet, only a fortified mound beside the water. Over time, a stronghold grew here, and it became the seat of a local lord who watched over the river. The core of the present gatehouse dates to the early Middle Ages, often placed in the early thirteenth century. Around it once stood walls, houses, a church, and narrow lanes. All of that formed a small fortified settlement — the seed of the city.

Now look closely at the walls. The stone is not uniform. Darker, rougher blocks sit beside smoother, paler ones. This is because the castle has been rebuilt many times. In the sixteenth century, under Emperor Charles V, it received a major reconstruction. Charles ruled a vast empire across Europe, and Antwerp was then one of the richest trading cities in the world. Later, in the nineteenth century, restorers added towers and details in a romantic medieval style. So the castle you see is a mix of real medieval fabric and later imagination.

Here is something many visitors miss. For centuries, Het Steen was not a palace or a museum. It was a prison. Behind these thick walls, people waited for trial, for punishment, and sometimes for execution. During the religious conflicts of the sixteenth century, Protestants and others accused of heresy were held in the region's prisons, and this building carries that dark memory. Its use as a prison ended in the early nineteenth century. When you hear the gulls now, imagine how different this place sounded to someone locked inside.

Walk toward the main gate and raise your eyes. Above the entrance, you will find a small, worn stone relief of a figure. Locals tell a story about it. According to one popular tale, the figure represents Semini, an old pagan fertility god, and young women once came here to pray for children. The tale also says that later clergy damaged the carving because they found it improper. Historians treat much of this as folklore. Still, the battered little figure has survived, and it gives the gate a quiet, human strangeness.

Near the entrance stands a large bronze statue of a giant looming over two small men. This is Lange Wapper, a figure from Antwerp folklore. Legend has it that he could grow tall or shrink at will. He liked to frighten people who stumbled home drunk at night, and he played tricks on the townsfolk. Yet in some versions of the story he also cared for children. The statue dates from the twentieth century. Children often stop here first, and it is easy to see why.

There is a second giant in Antwerp's stories, and he explains the city's name — or so the legend goes. A cruel giant called Druon Antigoon is said to have lived by the river. He demanded a toll from every passing ship. Anyone who refused lost a hand. Then a brave young Roman soldier named Brabo defeated him, cut off the giant's own hand, and threw it into the Scheldt. 'Hand werpen' means 'to throw a hand'. Linguists prefer a duller explanation, linked to a raised mound of land. But the city clearly loves the legend more.

Turn around and look at the empty space along the quay. That openness is also part of the story. In the late nineteenth century, Antwerp straightened and rebuilt its riverbank to handle bigger ships and growing trade. The old medieval quarter around the castle was largely demolished. Streets, houses, and much of the fortress vanished. Het Steen survived because it was valued as a monument. So when you stand here, you are standing where a whole neighbourhood once stood, now replaced by wind and open stone.

In the twentieth century, the castle found a new life. For decades it housed a maritime museum, full of ship models, maps, and objects from Antwerp's long relationship with the sea. That collection later moved to the MAS, the large red stone museum tower further north along the docks, which opened in 2011. If you look up the river, you may see its stacked shape on the skyline. The old castle and the new museum make an interesting pair — one low and grey, one tall and red.

In recent years, Het Steen has been renovated and opened again as a welcome point for visitors. Inside, you can find tourist information and an exhibition that tells the story of Antwerp. A modern extension now connects the old stone with the new port of arrival for river cruise passengers. Take a moment to compare the materials. Clean modern surfaces meet medieval masonry. The contrast is deliberate. It shows the building's long habit of changing purpose while keeping its old shell.

If you climb to the higher level of the site, the view opens across the Scheldt. On a clear day, the light on the water shifts from silver to brown to grey within minutes. Barges and cargo ships pass slowly, often closer than you expect. Across the river lies the left bank, flatter and greener. Behind you, the spire of the Cathedral of Our Lady rises above the rooftops of the old town. From here, you can see how the city grew outward from this one small rock of power.

Before you leave, rest your hand on the stone for a second. It is cool, rough, and slightly damp, even on a warm day. Prisoners, soldiers, sailors, museum keepers, and many travellers have touched these walls before you. Now, as you walk back toward the Grote Markt, listen again. The gulls are still calling. The river is still moving. And the small grey castle stays where it has always been — at the place where Antwerp first began.
