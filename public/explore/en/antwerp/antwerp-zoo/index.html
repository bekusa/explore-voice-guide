The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, trains brake and hiss under the great roof of Antwerp's central station. In front of you, under old trees, a bird calls from somewhere you cannot quite see. Few places put a railway terminus and a zoo this close together. Here they share a wall. You can step off an international train and, a few minutes later, stand on a gravel path beside flamingos. That closeness is the first surprise of Antwerp Zoo, and it says a lot about how this city grew.

The zoo opened in 1843, which makes it one of the oldest zoos in the world still working on its original site. It was founded by a society of Antwerp citizens, and the naturalist Jacques Kets became its first director. In those early decades it was meant as much for study as for entertainment. Animals, plants and collections were gathered together so that people could learn about the natural world. That idea of a garden of knowledge still shapes the grounds today.

Notice the scale of the place. The zoo covers only about ten hectares, a compact space by modern standards. Yet the paths curve and fold so that you rarely see the whole of it at once. Tall trees, many of them old, close over the walkways and keep the light soft and green. The city is right outside the gates, but inside, the noise of traffic fades quickly. Instead you hear gravel underfoot, children's voices, and the calls of birds.

The railway came after the zoo, not before. When the station was built at the end of the nineteenth century, it rose beside a zoo that was already decades old. So the two grew up as neighbours. For generations of Antwerp families, a trip to the zoo began with a train ride. The station and the zoo share the same era of civic pride, when a busy port city wanted grand public places to show its confidence and wealth.

That ambition shows clearly in the buildings. Antwerp Zoo is not only a collection of animals. It is also a collection of nineteenth-century architecture, and the site is protected as heritage. Many of the old animal houses were designed in styles borrowed from distant lands. Their builders wanted visitors to feel they were travelling to the places the animals came from. Today we might see this as a European fantasy of faraway countries. But it tells you how people in that time imagined the wider world.

The most famous example is the Egyptian Temple. It was built in the 1850s, and it was first made to house elephants and giraffes. Look at its heavy columns, its painted surfaces and the rows of carved figures that copy the look of ancient Egyptian monuments. It is a building made to impress, and it still does. The elephants no longer live here. Antwerp's elephants now have far more room at Planckendael, the society's larger animal park outside the city.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Antwerp Zoo has a long connection with the okapi, a shy forest animal from Central Africa. It looks like a small horse with zebra stripes on its legs, but its closest relative is the giraffe. In 1919, Antwerp received the first living okapi ever shown in Europe. Belgium's colonial past in the Congo explains why it arrived here first. Today the zoo is known for work on okapi breeding and conservation, a quieter and more hopeful chapter of that story.

That colonial link deserves a moment of honesty. Like many European zoos of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, this one grew partly through empire, trade and the shipping that passed through Antwerp's port. Animals came by sea, and so did ideas about other peoples. Modern zoos, including this one, now speak much more about conservation, research and breeding programmes for endangered species. The old buildings remain, but the purpose behind them has changed a great deal.

As you walk, look beyond the enclosures. The zoo has long collected art, and you will find animal sculptures placed along the paths and in the gardens. Some are in bronze, some in stone. They catch the light differently through the day, and children often stop to touch them. These statues are a reminder that the zoo was once a place for artists too. Painters and sculptors came here to study animals they could not see anywhere else in the country.

The zoo is also a place for music and gatherings. Within the grounds stands the Queen Elisabeth Hall, a concert venue connected to the zoo's long tradition of cultural events. In the nineteenth century, members of the society came to the zoo for concerts as well as for the animals. A Sunday visit could mean a walk past the lions, a coffee, and then an evening of music. That mix of leisure and culture was part of city life in Antwerp.

Today the animal areas range from the historic to the modern. You will find an aquarium, houses for reptiles and smaller creatures, and newer spaces designed with more room and more natural surroundings. Some animals are easy to see. Others take patience. In darker houses, give your eyes a minute to adjust before you move on. Many visitors walk straight past a sleeping animal tucked into a corner, simply because they did not wait long enough.

Pay attention to the plants as well. Old trees, flower beds and greenhouse spaces are part of the zoo's design, not just decoration. In spring the grounds fill with fresh green. In autumn the paths collect fallen leaves, and the smell of damp earth mixes with the smell of the animal houses. The gardens make this a quieter place than you might expect so close to a major station, and they give the old buildings a softer setting.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute near one of the older buildings. Look at the columns, the carved details, the way the paint has been renewed again and again over many decades. Then listen. The trains are still there, just beyond the wall. The birds are still calling from the trees. As you walk back toward the station, you are following the same short route that Antwerp families have taken for generations... from the city, into a garden, and home again.
