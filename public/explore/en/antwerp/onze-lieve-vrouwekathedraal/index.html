The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the cobbles and tilt your head back. The white stone of the north tower climbs and climbs, thinning into pointed arches and pinnacles until it looks almost woven. Around you there is the clatter of café chairs, bicycle bells, and the low murmur of the old town. Then, every quarter hour, the bells cut through it all. This is the Cathedral of Our Lady, and for centuries it has set the rhythm of Antwerp.

The church you see took a very long time to build. Work began in the middle of the fourteenth century, and the builders did not finish until the early sixteenth century. That is roughly 170 years of masons, carpenters and glaziers. Several generations of craftsmen worked on this site. Many of them never saw the finished building. It became a cathedral, the seat of a bishop, only in 1559, long after the first stones were laid.

The style is Gothic. In simple terms, that means pointed arches, tall windows and stone ribs that carry the weight of the roof. Gothic builders wanted light and height, and they wanted the eye pulled upward. Here, the effect works best on the north tower. It rises to about 123 metres, and it is often described as the tallest church tower in the Low Countries. On a clear day, its tip is visible from far outside the city.

Now look at the other tower, to the right as you face the main doors. It stops abruptly, well short of its partner, with a modest cap on top. This is the first surprise most visitors miss. The cathedral was meant to have two equal spires. Money, fire and changing priorities ended that plan. So the building has stood lopsided for about five hundred years, and the people of Antwerp have simply accepted it.

The delicate stonework of the north tower has inspired many stories. A popular tale says that the Emperor Charles the Fifth admired the spire so much that he wanted it kept under glass. Another legend says that Napoleon compared it to fine Mechelen lace. Nobody can prove that either man said these words. Still, the comparison makes sense when you look up. The stone is cut so finely that the upper levels seem almost transparent against the sky.

The history of the interior is less peaceful. In 1533, a serious fire damaged the church. Then, in 1566, a wave of Protestant image-breaking swept through the Low Countries. Crowds entered churches and smashed statues, altars and paintings that they saw as idolatry. This cathedral lost much of its medieval decoration in that storm. Later, at the end of the eighteenth century, French revolutionary forces looted the building again. At one point, it was even in danger of being torn down.

The cathedral survived, and the city refilled it with art. The most famous works are by Peter Paul Rubens, the great Flemish painter who lived and worked in Antwerp. His home and studio stand only a short walk from here. Inside, you can see two huge altarpieces by Rubens: The Raising of the Cross and The Descent from the Cross. They are full of muscle, strain and dramatic light. Seeing them in the space they were made for is very different from seeing them in a book.

Here is a second surprise. Some of these paintings left Antwerp for years. During the French occupation, they were taken to Paris. They came back only after the fall of Napoleon. So the canvases you see today have travelled across a continent and returned home. It is worth remembering this when you stand in front of them. Their presence here is the result of loss, recovery and a city that refused to let them go.

The interior also surprises people with its width. The nave, the long central space, has several rows of aisles on each side, so the columns stand in deep lines that seem to multiply as you walk. Light enters through tall windows and falls across pale stone. Your footsteps echo. Sound behaves differently in here. Voices soften, and even a large group of visitors seems to grow quieter once the doors close behind them.

Step back outside and look at the square directly in front of the main entrance. This is the Handschoenmarkt, the old glove market. Near the front of the cathedral is a decorative iron well with a canopy. Local tradition links it to Quinten Matsijs. According to legend, he began as a blacksmith and became a painter because he fell in love with a painter's daughter. It is a romantic story, and Antwerp has told it for a very long time.

The cathedral also appears in a Victorian novel called A Dog of Flanders. It tells the story of a poor boy named Nello and his loyal dog, Patrasche. Nello dreams of seeing the Rubens paintings inside this church. The book is especially loved in Japan, and many Japanese visitors come here because of it. In the square nearby, look for a memorial to the boy and his dog. It is a small, gentle detail in the shadow of the huge façade.

The tower is also part of a group of belfries in Belgium and France that are listed by UNESCO, the United Nations cultural agency. Belfries were symbols of civic pride and freedom in medieval towns. They carried bells that told people when to work, trade and gather. The carillon in the north tower still plays. A carillon is a set of tuned bells played from a keyboard. If you hear a melody floating over the rooftops, it is coming from up there.

Before you move on, find a spot slightly back from the façade — perhaps the corner of a café terrace, or the edge of the Groenplaats square to the south. From there, you can see both towers at once: one complete, one unfinished. Notice the pigeons on the ledges, the late light on the stone, and the narrow streets that lead away like spokes. When the bells ring again, you will hear the same sound that Antwerp has heard for centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Buy a separate ticket to see the interior artworks.
Check for Mass times before visiting, as access may be limited.
View both towers from the edge of Groenplaats.
Visit Rubens House nearby to see where the painter worked.
Listen for the carillon from a quiet café terrace.
