The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Leaves move in the old trees overhead. Gulls call somewhere above Clifton. Underfoot there may be gravel, or soft lawn, or the worn edge of a Victorian path. This is a place that held the sound of animals for nearly two centuries. Now it is learning a new kind of quiet.

Bristol Zoo began here, in Clifton, in the 1830s. A group of local citizens formed the Bristol Zoological Society, and the gardens opened to visitors in 1836. That makes it one of the oldest zoos anywhere in the world. When the gates first opened, Queen Victoria had not yet come to the throne. Think about that as you look around. These paths are older than most of the city's railways.

The founders wanted more than a menagerie, which means a private collection of exotic animals kept for show. They wanted a garden for learning. The early zoo mixed rare animals with careful planting, and this was a setting for science as much as entertainment. That balance shaped the site for generations. Even today the trees and flower beds feel as deliberate as anything an animal ever lived in.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. If you have come hoping to see lions or gorillas on this spot, they are no longer here. The Clifton site closed to the public in 2022, after a very long run. The animals did not vanish, though. They moved to a much larger site on the northern edge of Bristol, now called the Bristol Zoo Project.

Why leave such a loved place? The answer is space. The Clifton gardens are compact, wedged between the streets and the open grass of the Downs. Modern thinking about animal welfare asks for room to roam, to hide, and to behave naturally. The Society decided that its future lay in wider landscapes, with fewer enclosures and more open habitat. Clifton could not offer that.

For many Bristolians, the closure felt personal. Generations of families came here on Sunday afternoons. Grandparents remember feeding time and school trips. Parents remember first visits in pushchairs. Locals still talk about the zoo in the present tense, as if it might open its doors tomorrow. That loyalty tells you something about how deeply a place like this settles into a city.

One animal above all became a Bristol legend. His name was Alfred, a western lowland gorilla who arrived in the 1930s. He became a celebrity, and crowds came to watch him. Stories about him travelled far beyond the city. After his death in the 1940s, Alfred was preserved, and he went on display at Bristol Museum and Art Gallery. Many locals still visit him there, which is a surprise for most newcomers.

The zoo also has a place in British television history. The much-loved BBC children's programme Animal Magic was filmed here for many years. Its presenter, Johnny Morris, played a keeper and gave the animals funny voices. For a whole generation of children across the country, this garden was their window onto the animal world. Bristol has long been a centre for natural history filmmaking, and that link is part of the reason.

Behind the fun, the Society built a serious reputation. It took part in breeding programmes for endangered species and supported conservation work in the wild. Its scientists studied animals in places far from Bristol. That work did not stop when the Clifton gates closed. It continues today at the newer site, and through projects that the Society runs abroad.

So what happens to this land now? The plan is a new neighbourhood, with homes built across part of the site. A large share of the historic gardens is meant to remain as open green space for the public. Some older buildings and planted areas are intended to stay as well. Plans like these can change, so check what is open before you explore. But the idea is that the garden survives, even as the animals live elsewhere.

Take a look at the wider setting while you are here. Clifton is one of Bristol's grandest districts, full of tall Georgian terraces in pale stone. The Downs stretch out nearby, wide and windy, with dog walkers and kite flyers. A short walk away, the Clifton Suspension Bridge crosses the Avon Gorge. The zoo was always part of this landscape of fresh air and long views.

If you want to see the animals themselves, head north to the Bristol Zoo Project. It sits in open countryside close to the motorway, and it feels very different from these gardens. There is more grass, more sky, and much more distance between you and the animals. Some visitors miss the closeness of the old zoo. Others enjoy seeing animals in larger, wilder spaces.

Standing here, you are in the middle of a story that is still being written. For almost two hundred years, this ground held cages, ponds, gardens, and the voices of millions of visitors. Now it is turning into something new, and also something older: a green space in the city for people to walk through. Before you go, look up at the trees once more. Many of them grew while the zoo was busy around them. Listen again to the birds, the traffic on the Clifton streets, the wind from the Downs. The animals have moved on. But this garden still carries their story... and perhaps a little of yours, too.
