The guide

The story

Picture wet sand under your boots, cold and ribbed by the last tide. The wind comes off the Wash with a smell of salt and seaweed. Along this stretch of the Norfolk coast, a few kilometres east of Hunstanton, the beach at Holme-next-the-Sea runs flat and wide. In 1998 the sea uncovered something here that had stayed hidden for about four thousand years. It was a ring of oak posts, dark and glistening, standing in the sand.

People soon called it Seahenge. The name is a little misleading. There are no great stones here, and the circle was never built beside the sea. Its builders raised it on salt marsh, some distance back from the open water. Over the centuries peat formed over the timbers and sealed them away from the air. Slowly the coastline moved inland. Eventually the waves stripped the peat away and exposed the wood on the open beach.

So what did people actually find? The circle had fifty-five oak posts, set close together like a fence. Most had been split, with the bark still facing outward. At the centre was the strangest part. A great oak stump stood upside down, its roots spread toward the sky like a giant open hand. No one had seen a monument quite like it. Archaeologists and the public quickly understood that this was something unusual.

The date of the timbers is the first surprise. Scientists used tree rings, a method called dendrochronology, to study the oak. Each year a tree adds a ring, and the pattern of thick and thin rings works like a fingerprint. The rings showed that the trees were cut down in the spring or early summer of 2049 BC. That is a single season, more than four thousand years ago. Few prehistoric monuments can be dated so precisely.

That date places Seahenge in the early Bronze Age, the period when people in Britain first used metal tools. The wood itself carries evidence of this. Specialists studied the cut marks on the posts and found the traces of many different bronze axes. Each axe blade leaves its own small pattern of nicks. The study suggested that dozens of people worked on the circle together. This was a community project, not the work of one family.

Why did they build it? Here we must be honest. Nobody knows for certain. One popular idea links the circle to the dead. In this theory, a body was laid on the upturned stump and left open to the sky and the birds. The fence of posts would have screened the space from view. It is a strong idea, but it is still an interpretation. No human remains were found at the site.

The stump has another curious detail. Upside down, its roots became a kind of platform or altar. Turning a whole tree over must have taken great effort and careful planning. People sometimes see a deliberate symbol in this act — the world below raised up into the world above. That reading is modern, of course. Still, as you stand on this beach, it helps to imagine the effort of those people working in the marsh.

The discovery soon led to an argument. The sea was eroding the timbers quickly, so archaeologists decided to remove them in 1999. Many people disagreed. Modern pagans and druids came to the beach in protest, and some local people also wanted the circle to stay in place. They believed it belonged to this coast. The debate became national news. In the end the excavation went ahead, and the posts and the central stump were lifted out.

After the excavation the wood needed years of care. Ancient waterlogged oak is soft and fragile, and it cracks as it dries. The timbers were first studied at Flag Fen, a Bronze Age site near Peterborough. Later they went to specialists in Portsmouth, where the team that conserved the Tudor warship Mary Rose worked on them. The wood was treated with a wax-like solution that slowly replaced the water inside the cells.

This leads to the second surprise, and it matters for your visit. The original circle is no longer on this beach. Today the preserved timbers are on display at the Lynn Museum in King's Lynn, about half an hour's drive south. There you can see the real posts and the great stump, along with a full-size reconstruction. You can also walk inside the circle. Many visitors come to Holme expecting to find the timbers in the sand.

The sea had one more secret. Close to the first circle, archaeologists recorded a second timber ring, often called Holme Two. It was a little larger and had a different design, with logs laid around a central pit. Studies suggest the two circles were built in the same period. This second monument was left where it was found. Over time the sand and the tides have covered it and uncovered it again.

The landscape here is part of the story. Today this shore is a nature reserve, with dunes, pine trees and wide sand flats. Wading birds feed along the waterline, and the tide goes out a very long way. Back in Hunstanton you can see the famous striped cliffs, with bands of red and white chalk. The town faces west across the Wash, so the sun sets over the sea here. That is unusual for the east coast of England.

Now look around you once more. Notice the flat, shining sand, the line of the dunes, and the low grey sky over the water. Somewhere under this shore, wood cut by Bronze Age hands may still lie in the dark. When you walk back across the beach, watch the patterns the tide leaves behind. This coast keeps changing. Every tide moves the sand a little, and one day it may uncover something else.
