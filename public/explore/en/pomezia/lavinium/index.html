The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, south of Rome, the city noise falls away. You may hear wind moving through tall grass, a few birds, perhaps the distant drone of an aircraft. The ground underfoot is soft, sandy earth, the kind that sits close to the sea. Low stone shapes rise from it in a line. They look modest at first. But this quiet patch of countryside is one of the places Romans believed their whole story began.

This is Lavinium, an ancient Latin town. Its remains lie around the small village of Pratica di Mare, within the modern town of Pomezia. Rome itself is a short drive to the north. For the Romans, though, the distance was never about kilometres. Lavinium was their sacred starting point. It was the place they returned to, again and again, to honour gods older than Rome itself.

To understand why, we need the legend. According to the story told by the poet Virgil and the historian Livy, the Trojan hero Aeneas escaped the burning city of Troy. He sailed across the Mediterranean with his family and his household gods. After long wandering, he landed on this coast of Latium, the region around Rome. Here he married Lavinia, daughter of the local king Latinus. He then founded a town and named it after her.

That is legend, not documented history. But the legend mattered enormously. Roman families, and later emperors, traced their ancestry back to Aeneas. Julius Caesar's family claimed descent from his son. So Lavinium was more than a ruin to the Romans. It was a family origin, a national origin, and a religious one. Ancient writers also say the Penates were kept here. These were the protective household gods Aeneas carried from Troy.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. For centuries, the highest officials of Rome travelled here in person. Ancient sources describe consuls and other magistrates coming to Lavinium to offer sacrifices to the Penates and to Vesta, the goddess of the hearth. Even when Rome ruled a vast empire, its leaders still made this journey to a small Latin town. In Roman times, the people of Lavinium were known as the Laurentes Lavinates, and they kept these old rites alive.

Now look at those stone shapes in a row. This is the sanctuary archaeologists call the Thirteen Altars. These are large stone altars, set side by side, facing roughly the same direction. They were built over a long period, with the earliest dating to around the sixth century before Christ. Not all of them stood at the same time. Some were added, some rebuilt, some went out of use. Together they form one of the most remarkable groups of early altars in all of Italy.

Take a moment to picture the scene here. Smoke would rise from offerings. Priests would move between the altars. The stone surfaces you see were once worked, painted, and handled by worshippers. Today they are weathered and pale, softened by wind and rain. Yet their shapes are still clear. You can trace the steps, the bases, and the carved mouldings along their edges.

The second surprise lies close by. Archaeologists found a burial mound here, an early tomb dating back to roughly the seventh century before Christ. Later, perhaps several centuries after the burial, the tomb was rebuilt and given a monumental entrance. The Greek historian Dionysius of Halicarnassus, writing in the time of Augustus, described a shrine near Lavinium honouring Aeneas. Many scholars believe this rebuilt tomb is that shrine, a memorial to the founding hero.

We should be careful here. No one claims that Aeneas was actually buried in this ground. The point is something else. Ancient people looked at an old tomb and decided it belonged to their founder. They then honoured it for generations. In a sense, you are standing at the meeting point of archaeology and belief. The stones are real. The story the Romans attached to them is a legend they chose to keep.

Serious excavation here began in the twentieth century, led by scholars from Rome. Their work changed how historians understand early Latium. Before, much of this story lived only in ancient texts. After the digs, there were altars, a tomb, and thousands of objects to study. Lavinium became evidence that the early Latin world had rich sanctuaries, strong links to the Greek world, and a lively religious life long before Rome became powerful.

Many of the finest finds are now displayed in the local archaeological museum in Pratica di Mare. The most memorable are terracotta statues from a sanctuary of Minerva, the goddess of wisdom. Rows of figures, made of fired clay, show worshippers and offerings. Some are nearly life size. The faces, the hairstyles, and the folds of clothing give a close look at the people of this region. If you have time, seeing the museum and the site together makes the story complete.

The village of Pratica di Mare itself stands on the higher ground of the ancient town. Its old centre is small, with a large palace that belonged to the Borghese family, a powerful Roman noble family. The village feels calm and compact. Around it you will see farmland, newer housing, and the open coastal plain. It is worth walking slowly. The modern buildings sit above layers of much older life, most of it still underground.

As you prepare to leave, look once more along the line of altars. Notice how simple they appear against the sky. There are no golden domes here, no towering columns. There is only stone, earth, and open air. Yet for more than a thousand years, people walked this ground with a sense that it was the beginning of something. Listen again to the wind in the grass, and carry that quiet with you back toward Rome.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the archaeological museum in Pratica di Mare alongside the site.
Check visiting arrangements in advance; site access can be limited.
Wear sturdy shoes for sandy, uneven ground around the altars.
Bring water and sun protection; the coastal plain has little shade.
Read Virgil's Aeneid opening books before visiting for context.
