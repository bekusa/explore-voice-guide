The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through palms and pine. Somewhere below, a boat engine hums across the water. Underfoot, the path is warm stone, and the air carries the dry, sweet smell of Galilee hillsides in the sun. You are standing on a slope above the Sea of Galilee, on the hill that Christian tradition calls the Mount of Beatitudes.

A quick note on where you are. Many visitors use Safed as their base, up in the high hills of the Upper Galilee. The Church of the Beatitudes is not in Safed itself. It sits lower down, above the northwestern shore of the lake, a drive down from Safed's cool, mountain streets. The change in height is part of the experience. You leave thin mountain air behind and arrive in the softer warmth of the lakeshore.

The name comes from a famous passage in the Gospel of Matthew. In it, Jesus speaks to a crowd from a hillside. He begins with a series of blessings — blessed are the poor in spirit, blessed are the meek, blessed are the peacemakers. These short lines are called the Beatitudes, from a Latin word meaning happiness or blessedness. The longer speech they open is known as the Sermon on the Mount.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not realise. No one knows for certain which hill the Gospel describes. This spot is a tradition, not an archaeological proof. Pilgrims have honoured hillsides along this shore since early Christian times, and the remains of an older Byzantine-era church were found further down the slope. The present church was placed here because the setting fits the story so well.

Now look at the building itself. It has eight sides. That is not an accident. The architect shaped the church as an octagon so that each side would stand for one of the eight Beatitudes. Walk slowly around the outside, and you are, in a sense, walking through the sermon line by line. The plan is simple, but once you know the idea, the shape of the whole building makes sense.

The architect was Antonio Barluzzi, an Italian who designed many of the Holy Land's Catholic churches in the first half of the twentieth century. His buildings often try to express the event they remember. Here he wanted calm and openness, not drama. The church was completed in 1938. Its construction received support from the Italian state of that era — an uncomfortable detail of politics, now mostly forgotten under the quiet of the gardens.

Notice the materials. Barluzzi used local black basalt, the dark volcanic stone that you see in old walls all around the Sea of Galilee. He paired it with pale limestone and light travertine, a cream-coloured stone common in Italian buildings. The contrast is the point. Dark and light stone sit side by side, and the building looks both rooted in this landscape and touched by something from Rome.

A covered arcade runs around the church. Stand in its shade for a moment. The columns frame the lake like a row of windows, and each opening shows a slightly different view of the water, the far shore, and the hills beyond. Barluzzi used this kind of walkway so that people could pause, rest, and look outward. The arcade is a place for slowing down, not just a way to reach the door.

Step inside, quietly. The interior is small and bright. Look up into the dome, where gold mosaic catches the light. High in the walls, the windows carry the words of the Beatitudes in Latin, one blessing for each side. Around the central altar, the floor holds mosaic symbols of the virtues. Most visitors look up and never look down — take a moment to do both.

This is still a living place of prayer. Franciscan sisters care for the church and the grounds, and groups often hold short services in the gardens. You may hear a hymn in Korean, a reading in Spanish, a prayer in Polish, all within the same hour. The church has also welcomed popes. Pope Paul VI came to this area in 1964, and Pope John Paul II celebrated a large open-air Mass on this hillside in 2000.

Walk out into the gardens. They fall gently toward the water in terraces of flowers, shrubs, and trees. Small outdoor chapels and benches sit among them, where groups gather in a circle to read and sing. Even on busy days, it is usually possible to find a quieter corner. Sit for a minute. The slope is open to the lake, and the sound of voices fades quickly into the breeze.

Here is a second surprise. Some people believe a hillside like this works as a natural amphitheatre — a curved slope where a speaker's voice can carry down to a large crowd. Whether or not the sermon happened on this exact hill, you can test the idea yourself. Listen to the guides and readers on the slope below. Notice how far a calm, clear voice travels through the open air.

From the terraces, look down the shore. Close by are Tabgha, where tradition places the miracle of the loaves and fishes, and Capernaum, the lakeside town where the Gospels say Jesus lived and taught. Many visitors join these sites in one morning. Seen from here, the whole story lies within a few kilometres, a small stage of fishing villages, hills, and water.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the gardens once more. Watch the colour of the lake change as clouds move over it. Listen for the wind in the palms and the faint engine of a boat crossing the water. The church behind you is less than a century old. The view in front of you is much older. Let both stay with you as you walk back up the path.
