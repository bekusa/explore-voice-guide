The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the road, the upper Jordan runs fast and cold, pushing through reeds and over dark stones. The water here is green, not the wide blue you may picture from paintings of the Holy Land. Eucalyptus leaves move in the wind. Somewhere a truck changes gear as it climbs toward the Golan Heights. Welcome to the Daughters of Jacob Bridge, a crossing point that people have used for longer than almost anywhere else on Earth.

The bridge sits a short drive east of Safed, where the Galilee hills fall away into the Jordan Valley. To the north lies the Hula Valley. To the south, the river runs on toward the Sea of Galilee. Across the water, the land rises in black basalt toward the Golan. This narrow point in the river is the reason everything else here happened. For thousands of years, if you wanted to travel between Damascus and the Mediterranean coast, you often crossed right here.

Start with the deepest layer, because it is the most surprising. Along these banks, archaeologists excavated one of the most important prehistoric sites in the region. The finds are close to 800,000 years old. Early humans worked stone hand axes here, often from the same basalt you see around you. Researchers also found burned flint, wood, and seeds. Many scientists read these traces as some of the earliest evidence of humans controlling fire. In other words, people may have gathered around flames on this riverbank long before modern humans existed.

The site also kept remains of plants, fruits, and fish from that distant time. Waterlogged mud preserved what would normally rot away. So researchers could ask unusual questions. What did these early people eat? Which nuts did they crack? How did they use the lake that once spread across the valley? When you look at the muddy banks today, remember that the same kind of ground protected a picture of daily life from almost a million years ago.

Now to the name. In Hebrew, the place is called Gesher Bnot Ya'akov, which means the Bridge of the Daughters of Jacob. Nobody can say with full certainty where the name comes from. According to one popular tradition, the biblical patriarch Jacob crossed the river near here on his journeys. Another explanation links the name to a medieval religious community connected with Saint James, whose name is Jacob in many languages. Treat all of these as tradition, not proven history.

The medieval story is better documented, and it is dramatic. In the late 1170s, the Crusader king Baldwin the Fourth of Jerusalem decided to fortify this crossing. The Crusaders were Christian knights from Europe who ruled parts of this land at the time. They called the place Jacob's Ford. Here they began a strong castle that later writers call Chastellet. The Knights Templar, a military religious order, took charge of it. The castle faced directly toward Damascus, the power base of their great rival.

That rival was Saladin, the Muslim sultan who ruled Egypt and Syria. He understood the threat at once. A fortress at this ford could control traffic across the Jordan. In 1179, before the walls were finished, his army attacked. His men dug under the stones and set fires to weaken them. The defenders could not hold. The castle fell, and many of its garrison died. The whole project lasted only around a year. Ambition and ruin came almost together here.

Here is the second surprise. Modern excavations of the castle site found remains of that siege. Archaeologists recovered arrowheads, tools, and the skeletons of people who died in the fighting. Some of the bones show wounds from weapons. The workers had left building materials where they stood, as if construction had stopped in a single day. For historians, this is rare. A battle that is usually known from chronicles can also be studied in the ground, almost frozen at the moment it happened.

After the Crusader period, the crossing stayed important. Later rulers built a stone bridge with arches, using the local dark basalt. Caravans of merchants, pilgrims, and soldiers used it for centuries. An inn for travellers stood nearby, a place where people and animals could rest after the river. The road here was a working road, not a scenic one. Imagine camels, mules, and dust, and the constant sound of water under the arches.

The twentieth century brought new conflict. In June 1946, during the British Mandate period, Jewish underground fighters of the Palmach blew up bridges across the country in a single coordinated operation. This crossing was one of the targets. The event is remembered as the Night of the Bridges. The goal was to cut the routes that linked this land to its neighbours. Two years later, in the 1948 war, fighting again reached this area, close to the river and the hills beyond it.

Today you will see modern bridges carrying traffic over the Jordan. The road on the far side climbs into the Golan Heights, so military vehicles are a common sight. Old stones from earlier structures remain in the area, but they are easy to miss among the newer concrete. It helps to slow down. Look at the riverbanks, the reeds, and the line of the old road. The layers of this place do not sit in a museum case. They sit in the landscape itself.

While you are here, look up as well. The Hula Valley to the north lies on a major route for migrating birds between Europe, Asia, and Africa. In the right season, you may see large birds circling high above the valley. Down at the water, the Jordan is narrow enough that you can almost imagine wading across. That narrowness is exactly the point. Geography made this a ford, and the ford made this a place where people fought, traded, and rested.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen again. The river sounds the same as it did for the Crusader masons, for Saladin's soldiers, and for those early humans by their fires. The stones under the water are the same dark basalt. Notice the hills of the Galilee behind you and the Golan rising ahead. As you walk back to the road, you are taking one more small step along one of the oldest crossings in human history.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in spring or autumn to spot migrating birds overhead.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven basalt ground near the river.
Bring water and a hat; the valley floor gets hot.
Pair the visit with a drive through the nearby Hula Valley.
Stay alert near the road; traffic and military vehicles pass often.
