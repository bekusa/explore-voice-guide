The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the mound, the wind moves through dry grass and thistle. Somewhere below, a tractor works a field. Under your feet the ground is not quite natural. It rises in a long, flat-topped hill above the Hula Valley, and every step you take sits on layer after layer of human life. This is Tel Hazor, in the Upper Galilee, a short drive from the hilltop town of Safed. It looks quiet now. For much of the Bronze Age, it was one of the largest cities in the land.

First, a word about the name. A "tel" is an artificial hill. People built a town, it was destroyed or abandoned, and new people built on top of the ruins. Over many centuries those layers stacked up like pages in a closed book. Archaeologists read the hill from the top down. At Hazor they have identified more than twenty separate layers of settlement. So the path you walk today is a kind of timeline, and every trench is a page held open.

Why here? Look out across the valley. Ancient roads between Egypt in the south and Syria and Mesopotamia in the north passed through this corridor. A city on this hill could watch that traffic, tax it, and protect it. Water was close, the soil was good, and the view was long. Hazor grew rich on that position. Its upper city covered this mound, and a much larger lower city once spread across the plateau to the north, protected by huge earthen ramparts.

Here is the first surprise. Hazor is one of the very few Canaanite cities named in documents from far away. Clay tablets from the palace archive at Mari, a royal city on the Euphrates in modern Syria, mention Hazor and its king. Later, letters found in Egypt, from the age of the pharaoh Akhenaten, mention it again. To scribes hundreds of kilometres away, this hill was a name worth writing down. That kind of outside record is rare for a site in this region.

The Bible also remembers Hazor's power. The Book of Joshua calls it "the head of all those kingdoms", and describes its king leading a coalition of northern cities. The same book says the city was burned. Hold that thought, because the ground seems to agree on one part of the story. Whether it agrees on the rest is still one of the most lively debates in biblical archaeology.

Modern excavation began in the 1950s under Yigael Yadin, an Israeli army chief of staff who became an archaeologist. His large team worked here through the late 1950s and returned in 1968. From 1990, a new expedition led by Amnon Ben-Tor of the Hebrew University resumed the work, season after season. Much of what you see, cleaned and labelled, comes from these two long projects. Many of the original finds now sit in museums, including the Israel Museum in Jerusalem.

Now, the fire. In the layers of the late Canaanite city, excavators found a thick destruction level. Mud brick had baked hard and red in the heat. Basalt slabs had cracked. Ash covered floors. This happened around the thirteenth century before the common era. The great palace and temples burned and were never rebuilt in the same form. Who set the fire is not settled. Some scholars point to the early Israelites. Others suggest rival cities, Egyptian campaigns, or even an uprising inside Hazor itself.

One detail is striking. In the burned buildings, some basalt statues of gods and rulers were found with their heads and hands deliberately broken off. Someone took the time to mutilate them. Breaking a ruler's image was a common ancient way to destroy his power, so the damage looks intentional rather than accidental. It is a small, very human clue. Whoever burned Hazor wanted more than its walls to fall. They wanted its gods and kings to fall too.

After the fire, Hazor stayed small for a while. Then it rose again as an Israelite town. Walk to the gate area and look for the gateway with chambers on each side. Yadin linked it to a verse in the First Book of Kings, which says King Solomon built Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer. He found similar gates at those other two cities. Today scholars still argue about the date. Some agree with Yadin. Others place the gate somewhat later.

Now the second surprise, and perhaps the best part of a visit. Later Israelite engineers dug a water system straight down into the hill. A great vertical shaft drops through the rock, and a sloping tunnel continues down to the natural water level. In a siege, the people of Hazor could reach water without leaving the walls. Modern stairs let you descend into it. The air cools as you go down. The light changes from hard sun to soft grey, and your footsteps echo against cut stone.

Hazor's Israelite chapter ended in the eighth century before the common era. The Second Book of Kings records that the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser the Third took Hazor along with other northern towns. Archaeologists found another destruction layer from that period. After that, the hill held only smaller settlements and fortresses, and slowly it became the quiet green mound you see. The great age of Hazor was over, but its layers stayed in the ground for later diggers.

In 2005, UNESCO listed Hazor as a World Heritage Site, together with Megiddo and Beersheba, under the title of the Biblical Tels. The three sites show how cities in this land were planned, defended and supplied with water. Notice the pillared building near the path. Its rows of stone pillars likely held up a roof over storerooms or stables. Notice also the dark basalt. This black volcanic stone comes from the region and gives the site its sombre, heavy look.

Before you leave, find a high point on the mound and turn slowly in a full circle. To the east, the Golan Heights rise. To the north, on clear days, Mount Hermon may show its pale summit. To the west, the hills climb toward Safed. Below, fields and fish ponds fill the Hula Valley. Kings, scribes, soldiers and farmers saw much of this same shape of land.

So take a moment. The grass is moving. The stones are warm. Somewhere under your shoes lies a floor that someone swept more than three thousand years ago. As you walk back down the path, look at the bumps and hollows in the ground. Each one may be a wall that no one has uncovered yet. The hill is still holding most of its story, and it is in no hurry.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for the water system stairs.
Visit early morning to avoid the strong midday heat.
Bring water and a hat; the mound has little shade.
Combine your visit with the small site museum nearby.
Climb to the highest point for views over the Hula Valley.
