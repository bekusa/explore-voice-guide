The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Cicadas drone in the trees, birds call from the canopy, and the air is warm and heavy with moisture. Underfoot the ground is soft and damp, and a green slope runs down toward the Pacific. Around you, low grassy mounds rise out of the earth. They look like gentle hills at first. They are not hills. Each one is a building, shaped by human hands more than two thousand years ago.

This is Takalik Abaj, an ancient city on the Pacific slope of southwestern Guatemala, close to the town of El Asintal. The name comes from the Kʼicheʼ Maya language, and it means 'standing stone.' That name is a modern one, given because of the carved monuments that still stand here. Nobody knows for certain what the people who built this place called it. Keep that question with you as you walk.

What makes this site unusual is its timeline. People lived and built here over a very long span, from the Preclassic period, the early era of Mesoamerican civilisation, into the Classic period that followed. That gives Takalik Abaj a rare position in the story of the region. It sits at the point where two great traditions meet. One is the older Olmec world of the Gulf Coast of Mexico. The other is the early Maya world that grew after it.

You can see that meeting point in the stone itself. Some sculptures here carry the rounded, heavy style linked to the Olmec, the culture many scholars call one of the first complex societies in Mesoamerica. Other monuments show figures, profiles and hieroglyphs in a style that belongs clearly to the early Maya. Few places let you see both traditions side by side, on the same terraces. Here, the shift from one artistic world to the other is recorded in carved rock.

Now, a detail that many visitors miss. Several monuments here carry dates written in the Long Count, the Maya system of recording time as a count of days from a fixed starting point. These are among the earlier known examples of this calendar in the Maya area. In other words, on these stones, people were already marking history in a precise, written form. It is a quiet piece of carving, but it records something very important... the moment when this region began to write down its own past.

Why here, on a hot and green slope? Geography explains a lot. This land sits on routes that linked the coastal lowlands of the Pacific with the volcanic highlands further inland. Traders carried goods along these paths, and Takalik Abaj was well placed to control the flow. Cacao, the bean behind chocolate, grew well in this warm, wet climate. In the ancient world it was valuable enough to be used as a kind of currency.

As you walk, notice how the city is laid out on a series of large terraces. The builders cut into the slope and built it up again to create flat platforms. Then they raised their structures on top. Many of these buildings were made of earth and clay, often faced with river stones. That is why they look so soft today. There is no great white limestone here, as in the northern Maya cities. This city was built from the materials of its own landscape.

Along the paths, look out for the sculptures sitting under simple thatched shelters. These roofs protect the stone from the heavy rain and the strong sun. Look closely at the surfaces. Some carvings are crisp, while others have softened into gentle shapes after many centuries of weather. You will find full figures, animals, faces, and a group of rounded, heavy-bellied sculptures. Archaeologists often call these 'potbelly' figures. They appear at several sites along the Pacific coast.

Here is a second surprise. Archaeological work at Takalik Abaj did not stop long ago. Excavations have continued here for decades. In recent years, archaeologists reported an elite burial that they described as one of the oldest royal tombs known in the Maya world. Discoveries like that one remind us that much of this city is still underground. The mounds around you hold more questions than answers. Every season of digging can change what we know.

In 2023, UNESCO added the site to its list of World Heritage, as the National Archaeological Park Takalik Abaj. The recognition was for exactly the story you have been hearing. This site records the long transition from the Olmec world to the Maya world. It also shows centuries of trade and contact across the Pacific side of Mesoamerica. This green and quiet place now sits in the same global list as far more famous ancient cities.

But this is not only a place of the past. Takalik Abaj remains a sacred place for many Maya people today. Maya spiritual guides still come here to carry out ceremonies. You may see small altars with traces of candle wax, ash, flowers or offerings. If you come across a ceremony, keep a respectful distance and stay quiet. Do not take photos without permission. For the people taking part, this is not a museum. It is a living place of prayer.

Take a look at the wider landscape, too. The park sits within farming country, with coffee and other crops growing on the slopes around it. On clear days, the volcanoes of the highlands can rise above the horizon. In the morning, mist often lies over the trees. Later the heat builds, and in the rainy season afternoon storms can roll in quickly. The forest here is very much alive. Listen for birds, and watch the ground for insects and small lizards.

When you reach the end of your walk, pause beside one of the standing stones. Think about the people who carved it. They worked with stone tools, in this same heat, under these same trees. They chose to record rulers, gods and dates, so that later generations would remember. We still cannot read every sign they left. Even so, the stone has stayed in this place across the centuries, just as they intended.

As you walk back, look again at the grassy mounds. Now you know they are temples and platforms, not hills. Listen to the cicadas once more, and feel the warm, damp air. People lived, traded and prayed on this slope long before the Maya cities of the far north reached their height. Others still pray here today. Let the quiet go with you, and take one last look at the standing stones.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early in the morning before the humid heat builds.
Bring insect repellent, water, and a hat for the sun.
Pack a light rain jacket during the rainy season.
Wear closed shoes with grip for damp, uneven paths.
Ask permission before photographing any ongoing Maya ceremony.
