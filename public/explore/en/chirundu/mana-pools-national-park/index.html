The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close, a hippo grunts. It is a deep, rolling sound, like a laugh from under the water. Then the quiet returns, and you hear the dry rattle of leaves overhead, and the soft thud of seed pods dropping onto the sand. The ground here is pale and powdery. The air smells of warm dust and river mud. You are standing on the southern bank of the Zambezi, in the valley floor of northern Zimbabwe.

This is Mana Pools National Park. The name comes from Shona, one of the main languages of Zimbabwe. In Shona, mana means four. It refers to four large pools that sit back from the main river channel. These pools are old pieces of the Zambezi itself. Over long centuries, the river shifted its course and left these curved stretches of water behind. Today they hold water long after the floods have passed.

The largest of them is called Long Pool. It runs for a good distance through the trees, and it is a favourite place for hippos and crocodiles. If you see what looks like a floating log, look twice. Crocodiles lie very still here, with only their eyes and nostrils above the surface. Hippos gather in groups, pressed close together, rising every few minutes to breathe with a loud spray of water.

Now look up at the trees around you. Many of the tallest ones are albida trees, also called winter thorns or apple-ring acacias. They have a strange habit. Most trees in this valley are green in the rains and bare in the dry months. The albida does the opposite. It drops its leaves in the wet season and grows them back in the dry season, just when shade is hardest to find. That is one reason the floodplain feels like an open park in the dry months.

Here is the first surprise. Those albida trees produce curled, orange-brown seed pods, shaped a little like dried apple rings. Elephants love them. And at Mana Pools, some elephants have learned to reach the higher branches in an unusual way. They rise up onto their hind legs, stretch their trunks, and pull the pods down. One old bull became famous among guides and photographers for doing this. Seeing an animal of that size stand upright is something few visitors forget.

The open ground beneath the albidas is also part of the story. Elephants, antelope, and baboons browse the low branches, so the trees are trimmed to an even height. This is called a browse line. From a distance, it looks almost like someone has cut a straight hedge along the underside of the forest. Stand back and look along the floodplain. You may see the line running through the trees, as level as a ruler.

Lift your eyes beyond the river now. On the far bank, the land belongs to Zambia. That side is protected too, as the Lower Zambezi National Park. Animals do not see a border here. Elephants and buffalo sometimes cross the channels between the two countries. Behind you, to the south, the land climbs toward the Zambezi escarpment, a long wall of hills that marks the edge of the valley. On a clear day, it shows as a blue line on the horizon.

In 1984, Mana Pools was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The listing also covers two neighbouring areas, the Sapi and Chewore safari areas. Together they protect a large stretch of the middle Zambezi valley. UNESCO recognised the area for its wild landscape and the large numbers of animals that gather along the river, especially in the dry season. Elephant, buffalo, lion, leopard, and many antelope species all live here.

The river itself has changed over the last century. Upstream lies Lake Kariba, held back by the Kariba Dam, which was completed in the late 1950s. Before the dam, the Zambezi flooded this valley in strong seasonal pulses. Today the flow is far more controlled. Scientists and conservationists still study how this change affects the floodplain, the sandbanks, and the slow renewal of the albida forest. The valley you see is partly shaped by that concrete wall far upstream.

Here is the second surprise. Unlike many parks in Africa, Mana Pools is known for allowing people to explore parts of it on foot. The landscape is open, so you can see a long way ahead. Even so, walking here is serious. These are wild animals, and there are no fences around the campsites. Most visitors walk with a licensed guide, who reads the wind, the tracks, and the behaviour of every animal nearby.

Keep an eye out for one special resident. Mana Pools is one of the better places in southern Africa to see African wild dogs, also called painted dogs. Their coats are patched with black, brown, cream, and gold, and no two dogs look the same. They live and hunt in close family packs. They are endangered across the continent, so a sighting here is a real privilege. Listen for their soft, bird-like contact calls in the early morning.

The river also offers another way to see the park. Canoe trips run along parts of the Zambezi, usually with experienced guides. From the water, the view is lower and quieter. You see elephants drinking at the bank, and kingfishers diving from overhanging branches. You also share the channel with hippos, which is why guides keep to the shallows and give the animals plenty of space. It is a slow, careful way to travel.

Before you move on, take one more moment. Notice the light on the water and how it turns gold as the sun drops. Notice the dust hanging in the air where animals have walked. Listen for the hippos again, and the snap of a branch somewhere behind the trees. When you walk away from the river, look down at the sand. The tracks you see were made only hours ago. The valley is always moving, even when it seems completely still.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk on foot only with a licensed professional guide.
Book walking and canoe trips well in advance.
Keep well back from the riverbank because of crocodiles.
Come at dawn or dusk for the most animal activity.
Bring binoculars to scan the far Zambian bank.
