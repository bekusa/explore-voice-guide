The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Vltava River runs over a low weir with a steady rush of white water. Above you, a painted tower rises in soft pink, green and gold. The cobbles underfoot are worn smooth and uneven, and they tilt slightly as you climb. The air smells of stone, river damp and, on some days, woodsmoke from the town below. This is Český Krumlov Castle, and it sits on a rock above one of the tightest bends of the river.

The castle is not one building. It is a long chain of structures, stretched along a ridge of rock. Courtyards follow courtyards, and each one opens into the next through a gate or a passage. In the Czech Republic, only Prague Castle is a larger castle complex. Together with the old town in the river loop below, it is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. As you walk, notice how the buildings follow the shape of the rock instead of fighting it.

The story begins in the thirteenth century. A powerful noble family, the Lords of Krumlov, built the first fortress here on the high ground above the river. The name Krumlov is usually explained as coming from an old German phrase for a crooked meadow. Look down at the river and the name makes sense. The water curves almost in a full circle, and the town sits inside that curve, protected on nearly every side.

In 1302, the castle passed to the Rosenberg family, known in Czech as the Rožmberks. They held it for three centuries, and they became one of the most powerful noble houses in the Kingdom of Bohemia. Their symbol was a red rose with five petals. Keep that rose in mind. You will find it on walls, gates and painted ceilings across the castle, and in the town below. It is the signature of the family that shaped this place most.

Now turn to the round castle tower. It is one of the first things that visitors see from the town, and it is easy to admire from a distance. But come closer and look carefully. Much of what looks like carved stone, columns and decoration is actually paint. In the sixteenth century, the tower was decorated in the Renaissance style, which means the style that revived ideas from ancient Greece and Rome. Painted illusion was cheaper than real carving, and from the street it works perfectly.

That trick appears again and again here. On many façades, the corner stones and window frames are painted onto flat plaster. Some walls carry sgraffito, a technique where the craftsman scratches through a top layer of plaster to show a darker layer underneath. Run your eyes along a wall in good light and you can often see where the illusion stops. It is one of the details most visitors miss... the castle is partly a beautiful painting of a castle.

Before you cross into the inner courtyards, you pass over a deep moat. Look down. Bears have lived in this moat for a very long time, and records of bears here go back to at least the eighteenth century. According to a popular story, the Rosenbergs liked to claim a family link with the Orsini, a noble family from Rome whose name sounds like the Italian word for bear. Historians treat that claim as family pride rather than fact. The bears, however, are very real.

In 1602, the last Rosenberg sold the estate to Emperor Rudolf the Second. Later, the castle went to the Eggenberg family, and then, in the early eighteenth century, to the Schwarzenbergs. The Schwarzenbergs held it for more than two hundred years, until the state took it over after the Second World War. Each family added its own layer. The Rosenbergs gave the Renaissance colour. The later owners added Baroque rooms, theatres and gardens, full of gilding, drama and movement.

One of those additions is a surprise for many visitors. Inside the complex is a Baroque castle theatre, finished in the 1760s. It is one of the best preserved theatres of its kind anywhere in the world. Much of the original stage machinery survives, with wooden wheels, ropes and sliding scenery that change a scene in seconds. The costumes and painted backdrops survive too. Because it is so fragile, the theatre can only be seen on a special guided tour, and not in every season.

Next, walk out onto the Cloak Bridge. This is a covered bridge built in several levels, one stacked on top of another, across a deep gap in the rock. The upper level is a corridor that connects the castle with the theatre and the garden beyond. Stand at the open arches and look down at the drop below. Then look out across the red roofs of the town. From here, you can see how the castle and the river bend fit together like two pieces of one design.

The Masquerade Hall is another room to remember if you join a guided tour. Its walls are covered with painted figures in carnival costumes, from the middle of the eighteenth century. Musicians, guests, jesters and masked dancers seem to watch you from every side. In the candlelight of a real ball, those painted crowds would have mixed with the living guests. It was a room designed to confuse the eye — the same game of illusion you saw on the painted tower outside.

And of course, there is a ghost story. Locals say that a White Lady walks through the castle. In most versions of the tale, she is Perchta of Rosenberg, a noblewoman of the fifteenth century who suffered in an unhappy marriage. It is said that her appearance in white gloves meant good news, and black gloves meant bad. This is folklore, not documented history. Still, as you walk through the dim corridors, it is easy to understand why the story has lasted.

Beyond the bridge, the castle garden stretches along the top of the hill. It is a long, quiet space of clipped hedges, gravel paths, fountains and old trees. Near its far end stands a small summer pavilion called Bellarie. The garden also holds an open-air theatre with a revolving stand for the audience, which turns to face different parts of the garden. In summer, the garden is a good place to slow down, away from the crowds in the courtyards.

Before you leave, stop at one of the high points and look back. The painted tower, the stacked arches of the bridge and the river below are all there in a single view. Listen again to the water at the weir. It has been running past this rock through every family, every rebuilding and every story you heard today. As you walk down the cobbles into town, look for the five-petalled rose. Now that you know it, you will see it everywhere.

What to look for

The red five-petalled Rosenberg rose on gates and walls.
Bears in the moat below the entrance bridge.
Sgraffito patterns scratched into courtyard façades.
Stacked arches of the Cloak Bridge over the ravine.
Before you go

Practical tips

Book the Baroque theatre tour in advance; it runs seasonally.
Climb the castle tower for views over the river bend.
Wear shoes with grip for the steep, uneven cobbles.
Visit the garden late in the day for quieter paths.
Walk the Cloak Bridge's upper corridor toward the garden.
