The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves through the cedars with a low, steady hush, like distant surf. Underfoot, the ground is soft with fallen needles and dry leaves. The air smells of resin and cool stone. Here in the hills above Taza, the light falls through the branches in narrow golden strips. You are standing in Tazekka National Park, one of the oldest protected landscapes in Morocco.

The park sits at the eastern edge of the Middle Atlas, the range of mountains that runs through the heart of the country. To the north, across a broad valley, rise the Rif Mountains. Between these two ranges lies a natural corridor known as the Taza Gap. For centuries, this narrow passage was the main overland route between the Atlantic plains of Morocco and the lands to the east. Armies, merchants and pilgrims all moved through it. The hills around you watched over that traffic.

Tazekka was declared a national park in 1950. At first, the protected area was small. Its main purpose was to shelter a stand of Atlas cedar on the slopes of Jebel Tazekka, the mountain that gives the park its name. Jebel simply means mountain in Arabic. Later, the boundaries were widened considerably. Today the park takes in forests, limestone plateaus, gorges, caves and seasonal lakes. It is a landscape of many layers, both above and below the ground.

The Atlas cedar is the tree that defines this place. It grows only in the mountains of Morocco and Algeria, and nowhere else in the wild. Look at the shape of an older tree. The branches spread out in flat, wide tiers, almost like the floors of a building. The bark is grey and deeply cracked. The needles grow in small, tight clusters and carry a faint blue-green tint. On a warm day, the scent of the resin is strong enough to notice from the path.

The cedars are not alone. Lower down, the forest changes. You will see holm oak, a hardy evergreen oak with small, leathery leaves. In some areas there is also cork oak. Its thick, spongy bark is the same material used for bottle stoppers, and the trees can be stripped and will regrow it. If you see a trunk with a smooth, reddish lower section, that is a sign the cork was harvested at some point. It is one of the quieter human marks in this forest.

Now for something many visitors miss. The most dramatic part of Tazekka is not on the mountain. It is beneath it. These hills are made largely of limestone, a soft rock that water slowly dissolves. Over a very long time, rain and underground streams have carved a hidden network of shafts and caverns. The landscape above can look calm and ordinary. Under your feet, the rock may be hollow.

The best known of these is the Friouato Cave, often called the Gouffre du Friouato. Gouffre is the French word for a chasm or abyss. It is considered one of the largest and deepest cave systems in North Africa. The entrance is a vast, open pit in the ground. Visitors descend on a long stone stairway with hundreds of steps. As you go down, the temperature drops and the daylight from above grows thin. The walls are damp, and the sounds of the surface fade away.

Deeper inside, the cave opens into chambers decorated with stalactites and stalagmites. These are formations of mineral that build up, drop by drop, over thousands of years. Stalactites hang from the ceiling. Stalagmites rise from the floor. Some passages are tight and muddy, and the lower sections are best explored with a local guide. If you go in, bring a good light and shoes with grip. Your clothes may not come out clean. That is part of the experience.

Above ground, the limestone creates another surprise. In some hollows of the plateau, water gathers in winter and spring to form shallow lakes. The best known is Daya Chiker. Daya is a local word for this kind of seasonal pond. In a wet year, it can hold a wide sheet of water that reflects the sky. In a dry summer, it may shrink or disappear, leaving pale ground and grazing animals. The lake can drain away through cracks in the rock below. It is the same water that carved the caves.

The park is also home to wildlife, though most of it is shy. Wild boar move through the oak forest, and you may see the ground churned up where they have been digging for roots. Birds of prey circle over the open ridges. In the trees, listen for woodpeckers and smaller songbirds. Conservation work in the region has focused on restoring native animals. Patience and quiet steps give you the best chance of seeing anything at all.

This is not an empty wilderness. People have lived in these hills for a very long time. Many of the villages around the park are Amazigh communities. The Amazigh, sometimes called Berbers, are the Indigenous people of North Africa, with their own languages and traditions. On the roads you may pass shepherds with flocks of sheep and goats, small terraced fields, and houses built from local stone. Sometimes a family sells honey or bread by the roadside. These encounters are part of Tazekka too.

Many visitors explore the park by following a scenic road that loops through the hills from Taza. It is often called the Tazekka circuit. The route climbs through forest, passes the cave entrance and the area of Daya Chiker, and offers wide views over the Taza Gap. A side track leads toward the summit of Jebel Tazekka, which rises to nearly two thousand metres. On a clear day, the view stretches across the folded ridges of the Rif to the north. In winter, the higher slopes can carry snow.

The character of the park changes with the seasons. In spring, the meadows fill with wildflowers and the streams run full. Summer brings dry heat to the lower valleys, while the cedar slopes stay cooler and shaded. Autumn light is softer and the air is clear. Winter can be cold, wet and quiet, with mist hanging in the trees. Each season gives you a different version of the same hills.

Before you move on, stand still for one more moment. Notice the layered branches of the nearest cedar, the grey rock showing through the soil, the distant line of the next ridge. Somewhere below you, water is still working its way through the limestone, slowly shaping caves no one has yet seen. As you walk away, listen again for the wind in the needles. It is the same sound this forest has made for a very long time.
