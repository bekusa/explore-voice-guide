The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days, the loudest thing here is the wind. It moves low across the flat ground and carries a fine, pale sand that settles in the creases of your shoes. The light is hard and white, and it bounces off the earth so strongly that you squint even with sunglasses. Out toward the water, the Gulf is a thin band of turquoise. Between you and that colour lies a town. Most of it is underground.

This is Al Zubarah, on the northwest coast of Qatar. Today it looks like an empty plain marked by low ridges and the outlines of old walls. But for a time, this was one of the most important trading and pearling towns in the Gulf. Ships came and went. Merchants bargained over pearls, dates, cloth and spices. In 2013, the archaeological site became the first place in Qatar to join the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Let us begin with the building most visitors walk toward first — the fort. It is square, sand-coloured, and very solid. Three of its corner towers are round, and one is rectangular. That mismatch is worth a second look. The fort was built in the twentieth century, generally dated to 1938, under the rule of Sheikh Abdullah bin Jassim Al Thani. So it is much younger than the ruined town. It stands guard over a place that was already long abandoned when its walls went up.

The fort had a practical life well into recent decades. For years it served as a post for coast guards and soldiers watching this stretch of shore. Later it was converted into a museum. Inside, the rooms are cool and dim compared with the glare outside. The thick walls are made from local stone and plastered with a lime-based render. Run your hand along them and you will feel how rough and porous the surface is. That texture helps the walls breathe in the heat.

Now turn your attention to the coast. The old town lies a short distance away, closer to the sea. It was founded around the middle of the eighteenth century. Historians link its rise to merchant families who arrived from Kuwait. Among the families connected with Zubarah were the Al Khalifa, who later became the ruling family of Bahrain. The town grew quickly, because its position suited trade across the Gulf and beyond.

What drove that wealth? Mostly pearls. For centuries, the shallow waters of the Gulf held oyster beds that produced some of the finest natural pearls in the world. Every summer, boats left shore for weeks at a time. Divers went down on a single breath, often with a weight on a rope and a clip on the nose. They gathered oysters by hand. It was dangerous, exhausting work. The pearls they found travelled to markets as far away as India.

Here is something many visitors miss. Zubarah was not a small fishing village. At its height, it was a walled town with houses, markets, mosques, workshops and large courtyard homes for wealthy merchants. Archaeologists have mapped streets and building plots across a wide area. The town had an outer defensive wall that curved around it in a long arc. If you look carefully at the ground, you can trace some of these lines as faint ridges in the sand.

The excavation story is recent. Since 2009, Qatar Museums has worked with the University of Copenhagen to study the site in detail. The teams uncovered houses, a market area and even a palace compound. They found pottery from far away, including Chinese porcelain and wares from other parts of the Indian Ocean world. Small objects like coins and fragments of glass show how connected this quiet coastline once was. Much of the town is still covered, waiting.

The good times did not last. The town was attacked and badly damaged in 1811, during a period of conflict between regional powers. It was partly reoccupied in the years that followed, but it never returned to its former strength. By the early twentieth century, the town was abandoned. The sand moved in. Over time, the wind buried the walls and doorways, and the place slipped from daily memory.

That burial is the second surprise. Because Zubarah was abandoned rather than rebuilt, the town was not covered by modern streets or towers. Most Gulf trading towns of the same period changed beyond recognition. Here, the plan of an eighteenth-century merchant town survives almost intact beneath the surface. That is a large part of why UNESCO valued it. It gives a clear picture of how people lived, traded and built along this coast before oil changed everything.

As you walk across the site, stay on the marked paths. The remains are fragile. The coral stone and plaster that built these houses break easily once exposed. Notice how low the surviving walls are, often just a few courses of stone. Try to imagine them full height, with wooden beams, shaded rooms and narrow lanes. Picture the noise of a market, the smell of fish and dates, and the shouts from the shore when boats came in.

A little inland lies another ruin connected with the town, often called Qal'at Murayr. It was a fortified position that helped protect Zubarah's water supply and inland approach. Archaeologists believe it was linked toward the coast by walls running across the flat land. Water mattered enormously here. Rain is rare, summers are fierce, and wells were precious. Any town that wanted to survive on this shore needed to defend its fresh water as closely as its harbour.

Take a moment to look at the landscape around you. It is almost completely flat. The horizon is wide in every direction. You may see the low scrub plants that cling to the salty ground, and perhaps birds moving along the shoreline. In the late afternoon, the light softens and the fort turns golden. The shadows of the towers grow long across the sand. It is a good time simply to stand still and watch.

Before you leave, look once more at the space between the fort and the sea. It looks empty. But under that pale ground lie streets, doorways, kitchens and courtyards where families once lived. As you walk back toward the road, listen again to the wind. It is the same wind that buried this town, grain by grain. And it is still moving the sand at your feet right now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon for softer light and cooler air.
Bring plenty of water and a sun hat.
Stay on marked paths to protect fragile remains.
Wear closed shoes for walking on sand and rough ground.
Plan for a long drive north from Doha.
