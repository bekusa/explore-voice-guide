The guide

The story

Listen first. A meadow, a stream somewhere below, cowbells drifting from the slopes. The air here is cool and clean, with a smell of cut grass and pine resin. Ahead of you, a long pale building with a steep roof sits at the end of a quiet valley. Behind it, grey limestone walls rise almost straight up. This is Schloss Elmau. It is a hotel, a concert hall, a retreat, and a piece of German history.

Before we go further, notice how isolated this place feels. You are only a short drive from Garmisch-Partenkirchen and Mittenwald. Yet the valley closes around you like a cupped hand. The mountains behind the building belong to the Wetterstein range, the same range that holds the Zugspitze, Germany's highest peak. The founder chose this spot on purpose. He wanted guests to leave the noise of the cities behind and arrive somewhere that felt set apart.

That founder was Johannes Müller, a Protestant theologian and popular writer of the early twentieth century. Müller was not a priest in the usual sense. He was a lecturer and author who drew large audiences across Germany. He believed modern people were losing touch with their own inner life. Elmau was his answer — a place where people could rest, walk, listen to music and, in his words, find themselves again. The building went up during the First World War and opened around 1916.

The architect was Carl Sattler, a Munich designer. Look at the shape of the main house. It is not a fairy-tale castle with towers and battlements. The word Schloss can suggest that, but this building is long, calm and rather plain. It has whitewashed walls, rows of shuttered windows and a heavy, sheltering roof. The design takes its cues from Alpine farmhouses and monasteries. It was meant to look settled into the land, not to dominate it.

Here is the first thing most visitors do not expect. In Müller's time, music and dance sat at the very centre of daily life. Guests gathered in the evenings to listen to chamber music. Many of them also danced, in a simple, social way that Müller saw as a form of joy and release. Elmau was never meant to be a silent spa. It was meant to be a place where people came together around sound.

That tradition continues today, and it is the second surprise. Schloss Elmau runs one of the most active concert programmes of any hotel in Europe. Classical soloists, string quartets, jazz musicians, writers and thinkers come through every year. Some of the performers are very well known. The concerts take place in an intimate hall inside the building, so the audience sits close to the players. For a remote valley, that is a remarkable amount of music.

Now the harder part of the story. Johannes Müller's legacy is complicated. In the 1930s he wrote in support of Adolf Hitler and the new regime. That fact sits uneasily beside his ideas about personal freedom. During the Second World War, the building was put to military use. After the war, it passed through several uses before returning to the family. Today, his grandson Dietmar Müller-Elmau runs the hotel. He has spoken openly about this past rather than hiding it, and that honesty is part of the place now.

Under the grandson, Elmau changed from a somewhat old-fashioned guesthouse into a luxury hotel with a strong cultural identity. Then, in 2005, a serious fire destroyed a large part of the historic building. Much of what you see was carefully rebuilt in the following years. As you walk, try to read the walls. Some parts carry the weight of the original structure. Others are newer work that follows the old lines closely.

In 2015, the world arrived in this quiet valley. Schloss Elmau hosted the G7 summit, the annual meeting of leaders from seven of the largest industrial democracies. Images of presidents and prime ministers on the meadow went around the globe. In 2022, the summit came back again. Few small Alpine places have been the backdrop for world politics twice. For a few days each time, security fences and helicopters replaced cowbells.

Around the same time as the first summit, a second building opened a little apart from the main house. It is called the Retreat. Its style is more modern, with clean lines, wood and large windows facing the mountains. Together, the two buildings show two ideas of rest. One is historic and sociable. The other is quieter and more private. Look between them and you can see how the old and new parts of Elmau sit in conversation.

Inside the main house, many guests are drawn to an unusual feature for a hotel — a proper bookshop. Books are everywhere at Elmau, on shelves, in reading corners and in libraries. This connects back to Müller, the writer and lecturer. The hotel likes to present itself as a place for conversation and thinking, not only for spa treatments and fine dining. Even if you are only passing through, the love of reading is easy to see.

The landscape around you is worth as much attention as the building. Walking paths lead out across the meadows and up into the forest. One well-known route climbs towards the Schachen, where King Ludwig the Second of Bavaria built a small royal hunting lodge high in the mountains. Ludwig is the king behind Neuschwanstein. It is a long, demanding hike, but it shows how this valley connects to a much older story of Bavarian royalty and mountain retreats.

Remember that Schloss Elmau is a private hotel, not a public monument. You will usually need a reservation to go inside, whether for a stay, a meal or a concert. Be respectful of guests and staff. Much of the experience for a day visitor happens outside — in the meadow, on the paths, and in the view of the house against the rock wall. That view alone explains why Müller chose this place.

So take a moment before you leave. Look at the long white building, the dark roof, the steep grey cliffs behind it. Notice how small the house looks against the mountains. Listen again for the stream and the cowbells. A philosopher, two summits, a fire, a century of concerts — all of it happened right here, in this one quiet fold of the Alps. As you walk away, let the valley close behind you, the way Müller hoped it would.
