The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel first. It crunches under every step along the wide approach. Ahead, a long white façade rises above the lawn, pale against the sky. On a sunny day the stone looks almost too bright. On a grey morning it turns soft and chalky, like old paper. There are no moats here, no crumbling towers. Instead, there is calm symmetry: one central block, two square pavilions, and dark slate roofs. Welcome to Château de Cheverny, a short drive south of Blois, on the gentle edge of the Sologne forest.

Many castles in the Loire Valley grew slowly, wing by wing, over many generations. Cheverny is different. It was built in a single campaign in the early seventeenth century, in the 1620s and 1630s. That is why it looks so unified. It belongs to a calmer age than the fortresses of the Middle Ages. Its builders did not need walls for defence. They wanted order, proportion, and a home that announced status without shouting. The result feels less like a fortress and more like a grand country house.

The family behind it is the Hurault family. Their connection to this land goes back to the late fifteenth century. Over the centuries, members of the family served French kings as officials, financiers, and soldiers. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Descendants of that same family still own Cheverny today — and they still live in part of it. The château passed out of their hands for a period in the eighteenth century. In the early nineteenth century, the family bought it back.

There is also a darker story attached to the building of this house. According to a well-known tale, Henri Hurault, who commissioned the château, discovered that his first wife was unfaithful. The story says he gave her a grim choice of how to die. Later, the tale continues, his second wife oversaw much of the work on the new château. Treat this as a family legend rather than a confirmed record. Still, it adds a shadow to such a serene façade.

Now look closely at the stone. It is a fine, pale limestone, quarried in the Loire region. This kind of stone is soft enough to carve but hardens as it weathers. Along the façade, notice the oval niches. They hold sculpted busts, in the style of Roman emperors. This was a fashionable way to show learning and taste. The design follows the classical rules of the period, with strong horizontal lines and regular rhythm. Your eye moves from left to right without any break.

Here is the second surprise. If you grew up with the Tintin comics, this façade may look strangely familiar. The Belgian artist Hergé used Cheverny as his model for Marlinspike Hall, the home of Captain Haddock. Hergé made one important change. He removed the two outer pavilions and kept only the central section. Stand back on the lawn and cover the side wings with your hands. Suddenly, you are looking at Captain Haddock’s front door. The estate even has a permanent Tintin exhibition in the grounds.

Inside, the mood changes. Cheverny is famous for its furnished interiors, which still look lived in. One reason is simple: people still live here. The rooms are full of period furniture, tapestries, clocks, and portraits. In the dining room and in other state rooms, look up. Painted wooden panels and ceilings show scenes in deep reds, blues, and gold. Much of this decoration is linked to the painter Jean Mosnier, who worked in the region in the seventeenth century.

The grand staircase is worth a slow climb. Its stone carving is detailed and crisp, with fruit, flowers, and symbols cut into the walls. Upstairs, the King’s Bedchamber is one of the richest rooms in the house. Painted panels cover the walls and ceiling. Heavy tapestries soften the space. This was a room prepared for a royal guest, so every surface had to impress. Take a moment to imagine the candlelight. Gold paint was chosen partly because it glowed in dim light.

Cheverny also opened its doors to visitors very early. The family welcomed the public in the early twentieth century, in the 1920s. That makes it one of the first private châteaux in France to do so. This early decision shaped the house you see now. Rooms were preserved and arranged for visitors, while the family kept private quarters elsewhere in the building. It is an unusual balance — a museum and a home under the same roof.

Now follow the sound of barking. Out in the grounds are the kennels. Cheverny keeps a large pack of hunting hounds, often around one hundred dogs. They are tall, with white, black, and tan coats. These dogs are part of a living tradition of hunting on horseback with hounds, which is still practised in this forest region. At certain times, visitors can watch the feeding of the pack. The dogs wait in perfect stillness until the huntsman gives the signal. Then the calm breaks all at once.

Near the kennels, the trophy room tells the other side of this tradition. Its ceiling is covered with deer antlers, row after row, from many generations of hunts. Some visitors find it striking; others find it uncomfortable. Either reaction is natural. The room shows how deeply hunting is tied to the identity of the Sologne, a region of woods, ponds, and wild game. Here, the hunt was never only a sport. It was a social ritual and a mark of rank.

The grounds deserve time too. Wide lawns lead to tall trees and long avenues. There is a kitchen garden, often planted with vegetables and flowers in neat patterns. In spring, the estate is known for large displays of tulips. Further away stands the orangery, a long building once used to shelter citrus trees in winter. During the Second World War, artworks from Paris museums were stored at Cheverny for safety. Some accounts say the Mona Lisa was among them, though this detail is often debated.

Before you leave, walk back toward the main lawn. Turn around and look at the façade one more time. Notice how the light moves across the pale stone as clouds pass. Notice the slate roofs, the busts in their niches, the dark windows of rooms you just walked through. Somewhere behind one of them, a family is going about its ordinary day. Listen again to the gravel under your feet. Perhaps, from the kennels, you will hear the hounds — one last sound of Cheverny as you go.
