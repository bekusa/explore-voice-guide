The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your shoes. A crow calls from the forest edge. Ahead of you, pale stone rises out of flat green land, and the roofline looks crowded, almost like a small city has been placed on top of a palace. Chimneys, turrets, little domes and lanterns stand together against the sky. This is Chambord, a short drive east of Blois, and it is one of the strangest buildings in all of France.

The man behind it was Francis the First, King of France. Work began in 1519, when he was still a young king. He was ambitious, he loved the arts, and he loved to hunt. Chambord was meant as a hunting residence. But it is very hard to look at this building and think of a simple lodge. Francis wanted something that would impress every visitor who rode out of the forest and saw it.

Here is the first surprise. For all this effort, Francis spent very little time here. Historians generally agree that his stays added up to only a few weeks over his whole reign. The château was cold, hard to supply, and far from the main roads. It was a statement more than a home. Kings in this period travelled constantly with their court, and furniture often came with them. So many rooms here stood empty for long stretches.

Look at the shape of the building as you approach. The plan is organised around a central block, called the keep, with four large round towers at its corners. That layout comes from medieval castles. But the details are Renaissance, the new style that came to France from Italy, with its interest in symmetry, columns and classical proportion. Chambord sits exactly at the moment when the old castle met the new palace.

The Italian connection matters. Francis admired Italian art, and he invited Leonardo da Vinci to France. Leonardo lived his final years near Amboise, not far from here, and he died in 1519, the same year work at Chambord began. No document proves that Leonardo designed this château. But many scholars believe his ideas may have influenced its plan. Treat this as an attractive possibility, not a settled fact.

That possibility becomes most real inside, at the heart of the keep. Here is the famous double staircase. Two separate spiral flights turn around the same hollow core, one above the other. Two people can climb at the same time, on different flights, and never meet. Through the openings, they can see each other passing. Leonardo made sketches of similar double spirals, which is one reason his name stays attached to this place.

Try it yourself if you are with a friend. One of you takes one entrance, the other takes the opposite one. Climb, and watch for each other through the gaps in the central column. It is a small game, but it shows exactly how clever the design is. Light falls down the open centre from the lantern above. At the top, the staircase leads you out onto the roof.

The roof terraces are the second surprise. From the ground they look decorative. Up here, you can walk between the chimneys and towers, like streets between houses. The court used these terraces to watch hunts and festivities in the park below. Look closely at the stonework. Dark slate pieces are set into the pale limestone in diamonds and circles. It is an effect of colour that you cannot see from far away.

As you walk through the rooms, watch the ceilings for two symbols. The first is a crowned letter F, for Francis. The second is a salamander, a lizard-like creature surrounded by flames. The salamander was the king's personal emblem. In old belief, it could live in fire without burning. You will find it carved again and again in the stone vaults, sometimes hundreds of times in a single passage.

After Francis, the château had a long and uneven life. Later kings came only now and then. Louis the Fourteenth stayed here and had work done on the building. In 1670, the playwright Molière presented his comedy The Bourgeois Gentleman before the king at Chambord. Imagine the court gathered in these cold stone halls, laughing at a play that people still perform around the world today.

The twentieth century brought a quieter role. When the Second World War began in 1939, French museums moved their treasures out of Paris to protect them. Chambord served as a central sorting depot for works from the Louvre. Crates of paintings and sculptures passed through these rooms before going on to safer hiding places. The Mona Lisa was among the works that came through here. Most visitors walk these halls without knowing that.

Now look outward, at the land. The château stands inside a huge walled estate of forest and heath, one of the largest enclosed parks in Europe. A long stone wall surrounds it. Deer and wild boar still live in these woods. In front of the château, the formal French gardens were restored in recent years, with neat lawns and lines of trees. They give back the order that the builders once planned around the stone.

Before you leave, stand back from the façade and let your eyes rest on the roofline once more. Notice how the towers catch the light differently as the sun moves. Listen to the wind in the trees and the voices of other visitors fading behind you. A king built this for pleasure and for pride, and then barely lived in it. And yet, five centuries later, here you are... still looking up.

Visit early morning for softer light on the stone.
