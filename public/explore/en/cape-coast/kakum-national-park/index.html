The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look up, before you climb, just stand still at the edge of the trees. The air here is heavy and warm, and it smells of wet leaves and red earth. Somewhere above you, an insect hums a steady note. A bird calls once, then again, from a place you cannot see. Under your feet the ground is soft, damp, and covered in fallen leaves. You are standing at the doorway of one of the last large stretches of tropical forest in southern Ghana.

Welcome to Kakum National Park. It lies in Ghana's Central Region, roughly half an hour's drive north of Cape Coast. Most visitors arrive here after a morning among the old coastal forts. The change is sudden. You leave the open sky, the sea breeze, and the busy roads behind. In a short drive the land turns green and closes in around you. The forest begins almost like a wall, tall and dark and alive with sound.

This forest is old, and so is the effort to protect it. The area was first set aside as a forest reserve in 1931, during the colonial period. For decades, though, logging continued in parts of the reserve. Hunters also worked these trees. Then, in 1992, Kakum was declared a national park. That change shifted the forest's purpose. It was no longer mainly a source of timber. Instead, it became a place to protect, study, and share with visitors.

The park covers several hundred square kilometres of what scientists call moist semi-deciduous forest. That name sounds technical, but the idea is simple. It is a wet, green forest, yet some of its trees drop their leaves in the drier months. So the canopy changes through the year. In one season it is thick and dark. In another, more light reaches the forest floor. If you come back at a different time of year, you will see a different forest.

Now, the reason most people come here hangs high above your head. Kakum's canopy walkway is a chain of narrow rope bridges strung between giant trees. It opened in the mid-1990s and was one of the first walkways of its kind in Africa. The full route runs for around 350 metres, across seven bridges. At its highest point, you are roughly forty metres above the ground. That is about the height of a ten-storey building, with nothing beneath you but leaves.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The bridges are not attached to poles driven into the ground. They hang from the trees themselves, and wooden platforms wrap around the trunks. The builders designed it this way so the walkway would harm the forest as little as possible. As you cross, the bridge sways gently under your weight. That movement is intentional. A rigid bridge would fight the trees, and the trees move with the wind.

Step onto the first bridge and the world changes again. The floor is a narrow strip of planks, with netting on both sides. Your hands find the ropes almost by instinct. Look down if you dare... the forest floor disappears into shades of green and shadow. Look out instead, and you are level with the crowns of trees you could never see from below. Here, the canopy is not a ceiling. It is a whole landscape of its own.

The canopy is where most of the forest's life happens. Leaves, flowers, and fruit grow up here, in the sunlight. So many animals live here too, and some rarely touch the ground. Kakum is home to several monkey species, including the Diana monkey, a striking animal with a dark coat and a white beard. The park is also known for its birds and for hundreds of kinds of butterflies. On a quiet morning you may hear far more than you see.

And there is a second surprise. Elephants live in this forest. These are African forest elephants, smaller and shier than the elephants of the open savanna. They move quietly through thick vegetation and are very hard to spot. Most visitors never see one. Still, guides sometimes point out signs of them, such as broken branches or tracks on muddy paths. Other shy residents include antelopes like the bongo and several kinds of small forest duikers.

The forest also matters to people far beyond its edges. Rivers that begin in and around these hills flow south toward the coast. The Kakum River, which gives the park its name, has long been an important water source for the Cape Coast area. So the trees around you do more than shelter animals. They protect soil and streams that many communities depend on. When you drink water in Cape Coast, part of its story may begin here.

The communities around the park are part of this story too. Many local people once relied on the forest for timber, meat, and medicine. When the park was created, those uses had to change. Over the years, conservation groups and park authorities have worked with nearby villages on new ways to earn a living, including tourism. Many of the guides who lead you along the trails grew up nearby. Ask them about the plants. Their knowledge often goes far beyond the textbook.

If the height makes you nervous, you are not alone. Many visitors feel their heart beat faster on the first bridge. But the platforms between bridges give you a place to rest and breathe. Some people stop on every platform just to look. Others move quickly and laugh at themselves at the end. Either way is fine. There are also trails on the ground, where you can walk among the roots and huge trunks and see the forest from below.

As you finish your visit, stop once more at the edge of the trees. Notice how the sounds have changed since you arrived. Perhaps the insects are louder now, or a group of birds has moved closer. Feel the warm air on your skin and the soft ground under your shoes. Then look up, toward the bridges you crossed. From down here they almost disappear into the green... and the forest carries on, as it has for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early in the morning for cooler air and more birdsong.
Wear closed shoes with good grip for damp trails.
Bring insect repellent and a refillable water bottle.
Keep phones and cameras on a strap while crossing bridges.
Ask your guide to identify medicinal plants along the trail.
