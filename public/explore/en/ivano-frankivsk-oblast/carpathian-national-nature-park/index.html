The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, water is moving over stone. It is a steady sound, cold and constant, and it rises up through the spruce trees with the smell of resin and wet moss. The air here feels thinner and sharper than in the valley towns. Underfoot, the path is a mix of roots, loose shale and dark forest soil. Welcome to the Carpathian National Nature Park, in the mountains of western Ukraine.

This park holds a special place in Ukrainian conservation. It was created in 1980, and it was the first national nature park in Ukraine. At the time, Ukraine was still part of the Soviet Union, and the idea of a national park was new here. The goal was twofold. Protect the mountain forests and rivers, and also protect the way of life of the people who have lived in these valleys for centuries.

The park covers roughly fifty thousand hectares of the Ivano-Frankivsk region. Its administrative centre is the resort town of Yaremche, on the Prut River. From there, the protected land stretches south and west into higher and wilder country. It climbs from river valleys full of small wooden houses up to bare, wind-swept ridges. In a single day of walking, you can pass through several completely different landscapes.

The lower slopes belong to beech. In summer, beech forest is green and full of soft, filtered light. In autumn it turns copper and gold. Higher up, the beech gives way to spruce and fir. These dark conifers stand close together and keep the ground in shade for most of the day. Higher still, the trees shrink and thin out. Then comes a belt of dwarf mountain pine, low and twisted, and finally open alpine meadow.

Those high meadows are called polonyny. The word refers to the grassy summer pastures above the tree line. For generations, shepherds drove their flocks up to these pastures in late spring and stayed with them until autumn. They lived in simple huts, made cheese, and called to each other across the slopes. In some places this tradition continues today, and you may still meet sheep, a shepherd's dog, and the smell of woodsmoke on a high ridge.

The people of these mountains are the Hutsuls. They are a Ukrainian highland community with their own dialect, music, dress and crafts. Hutsul woodcarving is known for fine geometric patterns, often inlaid with beads, metal or other woods. Their embroidery uses deep reds, oranges and blacks. And their signature instrument is the trembita — a very long wooden horn, longer than a person is tall. Its low call once carried news across the valleys: a birth, a death, a gathering of the flock.

Here is something many visitors miss. The trembita was not mainly a concert instrument. It was a working tool, a way to send a message over distances where no voice could reach. Today you are more likely to hear it at a festival or a souvenir market in Yaremche. But when it plays, notice how the sound does not seem to come from one place. It rolls along the slopes and returns from the far side of the valley.

Now look up toward the south, toward the highest ground. The park includes part of the Chornohora ridge, the tallest range in the Ukrainian Carpathians. Its name means, roughly, the Black Mountain. And on this ridge stands Hoverla, the highest point in Ukraine, at 2,061 metres above sea level. Climbing Hoverla is almost a rite of passage for Ukrainians. On a clear summer weekend, you can see a long line of walkers moving up its slopes.

From the summit of Hoverla, the view on a good day runs for many kilometres over ridge after ridge of forest. But the weather on Chornohora changes fast. A bright morning can turn into cloud, wind and cold rain by the early afternoon. Even in summer, it can feel like a different season at the top. That is part of the character of this ridge, and it is the reason local walkers start early.

Along the same ridge sits a small, cold glacial lake called Nesamovyte. Its name can be translated as something like "the frantic one" or "the furious one". Local legend says that if you throw a stone into its water, a storm will rise. This is folklore, not science. But stand beside it as the clouds pull across the ridge, and you will understand why people told the story.

The forests here are home to animals that have become rare in much of Europe. Brown bears, wolves, lynx, red deer and wild boar all live in the Ukrainian Carpathians. You will very probably not see the large predators — they avoid people, and that is how it should be. What you may notice instead are signs of them. A scratched tree trunk. A track in the mud near a stream. A path through the grass that no person made.

Closer to Yaremche, the Prut River gives the park one of its most visited spots. Here the river squeezes through a narrow rocky channel and drops in a short, powerful waterfall called Probiy. It is not tall, but the force of the water is real. Spray hangs in the air, and the noise fills the small gorge. Around it, local stalls sell wool, wooden spoons, honey and smoked sheep's cheese. This is where the protected landscape and daily mountain life meet.

So take a moment where you are standing now. Notice the colour of the light through the branches, and the temperature of the air on your face. Listen again for the water — it is almost always there, somewhere close. Every stream on these slopes runs down toward the Prut, and then out toward the plains. As you walk on, look at the ground, the bark, and the line of the ridge above you. The mountain is quiet, but it is full of life.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start any Hoverla climb early to avoid afternoon weather.
Pack a warm, waterproof layer even on summer days.
Wear sturdy boots for roots, shale and muddy trails.
Visit Probiy waterfall early morning before the crowds arrive.
Stay on marked trails to protect fragile alpine meadows.
