The guide

The story

The room is quiet and the light is low. In front of you, behind glass, lies a flat slab of pale stone. On it, a young man hangs in the air. His body is stretched out and his arms point down toward a curve of blue-green water. He has left the ground but has not yet touched the surface. He has stayed in that moment for about two and a half thousand years.

This is the Tomb of the Diver, kept in the National Archaeological Museum of Paestum. It is a small stone box built for one person. The slabs are plastered and painted on their inner faces. Scholars usually date it to around 470 BCE, the early fifth century before Christ. That places it in the same years when the great temples outside this museum were standing new and bright.

First, some context. Paestum was not Roman when this tomb was made. It was a Greek city called Poseidonia, named for Poseidon, the god of the sea. Greek settlers founded it on this plain in southern Italy, inside the wider world the Romans later called Magna Graecia, meaning "Great Greece". The Romans gave it the name Paestum much later. The people buried here spoke Greek and worshipped Greek gods. Their dining customs were Greek too.

The tomb came to light in 1968. Archaeologists working under Mario Napoli were excavating a cemetery a short distance south of the ancient city. Most graves there were plain. This one was different. When the team lifted the lid, they found painted walls on every side. It caused a sensation, and the reason is simple. Greek wall painting from this period almost never survives. Ancient writers praised famous Greek painters, but nearly all of their work is lost.

Here is the first surprise. Most of what we call "Greek painting" from this era is really painting on pottery — black and red figures on vases. Large paintings on walls and panels have mostly disappeared. So this modest grave, made for a local man in a provincial city, gives us one of the very few direct views of how Greeks painted figures on a flat wall. It is a rare survivor, and it survived because it was sealed underground.

Now look at the technique. The painter worked on fresh plaster, which is the method we call fresco. Colour soaks into wet plaster and stays fixed as it dries. Up close, you can sometimes see faint lines cut into the surface. These were guide marks, made before the paint went on. The palette is small: reds, browns, blacks, a dull blue-green. The figures are drawn with confident outlines and very little shading.

The four side walls show a different world from the diver. They show a symposium. For ancient Greeks, a symposium was a drinking party for men, held after the meal. Guests lay on couches, propped on one elbow. They drank wine mixed with water, sang, recited poetry, and talked. On these walls, pairs of men recline together on couches. Some hold cups. One plays a lyre, a small stringed instrument. They turn toward one another in conversation and affection.

Watch for a small party game. One man holds a shallow cup by its handle as if he is about to flick it. This is likely kottabos, a favourite game at Greek drinking parties. Players flicked the last drops of wine from their cups toward a target. It was a game of skill, and sometimes of flirtation. On a short wall nearby, another scene shows people arriving. A figure plays a double pipe, called an aulos, and leads the group in.

And now the second surprise. These paintings were never meant for living eyes. They faced inward, toward the body. Once the tomb was closed, no one would ever see them again. The family paid for fine work, then buried it in the dark. Whatever the images meant, they were made for the dead person alone. Today you are looking at something that was hidden on purpose.

So what does the diver mean? No ancient text explains it, and scholars still disagree. Many read it as a picture of death itself — a leap from the known world into the unknown. The water becomes the boundary between life and what comes after. Others link it to real diving as a sport or a rite of passage. The honest answer is that we are not certain. That uncertainty is part of the image's power.

Look at the composition once more. On one side stands a simple structure, a kind of platform built from blocks. The diver has pushed off from it. Two slim trees frame the scene, with a few sparse branches. There is nothing else. No crowd, no gods, no story. The painter leaves a large area of pale empty plaster around the figure. That open space makes the jump feel quiet and lonely.

When you leave this room, the ancient city is waiting just outside. The temples of Paestum stand on the same plain, built from local stone. The man buried in this tomb would have known them. He may have walked past them on his way to a friend's house, to lie on a couch and sing. The paintings in this museum and the columns outside belong to one world.

Before you move on, give the diver one last look. Notice how the painter caught him at the exact instant of balance, with everything still possible. Then turn to the drinkers on the walls, still talking and still holding their cups. Two moods share this one small box: the warmth of friends, and the silence of the leap. Take both with you as you walk back into the light.
