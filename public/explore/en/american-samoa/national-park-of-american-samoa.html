The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the canopy above you, a soft rush that rises and falls like breathing. Somewhere below, surf hits the reef with a low, steady thud. The air is warm and wet. It smells of damp soil, rotting fruit, and salt. Under your feet the path is red earth and black volcanic stone. You are standing in one of the most remote corners of the American national park system — and one of the least visited.

This is the National Park of American Samoa. It lies in the South Pacific, far from the American mainland, closer to New Zealand than to California. It is the only park in the United States national park system that sits south of the equator. Here, winter in the north means summer, and the stars overhead include constellations that most Americans never see from home. Look up on a clear night, and you may find the Southern Cross.

The park is not one single block of land. It is spread across three islands. The largest section is here on Tutuila, the main island, around the harbour of Pago Pago. Two more sections lie far to the east, in the Manu'a group, on the islands of Ofu and Ta'u. Reaching them takes a small plane or a boat, and plans depend on weather. Each island gives you a different face of the same volcanic world.

Now for the first surprise. The United States government does not own this land. The park was authorised by Congress in 1988, but the land and reefs belong to Samoan villages and families. The federal government leases them. That arrangement exists because land in Samoa is held communally, under the authority of village chiefs, called matai. So when you walk these trails, you are a guest of the villages — not only a visitor to a federal park.

That idea runs deep here. Samoans speak of fa'a Samoa, which means "the Samoan way." It is a code of family, respect, chiefly leadership, and shared responsibility. It shapes daily life in the villages around the park. Families gather in open-sided houses called fale, with thatched or tin roofs on wooden posts and no walls. The design lets the sea breeze pass through. It also means that village life is lived in the open, visible to everyone.

So a few customs matter. Many villages hold an evening prayer time called sa. During it, people stop moving through the village, and visitors are expected to pause quietly too. Sunday is a day of church and rest. Dress modestly in villages, and cover your shoulders and knees. If you want to swim at a village beach or take photographs of people, ask first. Small gestures of respect open doors here.

Look around at the forest. This is a paleotropical rainforest, meaning a tropical forest whose plants and animals trace their origins to Asia and the Old World tropics, not to the Americas. It is the only forest of its kind protected in the United States park system. The trees form layers. Tall canopy giants rise above smaller trees, ferns, and vines. On steep slopes, the green runs almost straight down into the sea.

And here is the second surprise. The animals that most visitors notice first are not birds. They are bats. The Samoan flying fox is a large fruit bat, and unlike most bats it often flies in daylight. Watch the ridgelines in the late afternoon. You may see dark shapes gliding slowly over the canopy, with broad wings and a calm, rowing motion. These bats spread seeds and pollinate flowers. Much of this forest depends on them.

The park also continues under the water. Coral reefs fringe these islands, and a large share of the park is marine. Off Ofu, the water inside the reef is clear and shallow, and the coral gardens lie close to shore. Scientists study the corals around Ofu because some of them survive in unusually warm pools. That makes them important for understanding how reefs may cope with a warming ocean. Even from the beach, you can often see the colour change where coral begins.

On Tutuila, the most popular walk climbs toward Mount Alava, above the harbour. The path is steep and often muddy after rain. It rises through forest until the view opens over Pago Pago Harbour, a deep, sheltered bay surrounded by green walls of rock. Geologists explain that this harbour formed from an old volcanic landscape later shaped by erosion and the sea. From above, the bay looks almost enclosed, like a basin of dark blue water.

Far to the east, Ta'u holds the highest point in American Samoa, Mount Lata, which rises close to a thousand metres above the sea. Its upper slopes are often hidden in cloud. On Ta'u, sea cliffs drop sharply to the water, among the tallest in the region. Traditional Samoan accounts link the Manu'a islands to the creation of the Samoan people and to the god Tagaloa. That is oral tradition, honoured here, and it gives these eastern islands a special weight in Samoan memory.

Notice how quiet the park can feel. There are no great crowds, no long queues, no ring of hotels. This is one of the least visited parks in the whole system, mainly because it is so far away. That distance has a gift in it. You may have a trail to yourself. You may hear only birds, wind, and water. The rain can arrive suddenly, so keep a light jacket and a dry bag close.

When you rest, look closely at small things. Hermit crabs drag borrowed shells across the sand. Fallen fruit lies split open on the path. Moss grows on the shaded side of the rocks. Along the shore, black volcanic stone meets pale coral sand in sharp lines. These details tell you how young and active this landscape is, how quickly life moves in, and how closely the forest and the reef depend on each other.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Feel the warm air on your skin and the soft ground under your feet. Listen again to the surf on the reef and the wind in the canopy. Somewhere nearby, a village is going about its day, as it has for generations. As you walk away, watch the ridgeline once more. If you are lucky, a flying fox will cross the sky above you.
