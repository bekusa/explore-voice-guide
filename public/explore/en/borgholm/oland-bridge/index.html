The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind rarely stops. It comes in low across the water of the Kalmar Strait, steady and salty, and it carries the faint hum of tyres on concrete. Above you, a long grey line runs from the mainland to the island. It is so long that the far end fades into haze on many days. This is the Öland Bridge, the road link between the city of Kalmar and the island of Öland.

A quick note on where you are. The bridge does not touch Borgholm itself. Borgholm, the island's main town, lies roughly twenty-five kilometres to the north. The bridge lands on Öland at the small community of Färjestaden. So if you came here from Borgholm, you drove south along the island's western shore. That road gives you a view of the strait the whole way down.

Here is the first small surprise. The name Färjestaden means, quite simply, the ferry town. Färja is the Swedish word for ferry. For generations, this is where boats crossed the strait between Öland and the mainland. People, livestock, mail and goods all moved by water. The bridge replaced that old rhythm, but the town kept its name. Every road sign here still remembers the boats.

The bridge opened in 1972. Its length is just over six kilometres, about 6,070 metres from end to end. At the time of its opening, it was the longest bridge in Sweden. Today the Öresund Bridge to Denmark is longer. But the Öresund link crosses an international border. Many Swedes still describe the Öland Bridge as the longest bridge lying entirely within the country.

Now look at the shape. Most of the bridge sits fairly low above the water. It runs on a long row of concrete piers, one after another, like steady footsteps across the strait. Then, close to the Kalmar side, the road climbs sharply into a high arch. That rise is there for shipping. Larger vessels move through the strait, and they need clearance. The result is the bridge's unusual profile — long and flat, then a sudden hump near the mainland.

From the Öland shore, that hump is easy to spot. It looks a little like the back of a creature surfacing from the water. Photographers like to frame it at the end of the long line of piers. In low evening light, the piers throw shadows across the surface of the sea. On still days, the whole structure appears again, upside down, in the water below.

Think for a moment about what changed in 1972. Before the bridge, the island depended on the ferry timetable. Weather could cut the crossing. Winter ice could make travel slow and uncertain. Once the bridge opened, a car could cross in a few minutes, at any hour. Tourism on Öland grew strongly in the decades that followed. Many summer cottages, campsites and holiday traditions on the island date from this new ease of access.

That change was not simple for everyone. Island communities often feel a mix of pride and worry when a fixed link arrives. More visitors bring income, but they also bring traffic, noise and pressure on quiet places. On Öland, summer and winter now feel like two different islands. In July, the road over the bridge can be busy with caravans and bicycles on roof racks. In January, the same road can feel almost empty.

Here is the second surprise for many visitors. The bridge is built for motor traffic. Walking and cycling across it are not permitted. Cyclists who want to reach the island usually rely on public transport or a special arrangement in summer. So the best experience of the bridge for a walker is not on it, but beside it. Stand on the shore and let the scale of it sink in.

Turn and look at the water itself. This is the Kalmar Strait, a long, narrow channel of the Baltic Sea between Öland and mainland Sweden. The water here is brackish, which means it is only slightly salty. The Baltic is much less salty than the open ocean. Seabirds work the shallows along the shore. On a windy day, small whitecaps run in lines between the piers.

Now look west, across to the mainland. On clear days, you can make out the skyline of Kalmar. It is an old trading and fortress city, and its castle and cathedral are among Sweden's best known historic buildings. Kalmar once stood close to the border with Denmark, and for centuries it was a place of real strategic weight. The bridge ties the island to that old city every single day.

Behind you lies the island the bridge was built to reach. Öland is long and narrow, with low limestone plains, stone walls and old windmills dotted across the landscape. The southern part of the island has open grasslands called alvar. These are thin soils over flat limestone, with unusual plants and wide skies. The agricultural landscape of southern Öland is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. Most visitors meet all of this only after crossing the bridge.

Before you leave, stay a moment longer at the shore. Watch a car climb the high arch near Kalmar, small as a toy at this distance. Notice how long it takes to come all the way across. Listen again to the wind and the low hum of traffic. When you drive back north toward Borgholm, keep the strait on your left. You will see the bridge in your mirror for a long time... a thin grey line, still holding the island to the mainland.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow about twenty-five kilometres' drive from Borgholm to Färjestaden.
View the bridge from the Färjestaden shore for full perspective.
Plan public transport if travelling to the island by bicycle.
Come at sunset for long shadows across the piers.
Expect heavier traffic on the bridge during July.
