The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days a steady wind comes off the water and moves across the open ground in front of you. Underfoot the earth is thin, with pale limestone close to the surface, and the grass is short and tough. Ahead, enormous walls of grey stone rise with no roof above them. Empty windows frame the sky. This is Borgholm Castle, on the island of Öland, just off the southeast coast of Sweden.

Before the history, look at where you are standing. The castle sits on the edge of a long limestone ridge. Locals call this kind of ridge a landborg. It drops toward the town of Borgholm below and to the strait beyond. On a clear day you can follow the line of water toward the mainland. The position was chosen for a reason. Anyone holding this ridge could watch ships moving through the Kalmar Strait, one of the key sea lanes of the Baltic.

The story begins with something much smaller than these walls. In the Middle Ages, probably around the late twelfth century, a defensive tower stood on this spot. It was a lookout and a stronghold, nothing more. Over the following centuries the fortress grew. Walls were added, then towers, then a larger enclosed castle. Each generation built on top of the last, so the stone in front of you carries several centuries of work layered together.

For much of its life, this was a frontier building. Sweden and Denmark fought over the Baltic again and again, and Öland sat right in the middle of that rivalry. The castle was besieged, captured, and repaired more than once. Think of it less as a royal home and more as a border fortress on an island that two kingdoms both wanted to control.

The great change came in the late sixteenth century. King John the Third of Sweden wanted more than a fortress. He began turning Borgholm into a Renaissance residence, a palace with grander rooms and a more regular, elegant plan. Renaissance, in this sense, means the style that spread north from Italy, with balance, symmetry, and classical ideas about proportion. The military shell stayed, but inside it a royal residence started to take shape.

Then came the most ambitious plan of all. In the seventeenth century, under King Charles the Tenth Gustav, the architect Nicodemus Tessin the Elder designed a vast baroque palace here. Baroque was the dramatic, monumental style of that age, built to impress. The scale you see today comes largely from this vision. And here is the first surprise... the palace was never fully finished. Money ran short, priorities changed, and the great royal project slowly lost momentum.

So the building drifted. Over the following decades it was used, neglected, and partly repurposed. A palace designed for kings did not find a clear role. Then, in 1806, fire swept through the castle. After that, nobody rebuilt it. The roofs were gone, the floors were gone, and the walls were left standing open to the weather. What you see now is essentially the shape that fire left behind more than two centuries ago.

Walk closer to the walls and look at the stone itself. Much of it is local limestone, the same rock that lies just under the grass of the island. In places you will see rough rubble masonry. In others, finer cut stone frames the windows and doorways. These differences matter. They show different periods of building side by side, the medieval fortress and the later palace sharing the same walls.

Here is the second surprise. Look up at the inside faces of the walls. You may notice rows of holes and ledges high above the ground. These once held the beams for floors that no longer exist. Try to picture several storeys of rooms stacked one above another, with fireplaces, staircases, and painted ceilings. Today the courtyard is open to the sky, and you are standing where cellars and ground floors once were.

The ruin has found a new life in recent decades. The large open courtyard works well as a stage, and in summer it hosts concerts and events. The walls hold the sound, and an audience sits under open sky inside a seventeenth century shell. Inside part of the building there is also exhibition space that explains the castle's long story. Check locally to see what is happening during your visit.

From the castle grounds, look south along the ridge. Not far away lies Solliden, the summer residence of the Swedish royal family. It was built in the early twentieth century for Queen Victoria, the Swedish queen who was born a princess of Baden in Germany. So the royal connection to this corner of Öland did not end with the fire. It simply moved a short walk away, into a lighter and much more modest home surrounded by gardens.

The landscape around you is part of the experience too. Öland is long, low, and flat, with wide skies and strong light. Much of the island is covered by thin soil over limestone, which produces open grassland with low plants and wildflowers. On the ridge here, the wind and light change quickly. A wall that looks flat grey at noon can turn warm gold in the evening, when the sun drops low across the stone.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the middle of the courtyard. Look at the empty windows one by one. Each opening once looked out from a room someone lived or worked in. Now each one frames a small piece of sky. Listen again for the wind moving through the gaps. As you walk back toward the town, turn once and look up at the ridge. From below you can see how the castle still watches over the strait.
