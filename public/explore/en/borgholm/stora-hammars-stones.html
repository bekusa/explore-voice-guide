The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves through the grass, and somewhere a sheep calls across a stone wall. In front of you, grey limestone rises out of the ground. The surface is weathered and slightly rough, pale where the light hits it. At first it looks like plain rock. Then your eyes adjust, and shallow lines appear. A ship. A row of figures. A bird with spread wings. These are the Stora Hammars stones, and they are full of stories.

A quick note on where you are standing. The picture stones known by this name belong to the farm of Stora Hammars, in the parish of Lärbro on northern Gotland. Gotland is the large island out in the Baltic Sea, east of Öland and Borgholm. If your route began on Öland, you have crossed the water to get here. Gotland is the place where this unusual art form grew strongest. Nowhere else in Scandinavia produced picture stones in such numbers.

So what is a picture stone? Think of it as a carved slab of local limestone, set upright in the landscape. Islanders made them over many centuries, before and during the Viking Age. Early stones carry simple patterns and spirals. Later ones, like the tallest stone here, are packed with scenes arranged in horizontal bands. You read them a little like a comic strip, from top to bottom. Each band holds its own episode, separated by carved borders.

The best known of this group is usually called Stora Hammars One. Most scholars date it to around the eighth century. Notice its shape. The top is rounded and wider, and the body narrows below, so the outline looks a bit like a keyhole or a mushroom. This shape is typical for Gotland stones of that period. Some people have compared it to a doorway. Whether that was intended, nobody can say for certain.

Now look near the bottom of the tallest stone. There you will find a ship, and it is one of the clearest scenes on the slab. It has a large square sail, crossed with a pattern of lines that may show woven or reinforced cloth. Rows of small figures sit or stand aboard, many with weapons. For an island people, the ship was everything. It meant trade, raiding, travel, and contact with distant coasts. Here it fills a full band of the stone.

Now move your eyes upward through the bands. One scene is among the most discussed images in Viking studies. It appears to show a human figure hanging from a tree or a frame. Nearby stand armed men, and a bird hovers close by. Many researchers read this as a sacrifice scene. Some link it to the god Odin, who in later Norse poetry is tied to hanging and to ravens. Others are more cautious. The stone does not come with captions.

Another band is often connected to an old Norse legend about a woman named Hild. In the tale, two armies fight a battle that never truly ends. Each night, according to the story, Hild wakes the fallen warriors, and they rise to fight again. Some scholars see a woman standing between two groups of armed men on this stone. They suggest it shows that endless conflict. It is an interpretation, not a certainty, but it is a powerful one to keep in mind.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. These stones were very likely painted. Today you see bare grey rock, softened by more than a thousand years of rain and frost. But traces on other Gotland stones suggest bright colours once filled the carved lines. Picture the figures picked out in strong reds and dark tones against the pale stone. The landscape around you would have looked very different, with these slabs glowing like signboards beside the old routes.

Why were they raised at all? The honest answer is that we do not fully know. Many picture stones seem connected to memory, standing for people who had died, perhaps far from home. Some later stones carry runic inscriptions naming the dead. Others were placed near roads, boundaries, or farms, where everyone would pass them. They seem to speak to the living as much as to the dead. Families wanted to be seen, and remembered, in a very public way.

Look now at the other stones in the group. They are smaller and less detailed, and some are more worn. It is easy to walk past them on the way to the tallest slab. Spend a minute with each one. Follow the edges with your eyes. Notice where the surface has flaked away and where carved lines still hold. Each stone stands as a separate voice. Together they form a small gathering in the field, as they have for many generations.

The surrounding countryside matters too. Northern Gotland is low and open, with thin soil over limestone bedrock. You can see the same grey rock in field walls, in old farm buildings, and in the medieval churches that dot the island. The material of the picture stones did not come from far away. It came from the ground under your feet. That connection between rock, land, and story runs through the whole island.

The light changes these stones completely. At midday, under a high sun, the carvings almost disappear into flat grey. Early in the morning or late in the afternoon, low light falls across the surface at an angle. Then every shallow groove casts a thin shadow, and the figures sharpen. If the scenes look faint right now, wait for a cloud to pass, or step to one side. Changing your angle by only a few steps can bring a whole band into focus.

Before you leave, stand back from the tallest stone and take in the whole group at once. Feel the wind again, and notice how quiet it is here. People stood in this same field long ago and looked at these same ships and warriors. They likely knew the stories that we can only guess at. As you walk away, glance back once more. In the right light, the ship near the bottom still seems ready to sail.

Before you go

Practical tips

Confirm your route: the stones stand on Gotland, not Öland.
Visit in early morning or late afternoon for raking light.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven grass and farmland ground.
Change your viewing angle slowly to make faint carvings appear.
Stay off the stones and avoid touching the fragile surface.
