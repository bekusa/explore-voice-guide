The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through needles high above you, a dry and steady sound, like breath through a comb. The air carries resin and cold stone. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of pale limestone chips and dark, soft earth from many years of fallen leaves. Somewhere below, a dog barks in a village you cannot see. This is the Djurdjura, the long mountain wall of Kabylie in northern Algeria, and you are standing on its slopes.

From the foothills around Tizi N'Tleta, the range looks like one great ridge. It runs roughly east to west, a grey spine above green valleys of olive and fig. The peaks are limestone, folded and broken by ancient forces, then cut by rain and snow. In winter, that grey turns white. Snow can sit on the high crests for months. This is North Africa, yes — but it is not the Africa of sand that many visitors imagine.

The national park was created in 1983 to protect this high country. Later, in 1997, UNESCO recognised it as a Biosphere Reserve. That label means the park belongs to a world network of places where nature and human life are studied side by side. It is a fair description here. People have farmed, grazed and built on these slopes for a very long time. The mountain was never empty wilderness. It was always lived in.

The people of these mountains are Kabyles, one of the Amazigh peoples of North Africa. Amazigh is the name these communities use for themselves; outsiders often say Berber. Their language, Kabyle, is still spoken in the villages around you. Many villages sit right on the ridgelines, their houses packed tightly together. That choice made sense in the past. High ground was easier to defend, and it left the valley floors free for crops and orchards.

Now look up toward the higher slopes. Those dark, flat-topped trees are Atlas cedars. They are native to the mountains of Algeria and Morocco, and they grow in few places on Earth. Their branches spread in layers, almost like shelves. In the cedar groves, the light becomes green and soft, and the ground goes quiet under thick needles. Lower down, you will find holm oak, a tough evergreen oak with small, leathery leaves. Each tree marks a different band of altitude.

Here is something many visitors miss. These forests are home to the Barbary macaque. It is the only macaque species living in Africa — every other macaque lives in Asia. The animals are wild, and they move in family groups through the trees and rocks. You may hear them before you see them: a crack of branches, a short sharp call. If you meet them, keep your distance. Do not feed them. Feeding harms their health and makes them bold around people.

The highest point of the range is Lalla Khedidja, just over 2,300 metres above the sea. Its name comes from local tradition. Locals connect the peak with a holy woman, and several stories about her are told in the region. The details change from village to village, so treat them as legend rather than history. What is certain is the view. On a clear day, the summit area looks across Kabylie toward the Mediterranean in the north.

The second surprise lies under your feet. Limestone dissolves slowly in rainwater. Over a very long time, water has carved shafts and caves deep inside the Djurdjura. One of them, the Anou Ifflis chasm, ranks among the deepest known caves in Africa. Cavers have descended more than a kilometre into it. You cannot simply walk in — it is a place for trained expeditions. But it is strange to think of that dark space beneath these quiet slopes.

This hidden water system matters for the people below. Snow melts on the high ground and sinks into the rock. Later, it appears again as springs further down the mountain. For generations, villages have depended on those springs for drinking water and for their gardens. So when you see a small stone fountain beside a path, it is part of that same story. The mountain stores water through the winter and releases it in the dry months.

The park is also a place where people come to rest. Tikjda, on the southern side of the range, has long drawn hikers in summer and snow visitors in winter. Tala Guilef, on the northern side, is known for its forests and mountain air. Small lakes, such as Agoulmime, sit in hollows between the peaks. In spring, the meadows around them fill with wildflowers. Every season changes the colours of the range.

The Djurdjura has also played a part in Algeria's modern history. During the war of independence, from 1954 to 1962, these mountains and their villages were deeply involved in the struggle. Many families here still carry memories of that time. You may see memorials in villages along the way. They are worth a quiet pause. The slopes that look so peaceful today were, within living memory, a difficult place to live.

As you walk, pay attention to the human details. Look for terraces cut into hillsides, old stone walls, and paths worn smooth by animals and feet. Olive trees with thick, twisted trunks may be very old. In autumn, families gather olives and press them for oil, a central part of Kabyle life. In the villages, you may notice pottery or woven cloth with bold geometric patterns in red, black and yellow. These designs are part of the regional tradition.

The weather here can change quickly. A clear morning can turn misty by the afternoon, as clouds rise from the sea and catch on the ridge. When that happens, the cedars disappear into grey, and sound becomes close and muffled. Even in summer, the high ground can feel cool. It is a good reminder that this is a real mountain, with its own rules, and the safest visitor is a prepared one.

Before you move on, stand still for one more moment. Look at the line where the forest meets the bare rock. Notice how the light falls differently on the limestone and on the dark cedars. Listen again for the wind, the birds, perhaps a far-off voice from a ridgetop village. People and mountain have shaped each other here for a very long time. As you walk away, that conversation goes on around you, one season after the next.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring warm layers; mountain weather changes quickly here.
Keep a respectful distance from wild Barbary macaques.
Start hikes early to avoid afternoon cloud on the ridges.
Visit in spring for wildflowers in the high meadows.
Ask before photographing people in the mountain villages.
