The guide

The story

Start with the light. Along this stretch of Nile Street, the sun comes off the water in a flat, white glare, and the air carries river damp and the dust of the road. Somewhere nearby, the Blue Nile and the White Nile meet and run north as one river. Before you reach the museum building, you will see something unexpected in the garden: ancient Egyptian-style temples, standing in a modern city, far from where they were first built.

This is the National Museum of Sudan, the country's largest archaeological museum. It opened in the early 1970s, in a plain modernist building that looks almost modest from the street. A note before we go further. Since 2023, Khartoum has been at the centre of a war, and there have been serious reports of damage and looting at this museum. What you find today may differ from what this guide describes. Please treat what follows as a portrait of the collection and its story, and check the current situation on the ground.

The temples in the garden come first, because they explain why this museum exists in its present form. In the 1960s, Egypt built the Aswan High Dam. The lake behind it, called Lake Nasser in Egypt and Lake Nubia in Sudan, was set to flood a long stretch of ancient Nubia. Nubia is the old name for the Nile lands between southern Egypt and central Sudan. An international rescue campaign, led by UNESCO, moved monuments out of the water's path.

Several of those rescued temples were taken apart, stone by stone, and rebuilt here. Among them is the temple from Buhen, a fortress town near the second cataract of the Nile. A cataract is a rocky stretch of rapids that made river travel difficult. The Buhen temple dates to Egypt's New Kingdom and is linked to the reigns of Queen Hatshepsut and Thutmose the Third. Walk slowly along its walls. Painted relief and carved inscriptions still survive on the sandstone.

Nearby stand temples from Semna and Kumma, two fortress sites that once faced each other across the river. Egyptian pharaohs built forts there to control trade and movement along the Nile. There is also material from Aksha. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. These pieces were not shipped abroad as prizes. They stayed in Sudan, because the land they came from is Sudanese land, and the story they tell is Sudan's own.

Now step inside. The ground floor follows the long story of the Sudanese Nile Valley, from Stone Age tools and early pottery onward. Much of that pottery is black-topped and red-bodied, with walls so thin they seem almost fragile. Then comes Kerma, a powerful kingdom that grew up near the third cataract more than four thousand years ago. Kerma was a rival to Egypt, not a province of it. Its people built huge mud-brick structures and buried their rulers in vast round tombs.

The next chapter is the Kingdom of Kush, and this is where the second surprise arrives. In the eighth century before Christ, Kushite kings from this region marched north and conquered Egypt itself. They ruled as pharaohs of what historians call the Twenty-Fifth Dynasty. Names like Piye and Taharqa belong to this era. For a time, the rulers of Egypt came from what is now Sudan, not the other way around.

The museum's most powerful objects come from this Kushite world. Look for large granite statues of kings, carved in the Egyptian style but with their own features. Some wear a crown with two cobras at the brow, a sign of rule over two lands — Kush and Egypt together. These rulers worshipped the god Amun at Jebel Barkal, a flat-topped sandstone mountain far to the north. They treated it as a holy place.

Later, the centre of Kushite power moved south to Meroë. The Meroitic kings and queens built steep, narrow pyramids, and Sudan has more pyramids than Egypt. The Meroites also developed their own writing system. Scholars can read the sounds of the script, but much of the language is still not understood. As you pass inscriptions from this period, remember that you are looking at words that nobody alive can fully translate.

Upstairs, the mood changes. This floor holds wall paintings from Christian Nubia, the period after the sixth century when kingdoms along the Nile adopted Christianity. Many come from the cathedral at Faras, which also lay in the path of the rising lake. A Polish archaeological team removed the paintings in the 1960s. The collection was divided, so part of it is in Warsaw and part of it is here. Saints, bishops and angels look out with wide, dark eyes.

Stand close to one of these frescoes. You can see the layers of plaster, and sometimes one painting placed on top of an older one. The colours are mostly earth tones — deep reds, ochre yellows, soft blues and white. Some figures are Nubian rulers and church leaders, painted with darker skin, under the protection of the holy figures beside them. These faces carry the identity of a local Christian kingdom that lasted for centuries.

It is worth thinking about what this building has been through. Collections like this one depend on careful storage, stable temperatures and quiet, patient staff. War does not allow any of that. Archaeologists around the world have raised concern about the safety of these objects. If you are standing here now, you are seeing a place that people have worked hard to protect. Whatever remains on display has a fragile and recent history of its own.

Before you leave, go back out to the garden. Stand beside the Buhen temple for a minute and listen. You will hear traffic on Nile Street, voices, maybe birds in the trees. The river is close by, the same river that carried these stones for thousands of years. Notice the stone under your hand, warm from the sun. Then walk toward the water, and look north, the direction the Nile has always taken.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current safety and access status before travelling here.
Visit the garden temples early, before the midday glare.
Save the upstairs fresco gallery for the end of your visit.
Bring water; the open garden offers little shade at noon.
Combine your visit with a walk toward the Nile confluence.
