The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel under your shoes. It is grey and coarse, and it crunches with every step. Around you the air is often quiet, apart from wind in the pine trees and the call of a crow. This is the site of the former Gross-Rosen concentration camp, at the edge of the village of Rogoźnica in Lower Silesia. Walk slowly here. The ground you stand on was shaped by forced labour, and by the people who did not survive it.

Before anything else, the reason this camp exists at all is close by. It is the granite. Just beyond the camp grounds lies a quarry of pale grey stone. Granite from this region was valued as building material. In Nazi Germany, the SS ran its own business to extract stone and make bricks, a company known as the German Earth and Stone Works. That company wanted this quarry. A camp was placed beside it to supply the workers.

The camp began in the summer of 1940. At first it was a subcamp of Sachsenhausen, a larger concentration camp north of Berlin. The first prisoners were sent here to build the camp and to break stone. In 1941 Gross-Rosen became an independent camp, with its own command and its own network. The name comes from the German name of the village. Today the village carries its Polish name, Rogoźnica, because this land became part of Poland after the war.

Work in the quarry was the centre of daily life, and of daily death. Prisoners cut and carried heavy blocks with simple tools. They worked long hours in thin clothing, through Silesian winters. Food was poor and beatings were common. For many prisoners, a posting to the quarry was close to a death sentence. Historians describe the early years here as among the harshest of any camp in the system, because the labour itself was designed to wear people down.

Now look toward the entrance. The gate building is one of the structures that survived. Like several other Nazi camps, its gateway carried the slogan "Arbeit macht frei" — German for "work sets you free." Think about those words next to the quarry you have just heard about. The phrase was not a promise. It was a lie written over the door. Many visitors pause here longer than they expect.

Here is something many people do not know. Gross-Rosen was not only this one fenced area. As the war went on, it grew into a large web of subcamps — roughly one hundred of them. They spread across Lower Silesia, into the Sudetenland in today's Czech Republic, and into parts of eastern Germany. Prisoners worked in factories, in construction, and in armaments production. At its peak, the Gross-Rosen system held one of the largest prisoner populations of any camp complex.

A second surprise is who those prisoners were. Many people assume the camps in this region held only men. In fact, a large share of the subcamp prisoners were Jewish women, sent from other camps to work in German industry. Prisoners came from many nations. Poles and Soviet citizens were among the largest groups. There were also Jews from Hungary, Poland and elsewhere, as well as people from France, Czechoslovakia, Germany and other countries.

The camp was also a place of execution. In 1941, Soviet prisoners of war were brought here and killed in large numbers. Historians estimate that around 125,000 people passed through Gross-Rosen and its subcamps during the war. Estimates of the dead are around 40,000, though exact numbers remain uncertain. Many records were destroyed or lost. Behind each number is a name, and many of those names are still not known.

In the nearby Owl Mountains, the Gross-Rosen network supplied prisoners to a vast secret project known as Riese, which means "giant" in German. Prisoners dug long tunnels deep into the mountains. Even today, historians debate what those tunnels were meant to become. What is certain is the cost. Thousands of prisoners worked there in terrible conditions, and many died. Some of those tunnels can still be visited in the region today.

By early 1945, the Soviet army was advancing from the east. The SS began to evacuate the camp in January and February. Prisoners were forced onto trains and marches toward camps further west, in bitter cold. Many died on the way. When Soviet troops reached the camp in February 1945, most prisoners were already gone. The empty barracks were left behind, along with the quarry and the gate.

After the war, the site slowly became a place of memory. Today it is run as a museum and memorial. You will see foundations where barracks once stood, outlined on the ground like a plan drawn in stone. You will also see a large memorial monument, built from the same grey granite that prisoners were forced to cut. That choice of material is quiet, and it is deliberate. The stone that consumed their lives now carries their memory.

As you walk the site, notice the scale. The roll-call square is open and wide. The paths are straight. The surrounding forest feels ordinary, almost peaceful. That contrast is part of what this place teaches. Terrible things happened in a landscape that looks, on a summer day, like any other corner of the Polish countryside. Take your time at the exhibitions. They carry personal stories, photographs and objects left by the prisoners themselves.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the grey stone around you — in the paths, in the memorial, at the edge of the quarry. Listen again to the wind in the pines. Many visitors leave a small stone or a candle at the monument. You do not need to say anything. Simply notice where you are, and carry this place with you as you walk back toward the gate and out into the village.
