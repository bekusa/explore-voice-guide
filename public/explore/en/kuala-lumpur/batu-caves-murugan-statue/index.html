The guide

The story

Before you see the face, you feel the heat on your shoulders and hear the pigeons. Their wings clatter across the open plaza in waves. Somewhere a bell rings from a small shrine. The smell of jasmine garlands and burning camphor drifts past. Then you look up, and up again, and a golden figure fills the sky. It stands against a grey wall of limestone that seems almost too large to belong here.

This is Lord Murugan, the Hindu god of war and victory, standing guard at the foot of Batu Caves. The statue rises about 42.7 metres, roughly the height of a fourteen-storey building. It was unveiled in January 2006, during the great festival of Thaipusam. For years it held the title of the tallest Murugan statue in the world. It remains one of the largest Hindu statues anywhere.

Look closely at the surface. The figure is not solid gold, of course. It is built from concrete and reinforced steel, and then coated in gold paint. Craftsmen brought from India shaped it over a period of about three years. When the sun hits it in the late afternoon, the paint turns warm and almost liquid. Under cloud, it becomes a flatter, brassy yellow.

Notice what Murugan holds in his right hand. That tall, leaf-shaped spear is called the vel. In Hindu tradition, his mother, the goddess Parvati, gave it to him. With it, he defeated a demon who threatened the world. For many devotees, the vel stands for wisdom that cuts through ignorance. You will see small versions of this spear in shrines, on pendants, and pierced through the skin of pilgrims during festival days.

Who is Murugan, exactly? He is the son of Shiva and Parvati, and a much-loved god among Tamil Hindus. Malaysia has a large Tamil community, whose ancestors came from southern India. Many arrived in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries to work on rubber plantations and in railways. Here, far from Tamil Nadu, Murugan became a figure of identity as well as faith. This statue carries that story in its scale.

Now turn your attention to the hill behind him. It is limestone, and geologists estimate it is around four hundred million years old. Long before any temple, this rock held caves carved by water over enormous spans of time. The indigenous Temuan people knew these caves. Later, Chinese settlers collected bat droppings here to use as fertiliser. The name Batu simply means stone or rock in Malay.

The temple story begins in the late nineteenth century. A Tamil merchant named K. Thamboosamy Pillai was struck by the shape of the main cave entrance. Locals say it reminded him of the vel itself. He dedicated a shrine to Murugan inside, in the early 1890s. Soon after, the first Thaipusam celebrations were held here. At first, pilgrims climbed to the cave on rough paths through the jungle.

Those paths are now the staircase beside the statue. There are 272 steps, and in 2018 they were repainted in bright bands of colour. Red, yellow, green, blue, and pink run upward in a long ribbon. Some people climb slowly, pausing at each landing. Others count the steps aloud. If you go up, take your time. The steps are steep, and the humidity makes every landing feel earned.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The monkeys on the stairs are long-tailed macaques, and they are not pets. They know exactly what a plastic bag means. They will grab drinks, snacks, and sunglasses with real speed. Keep food out of sight and do not try to feed them. Watch them instead. The mothers carry tiny infants clinging to their bellies as they move along the railings.

At the top waits the Temple Cave, sometimes called Cathedral Cave. The ceiling opens far above, and daylight falls through openings in the rock. Small shrines stand inside, with priests, flames, and the sound of chanting. The air is cooler than outside, and slightly damp. Drops of water fall now and then from the stone. The contrast with the bright plaza below is striking.

To understand this place fully, imagine it during Thaipusam. The festival falls in the Tamil month of Thai, around January or February, on the full moon. Hundreds of thousands of people come here. Many walk overnight from the city, carrying offerings of milk in brass pots. Some carry a kavadi, a heavy decorated frame, as an act of devotion. Some pierce their cheeks or backs with hooks and small spears.

This is the second surprise for many travellers. Those devotees often prepare for weeks before the festival. They fast, pray, and live simply. Many say they feel little pain in the moment, because of deep focus and trance. Drums and chanting surround them as they climb. For a single day, the steps you see now become one of the largest religious gatherings outside India. The statue stands above all of it.

Before you leave, stand back near the edge of the plaza. Frame the golden figure, the coloured steps, and the dark mouth of the cave together. Listen to the pigeons again, and the trains arriving at the nearby station. Watch a family touch the ground before they start to climb. The statue is enormous, but the small gestures around it hold the meaning. Take one more look at the face... calm, steady, looking out over the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cover knees and shoulders before entering the temple areas.
Arrive early morning to avoid heat and crowds.
Keep food and plastic bags hidden from the macaques.
Bring water and pause on landings during the climb.
Take the train from KL Sentral to Batu Caves station.
