The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here on the eastern edge of Paris, the loudest sounds are often birds, a passing bus, and the slow traffic along the Marne. It feels like a quiet suburb. Yet somewhere behind these walls, France keeps much of its recorded memory: decades of radio voices, television news, game shows, speeches and songs. You are standing beside the Institut national de l'audiovisuel, known across France simply as INA.

The name sounds formal, so here is the simple version. INA is the national institute for radio and television archives. Its job is to collect what French broadcasters put on the air, to preserve it, and to make it available again. Think of it as a library. The difference is that its shelves hold sound and moving pictures instead of books. This campus in Bry-sur-Marne is one of its main homes.

The institute was created in the mid-1970s. Before that, French public radio and television were run by one large state body called the ORTF. In 1974, the government broke that body into several separate companies. One question remained: who would look after the enormous archive of everything already broadcast? INA was the answer. It inherited those reels, tapes and films, and with them the job of keeping them safe.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. In France, radio and television are covered by legal deposit. That is the same principle that requires publishers to send copies of their books to the national library. A law in the early 1990s extended this duty to broadcasting. Since then, INA has recorded and stored a vast share of French radio and television output. Every day, new hours of programming join the collection, whether or not anyone thinks they matter.

That last point is important. Nobody can predict which broadcast will interest people in fifty years. A weather forecast, a cooking segment, or an advertisement may seem trivial today. Later it may tell a historian how people spoke, dressed, ate and argued. So the archive keeps the ordinary along with the historic. Coverage of a moon landing sits in the same collection as a forgotten Sunday afternoon variety show.

Preserving this material is a technical challenge. Old film can shrink and fade. Magnetic tape can lose its signal, and the machines that play some early formats are now rare. For years, a central task of INA has been digitisation. That means transferring fragile originals into digital files before they decay. Much of this careful work happens away from public view. Specialists clean, repair and copy media that may be older than they are.

Now, a practical note, said honestly. This is a working institution, not a museum with galleries. The Bry-sur-Marne campus holds offices, technical spaces and teaching rooms, and it is generally not open for casual visits. Most people experience the site from the outside. If you want to watch the archive itself, the easiest route is online. INA runs a large public website and a streaming service, and you can explore them from your phone right here.

Researchers have another option. The legal deposit collections can be consulted at the national library of France, in its large site in eastern Paris. There, students and scholars sit at screens and study broadcasts for their work. So the material preserved here travels, in digital form, to readers across the city. Bry-sur-Marne is the engine room. The reading room is somewhere else.

INA is also a place of learning. The institute trains people in audiovisual skills, from archiving to production and broadcasting. It runs its own higher education school. So on a weekday you may see students crossing the area with laptops and camera bags. The building looks back at old broadcasts, but its classrooms prepare people for the media of the future.

There is also a link to music that surprises many listeners. INA is home to the Groupe de recherches musicales, a research group with roots in the work of Pierre Schaeffer. In the mid twentieth century, Schaeffer helped create musique concrète. That is music built from recorded real-world sounds, such as trains, doors and voices, which were cut and rearranged on tape. The group still supports experimental sound today, mostly from its base in Paris.

It is fitting that this institute sits in Bry-sur-Marne. The town has an older connection to images. Louis Daguerre, one of the pioneers of photography, spent his final years here and died here in the mid nineteenth century. His name lives on in the daguerreotype, an early kind of photograph on a silvered plate. In the town's parish church, a large painted illusion by Daguerre still survives behind the altar. It is worth a short walk.

Think about that line for a moment. In this small riverside town, one man helped people fix a single image of light onto metal. More than a century later, an institute here keeps millions of hours of sound and moving pictures. The tools changed completely. The wish stayed the same: to hold on to a moment that would otherwise disappear.

Before you leave, look again at the quiet street around you. Nothing here announces how much memory is stored nearby. Perhaps that is the right way for an archive to feel: calm, patient, and slightly out of sight. As you walk back toward the Marne, listen to the voices and engines around you. They are ordinary today. One day, recordings like them may be exactly what someone hopes to find.
