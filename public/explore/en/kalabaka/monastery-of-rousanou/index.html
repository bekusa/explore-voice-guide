The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across the rock, and somewhere below a goat bell rings in the valley. Under your feet the stone path is worn smooth by countless shoes. Ahead, a small cluster of walls and red-tiled roofs sits on a single pillar of grey rock. The buildings cover the summit so closely that they look as if they grew out of the stone. This is the Monastery of Rousanou, one of the communities of Meteora, above the town of Kalabaka.

First, the landscape itself. The rock pillars around you are not volcanic. Geologists describe them as sandstone and conglomerate, a natural concrete of pebbles and sediment laid down millions of years ago. Over a very long time, water, wind and earth movements cut that mass into towers. Look closely at a nearby cliff face. You can often see rounded stones set into the rock like raisins in a cake. That texture is the clue to how these giants formed.

The name Meteora means something close to 'suspended in the air'. Rousanou fits that idea perfectly. Its rock is lower than some of its neighbours, but it is narrow and sheer on every side. The monastery fills almost the entire flat top. There is very little space left over, so the builders went upward. Several storeys stack on top of each other, and the walls follow the edge of the cliff. In places, wall and rock meet with almost no gap.

Where does the name Rousanou come from? Honestly, nobody is completely sure. One explanation links it to an early hermit, possibly called Rousanos, who lived on the rock. Another tradition connects it to a place or family name. Treat both as tradition rather than settled fact. What is clear is that hermits were living in caves and on rocks across Meteora long before the larger monasteries appeared. Monastic life here began small, with individual monks seeking silence.

The monastery as you see it took shape in the sixteenth century. According to the monastery's own history, two brothers from the city of Ioannina, the monks Joasaph and Maximos, rebuilt the community on this rock around the middle of that century. They restored older structures and built the main church. This was a period when Greece was under Ottoman rule, the empire based in Istanbul. Orthodox monasteries like this one helped keep religious life, learning and the Greek language alive.

Now think about the practical problem. How do you build on top of a rock like this? For centuries, getting up to the Meteora monasteries meant climbing wooden ladders or being pulled up in a net on a rope. Every stone, every beam, every sack of food came up the same way. Locals still tell a well-known story about the rope. When a visitor asked how often it was replaced, a monk is said to have answered: when it breaks. That is a legend, but it captures the risk.

Today you arrive by a much calmer route. A path and stone steps lead to a short bridge that crosses the gap between the neighbouring rock and the monastery. Bridges like this replaced the old ladders in more recent times. As you cross, pause and look down into the gap. It is a small moment, but it shows you how isolated this rock once was. A few metres of air made the community almost unreachable.

One detail surprises many visitors. Rousanou is not a museum. It is a living religious community, and today it is home to nuns rather than monks. A women's community has lived here since the late twentieth century. So please remember that you are entering someone's home and place of prayer. Speak quietly. Modest clothing is expected, with shoulders and knees covered. At the entrance, skirts or wraps are usually available for visitors who need them.

Inside, the heart of the monastery is the main church, called the katholikon. It is dedicated to the Transfiguration of Christ, the moment in the Gospels when Christ appears in shining light on a mountain. The choice feels right for a building that sits so high above the plain. The church is small and intimate. Your eyes need a few seconds to adjust after the bright daylight outside. Then the walls start to fill with colour.

Those walls are covered with frescoes, paintings made on wet plaster so the colour binds into the wall. They date from the sixteenth century and belong to the Cretan school, a style of Orthodox painting from the island of Crete. Cretan painters used calm faces, long elegant figures and deep, controlled colours. Look for the scenes of saints and martyrs. Some show their suffering in direct, uncomfortable detail. These images were meant to teach faith to people who could not read.

Here is a second thing many visitors miss. The monastery also honours Saint Barbara, an early Christian martyr. Her memory is so closely tied to the place that some people call it the Monastery of Saint Barbara. Saint Barbara is traditionally linked to protection from sudden death, storms and lightning. On an exposed rock that takes the full force of the weather, that connection feels very practical. Look for her image among the icons.

The history of the monastery has not been peaceful. Like other monasteries of Meteora, Rousanou went through periods of decline, when only a few monks remained. During the conflicts of the twentieth century, including the Second World War, the monasteries of the area suffered damage and loss. Much of what you see today is the result of careful repair and restoration. The fact that the frescoes still exist is itself a quiet achievement of many generations.

Before you leave, find a window or terrace and look out. On one side you can see other monasteries on their own rocks, each one isolated, each one in sight of the others. Below lies the plain of Thessaly in central Greece, with its fields and villages. In 1988, Meteora was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list, for both its natural and its cultural value. From here, it is easy to understand why.

As you walk back across the bridge, slow down for a moment. Feel the change from the cool, dim church to the open air and the wind. Look back at the walls and notice how the windows follow the shape of the rock. For nearly five centuries, people have chosen to live on this small summit, between the sky and the valley. Listen once more for the bells below... and then take the steps down, one at a time.
