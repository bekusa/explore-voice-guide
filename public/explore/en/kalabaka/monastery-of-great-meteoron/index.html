The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the wind moves across bare rock with a low, steady hiss. Somewhere below, a bell rings in a village you can barely see. The stone under your feet is rough and grey, packed with pebbles, as if a riverbed had been lifted into the sky. That is very close to the truth. You are standing on one of the great rock pillars of Meteora, above the town of Kalabaka, at the oldest and largest monastery in this landscape.

The name Meteora comes from Greek. It means something like suspended in the air, or hanging in the heavens. Look out from any terrace and the meaning is clear. Dark columns of rock rise straight out of the green plain of Thessaly, with the Pindus mountains behind them. Monasteries sit on their summits like stone crowns. This one, the Great Meteoron, sits on the broadest and highest of the inhabited rocks, more than six hundred metres above sea level.

First, the rock itself. These pillars are not volcanic. Geologists describe them as sandstone and conglomerate, a natural concrete of sand, gravel, and rounded stones. Millions of years ago, rivers carried this material into a lake or delta and laid it down in thick layers. Later, the land rose, and water, wind, and frost cut it into towers. Run your eye over a cliff face and you can see the pebbles still set in the rock — small, smooth, and very old.

People came to these rocks long before the monasteries. From around the eleventh century, hermits lived alone in caves and cracks in the cliffs. They wanted silence, distance, and prayer. Some of their small shelters are still visible as dark holes high in the rock walls. If you looked down on the drive up, you may have noticed old wooden beams or ladders wedged into these openings. Each one marks a life of extreme solitude.

The monastery you are standing in began in the fourteenth century. Its founder was a monk named Athanasios, who came from the monastic world of Mount Athos. Tradition says he climbed this rock and found it so high, so close to the sky, that he named it Meteoron. He gathered monks around him and set rules for a shared life. Today the Orthodox Church honours him as Saint Athanasios the Meteorite. His rock gave its name to the whole region.

The monastery then gained a second founder, and this is a surprise for many visitors. He was Ioasaf, a monk of royal blood. He was the son of a Serbian ruler who governed lands in Thessaly in the later fourteenth century. Ioasaf gave up power and wealth to live here as a monk. His support helped the community grow, build, and expand. A prince who chose a cell on a rock over a palace — that story is still part of the monastery's identity.

Now think about how monks reached this place. For centuries, there were no carved steps. People and supplies came up by retractable wooden ladders, or in a rope net pulled by a winch. A monk inside the tower turned a large wooden windlass, and the net rose slowly over the void. Locals say a visitor once asked how often the rope was changed. The answer, so the tale goes, was simple: when the Lord lets it break. The steps you climbed today were cut into the rock only in the twentieth century.

Look for the old winch tower near the entrance. It hangs out over the edge of the cliff. The wooden drum, the thick ropes, and the open hatch are still there. The monks still use a lifting system for supplies, so this is not only a museum piece. Stand at a safe distance and look down through the space below the tower. It helps you feel how exposed the old journey really was.

The heart of the monastery is the main church, called the katholikon. It is dedicated to the Transfiguration of Christ, the moment in the Gospels when Christ shines with divine light on a mountain. A mountain-top church for a mountain-top miracle. The oldest part of the building goes back to the fourteenth century. The larger church was rebuilt and expanded in the sixteenth century. Step inside slowly, and give your eyes a moment to adjust to the dim light.

Every wall and vault inside is covered with frescoes, painted in the sixteenth century in the Byzantine tradition. Saints stand in long rows, with gold halos and calm faces. Above, Christ looks down from the dome. But in the entrance hall, the narthex, the mood changes sharply. Here, painters showed the martyrdoms of early Christians in direct, graphic detail. Many visitors are surprised by these scenes. They were meant to remind monks what faith had cost others.

Beyond the church, the monastery opens into rooms that show daily life. There is an old kitchen with a blackened hearth and heavy pots. Low ceilings carry centuries of smoke. Nearby, a cellar holds large wooden barrels for wine and storage. These rooms are plain and practical. They show that life up here was also about bread, fire, firewood, and water — all of it carried up a cliff. The museum rooms also display manuscripts, icons, and liturgical objects.

There is one room that many visitors walk past quickly. It is the ossuary, a small chamber where the skulls and bones of earlier monks are kept on shelves. In Orthodox monastic tradition, this is not meant to frighten. It is a quiet reminder that every life ends, and that the community continues. The monks of the past remain, in a very literal way, among the monks of today. Pause here for a moment, and keep your voice low.

When you return to the terraces, look across the valley. You will see other monasteries on other rocks. At its height, Meteora held many monastic communities. Today only a handful remain active, and Great Meteoron is the largest. In 1988, UNESCO listed Meteora as a World Heritage Site, for both its natural landscape and its religious history. It is still a working monastery. Monks pray here every day, before the first tour buses arrive and after the last ones leave.

Before you leave, stand at the railing once more. Feel the wind on your face. Look at the pebbles in the rock, the red roof tiles, and the plain far below with Kalabaka spread at the foot of the cliffs. Six centuries ago, a monk looked at this same view and decided to stay. As you walk back down the steps, notice how the sounds of the town slowly return... and how quickly the silence of the rock closes behind you.

Practical tips

Arrive early to climb the steps before tour groups.
Bring water for the climb up the rock-cut steps.
Keep voices low inside the church and the ossuary.
