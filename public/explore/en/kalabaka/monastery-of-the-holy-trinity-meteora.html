The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across bare rock, and somewhere below, a dog barks in a village you can barely see. Under your feet the path is worn smooth by countless shoes. Ahead, a small cluster of tiled roofs sits on top of a stone pillar. It looks as if the building grew out of the rock itself. This is the Monastery of the Holy Trinity, and it is one of the hardest places at Meteora to reach.

First, the stone itself. These towers are not volcanic, and they were not carved by people. They are made of sandstone and conglomerate, a rough mix of pebbles and sand pressed together. Geologists explain that this material settled at the mouth of an ancient river, millions of years ago. Over time, earthquakes, water and wind cut the mass into separate columns. Look closely at any rock face nearby. You can see the round stones set into it, like fruit in a cake.

The name Meteora comes from Greek. It is usually translated as suspended in the air, or hanging in the sky. Stand here at dusk, when mist fills the valley, and the meaning is easy to feel. The pillars seem to float. Long before any monastery stood here, hermits lived in the caves and cracks of these rocks. They came for solitude and prayer. Over the following centuries, small communities grew, and the first monasteries took shape on the summits.

The Holy Trinity monastery dates to the fifteenth century, during a period when many communities built on these heights. Exact founding dates vary between sources, so it is best to think of it as late medieval. The reason for building so high was simple. This was an unsettled time in the region. A monastery on a rock was safe from raiders and armies. It was also closer, the monks believed, to God and further from the noise of the world.

Here is the first thing that surprises most visitors. For centuries, there were no stairs to this monastery. To reach the top, people climbed long rope ladders, or they rode up in a net. The net was pulled by a hand-turned winch, sometimes called a windlass, inside the monastery. A popular story says that when a visitor asked how often the rope was replaced, a monk answered: when the Lord lets it break. It is a tale told across Meteora, so treat it as legend, not record.

The steps you climb today were cut into the rock in the 1920s. There are well over one hundred of them, and they include a short tunnel through the stone. The path first drops down into the gully, then climbs steeply to the gate. Take your time. Stop on the landings and look back. With every turn, the valley opens a little more, and the monastery's walls come closer above your head.

Now look across the gorge, if you can spot it. A thin cable runs from the monastery to the far side. A small cable car still travels along it. The monks use it to bring up food, supplies and, at times, people. So the old idea of hanging between earth and sky has not disappeared. It has simply changed form. Modern steel has replaced hemp rope, but the logic is the same as it was centuries ago.

The second surprise is from the cinema. In 1981, this monastery appeared in the James Bond film For Your Eyes Only. In the climax, the hero climbs the sheer rock face to reach a villain's hideout at the top. Many visitors arrive having seen it on screen. The monks were not pleased with the filming at the time, according to accounts from the region. Today the film is part of the story people tell, but the monastery remains a place of worship first.

Inside the gate, the scale becomes very human. The courtyard is small, and the buildings press close together on the narrow summit. The main church is modest in size and dedicated to the Holy Trinity. Its walls are covered with painted scenes, which were added later than the original building. In the Orthodox tradition, these images are not decoration. They teach the stories of faith to anyone who stands beneath them. Look up, and saints and angels look back down.

The Holy Trinity is a working monastery. Monks still live, pray and work here, following a daily rhythm of services that has continued for generations. That is why there is a dress code. Men should wear long trousers. Women should wear a long skirt and cover their shoulders. Wraps are often available at the entrance. Speak quietly in the church, and follow any signs about photography. You are a guest in someone's home.

Walk to the far edge of the summit, behind the church. This is the view many people remember most. Far below, the rooftops of Kalabaka spread across the plain, and the Pineios river valley stretches into the distance. On the other side, the rock pillars rise one after another. Several other monasteries sit on their own peaks. Meteora was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1988, for both its natural landscape and its religious history.

Think about what life here once required. Every stone, every beam and every roof tile arrived by rope. Water had to be collected and stored. In winter, the wind on this summit can be bitter, and the monks lived here without modern heating for most of history. At one time, more than twenty monasteries stood across these rocks. Only six are still active today. The Holy Trinity is one of them, and its survival took real effort.

Before you go, stand still once more. Feel the rough stone of the wall under your hand. Listen to the wind, and perhaps a bell from the church. Look down at the steps you climbed, and imagine making the same journey in a swinging net. As you walk back down, turn around at the bottom of the gully. The monastery will look small again, quiet and fixed on its rock, exactly as it has for centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear long trousers or a long skirt with covered shoulders.
Bring sturdy shoes for the steep stone staircase.
Visit late in the day for soft light over Kalabaka.
Carry water; there is little shade on the climb.
Check closing days before you go; each monastery differs.
