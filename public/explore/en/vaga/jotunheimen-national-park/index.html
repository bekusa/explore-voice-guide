The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the loudest sound is often water. Meltwater trickles under the stones, and somewhere out of sight a river works its way down a gorge. The air is thin and cool, even in summer, and it smells of wet rock and low heather. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of springy moss, loose gravel, and slabs of hard, dark stone. You are standing at the edge of Jotunheimen, the highest mountain country in Norway.

The name means 'Home of the Giants.' In Norse mythology, the jotnar were giants, ancient beings of frost and rock who lived beyond the world of humans. When you look at these peaks, the idea makes sense. The summits rise in jagged rows, streaked with snow, and they look less like hills than like the backs of sleeping creatures. The name is not ancient, though. It became popular in the nineteenth century, when the poet Aasmund Olavsson Vinje used it and it stuck.

This park holds the two highest mountains in Norway. Galdhøpiggen reaches 2,469 metres, the highest point in all of Northern Europe. Its neighbour Glittertind comes very close behind. Around them stand hundreds more summits above two thousand metres. Glaciers still sit in the high hollows between them. The area became a national park in 1980, and it covers well over a thousand square kilometres of rock, ice, lakes, and valleys.

You reached this place through Vågå, a farming municipality in the valley country of central Norway. For centuries, people here lived from the land below the mountains. Cattle and goats went up to summer farms in the high pastures, and families made butter and cheese there until autumn. The mountains were not a playground. They were a workplace, a hunting ground, and a dangerous road between valleys. That older relationship with the land still shapes how locals talk about this park.

Hunting is the oldest human story in Jotunheimen. Long before tourists arrived, people came up here to follow wild reindeer. Hunters built stone traps and hiding places in the high country. Here is something most visitors never hear about. As ice patches in these Norwegian mountains have melted in recent decades, archaeologists have found arrows and other hunting gear frozen for centuries, some of it very old. The warming climate is uncovering a history the ice kept hidden.

Now look toward the water, if you can see it from where you stand. Many of the lakes here are not blue. They are a pale, milky green. The colour comes from glacier meltwater. As glaciers grind against the rock, they produce a very fine powder, often called rock flour. This powder floats in the water and scatters the light. The best known example is Gjende, a long, narrow lake that runs deep into the mountains from the Vågå side of the park.

Above Gjende runs the most famous walk in Norway, the Besseggen ridge. On one side lies Gjende, green and deep. On the other side lies a smaller lake, Bessvatnet, which is clearly blue and sits higher up. The ridge between them is narrow in places. Walkers on a clear day can look down at both colours at once. It is a long, demanding day, and the terrain is steep, so it suits people with good fitness and proper boots.

This ridge has a famous place in literature. In Henrik Ibsen's play Peer Gynt, written in the 1860s, the hero tells a wild story. He says he rode a reindeer buck along this ridge, and the animal leapt off and fell toward the lake below. It is a tall tale, and Peer is a famous liar, so no one believes it. But the scene made this landscape part of Norwegian culture. Many Norwegians know the story before they ever see the ridge.

Here is a second detail that surprises many visitors. In Norway, there is a long tradition called allemannsretten, the right to roam. It means anyone can walk across uncultivated land, and camp for a night, as long as they respect nature and private homes. Jotunheimen is open country in that sense. There are no gates around the park. The boundary is a line on a map, not a fence.

The mountain cabins are part of this culture too. The Norwegian Trekking Association runs a network of huts across the park. Some are staffed lodges with hot meals. Others are simple cabins where walkers serve themselves. Marked trails connect them, and the route markers are often a red letter T painted on stones. Many hikers move from hut to hut for days. Boats on Gjende also carry walkers between the lake's cabins in summer.

Life in this park is small and tough. Above the tree line, there are no forests, only dwarf birch, willow, mosses, and lichens. Lichens grow very slowly here, and a single patch can be many decades old. Higher still, a few hardy flowers survive among the rocks. In the valleys you may see herds of reindeer, managed by local herders. Watch the sky too. Birds of prey hunt across the open slopes.

The weather deserves real respect. A sunny morning can turn into fog, cold wind, and sleet within an hour, even in July. Snow can stay on the high trails well into summer. Locals plan their walks around the forecast and turn back without shame. If you go walking, bring warm layers, a waterproof jacket, food, and water. A short walk from the road can still give you a strong sense of how big this landscape is.

Before you move on, take one slow look around you. Notice the layers. The green or grey valley floor. The bare grey rock above it. Then the white of snow and ice at the top. Each band is a different climate, stacked in one view. Notice how quiet it is between gusts of wind. And notice how small the trail and the people on it look against the mountains. That feeling of scale is the real gift of Jotunheimen... The giants are still here, and they are in no hurry.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the mountain forecast before any walk.
Pack waterproof layers even on sunny summer days.
Allow a full day for the Besseggen ridge hike.
Use the Gjende boat to shorten long lakeside routes.
Book staffed trekking cabins early in peak summer.
