The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against limestone, a soft hollow sound that echoes under the overhanging rock. The air smells of salt, warm stone, and wet leaves. Around you, grey and green islands rise straight out of the sea like broken teeth. Some are no bigger than a house. Others are tall, sheer, and wrapped in jungle. This is Mu Ko Ang Thong, a marine national park in the Gulf of Thailand. Its name is usually translated as the Archipelago of the Golden Bowl.

One small point of geography helps here. There is also a province called Ang Thong in central Thailand, far inland on the plains north of Bangkok. That province has rice fields and river towns, but no sea. This park is a different place entirely. It lies in the gulf, in Surat Thani province, west of the islands of Koh Samui and Koh Phangan. Most visitors arrive by boat from those islands. The shared name causes real confusion, so it is worth knowing which Ang Thong you are standing in.

The archipelago is usually counted as around forty-two islands. They are made of limestone, the same rock that forms the cliffs of Krabi and Phang Nga on Thailand's other coast. Limestone dissolves slowly in rain and seawater. Over a very long time, that slow chemistry carved these islands into towers, arches, and caves. Look at the waterline on almost any cliff here. You will see a deep groove, like a bite taken out of the rock. Waves and tiny marine creatures cut that notch, a little at a time.

Thailand declared the area a national park in 1980. Protection matters here, because the reefs, the forests, and the shallow bays are fragile. There are no resort hotels on these islands. There are no towns and no roads. The park keeps a headquarters with simple facilities, and that is close to all the human building you will find. Compared with the crowded beaches of nearby islands, the quiet can feel almost strange at first. Give it a few minutes, and the silence becomes part of the visit.

The park headquarters sits on Ko Wua Talap, which translates roughly as Sleeping Cow Island. Locals say the island's long, low shape looks like a cow lying down to rest. From the beach, a steep trail climbs to a viewpoint high above the sea. The path is rough in places, with sharp limestone, roots, and ropes to help you up. It is hot work. But from the top, the whole archipelago spreads out below, island after island fading into blue haze.

Now for the place most people come to see. On the island of Ko Mae Ko, a short climb of stairs leads to a viewpoint over Thale Nai, the inner sea. It is a saltwater lake, almost completely enclosed by cliffs. The water is a milky jade green, very different from the deep blue outside. Many people say this round lake, held inside a ring of rock, gave the whole park its name. It looks like a bowl, and in the right light the water glows.

Here is something most visitors miss. Thale Nai is not a freshwater pool. It is connected to the open sea through passages hidden inside the rock. So the lake rises and falls with the tides, even though you cannot see any opening. Because of its special conditions, the lake is protected, and swimming in it is not allowed. You simply stand at the railing and look down. That limit is part of why the colour has stayed so clear for visitors to enjoy.

There is a literary story attached to these islands too. The British writer Alex Garland set his novel The Beach on a secret island in the Gulf of Thailand. In the book, the travellers find a hidden lagoon among a group of islands, and the book refers to the Ang Thong area by name. The later film was shot elsewhere, on the Andaman coast. Still, many readers come here because of the novel. Standing above an enclosed lagoon, you can easily understand where that dream came from.

The forests on these islands hold more life than you might expect. Look up into the trees, and you may see dusky langurs. These are slender leaf monkeys with dark fur and pale rings around their eyes, as if they wear spectacles. Long-tailed macaques move along the rocks near the shore. Monitor lizards sometimes cross the paths, slow and heavy. Sea eagles turn above the cliffs. Please keep a respectful distance, and never feed the animals. Fed wildlife can become aggressive and sick.

The sea itself is the other half of the park. Many tours include time for kayaking, and a kayak is the best way to feel the scale of the cliffs. You paddle close to the rock, into shadowed coves and under low overhangs. The water often changes colour beneath you, from turquoise over sand to dark green over rock. Some trips also stop for snorkelling over shallow coral. Visibility depends a great deal on weather and season, so the experience can change from day to day.

Weather shapes every visit here. The gulf has a rainy season late in the year, when strong winds and high waves can make the crossing rough. During some of those weeks, the park may close to protect visitors and to let nature rest. Even in good months, the boat ride from Koh Samui takes a fair amount of time each way. Plan for a long day on the water, with sun, spray, and plenty of salt on your skin.

Most people see the park on an organised day trip. A typical day mixes boat travel, a climb or two, a beach stop, and some time on the water. It is also possible to stay overnight near the headquarters, in simple park accommodation or camping, if you arrange it ahead. Staying means you see the islands after the day boats leave. In the evening the colours soften, the monkeys call from the trees, and the only sounds are the waves and the insects.

Before you leave, pause for a moment where you are. Look at the nearest cliff, at the groove the sea has cut along its base. Look at how the trees grip the rock, finding soil in tiny cracks. Notice the colour of the water right beside your boat or your feet. Everything here was shaped slowly, by rain, tide, and time. As you head back across the gulf, watch the islands shrink behind you, one grey shape at a time, until the sea is open again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for sharp limestone on viewpoint trails.
Bring plenty of water for the hot, steep climbs.
Choose a tour that includes kayaking among the cliffs.
Check sea conditions before booking during the rainy season.
Arrange park accommodation ahead if you want to stay overnight.
