The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the grass with a dry, steady hiss. Under your boots, the ground crunches like broken pottery. Bend down and touch the slope nearby. The surface is soft and crumbly, almost like stale cake. When it rains, this same clay turns slick and sticky, and it clings to everything. In front of you, ridges of pale stone rise and fall in bands of cream, rust, grey, and gold. Welcome to the Badlands of South Dakota.

The name is old, and it is honest. The Lakota people called this country mako sica, which means, quite simply, land bad. Later, French fur trappers described it in similar terms, as bad lands to cross. Think about what that meant for someone on foot or on horseback. There was little shade and almost no reliable water. Summers were scorching, winters were brutal, and the clay became a trap after a storm. Yet people lived around this land, hunted here, and knew its paths well.

You are standing near the southern part of the park, in Oglala Lakota County. This matters. The South Unit of Badlands National Park lies inside the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation, the home of the Oglala Sioux Tribe. The park and the tribe share responsibility for this southern section. So this is not only a park. It is also tribal land, with deep spiritual and historical meaning for the Lakota. Please treat it as you would treat someone's home... with care and with respect.

Now look closely at those stripes in the rock. Each band is a page in a very long record. The deepest layers, near the base, are dark grey shale. That shale formed at the bottom of a shallow inland sea. That sea once covered much of the middle of North America, many millions of years ago. When the sea drained away, rivers and streams arrived. They spread mud, sand, and volcanic ash across a wide floodplain, layer after layer.

Here is the first surprise. Some of the paler bands are made partly of volcanic ash. That ash blew in from eruptions far to the west. It drifted across the continent and settled here, quietly, over long ages. The yellow and red colours often come from iron and from ancient soils. In some places, you can see the yellow mounds clearly. Those mounds are old soil, weathered long ago, now exposed to the open sky.

And here is the second surprise. These formations are disappearing fast, by geological standards. Rain, frost, and wind wear the soft rock away, often at roughly the width of a finger every year. That is very quick for stone. Geologists expect that the Badlands could erode away completely within a few hundred thousand years. In a sense, you are seeing a landscape in the middle of vanishing. The shapes you see today will not look the same for your great-grandchildren.

That fast erosion has a gift hidden inside it. As the slopes wear down, they expose fossils. The Badlands hold one of the richest fossil beds of its age anywhere in the world. Scientists have found ancient horses, early rhinoceros relatives, and sabre-toothed predators. They have also found oreodonts, plant-eating mammals that looked a little like a sheep crossed with a pig. Huge creatures called brontotheres also lived here, with blunt horns on their noses, larger than a modern rhinoceros.

At the Ben Reifel Visitor Center, in the northern part of the park, there is a fossil preparation lab. In the warmer months, you can often watch paleontologists clean and study real bones through the glass. It is quiet, patient work with small brushes and fine tools. And there is one rule that every visitor should know. If you spot a fossil on the ground, do not move it. Take a photo, note the place, and tell a ranger. Its position in the rock is part of the science.

Lift your eyes now from the rock to the grass. Beyond the bare ridges, the park protects one of the largest areas of mixed-grass prairie in the United States. Short grasses and tall grasses grow together here, with wildflowers in late spring. Bison graze in the wilder sections. Bighorn sheep climb the steep slopes with ease. Prairie dogs stand upright outside their burrows and give sharp warning calls. If you hear a squeaky bark, a prairie dog has probably seen you first.

One of the rarest animals in North America also lives here. The black-footed ferret was once thought to be extinct. A small surviving population was found in Wyoming, and the species was bred in captivity. In the 1990s, ferrets were released back into the Badlands. They hunt at night and depend almost completely on prairie dogs for food and shelter. You are very unlikely to see one. But knowing they are out there changes how the dark prairie feels after sunset.

The South Unit carries a heavier human history. On a high, flat plateau called Stronghold Table, Lakota people gathered in 1890 to take part in the Ghost Dance. This was a spiritual movement that promised renewal for Native peoples during a time of great loss. Soon afterwards, many Lakota were killed at Wounded Knee, which lies in this same county. The memory of those events is still alive here. Some remote parts of the South Unit are sacred, and access may require permission.

In the twentieth century, the United States government took part of this southern land for a bombing and gunnery range during the Second World War. Families were moved from their homes. Some unexploded ordnance may still remain in remote areas, which is another reason to stay on known routes. The national park itself grew in stages. It was first protected as a national monument in 1939, and it became a full national park in 1978.

Before you leave, give the light a little time. In the early morning and late evening, the low sun turns the pale ridges pink and orange. The shadows grow long and deep in the gullies. At midday, the same rock looks flat and white, almost like snow. If you can, stay after dark. The sky here is wide and very dark, and on clear nights the Milky Way stretches from horizon to horizon.

So take one more slow look around you. Notice the colours in the closest slope. Notice the crunch of the clay under your feet, and the smell of dry grass and sage on the wind. Every storm will change this place a little. Every year, a few more bones will reach the surface. As you walk away, remember that this land is older than any story told about it... and it still belongs, in many ways, to the people who first named it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry plenty of water; shade is almost nonexistent here.
Avoid clay slopes after rain; they become dangerously slick.
Report any fossil to a ranger instead of moving it.
Ask about tribal permission before exploring remote South Unit areas.
Stay after dark for some of the darkest skies around.
