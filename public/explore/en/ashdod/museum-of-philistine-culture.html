The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Inside these rooms, the sound of the modern city drops away. Outside, Ashdod is a port town of apartment towers, wide roads and sea air. In here, the light is softer, and the glass cases hold objects shaped by hands more than three thousand years ago. Lean in close to a clay jar and look at the painted lines. Red and black, still clear. You are standing in a museum dedicated to a people most of us only know from a single word in an old story.

That word is Philistine. In everyday English, it has become an insult. It means someone with no taste, no interest in art or ideas. This museum quietly argues the opposite. It is often described as the only museum in the world devoted entirely to Philistine culture. Its full name honours a donor, Corinne Mamane. The collection sets out to show who these people actually were. They were not a cartoon villain. They were potters, builders, traders and farmers, with their own gods and their own sense of beauty.

So who were they? Here is the short version that historians broadly agree on. Around the twelfth century before the common era, the eastern Mediterranean went through a period of collapse. Great kingdoms weakened. Trade routes broke. Groups of people moved by sea, and ancient Egyptian records call some of them the Sea Peoples. One of these groups settled along this southern coast. The Egyptians called them by a name scholars link to the Philistines. Their roots appear to lie in the Aegean world, the region of Greece, Crete and Cyprus.

Here is the first surprise. Ashdod is not just a convenient place for this museum. It is one of the original Philistine cities. The Hebrew Bible names five main Philistine centres, often called the Pentapolis, meaning five cities. They were Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron and Gath. The ancient mound of Ashdod lies a short drive south of the modern city centre. Archaeologists dug there in the 1960s and found layers of Philistine life. So the people in these cases once lived on ground very close to where you are standing now.

Now look again at the pottery. This is one of the clearest fingerprints the Philistines left behind. Archaeologists call much of it bichrome ware, which simply means pottery painted in two colours. You will often see red and black designs on a pale background. Watch for birds with long necks, often turning to look backwards. Watch for spirals and neat geometric bands. These patterns have close cousins in the Aegean world. To an archaeologist, a broken piece of this pottery is a strong clue. It says that people with Aegean traditions lived here.

One of the most famous objects linked to this city is a small figure nicknamed Ashdoda. It was found at the ancient mound of Ashdod. The figure is a woman whose body merges with a chair or throne. Her shape is simple, almost abstract, with painted details. Many scholars think she represents a goddess. The original is held in a major national collection, but images and versions of her appear widely, and she has become a kind of symbol for Philistine Ashdod. If you see her here, notice how modern she looks. She could almost sit in a design shop today.

And here is the second surprise. Some of the best evidence about the Philistines comes not from temples or palaces, but from kitchens. At several Philistine sites, archaeologists found many pig bones, along with signs of different cooking traditions. That sets them apart from many neighbouring peoples in the region at the time. Cooking pots, hearths and food remains tell a quiet story. People who arrive in a new land often hold on to their recipes longest of all. Food, it turns out, is one of the most stubborn parts of any identity.

There is newer science, too. In 2016, archaeologists working at Ashkelon, another of the five cities, uncovered a large Philistine cemetery. Later DNA studies of ancient remains from that city found a genetic trace linked to southern Europe in some early individuals. Over the following generations, that trace faded as families mixed with the local population. In other words, the newcomers did not stay separate. Within a few centuries, they had become part of the land around them. Keep that idea in mind as you walk through the rooms.

Of course, most people first meet the Philistines in the Bible. The best known is Goliath, the giant warrior from Gath who fights the young David. Ashdod has its own story in the Book of Samuel. In that account, the Philistines capture the Ark of the Covenant, the sacred chest of the Israelites, and place it in the temple of their god Dagon in Ashdod. The statue of Dagon is then found fallen on its face. This is a religious text, not an archaeological report. But it shows how strongly this city lived in the memory of its neighbours.

The museum tries to balance those two voices. On one side, there are the stories written by the Philistines' rivals. On the other side, there are the objects the Philistines left themselves. The stories show enemies. The objects show daily life. A loom weight. A cooking jug. A small painted cup. None of these things fight battles. They simply show that families lived, worked and ate here. As you move between the cases, try to picture the hands behind each object, not the armies.

The displays are designed for curious visitors of all ages. The museum has been renovated with a more hands-on approach, so depending on the current exhibition, you may find interactive stations, reconstructions and objects you can examine closely. Take your time with the labels. Small details matter here. A difference in clay, a change in decoration, a new shape of pot... each one can mark a step in how this culture changed. If a guide or staff member is nearby, ask them which object they would save first in a fire.

It is worth thinking about why a modern city builds a museum like this. Ashdod today is a young city in many ways. Much of what you see outside was built in recent decades. Many residents have roots in other countries. Yet underneath the roads and housing blocks lies a much older city, also built by people who came from across the sea. There is an echo there. A place of arrival, again and again, over three thousand years. The museum gives that long story a home.

Before you leave, go back to one object that caught your eye. Look at it once more. Notice the colour of the clay, the small marks where a tool pressed in, the places where paint has worn thin. Then step outside into the bright coastal light and listen to the city around you. The traffic, the voices, the wind from the sea. The people who made those painted jars heard that same wind. Their name became an insult. Their pottery, quietly, tells a different story.
