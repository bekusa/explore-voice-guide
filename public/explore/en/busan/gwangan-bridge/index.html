The guide

The story

Feel the sand shift under your shoes. It is cool and slightly damp, and the air carries salt and the faint smell of grilled seafood drifting from the restaurants behind you. Small waves fold onto the shore of Gwangalli Beach, one after another. And across the water, stretched from one side of the bay to the other, is the long, pale line of Gwangan Bridge. Let it hold your eye for a moment. Everything here faces that line.

The bridge crosses Suyeong Bay, a wide curve of sea on the eastern side of Busan. It links the Suyeong district, near where you may be standing, with the Haeundae side of the city, where glass towers crowd the shoreline. The full crossing runs for more than seven kilometres. That makes it one of the longest bridges over the sea in South Korea. From the beach, though, it looks almost light. It seems to float above the water rather than rest on it.

Here is something many visitors miss at first glance. Gwangan Bridge is a double-deck bridge. There are two roads, one stacked above the other, with traffic flowing in opposite directions. From the sand, the two decks merge into a single slim band. Watch the headlights for a minute, though, and you may notice them moving on two separate levels. It is a clever answer to a crowded city. Engineers doubled the capacity without making the bridge twice as wide.

The central section is a suspension bridge. Look for the two tall towers near the middle of the span. Heavy main cables hang between them in a long, gentle curve. Thinner vertical cables drop from that curve and hold up the road decks below. Further out, toward each shore, the structure changes. The decks run on lower sections supported by piers rising from the seabed. So you are really looking at several kinds of bridge joined into one long line.

Why build it at all? Busan grew quickly in the second half of the twentieth century. It became the country's largest port and its second-largest city. The roads that wound around the edge of Suyeong Bay grew slow and congested. A direct crossing over the water offered a shortcut between districts. Construction began in the 1990s, and the bridge opened to traffic in the early 2000s. Since then it has changed how people move along this coast.

It has also changed how this coast looks. Before the bridge, Gwangalli was a beach with an open view of the sea. Today the bridge forms a permanent horizon. Cafés, bars and restaurants along the beachfront road have tall windows and rooftop terraces turned toward it. Apartment towers were planned with that view in mind. In a real sense, the neighbourhood behind you arranged itself around this structure. The bridge became the backdrop for daily life here.

Locals often call it by another name, the Diamond Bridge. The reason becomes clear after dark. As evening falls, thousands of lights along the cables, towers and decks switch on. The colours shift through the night. Blues give way to purples, greens and warm golds. Sometimes the lights move in patterns that sweep across the span. On a calm night, the whole display also appears a second time, upside down, on the dark surface of the bay.

The lighting changes with the seasons and with special occasions. Some nights the colours are soft and slow. Other nights they pulse and run along the cables like waves. If you can, stay on the beach as the sky turns from blue to black. The moment when the bridge lights come on against the last daylight is quiet and easy to miss. Many people only look up when the sky is already fully dark.

Gwangalli has also become a stage for evening shows. On many weekend nights, a drone light show rises over the water in front of the bridge. Hundreds of small drones form shapes, words and moving images in the sky. Crowds gather along the sand to watch. Schedules change and shows can be cancelled for wind or rain, so check locally before you plan your evening around one.

The biggest night of all comes in autumn. Busan holds a large fireworks festival, and Gwangan Bridge is at its centre. Fireworks are launched from the bridge itself and from barges on the bay. Sparks fall from the decks toward the water like a glowing curtain. Huge crowds fill the beach and nearby rooftops for hours before it begins. If your visit falls on that weekend, expect packed streets, and come very early for a place on the sand.

A small surprise for walkers. Normally, the bridge carries only vehicles. There are no footpaths across it. On rare occasions, though, the city closes the decks to cars for special events, such as marathons or organised walks. On those days, people can cross the bay on foot. They look down on Gwangalli from high above, which is the exact opposite of the view you have now. For most visitors, the beach is the best place to experience the bridge.

The beach is not the only good viewpoint. Millak Waterside Park, a short walk toward the Suyeong end, puts you closer to the bridge, with steps leading down to the water. Across the bay, the Haeundae side offers the bridge with Gwangalli's lights behind it. Higher up, the slopes of Hwangnyeongsan, a hill rising behind the city, look down over the whole curve of the bay. Each place shows the same bridge in a different frame.

The bridge is also part of how Busan presents itself. It appears on postcards, in television dramas, and in countless wedding and travel photos. Yet for the people who live here, it is mostly an everyday thing. It is the road to work, the line on the horizon during an evening run, the lights outside a restaurant window. Both meanings sit together. It is a working road and a shared view at the same time.

Before you leave, look again at the line across the water. Notice the towers, the long curve of the cables, the steady flow of cars across both decks. Listen to the waves, and to the voices and music along the beachfront. As you walk away, turn around once more. From further back on the sand, the bridge looks even lighter, a thin line between sea and sky, joining one side of the bay to the other.
