The guide

The story

Feel the slope under your feet as you climb. The ground rises steadily here, and the wind often picks up near the top. Then the walls come into view, a broad, warm pink against the Swedish sky. Below you, the roofs of Uppsala spread out toward the twin spires of the cathedral. You are standing on a ridge that the ice left behind at the end of the last Ice Age. Uppsala Castle sits right on its crest.

That height was chosen on purpose. In the mid-sixteenth century, King Gustav Vasa began building a fortress here. He was the king who broke Sweden away from the Danish-led union and took control of the church's wealth. Uppsala was the seat of the archbishop, the heart of religious power in the country. Many historians read the location as a message. The new royal stronghold looks down on the cathedral, and on everyone else.

Look at the shape of the building as you walk around it. The castle feels heavy and solid, with thick walls and a low, wide outline. Round towers once anchored its corners. The original plan was large, and it was never fully completed. What you see today is only part of the vision, rebuilt and reshaped over the centuries. Some sections show their age clearly, while others feel much more polished and calm.

The darkest chapter of this place came in 1567. King Erik the Fourteenth, Gustav Vasa's son, had grown deeply suspicious of the powerful Sture family. Several of its members were held here, and they were killed inside the castle. The event is remembered as the Sture murders. The king's mental state at the time is still debated, and his reign soon collapsed. It is a quiet thought to carry as you stand beside these peaceful walls.

Less than a century later, the castle saw a very different kind of drama. In 1654, Queen Christina gave up the Swedish throne in a ceremony here. She was one of the most educated rulers of her age, a patron of scholars and philosophers. She had also converted, in secret, toward the Catholic faith, in a fiercely Lutheran country. After her abdication, she left Sweden and spent much of her later life in Rome.

Now turn your attention to the colour of the walls. The castle you see is not the castle Gustav Vasa built. In 1702, a great fire swept through Uppsala. It destroyed much of the town and left the castle badly damaged. For decades, large parts stood as ruins. Later in the eighteenth century, the surviving structure was rebuilt in a calmer, more elegant style, and the familiar pink-red colour came with it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Listen for a bell. In a small tower hangs the Gunilla Bell, named after Queen Gunilla Bielke, wife of King John the Third. It rings every morning and every evening, and its sound carries out over the town. For generations, people in Uppsala have marked the day by it. Locals often speak of it with real affection, as if it were an old neighbour.

Walk to the edge of the grounds facing the town, and you will find a line of old cannons. They point out across the rooftops, toward the cathedral and the river below. Today they are a favourite spot for photographs, and children often climb close to look down the barrels. Stand beside them for a moment. This is the clearest view of how the castle and the city relate to each other.

The building still has working lives today. Part of it serves as the official residence of the county governor, the regional representative of the Swedish state. Other parts house the Uppsala Art Museum, with changing exhibitions inside the old rooms. There are also historical displays about the castle itself, including traces of the earlier fortress. So the walls hold an office, a home, and a gallery, all at once.

If you go inside the exhibitions about the castle's past, watch for the rougher stonework. Beneath the smooth eighteenth-century surfaces, older masonry survives in places. These remains show the scale of the Vasa fortress before the fire. It is a strange feeling to see the two layers together. One belongs to a king who wanted to dominate. The other belongs to a later age that wanted order and balance.

Now look down the slope, away from the town side. Just below the castle lies the university's botanical garden, laid out in formal, geometric beds. Uppsala is home to Sweden's oldest university, and botany has deep roots here. The great naturalist Carl Linnaeus, who created the modern system for naming plants and animals, taught at this university. In the warmer months, the garden adds green, gravel paths, and the scent of flowers to the climb.

The grassy slopes around the castle change with the seasons. In spring, students and families spread out here in the sun, after the long northern winter. In autumn, the light turns low and golden by mid-afternoon, and it catches the pink walls beautifully. In winter, snow often covers the slopes, and children slide down toward the town. The castle stays the same, but the people around it keep moving.

Take one more slow look before you go. Find the cathedral spires in the distance, then turn back to the castle walls beside you. For centuries, these two buildings have faced each other across the town — church and crown, faith and power. Today, the argument is long finished. As you walk back down the ridge, listen. If you are lucky with the timing, the Gunilla Bell may send you on your way.
