The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere ahead water splashes into a wide stone basin. Old chestnut trees close overhead, and the traffic noise of central Warsaw drops away. Here, in the Saxon Garden, the city sounds softer, as if someone has turned the volume down. Pigeons move across the paths, prams roll past, and pale stone figures stand in the shade. This is one of the oldest public parks in Warsaw, and it has seen a great deal.

The garden takes its name from a king. At the end of the seventeenth century, Augustus the Second, known as Augustus the Strong, became King of Poland. He was also the ruler of Saxony, a German state centred on Dresden. He wanted a royal residence in Warsaw with grounds to match. So the palace and its formal gardens grew here, laid out along a long straight line that planners still call the Saxon Axis.

Here is the first surprise. In 1727, this royal garden opened to the public. That makes it one of the earliest publicly accessible parks anywhere in the world. At a time when most European gardens belonged to kings and nobles, ordinary Warsaw residents could walk here. For almost three hundred years, people have done exactly what you are doing now — strolling, sitting, meeting friends, and escaping the street.

The garden you see today, though, is not the original. The first design was strict and French in style, with clipped hedges, straight lines, and geometric beds. In the early nineteenth century, the garden was reshaped in the English style. Paths began to curve. Trees were allowed to grow in natural groups. Open lawns replaced the rigid patterns. Walk a little and you will notice it — the paths bend gently, and each turn shows you a new view.

Now look at the stone sculptures along the paths. These sandstone figures are baroque, a decorative style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries full of movement and drama. They represent ideas rather than people — virtues, sciences, the seasons, the arts. Their faces are worn and their edges are soft. Many of the figures you see are copies, because sandstone weathers badly in a northern climate. Several originals have been moved to safer places to protect them.

The fountain at the heart of the garden dates from the mid-nineteenth century. Its large basin and tiered bowl became a meeting point for generations of Warsaw families. Locals have long arranged to meet "by the fountain", and on a warm day you will see why. Children lean over the edge, couples sit on the benches, and the falling water covers the sound of the city. Stand close and feel the fine spray on your face.

Find the small round temple on a raised mound. It looks like a classical shrine, with columns in a circle and a domed roof. It was modelled on the ancient Temple of Vesta, the Roman goddess of the hearth. Here is the second surprise — this elegant building was practical. It was a water tower, built to supply the fountain and the garden. Engineers of the nineteenth century often hid useful machines inside beautiful shapes.

Now walk toward the edge of the garden that faces the large open square. You will see a short row of arches and columns standing alone. These arches are all that remain of the Saxon Palace. The rest of the building is gone. In December 1944, during the Second World War, German forces blew up the palace, as they destroyed much of Warsaw. Only this fragment of the colonnade survived the explosion.

Under these surviving arches lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. It was created in the 1920s, after Poland regained its independence. It honours soldiers who died fighting for the country and whose names are not known. Inside, a flame burns. Plaques on the pillars carry the names of battles, from many centuries of Polish history. The fact that the tomb survived while the palace fell gives this place a deep meaning for Poles.

A military honour guard stands here. Watch the soldiers — they hold very still, eyes forward, while visitors move around them. At regular times, the guard changes in a slow and precise ceremony, with marching steps and sharp turns. Many visitors time their walk to see it. Please keep a respectful distance and stay quiet. For many Polish families, this is a place to remember relatives, and people often leave flowers or candles here.

The open space in front of the tomb is Piłsudski Square. Over the past century it has held parades, state ceremonies, and huge public gatherings. Today it is often wide and windy, a strong contrast to the shaded garden behind you. Plans to rebuild the Saxon Palace have been discussed for many years. If construction is underway when you visit, you may see fences or works nearby. The city is still deciding how much of its past to restore.

Turn back into the trees and notice the people. An older man reads a newspaper on a bench. A student eats lunch on the grass. Someone walks a small dog along the same path every afternoon. In autumn, children collect shiny chestnuts from the ground. In summer, the shade here is several degrees cooler than the square. The garden is not a museum. It is a living part of the city, used every single day.

Before you leave, stop for a moment by the fountain or under the arches. Look at the chestnut leaves above you, and the worn stone faces along the path. Behind you is a fragment of a palace that no longer exists. In front of you, children are playing where kings once walked. As you step back out onto the city streets, listen for the moment the traffic noise returns — and remember how quiet it was in here.
