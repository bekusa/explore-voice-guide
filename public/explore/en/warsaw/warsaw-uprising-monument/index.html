The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the paving stones of Krasiński Square. Listen to the traffic on the street behind you, the trams, the pigeons. Then look up. Dark bronze figures seem to burst out of the ground in front of you. Slabs of concrete and steel tilt above them, as if a building has just collapsed. The figures do not stand on a pedestal. They run, they crouch, they shout... and they are frozen in the middle of it all.

This is the Warsaw Uprising Monument. It remembers one of the most important events in the city's story. On 1 August 1944, at five o'clock in the afternoon, the Polish Home Army rose against the German forces that occupied Warsaw. The Home Army was the main underground resistance army in occupied Poland. Its fighters hoped to free the capital before the Soviet Red Army arrived from the east. They expected the fight to last a few days.

It lasted sixty-three days. Young men and women fought from street to street, from cellar to cellar. Many fighters were teenagers. Some were children who carried messages and supplies through the ruins. They had very few weapons. Against them stood tanks, artillery and aircraft. The Soviet army stopped on the other side of the river Vistula and did not come to help. On 2 October 1944, the Home Army commanders signed the capitulation.

The cost was enormous. Historians estimate that around two hundred thousand civilians died during those weeks. After the surrender, the remaining population was forced out of the city. Then German units destroyed much of what was still standing, block by block, on purpose. When you walk through Warsaw today, a great deal of what looks old was rebuilt after the war. Keep that in mind as you look at the bronze figures in front of you.

Now look closely at how the monument is arranged. It has two separate parts. The larger group shows fighters coming out from under the falling structure. One leads with a raised weapon. Others follow behind him, low and fast. A little apart stands the second, smaller group. These figures are going down, not up. They are climbing into an opening in the ground. That opening is a sewer manhole.

This is the detail many visitors miss, and it is the reason the monument stands exactly here. During the uprising, Warsaw's sewers became secret routes under the battle. Fighters crawled through dark, narrow tunnels, sometimes for hours, to move between districts. At the start of September 1944, when the Old Town could no longer be held, thousands of fighters left it through the sewers. One of the entrances they used was here, at Krasiński Square. The bronze figures repeat that real journey.

The monument was designed by the sculptor Wincenty Kućma, working with the architect Jacek Budyn. It was unveiled on 1 August 1989, exactly forty-five years after the uprising began. That date matters. For decades, the communist government of Poland did not want the Home Army honoured in public. The Home Army had been loyal to the Polish government in exile in London, not to Moscow. Many former fighters were arrested or silenced after the war.

So for a long time, families remembered the uprising mostly in private. They lit candles at graves and told the stories at home. In 1989, Poland was changing fast. The communist system was coming to an end that same year. A large, open monument to the insurgents in the centre of the capital was a powerful sign of that change. For many older Varsovians, standing here at the unveiling meant the truth could finally be said aloud.

Walk slowly around the bronze. Notice the faces. They are not calm, heroic portraits. They are tense, tired and determined. Look at the clothing too. The fighters did not have one uniform. They wore what they could find. Some wore captured German helmets and jackets. The red and white armband, in the colours of the Polish flag, often showed which side a person was on. The sculptor gives the figures that same rough, improvised look.

Now turn and look behind the monument. The large green building with tall columns is the Supreme Court of Poland. It was built in the late twentieth century, long after the war. Its columns carry Latin legal sayings. The modern glass and the dark bronze stand very close together. To the side, you can see the Krasiński Palace, a baroque palace that was also badly damaged in the war and later rebuilt. Across the street stands the Field Cathedral of the Polish Army.

If you visit on 1 August, you will see this square in a very different way. At five o'clock in the afternoon, sirens sound across Warsaw. People stop where they are. Cars stop. Pedestrians stand still on the pavements. For one minute, the city is quiet. Here at the monument, crowds gather with flags, flowers and candles. Elderly veterans, now very few, are often at the centre of the ceremony.

On ordinary days, the mood is calmer. School groups sit on the steps while a teacher explains the story. Tourists walk between the two groups of figures. Local people cut across the square on the way to work. Sometimes you will find fresh flowers or small candles at the feet of the fighters. Nobody organises them. People simply bring them. This quiet, everyday remembering is part of the monument too.

Before you leave, stand for a moment beside the figures going down into the manhole. Imagine the darkness under your feet, the water, the smell, the silence needed to avoid being heard. Then look back toward the street, at the rebuilt city all around you. The trams are still running. The pigeons are still on the paving stones. As you walk away, notice how many of the buildings around you are not quite as old as they first appear... and remember why.
