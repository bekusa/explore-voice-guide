The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your feet, and somewhere above you a squirrel scratches along a branch. Ahead, a bronze figure sits beneath a great bronze tree. The metal has turned a deep, greenish brown, and the light moves across it as the real leaves of the park shift overhead. This is the Frederic Chopin Monument, in the upper part of Royal Łazienki Park. Take a moment before we begin. Let your eyes travel from the water at your feet, up to the composer's face.

The man in the bronze is Fryderyk Chopin, as Poles spell his name. He was born in 1810 in the village of Żelazowa Wola, west of Warsaw, and he grew up in this city. He studied here and gave his first concerts here. In 1830 he left Warsaw for Vienna, and then Paris. He never came home. He died in Paris in 1849, still a young man, and he spent his whole adult life missing the country you are standing in.

Now look at how the sculptor chose to show him. Chopin does not stand on a column like a general. He sits. His body leans slightly, his head is tilted, and he seems to be listening to something only he can hear. The sculptor was Wacław Szymanowski, a Polish artist working in the style known as Secession. That is the Central European branch of Art Nouveau, a movement that loved flowing lines, plant forms, and strong emotion.

Above Chopin rises a willow tree, bent and windswept. This is no random choice. The willow is a symbol of the flat Mazovian countryside around Warsaw, the landscape of Chopin's childhood. Many visitors notice something else. Look at the shape of the branches above his head. People often say they look like a giant hand, or like fingers spread over a keyboard. Whether the sculptor intended it or not, once you see it, it is hard to unsee.

The road to this monument was long. Szymanowski's design came out of a competition held in the first decade of the twentieth century. At the time, Warsaw was under Russian rule, and public tributes to Polish national heroes were a delicate matter. The design also had critics. Some found it too modern and too dramatic. Then came the First World War. The bronze was finally unveiled here in 1926, in a Poland that had regained its independence only a few years earlier.

And here is the part of the story that many visitors do not know. During the Second World War, Germany occupied Warsaw. In 1940, German forces blew up this monument. It was one of the first monuments in the city that they destroyed. The bronze was broken up and taken away. At the same time, the occupiers banned Chopin's music in public. They understood very well what this composer meant to Poles.

So the figure in front of you is not the original. It is a recast. A model of the monument survived the war, and from it a new bronze was made. The monument returned to this spot in 1958, more than thirty years after its first unveiling. Look closely and you are seeing an act of stubborn memory. The city rebuilt its old town brick by brick after the war, and it rebuilt its Chopin too.

Around the monument, the setting is part of the experience. In front of the figure lies a long, shallow pool. On a still day the bronze willow and the composer appear upside down in the water. Beds of roses frame the space, and in early summer the air here fills with their scent. Benches line the edges. The design of this whole terrace pulls your attention forward, toward that seated figure under the tree.

Now imagine this place on a summer Sunday. Since the late 1950s, pianists have performed Chopin's music here, beside the monument, through the warm months. A piano is set up near the statue, and the music carries across the pool. People sit on benches, on the grass, on folded jackets. Children run along the paths. Older listeners close their eyes. It is one of the most loved summer rituals in Warsaw, and it has run for generations.

There is something fitting about it. Chopin wrote much of his music for small rooms and intimate salons, not for vast concert halls. His mazurkas and polonaises grew from Polish folk dances he heard as a boy in the countryside. To hear those same melodies outdoors, under trees, a short walk from where he grew up, feels close to their roots. The music comes home, at least for an afternoon.

If this monument moves you, the rest of Chopin's Warsaw is close by. His heart, by his own wish, was brought back to Warsaw after his death. It rests inside a pillar of the Church of the Holy Cross, on the Royal Route that runs toward the old town. There is also a museum devoted to his life and work in the city centre. This bronze, though, is the place where Warsaw chose to show him as a dreamer.

Łazienki Park itself deserves your time once you leave this terrace. The park grew around a royal summer residence of the eighteenth century, built for the last king of Poland. Paths lead downhill toward the Palace on the Isle, which sits on the water. Along the way you may meet red squirrels, who are often bold enough to come close, and peacocks wandering across the lawns. The park is quiet, green, and easy to get lost in.

Before you go, stand once more at the edge of the pool. Look at Chopin's face, turned slightly aside, and at the willow bending over him as if in a strong wind. Notice the sounds around you right now — leaves, footsteps, perhaps a distant piano if you are lucky. A young man left this city and never returned. Today he sits here in bronze, rebuilt after war, listening. As you walk away, listen with him for a moment.
