The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the open square and listen. The traffic of Warsaw sounds softer here. Grass and paving stones stretch out in front of you, and the light hits a long, low building covered in pale glass fins. Look closely at that glass. It carries letters, printed faintly across the surface. Some are Latin letters and some are Hebrew. Together they repeat one word... Polin.

That word is the key to everything inside. Polin is the Hebrew name for Poland. There is also an old legend about it, and you will hear it told in many versions. Legend has it that Jews fleeing persecution in western Europe came into the forests of this land. They heard a voice, or found a sign, that said two words: po lin. In Hebrew, that can mean "rest here." So they stayed. Whether the story is true or not, it shaped how a people understood their home.

Now think about where you are standing. This square lies in Muranów, a district that was once the centre of Jewish life in Warsaw. Before the Second World War, Warsaw had one of the largest Jewish communities in the world. During the German occupation, this area became part of the Warsaw Ghetto. The streets that stood here were destroyed. The neighbourhood you see today, with its postwar apartment blocks, was built on top of rubble.

Turn around and face the dark stone monument across the square. This is the Monument to the Ghetto Heroes, unveiled in 1948. It honours the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943, when young Jewish fighters rose up against the Germans with very few weapons. They knew they would almost certainly lose. They fought anyway. The monument shows figures carved in relief, some in struggle and some in exile. It stood here alone for decades before the museum was built opposite it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In 1970, the West German chancellor Willy Brandt came to this monument on an official visit. He laid a wreath. Then, without warning, he dropped to his knees and stayed there in silence. The gesture surprised everyone, including his own staff. Photographs of that moment travelled around the world. People still describe it as one of the most powerful apologies of the twentieth century. It happened right here, a few steps from you.

The museum building itself was designed by the Finnish architects Rainer Mahlamäki and Ilmari Lahdelma, working with a Polish partner studio. It opened in 2013. From outside, the building looks calm and almost plain, like a glass box. But walk through the entrance and the mood changes. A huge curving gap cuts through the middle of the building. The walls rise up on both sides in smooth, wave-like shapes.

People read this great hall in different ways. Some see the parting of the Red Sea, from the story of the Exodus. Others see a crack, a break in history, the empty space left by the Holocaust. The architects have spoken about both ideas. Stand in the middle and look up. Light falls through the opening at the top. The space feels both open and wounded at the same time... and that tension is deliberate.

One important thing to understand: this is not a Holocaust museum. That surprises many visitors. The core exhibition tells a story that covers about a thousand years. It begins with the first Jewish merchants and travellers who reached these lands in the Middle Ages. It follows centuries of trade, religion, scholarship, politics and daily life. The Holocaust is a central and devastating chapter. But it is one chapter among many. The museum wants you to see how Jews lived here, not only how they died.

The core exhibition opened in October 2014 and is arranged as a journey through galleries. You move from early encounters, through the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, into the small market towns where much of Jewish life took place. One gallery takes its name from a Latin phrase, Paradisus Iudaeorum, meaning "paradise of the Jews." Critics once used that phrase to mock Poland for its tolerance. The museum uses it to ask a question. How much of a paradise was it, really?

Then comes one of the greatest surprises in the building. In the middle of the exhibition, you walk under the painted ceiling of a wooden synagogue. It is a reconstruction of the ceiling from the synagogue in Gwoździec, a town now in Ukraine. The original wooden building was destroyed in the Second World War. Volunteers and craftspeople rebuilt the roof structure and the ceiling by hand, using traditional tools and old techniques. Look up at the colours: animals, plants, zodiac signs and Hebrew inscriptions, all painted in red, green and ochre.

Later galleries move into the modern age. You see the growth of big cities, newspapers, theatres, political parties and Yiddish culture. Then the exhibition darkens as it enters the war years. The space becomes narrower and quieter. Visitors often slow down here without being told to. The final galleries deal with the decades after 1945, when most survivors left Poland, and with the small Jewish community that remains and rebuilds today.

A few practical thoughts before you go in. The core exhibition is large, and many people underestimate it. Give yourself at least two or three hours if you can. An audio guide or a guided tour helps a great deal, because the galleries are dense with text and objects. Comfortable shoes matter more than you might expect. And the museum also runs temporary exhibitions, concerts, lectures and family events, so check what is on during your visit.

When you leave, come back out to the square and pause. Look again at the glass with its printed word. Look across at the monument, and at the ordinary apartment blocks around it, where people hang laundry and walk their dogs. This is a living neighbourhood built over a lost one. As you walk away, notice the street names and the small memorial stones set into the pavement nearby. Each one marks something that once stood here. Polin... rest here. The word still waits on the glass behind you.
