The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere a crow calls from the old trees above. The air smells of damp earth, moss, and the faint wax of candles left burning in red and amber glass. Light comes down in patches through chestnuts, maples and lindens, and it falls on angels, broken columns, weeping women carved in stone. You are standing in Powązki, the oldest working cemetery in Warsaw, and one of the quietest places in this busy capital.

This is Stare Powązki, which simply means Old Powązki. The cemetery opened in 1790, near the end of the eighteenth century. The land came from a local nobleman, Melchior Szymanowski, who gave it to the parish. At that time this ground lay outside the city. Warsaw has since grown all around it, so the walls that once marked the edge of town now sit inside a dense neighbourhood of tram lines and apartment blocks.

Near the main gate you will see the Church of Saint Charles Borromeo. It was built at the same time as the cemetery, in a calm classical style designed by the Italian-born architect Dominik Merlini, who worked for the last king of Poland. The church is modest compared to the monuments that surround it. That feels right. Here the building is not the main attraction. The people buried around it are.

Now look at the walls themselves. Along parts of the perimeter run the catacombs, long brick structures with burial niches stacked in tiers, one above another. These are some of the oldest burial places on the site, from the early nineteenth century. Many plaques are worn smooth. Some lean slightly, some have lost their letters. Walk slowly beside them and you can read names, dates, and short lines of love written by families two centuries ago.

Here is the first surprise. Fryderyk Chopin, Poland's most famous composer, is not buried here. He died in Paris and lies there. But his parents, Mikołaj and Justyna, rest at Powązki. And Chopin's heart, brought home by his sister, is kept in the Holy Cross Church on Krakowskie Przedmieście, in the city centre. So the family is divided across Warsaw and Paris, which says a great deal about Polish history in the nineteenth century.

That history was hard. For much of the nineteenth century there was no independent Polish state. Warsaw was under Russian rule, and open patriotism was dangerous. Cemeteries became places where people could gather, remember, and quietly show what they believed. A funeral could turn into a demonstration. A gravestone could carry a symbol that a public monument never could. Many carvings here, the eagles, the broken chains, the grieving female figures, carry that hidden meaning.

In the 1920s, after Poland regained independence, the cemetery created the Avenue of the Meritorious. In Polish it is called Aleja Zasłużonych. This is where the nation buries people it wants to honour. Writers, composers, actors, scientists and public figures lie along this route. Walking it feels like turning the pages of a Polish encyclopedia, one stone at a time. Follow the signs or ask at the gate for a map, because the paths here can confuse even locals.

Among the names you may find are Stanisław Moniuszko, the composer often called the father of Polish national opera. Nearby lies Bolesław Prus, the novelist whose book The Doll is one of the great portraits of nineteenth-century Warsaw. Władysław Reymont is buried here too. He won the Nobel Prize in Literature for his novel The Peasants. Like Chopin, his heart was placed separately, in the Holy Cross Church. Poles have a long habit of honouring hearts.

Here is the second surprise. Look for the grave of Krzysztof Kieślowski, the film director known for the Three Colours trilogy and The Decalogue. His monument shows two hands, the fingers forming a frame, the way a director looks at a scene before filming it. It is a simple, clever idea. Not far away rests Irena Sendler, the social worker who helped save Jewish children from the Warsaw Ghetto during the Second World War.

The cemetery did not escape that war. During the Warsaw Uprising in 1944, fighting reached this area, and many monuments were damaged. You can still see chipped stone and marks on some older tombs. Do not confuse this site with the Powązki Military Cemetery, a separate cemetery a short walk to the north. Many soldiers of the Uprising are buried there, in long rows of simple graves. Both places matter, but they tell different stories.

Keeping all this stone alive takes work and money. Since the 1970s, a volunteer committee has organised a public collection to restore the old monuments. Every year around All Saints' Day, well-known actors, journalists and artists stand at the gates with collection tins. Visitors drop coins and notes, and the money goes to conservators. Many of the restored angels and urns you see today were saved this way, by thousands of small gifts.

If you visit at the start of November, you will see Powązki at its most moving. On All Saints' Day, the first of November, families come to clean the graves, lay chrysanthemums, and light candles. After dark, the whole cemetery glows with thousands of small flames. The smell of wax hangs in the cold air. Even on an ordinary day, you will notice fresh flowers and burning candles on graves of people who died a century ago.

Before you leave, pause on one of the side paths, away from the main avenue. Notice the moss on the north side of the stones, the ivy climbing an old cross, the soft sound of leaves. Read one name you have never heard before. Someone loved that person enough to carve their name in stone. As you walk back toward the gate and the trams, carry that quiet with you for a few steps longer.

Bring coins for the annual restoration collection.
