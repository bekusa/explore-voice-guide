The guide

The story

Before you see anything, you hear it. A low, steady thump comes from somewhere deep in the building. It sounds like a heartbeat. It is meant to. A tall steel column runs up through the floors of this museum, and inside it a recording of a beating heart plays all day. Put your hand against the metal and you may feel it. The sound belongs to the city of Warsaw in the summer of 1944.

The building around you was not made for remembering. It is a red-brick industrial hall, once a power station for the city's trams. It stands in Wola, a western district of Warsaw. Wola matters to this story. It was one of the first places to fight in the Uprising, and it suffered some of the worst losses. The museum opened here in 2004, on the sixtieth anniversary of the Uprising. Many veterans were still alive to walk through its doors.

So what happened? By the summer of 1944, Warsaw had been under German occupation for almost five years. The Polish resistance, called the Home Army, had built a secret force inside the city. Soviet troops were moving west and were getting close to Warsaw. The Home Army leaders made a hard choice. They decided to free the capital themselves, before the Soviets arrived. They wanted Poles, not a foreign army, to stand in the liberated city.

The fighting began on the first of August, at five in the afternoon. Poles call this moment W-Hour. Thousands of young fighters came out of doorways and cellars at the same time. Many of them wore red and white armbands over ordinary clothes. Many had only pistols, homemade grenades, or no weapon at all. They expected to fight for a few days. In the end, the Uprising lasted sixty-three days.

Here is something many visitors miss. The anniversary is still marked across the city today. Every year, on the first of August at five o'clock, sirens sound over Warsaw. Traffic stops. People on the pavements stand still. Drivers step out of their cars. For one minute, a modern capital holds its breath. If you visit in early August, you may see this yourself. It is one of the most moving moments in the Polish calendar.

As you walk through the rooms, look at the walls and floors. The designers made the space feel like the city during the fighting. There are rough surfaces, narrow passages, and posters pasted up as if on a street corner. You will find old radio equipment, weapons, armbands, and letters. The insurgents ran their own radio station and printed their own newspapers. For a short time, parts of Warsaw lived as a free Polish city, with its own post, press, and courts.

The young people in this story were often very young indeed. Scouts, some only children, carried messages between districts. They ran a field post that delivered letters across the city under fire. Girls served as couriers, nurses, and liaison officers. In the photographs here, look at the faces. Many of them are teenagers. They smile for the camera in borrowed helmets. It is hard to look away.

Now, one of the most surprising rooms. In a high hall stands a full-size replica of an American Liberator bomber. Allied aircrews, including Polish, British, and South African airmen, flew long and dangerous missions to drop supplies to the fighters. Many planes and crews were lost. Much of what they dropped fell into German-held streets. The aircraft fills the room. It shows how far help had to travel, and how little of it arrived.

Another part of the museum invites you to go down. There is a reconstruction of the city's sewers. As the Germans took district after district, fighters escaped through these tunnels. They moved in darkness, often in deep, foul water, for hours. Silence was a matter of life or death. The passage here is cramped and low. It gives you only a small idea of that journey, but even a small idea is enough.

The ending was terrible. The Soviet army stopped on the other side of the Vistula River and did not come to help. On the second of October 1944, the Home Army surrendered. Estimates say that around two hundred thousand civilians died, along with many thousands of fighters. Then the Germans did something else. They expelled the remaining people and destroyed the city, building by building. Most of Warsaw was left in ruins.

That destruction is the subject of a short film shown in the museum's cinema. It is called City of Ruins. It uses old aerial photographs to rebuild, in three dimensions, a flight over Warsaw in 1945. The camera moves over street after street of broken walls. There is almost nothing standing. When you leave the museum later and see the rebuilt city, remember these images. Nearly everything around you was raised again by the people who came back.

Outside, behind the building, there is a quiet park. Along one side runs a long memorial wall. It carries the names of thousands of insurgents who were killed. Families still come here to find a name and leave flowers or candles. You may see the Anchor symbol on the wall and around the museum. It is a letter P joined to a W, for Fighting Poland. During the occupation, people painted it secretly on walls across the city.

Before you go, take a moment out here. Listen to the trams in the streets nearby, the same kind of trams this building once powered. Look at the brick, the names, and the young trees in the park. The heartbeat inside is still going. As you walk back into Wola, look at the new towers and the old courtyards side by side. This is a city that was nearly erased, and it chose to remember.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least three hours for the dense multi-floor exhibition.
Watch the City of Ruins film near the end of your visit.
Visit on 1 August at five pm to hear the city sirens.
Wear comfortable shoes for the low sewer passage section.
Save time for the memorial wall in the park outside.
