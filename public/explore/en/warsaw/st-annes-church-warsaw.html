The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the top of Krakowskie Przedmieście, close to Castle Square. Listen. Trams rumble somewhere below the hill. Footsteps cross the cobbles, and pigeons lift off the stone steps. In front of you rises a pale, columned facade that looks almost like a Roman temple. Then you step through the doors, and the light changes completely. The cool air inside smells of wax and old wood. This is Saint Anne's Church, one of the oldest churches in Warsaw.

The story begins in the fifteenth century. A widowed duchess, Anna of Mazovia, invited the Bernardine friars to Warsaw. The Bernardines were a branch of the Franciscan order, known for their simple brown habits and their preaching. Anna gave them land at the edge of the town, on the high bank above the Vistula River. A modest Gothic church and a monastery grew here. The church took its name from Saint Anne, the mother of the Virgin Mary... and perhaps also from the duchess herself.

That first church did not survive unchanged. Fire, war and time damaged it more than once over the centuries. Each generation repaired and rebuilt what came before. So the building you see today is a layered thing. Its bones are Gothic. Its interior is Baroque. Its face is Neoclassical. Think of it as a church that kept changing its clothes while the city around it changed too. Look closely at the side walls and the roofline, and you can sense those different periods meeting.

Now turn back to the facade. It dates from the late eighteenth century, the age of King Stanisław August Poniatowski, the last king of Poland before the partitions. The design is associated with the architect Chrystian Piotr Aigner. Neoclassicism was the fashion of the moment — a style that looked back to ancient Greece and Rome for calm order and clean lines. Notice the tall columns, the triangular pediment, and the figures of the four Evangelists. It is a sober, elegant front for a much older building.

Here is the first surprise. That restrained facade gives no hint of what waits inside. Step through the doors and the mood flips. The interior is warm, rich and full of movement. Gilded altars climb toward the ceiling. Carved figures lean out from the walls. Your eyes go upward almost without effort. Many visitors stop just inside the entrance, because the contrast between outside and inside is so sharp. Give yourself that pause. It is part of the experience.

Look up at the ceiling and the vaults. The paintings here are frescoes, painted directly onto wet plaster so the colour becomes part of the wall. Much of this work comes from the eighteenth century and is linked to Walenty Żebrowski, a painter who was himself a Bernardine friar. Painted architecture seems to open the ceiling into the sky. Columns that look solid are only paint. Domes that seem deep are flat. It is a trick of perspective, and it still works beautifully.

Take a slow walk along the nave, the long central space of the church. The side altars each carry their own saints and stories. Dark wooden pews, worn smooth by generations of hands, line the floor. The organ sits above the entrance, and if you are lucky you may hear it during a service or a concert. When the music fills this space, the gilding and the painted ceiling make sense in a new way. Baroque churches were built to be heard as well as seen.

Now for the second surprise, and it is a dramatic one. The church stands at the very edge of an escarpment, a steep slope that drops toward the river. After the Second World War, Warsaw built a new east-west road, cutting a tunnel beneath this hill. In 1949, during that construction, the ground began to move. The slope slipped, and the church itself was in real danger of sliding away. Engineers worked fast to stabilise the foundations and save the building.

Think about that moment. Much of Warsaw had just been destroyed in the war. Saint Anne's had come through those years damaged but standing. And then, in the middle of rebuilding the city, the earth under it shifted. The rescue succeeded, and the church held. Today the road and its tunnel still run below, carrying traffic under the hill. Most people who pass through it never realise how close this old church came to being lost.

Today Saint Anne's has a special role. It is known as Warsaw's academic church, closely connected with the University of Warsaw just down the street. Students come here for Mass, for quiet, and sometimes simply to sit for a while between lectures. Over the decades, especially in difficult political times, young people gathered in and around this church. So it is not only a monument. It is still a living parish, with weddings, services and ordinary daily prayer.

Step back outside and look to the side of the church. There you will find the free-standing bell tower. Its upper level works as a viewing terrace, and it is one of the best places to understand the shape of the Old Town. From up there, Castle Square opens out below you. The Royal Castle stands in its red brick, and the column of King Sigismund rises in the middle of the square. Beyond, the river and the right bank stretch away.

The climb is worth it if your legs agree. The stairs are narrow, but the reward is a view that ties the whole area together. You can trace Krakowskie Przedmieście, the old Royal Route, as it runs south past palaces and churches. You can see the rooftops of the Old Town, rebuilt after the war from paintings, drawings and photographs. And you can see how high this church really stands above the river valley.

Before you leave, stand once more on the steps in front of the facade. Feel the stone under your feet, polished by centuries of visitors and worshippers. Listen again to the trams below, the voices on the square, perhaps a street musician playing nearby. Behind you, the painted ceiling waits in its quiet colours. Ahead of you, the city moves on. As you walk away along the Royal Route, glance back once... and notice how calmly the church keeps watch over the edge of the hill.
