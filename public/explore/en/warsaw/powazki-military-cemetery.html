The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees the city noise fades to a low hum. Gravel crunches softly underfoot. Somewhere a candle flickers inside a red glass jar, and the air carries a faint smell of wax and damp leaves. Rows of grey stone and birch-wood crosses run away in every direction. This is Powązki Military Cemetery, in the north-west of Warsaw. It is one of the places where Poland remembers, out loud and in silence.

Before we walk, a small point of geography that confuses many visitors. Warsaw has two Powązki cemeteries. The older one, often called Old Powązki, dates from the late eighteenth century and holds generations of the city's families. The cemetery where you stand now is a separate place. It began in the early twentieth century, when Warsaw was still under Russian rule, as a burial ground for soldiers of the garrison.

That origin surprises people. A cemetery that is now so deeply Polish began as a resting place for the soldiers of an occupying empire. After Poland regained independence in 1918, the cemetery became the main military burial ground of the new state. Over the following decades it grew far beyond its first purpose. Today soldiers rest here beside poets, scientists, politicians, actors and ordinary citizens of Warsaw.

The largest presence here belongs to the summer of 1944. On the first of August that year, the Home Army — the underground Polish resistance — rose against the German occupation. The Warsaw Uprising lasted sixty-three days. Thousands of young fighters died, and many civilians died with them. After the war, many of the fallen were brought here and laid to rest in long, orderly quarters.

As you walk through these sections, look at the names of the units. The resistance was organised into battalions with code names, such as Zośka and Parasol. Many of the fighters were teenagers or students. On the simple crosses you will often see a pseudonym beside a real name. In the underground, a nickname protected a person and their family. Even in death, many are still remembered by those wartime names.

Among them lies Krzysztof Kamil Baczyński, one of the most important Polish poets of his generation. He was killed during the first days of the Uprising, at the age of twenty-three. Polish schoolchildren still read his verses about love, war and a world ending too soon. His grave is visited constantly. Do not be surprised if you find fresh flowers or a handwritten note left beside it.

At the heart of the Uprising quarters stands the Gloria Victis monument. The Latin words mean glory to the defeated. It is a striking idea for a nation to honour. The Uprising failed in military terms, and the city was almost entirely destroyed afterwards. Yet Poles remember it not as a defeat to forget, but as a choice to resist. Each year on the first of August, crowds gather here as sirens sound across Warsaw.

Now for a quieter, darker part of the story. Near the edge of the cemetery is an area known locally as Łączka, which means the little meadow. After the war, the communist authorities executed many people they considered enemies of the new regime. Some had fought in the anti-communist underground. Their bodies were buried here secretly, in unmarked pits, and their families were never told where.

For decades, people walked past this ground without knowing what lay beneath it. Only in the early twenty-first century did historians and archaeologists begin systematic exhumations. They worked to recover remains and identify them, often using DNA from living relatives. A memorial now stands on the site, listing the names of those identified. It is one of the most moving places in the cemetery, because the story is still not finished.

The cemetery also carries the memory of Katyń. In 1940, the Soviet secret police murdered thousands of Polish officers and members of the educated class in Russia and Ukraine. For many years, the communist government did not allow open talk about who was responsible. A memorial here became a place where families could grieve when public truth was still forbidden. Look for the crosses and plaques that carry the names of those lost.

A more recent loss is also remembered here. In April 2010, a plane carrying the Polish president and many senior officials crashed near Smolensk in Russia. They were travelling to mark the anniversary of Katyń. Several victims of that crash are buried at this cemetery, and a memorial honours them. So one tragedy became linked to another, seventy years apart, in the same stretch of Warsaw earth.

Not every grave here speaks of war. Along the Avenue of Merit, the Polish state has buried people it chose to honour — figures from science, culture and public life. Here you can walk from a wartime courier to a dissident of the 1980s, to a well-known writer or actor. The headstones vary greatly. Some are plain slabs. Others are sculpted with care, and each one reflects the taste of its time.

If you come in early November, you will see the cemetery at its most remarkable. On All Saints' Day, the first of November, Poles visit their family graves. The paths fill with people carrying flowers and candles. By evening, thousands of small flames glow among the trees, and the whole cemetery shines softly in the dark. Even on an ordinary weekday, though, you will rarely find a grave without a candle.

Before you leave, pause for a moment where you stand. Notice the birch crosses, the low hedges, the moss on older stones. Notice the sound of other footsteps, always slow and careful. As you walk back toward the gate, look again at the candles. Somebody lit each one recently. Here in Warsaw, memory is not only a story in a book. It is something people still carry here by hand.

Look closer

What to look for

Wartime code names carved beside real names on crosses.
Gloria Victis monument at the centre of Uprising quarters.
Name lists at the Łączka memorial for executed prisoners.
Red glass candle jars glowing on almost every grave.
Plain birch-wood crosses in the Uprising battalion quarters.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on All Saints' Day evening to see candlelit graves.
Join the crowds at Gloria Victis on the first of August.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel and earth paths.
Keep voices low and respect families tending graves.
Bring a candle to leave at a grave yourself.
