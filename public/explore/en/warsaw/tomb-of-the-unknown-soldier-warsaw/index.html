The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the traffic of central Warsaw moves around a wide, open square. But here, under these stone arches, the sound drops away. You may hear the soft hiss of a flame, the click of boots on stone, and the wind moving through the trees of the park behind. This is one of the quietest places in the city, and it is quiet on purpose.

You are standing at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, on Piłsudski Square, in the heart of the capital. In front of you is a short row of columns and arches. It looks like a gateway, or perhaps a fragment of something larger. That second impression is the right one. These arches are almost all that remains of a great building that once filled this side of the square.

That building was the Saxon Palace. It took its name from the Saxon kings who ruled Poland in the eighteenth century. Over time it was rebuilt and expanded, and by the twentieth century it housed the Polish General Staff. The central arcade, the part you see now, connected the two wings of the palace. Through these arches, people once walked from the square into the Saxon Garden beyond.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the early nineteenth century, the palace held a school, the Warsaw Lyceum. The father of Frédéric Chopin taught there, and for a time the Chopin family lived in the building. So the young composer spent part of his childhood within these walls — long before they became a war memorial.

The tomb itself was created in 1925, a few years after Poland regained its independence. After more than a century of foreign rule, the new state wanted a place to honour the soldiers who had fought for it. Like other nations after the First World War, Poland chose to bury one unnamed soldier. That one soldier would stand for all the others whose names were lost.

The remains came from the battlefields around Lwów, the city now called Lviv, in Ukraine. Fierce fighting took place there in the years after 1918. According to the well-known account, the coffin was chosen by a mother whose own son had died in that fighting and was never found. She chose one coffin from several. The soldier she chose was carried to Warsaw and laid here.

Now look down, at the slab in the centre. The inscription is in Polish. It reads, in translation: here lies a Polish soldier, fallen for the Fatherland. The words are simple. There is no name, no rank, no date of birth. That absence is the point. Anyone who lost a son, a brother or a father without a grave could imagine that he rests here.

In front of the slab, a flame burns day and night. On the pillars around you, stone plaques carry the names of battles. They cover many centuries of Polish history, from medieval fields to the Second World War and later conflicts. Read a few as you walk around. Some names will be familiar. Many will be new, and each one marks a place where Poles fought and died.

The Second World War brought this whole area to ruin. After the Warsaw Uprising of 1944 was crushed, German forces systematically destroyed much of the city. In the final weeks of that year, they blew up the Saxon Palace. The explosions brought down almost the entire building. Yet the section around the tomb remained standing. After the war, Warsaw kept this fragment exactly as a fragment — a reminder of what the city lost.

The plaques carry another, quieter story. During the communist period, battles fought against Soviet Russia did not fit the official version of history. Those plaques were removed. After the political changes of 1989, they returned to the pillars. So when you read the list of battles, you are also reading a record of how Poland has argued with its own past.

You will notice the soldiers standing guard. They come from a ceremonial honour guard of the Polish Army, and they stand still in all weathers. The changing of the guard happens regularly through the day, with slow, exact movements. Foreign heads of state lay wreaths here on official visits. On national holidays, the square fills with uniforms, flags and crowds.

Now turn and look at the open square behind you. In 1979, it was called Victory Square, and Pope John Paul II celebrated a huge open-air Mass here on his first visit home as pope. He prayed for the Holy Spirit to come down and renew the face of the earth. Then he added: this earth. Many Poles still see that moment as an early spark of the change that came a decade later.

Before you leave, walk through the arches and into the Saxon Garden. It is one of the oldest public parks in the city, with old trees, fountains and quiet paths. The contrast is worth feeling. On one side, open stone and ceremony. On the other, shade and birdsong. For years there have been plans to rebuild the Saxon Palace, so this view may change in the future.

For now, take one last look at the flame and the empty arches above it. They hold up nothing. The building they once carried is gone. And still they stand, with a single unnamed soldier beneath them. As you walk away, listen again — for the boots on stone, the soft hiss of the flame, and the city slowly returning around you.
