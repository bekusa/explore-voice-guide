The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Around you is a wide, open square of grass and paving stones. Trees line its edges, and city traffic hums somewhere beyond them. It is a quiet space, almost too quiet for central Warsaw. In front of you rises a tall block of dark stone. Its surface looks heavy and smooth, with a faint grey-blue shimmer when the light catches it. Out of that stone, bronze figures push forward. This is the Monument to the Ghetto Heroes, and the ground beneath your feet was once inside the Warsaw Ghetto.

This district is called Muranów. Before the Second World War, it was the heart of Jewish Warsaw. It was a crowded neighbourhood of tenement houses, workshops, schools, synagogues and markets. Yiddish, Polish and Hebrew were all spoken on these streets. Warsaw then held one of the largest Jewish communities in the world. After the German invasion in 1939, the occupiers walled off this area and forced Jewish residents from across the city into it. Hundreds of thousands of people were packed into a few city districts. Hunger and disease killed many of them.

In the summer of 1942, the Germans began mass deportations. Trains left from a loading point called the Umschlagplatz, a short walk north of here. Most passengers were sent to the death camp at Treblinka. Within a few weeks, the great majority of the ghetto's people were gone. Those who remained understood what deportation meant. Small groups of young people began to organise. They gathered weapons, built hidden bunkers, and prepared to fight back, even though they knew they could not win.

On 19 April 1943, on the eve of Passover, German forces entered the ghetto to clear it. They met armed resistance. This was the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising. The fighters belonged mainly to two groups, the Jewish Combat Organisation and the Jewish Military Union. Their leader in the Combat Organisation was a young man named Mordechai Anielewicz. The fighting lasted for weeks. The Germans burned the district building by building. In May, Anielewicz and many of his comrades died in a bunker on Miła Street, not far from here.

After the uprising, the Germans destroyed the ghetto almost completely. When the war ended, this area was a field of rubble. Survivors described a landscape of broken brick that stretched for blocks. Here, among those ruins, the monument went up. It was unveiled on 19 April 1948, exactly five years after the uprising began. The sculptor was Nathan Rapoport, a Jewish artist born in Warsaw, who had spent the war in the Soviet Union. The architect who designed the setting was Marek Suzin.

Now look closely at the stone itself. According to a widely repeated account, the dark blocks were originally quarried in Sweden on the orders of the Nazi regime. The plan, the story goes, was to build a German victory monument with them. Instead, the stone ended up here, honouring the people the Nazis tried to erase. Historians still discuss the details of this story. But it is told so often in Warsaw that it has become part of how people understand the monument.

The side facing you, toward the museum, carries the large bronze group. Here are the fighters. A man stands in the centre with a grenade in his hand. He is often understood as Anielewicz, though the figures are symbols more than portraits. Around him are other fighters, a woman holding a child, and an older man. Flames seem to rise behind them. The bronze bodies lean forward out of the stone. Rapoport wanted them to look as if they were breaking out of the wall itself.

Here is something many visitors miss. Walk around to the back of the monument. The rear side tells a very different story. It is a low relief carved into the stone, and it shows a procession of people walking with bowed heads. They are the deported, the ones who went to their deaths. German soldiers with helmets and bayonets appear among them. The front shows resistance. The back shows suffering. Together, the two sides hold both memories of the ghetto at once.

Look also at the inscription. It is written in three languages — Polish, Yiddish and Hebrew. The words dedicate the monument from the Jewish people to their fighters and martyrs. On either side of the stone, notice the bronze candelabra, shaped like menorahs. Menorahs are the branched candle holders of Jewish tradition. Nearby, set into the ground, there is also a smaller, older memorial stone. It was placed here in 1946, before the larger monument existed, when the ruins were still everywhere.

In December 1970, a moment happened here that changed European history. Willy Brandt, the Chancellor of West Germany, came to lay a wreath. Then, unexpectedly, he knelt on the wet steps in silence. Nobody had planned it. Photographs of the German leader on his knees travelled around the world. Many people saw it as an act of humility and apology. Today a small memorial to that moment stands close to the square, on the street named after him.

Now turn to face the modern building across from the bronze fighters. This is POLIN, the Museum of the History of Polish Jews. Its pale glass walls form a quiet contrast with the dark stone. Through the middle of the building runs a curved opening, like a gap or a passage. The museum tells the story of a thousand years of Jewish life in Poland. It does not focus only on the war. That choice is deliberate. The monument remembers the end. The museum remembers everything that came before.

If you come here in April, you may see small yellow paper daffodils pinned to coats across the city. This tradition began with Marek Edelman, one of the last surviving commanders of the uprising. For many years, on each anniversary, he brought yellow flowers, often daffodils, to this monument. After his death, the museum began handing out paper daffodils every April. Volunteers walk the streets with them. For one day, Warsaw wears yellow in memory of the ghetto.

From here, you can follow a memorial route north through the neighbourhood. Granite blocks along the way mark people and events of the ghetto. The route ends at the Umschlagplatz memorial, where the deportation trains departed. Look at the buildings around you as you walk. Most are plain post-war apartment blocks. Some stand slightly raised, because they were built on top of the rubble. In a sense, the whole district rests on what was destroyed.

Before you leave, stand once more in front of the bronze fighters. Notice how the light falls across their faces and fists. Notice the people around you. Some are tourists, some are schoolchildren, and some are locals crossing the square on their way home. Life goes on here, on ground that once held unbearable loss. As you walk away, listen again to the quiet of the square. That quiet is part of the memorial too.
