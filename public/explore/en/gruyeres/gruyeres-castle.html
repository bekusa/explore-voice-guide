The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below the walls, a cowbell knocks against the slope. The cobbles under your feet are uneven, worn smooth by centuries of boots. The air smells of cut grass and, faintly, of woodsmoke and melted cheese drifting up from the little town. Ahead of you, pale stone walls and a round tower sit on a green ridge. Behind them, the pre-Alps rise in folds of forest and pasture. This is Gruyères Castle, and it has watched over this valley for a very long time.

The castle stands on a narrow hill above the valley of the Sarine river, in the canton of Fribourg, in western Switzerland. This is French-speaking country. The position was chosen with care. From up here, a lord could see anyone moving along the valley floor, whether traders, pilgrims or soldiers. Today the view is gentler. Look south, and you will often see the sharp summit of the Moléson, the local mountain, standing over the meadows where the region's famous cows graze.

The main structure dates back to the thirteenth century, although the site was likely fortified earlier. For roughly three centuries, this was the seat of the Counts of Gruyère. Nineteen counts in succession ruled from here. They held a small but proud territory of mountain pastures, villages and trade routes. Their wealth came from the land, and above all from the cattle that grazed the high alpine meadows each summer. Cheese and livestock were the real currency of these hills.

Now, a small surprise. Look for the bird that appears again and again — on flags, on shields, above doorways in the town. It is a crane, white on a red background. In French, a crane is a grue. The name Gruyères and the crane are tied together. According to one popular legend, a leader named Gruerius founded the settlement after he saw a crane fly overhead. That is folklore, not documented history. But the counts embraced the bird, and the town still carries it proudly.

The story of the counts ends with money troubles. The last count, Michel, lived in a costly way and fought expensive wars. By the middle of the sixteenth century, his debts had grown too large. In 1554, he went bankrupt. His creditors were the powerful neighbouring cities of Fribourg and Bern. They divided his lands between them. Michel left, and the long line of the Counts of Gruyère came to an end. A family that ruled for centuries was undone, in the end, by unpaid bills.

After that, the castle became an administrative house. For nearly three hundred years, bailiffs sent by Fribourg lived here. A bailiff was a governor who collected taxes, kept order and judged local disputes. The castle shifted from a noble home to an office of government. This period lasted until the political changes of the mid-nineteenth century, when Switzerland reorganised itself as a modern federal state. Around that time, the canton decided to sell the old building.

This is where the castle's second life begins. In 1849, the property passed to private owners, the Bovy family from Geneva, along with their relatives the Ballands. They did something unexpected. Rather than strip the castle, they filled it with art. The Bovys loved painting and invited artist friends to stay. The best known was the French landscape painter Camille Corot. He spent time here, and his work remains inside. So a medieval fortress became, for a few decades, a kind of artists' retreat.

As you move through the rooms, notice how the layers sit side by side. Heavy stone walls and deep window recesses belong to the medieval castle. Painted panels and Romantic-era decoration belong to the nineteenth-century owners. Romanticism, in simple terms, was an artistic movement that loved ruins, mountains, legends and strong emotion. The Bovys looked at this old fortress and saw a stage for those ideas. Their taste shaped much of what visitors see today.

In 1938, the canton of Fribourg bought the castle back, and it opened to the public as a museum. That is a second surprise for many visitors. This building was not preserved by an unbroken noble line. It was saved by bankruptcy, by bureaucrats, by art lovers, and finally by the canton itself. Each owner left marks. The result is less a frozen moment and more a record of how different centuries saw the same walls.

Step outside again, into the courtyard and the gardens. There is a formal garden in the French style, with clipped hedges and neat geometric beds. From the walls, the landscape opens in every direction. On a clear morning, the light comes sharply off the slopes, and every chalet roof stands out. In the late afternoon, the ridges turn soft and blue. This is a good place to slow down. Let the castle go quiet around you for a moment.

The town below is part of the experience. Gruyères is a small medieval settlement with one main street, closed to most traffic. Old houses with wooden shutters line it on both sides, and a stone fountain sits in the middle. Local tradition also remembers Chalamala, said to be a jester at the court of the counts. Stories about him are part legend, part memory. Near the town, you will also find a museum devoted to the Swiss artist H. R. Giger, a very different kind of art.

And of course, there is the cheese. Gruyère cheese takes its name from this region. It is a hard cheese made from raw cow's milk and aged for months. Down in the valley, a demonstration dairy shows how it is made, from copper vat to wheel. You may also see meringues with thick double cream on restaurant menus. That cream is another local speciality. Many visitors find the food tells the region's story as clearly as the stones do.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Feel the wind that comes up the slope from the valley. Listen again for the cowbells. Look at the crane on a nearby flag, and at the tower above you. Counts, bailiffs and painters all stood on this same ground and looked at these same mountains. As you walk back down the cobbled street, notice the old doorways and the worn steps. The castle will stay in view behind you for a long way.
