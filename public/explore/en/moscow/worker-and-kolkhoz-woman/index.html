The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along the wide avenue nearby, and above you, steel catches the light. On a bright day the surface glints silver, almost white. On a grey Moscow afternoon it turns soft and pewter-coloured, like the sky behind it. Two giant figures stride forward together, one arm each thrust high into the air. Between their raised hands, a hammer and a sickle cross. This is Worker and Kolkhoz Woman, one of the best-known sculptures of the Soviet era.

First, the name. A kolkhoz was a collective farm, the system that reorganised Soviet agriculture in the late 1920s and 1930s. So the two figures are symbols. The man is an industrial worker, and he holds the hammer. The woman is a farm worker, and she holds the sickle. Together they stand for the alliance of factory and field, the two pillars of the Soviet state. Their joined emblem became the hammer and sickle on the Soviet flag.

The sculptor was Vera Mukhina, one of the leading Soviet artists of her generation. She had studied in Paris before the Revolution, and her work blends classical training with modern energy. Look at the poses. The figures are not standing politely. They are walking, almost running, with their weight thrown forward. Mukhina wanted motion, and she achieved it in metal. Even from far away, the sculpture seems to push toward the horizon.

Here is the first surprise. This monument was not made for Moscow at all. It was created for the 1937 International Exposition in Paris. There, it crowned the top of the Soviet pavilion, a tall, stepped building designed by the architect Boris Iofan. The pavilion acted as a pedestal. Visitors walking along the Seine saw the two figures rising above the fairground, scarves streaming behind them.

That exhibition carried heavy political weight. Directly across from the Soviet pavilion stood the pavilion of Nazi Germany, crowned by an eagle. The two buildings faced each other like rivals on a stage. It was 1937, only a few years before war would turn that rivalry into catastrophe. In a sense, this sculpture was a message in steel, sent to the whole of Europe.

Now look closely at the material. The figures are covered in thin sheets of stainless steel, attached to an inner steel framework. In the 1930s, using stainless steel for a sculpture of this size was a bold experiment. Engineers used welding to join the sheets, a technique that was still new for monumental art. The result was light enough to transport and strong enough to stand outdoors. It also meant the surface would shine rather than rust.

Building it was a race against time. The parts were fabricated in Moscow, then cut into many pieces and sent to Paris by rail. There, workers reassembled the giant figures on top of the pavilion. Stories say the work went on day and night to meet the opening. Whatever the exact details, the finished piece drew large crowds, and it became one of the most talked-about images of the exposition.

After the exhibition closed, the sculpture came home. It was installed here, near the northern entrance of the great exhibition grounds now known as VDNKh. That name stands for the Exhibition of Achievements of the National Economy, a vast park of pavilions celebrating Soviet industry and farming. The location made sense. A monument to workers and farmers stood at the gate of a showcase for their work.

Here is the second surprise. For decades, the sculpture stood on a pedestal much lower than the Paris original. Many people felt it looked squat and lost some of its soaring effect. In the early 2000s the monument was taken apart for a long and careful restoration. When it returned, it stood on a new, taller pedestal. That pedestal was built to echo the shape of Iofan's Paris pavilion. Today there is an exhibition space inside it.

You may recognise this image even if you have never seen it in person. Since the late 1940s, the two figures have appeared in the opening logo of Mosfilm, the largest film studio in Russia. For generations of viewers, the sight of the hammer and sickle rising against the sky meant that a film was about to begin. Many people know the sculpture from the cinema screen long before they ever visit Moscow.

Walk slowly around the base and watch how the figures change. From the front, they form a single wedge, moving as one. From the side, you can see the long diagonal lines of their bodies and the great sweep of fabric behind them. That flowing scarf is not decoration. It helps the eye read speed and wind. It also gives the composition its strong horizontal line against the sky.

Think about what this sculpture meant to different people. For some, it is pure propaganda, a polished image of a system that caused enormous suffering in those same years. For others, it is a masterpiece of engineering and design, and a tribute to ordinary labour. For many Muscovites, it is simply a landmark, a meeting point, a familiar shape on the way to the park. All of these readings can be true at the same time.

Before you move on, look up once more. Notice the seams in the steel, the small lines where sheets meet. Notice how the light slides across the folds of the clothing. Listen to the city around you, the cars, the voices, the wind. These two figures have been walking forward for almost ninety years, and they have not arrived anywhere yet. As you leave, glance back over your shoulder. They will still be striding toward the sky.

What to look for

The long scarf streaming behind the two figures.
Thin seams where the steel sheets were joined.
The stepped pedestal echoing the Paris pavilion shape.
The forward-leaning stride of both figures' legs.
