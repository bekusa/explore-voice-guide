The guide

The story

Listen first. Trains announce themselves somewhere behind you, a low hum of engines and departure calls. Wheeled suitcases rattle over paving stones. Cars circle a wide square, and pigeons lift off the cornices of three very different railway stations. Then lift your eyes. Above all of this, a pale stone tower climbs in stepped layers toward a slender spire and a star. This is the Hotel Leningradskaya, which many maps list under the Hilton name, and it is one of the most unusual buildings in Moscow.

The tower belongs to a family. Muscovites call them the Seven Sisters. These are seven high-rise buildings raised across the city in the years after the Second World War, during the final years of Joseph Stalin's rule. Together they changed the Moscow skyline. They include Moscow State University, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and the Hotel Ukraina. Each one marks an important point in the city. This sister was placed to greet travellers arriving by train.

Here is the first surprise. This is the smallest of the seven sisters. Standing at its base, that is hard to believe. The tower still rises well over a hundred metres to the tip of its spire. But compare it with the University tower on the Sparrow Hills, and this one looks almost modest. Its builders seem to have accepted that. They did not try to win on height. Instead, they put their energy into ornament, detail, and a very particular style.

Look closely at the upper levels. The tower narrows in stages, like a stack of boxes, each one smaller than the one below. At the corners you can see pointed forms and small turrets. Many of these shapes come from old Russian church architecture. Think of the tented roofs of sixteenth and seventeenth century Moscow churches, tall and pointed like a closed umbrella. The architects borrowed that language and placed it on a modern steel-framed skyscraper. The result mixes medieval Russia with mid-twentieth century America.

That American echo is not an accident. Soviet architects of this period studied the skyscrapers of New York with great interest. The stepped silhouette, the vertical lines, the crowning spire... you can find similar ideas in Manhattan towers of the 1920s and 1930s. But the Seven Sisters were meant to answer them, not copy them. They had to look Soviet, and they had to look Russian. The Leningradskaya leans harder into the Russian side than most of its sisters.

Now turn around and look at the square itself. Its official name is Komsomolskaya Square. Locals often call it the Square of Three Stations. To one side stands the Leningradsky station, where trains leave for Saint Petersburg. Next to it is the Yaroslavsky station, the Moscow starting point of the Trans-Siberian Railway. Across the square rises the Kazansky station, with its own tall clock tower. Few places in the world gather so many long journeys into one open space.

This explains why the hotel stands exactly here. For generations, this square was the front door of Moscow for people from the north, from Siberia, and from the Volga. Arriving travellers stepped out of a station and saw this tower before anything else. It was a message, written in stone. The message said: you have arrived in the capital of a great state. The hotel opened in the mid-1950s, just as that state was about to change direction.

Here is the second surprise. Soon after the building was finished, its style fell out of favour. Stalin died in 1953. His successor, Nikita Khrushchev, wanted cheap, fast, practical housing for millions of people. In the mid-1950s, the Soviet leadership publicly criticised architectural excess, meaning costly decoration that served no practical purpose. Buildings like this one became examples of what not to do. The era of the Seven Sisters ended, and a new era of plain concrete apartment blocks began.

So in a way, this tower stands at the end of a road. It is one of the last expressions of a style that the country soon turned away from. That gives it a strange double life. Visitors today often find it romantic and theatrical. In its own time, critics saw it as wasteful. Both views are part of its story, and both are worth holding in mind as you look up at the spire.

If you step inside, the mood shifts. The lobby is not large, but it feels dense with material. Expect dark polished wood, warm bronze, heavy chandeliers, and carved detail on ceilings and columns. The light is soft and golden, very different from the grey daylight on the square. The hotel went through a major restoration in the 2000s, and much of the historic interior character was preserved. If you are not a guest, behave quietly and respectfully. This is a working hotel.

Do not leave the area without going underground. The Komsomolskaya metro station on the circle line lies right beneath the square. Its main hall is famous for its yellow vaulted ceiling, its mosaic panels, and its rows of chandeliers. It was designed in the same years and with the same ambition as the tower above you. Seeing the two together gives a clear picture of how this period imagined public space: grand, decorated, and built to impress ordinary passengers.

A practical note on names. Hotel brands in Moscow have changed in recent years, and the signage on the building may not match your map. Do not worry about that. The tower itself has not moved, and locals have always known it simply as the Leningradskaya. Ask for it by that name, and almost anyone around the square will point you to it.

Before you go, find a spot on the square where you can see the tower and at least one station in the same view. Notice how the pale stone of the hotel catches the light differently from the brick and plaster around it. Listen again for the trains. Some of the people walking past you have just crossed half a continent. For decades, this tower was the first thing they saw. For a few moments now, it is the last thing you will see, as you walk on into the city.
