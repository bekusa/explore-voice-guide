The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the granite pavement and feel the bridge under your feet. There is a faint tremor each time a heavy bus passes. Wind comes off the Moskva River and carries the smell of cold water and exhaust. Behind you, the red brick of the Kremlin wall rises from the embankment. Ahead, the onion domes of Saint Basil's Cathedral show their striped colours above the trees. This is Bolshoy Moskvoretsky Bridge, the Great Moskva River Bridge. It may be the most photographed river crossing in Russia.

The name is simple. Moskvoretsky means simply of the Moskva River, and Bolshoy means big or great. A smaller bridge with a similar name crosses a nearby channel to the south, so the word great tells the two apart. The bridge links the slope beside Red Square, a short descent called Vasilyevsky Spusk, with the old district of Zamoskvorechye on the far bank. That name means, quite literally, beyond the Moskva River. For centuries this crossing was the main road south out of the city centre.

People have crossed the river at roughly this point for a very long time. In earlier centuries the crossings here were wooden structures and floating bridges. They rose and fell with the water and were often damaged by spring ice. Later, in the nineteenth century, a more permanent bridge took their place. That was rebuilt again later in the same century with iron spans. Each version served carts, pilgrims, and traders moving between the Kremlin markets and the merchant streets to the south.

The bridge you are standing on now is a product of the 1930s. In 1935 Moscow adopted a sweeping general plan to rebuild the capital for the Soviet age. Streets were widened, embankments were faced in stone, and many old bridges were replaced. This crossing was rebuilt in the second half of that decade and opened in 1938. The new bridge is wider and flatter than the old ones. It was designed for cars, trolleybuses, and the parades that moved toward Red Square.

Look down over the parapet and you can see how it works. The structure is a reinforced concrete arch bridge in three spans, with the central span the longest. The concrete is dressed in pale granite, so the bridge looks like solid stone. This was typical of the period. Engineers built with modern materials, but architects wanted the result to look permanent and heavy, like something carved rather than poured. The long, low curve keeps the view of the Kremlin open from the water.

Now turn upstream, toward the west. From here you see one of the classic views of Moscow. The Kremlin wall runs along the riverbank, broken by towers with green and red tent roofs. Behind the wall, the gold domes of the Kremlin cathedrals catch the light. Further along, another bridge crosses the river, and on clear days the large white and gold dome of the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour appears beyond it. Painters and photographers have framed the city from this spot for generations.

Here is the first surprise. In May 1987, a young West German pilot named Mathias Rust flew a small private plane all the way from Finland into Soviet airspace. He was not stopped. He came down over central Moscow and landed on this very bridge. He then taxied toward Saint Basil's Cathedral and climbed out near Red Square. Crowds gathered, and he was soon arrested. The event embarrassed the Soviet military, and several senior officers lost their posts afterwards.

Think about that while you watch the traffic. The roadway that carries cars every few seconds once served as a runway for a light aircraft. Overhead wires for trolleybuses and street lighting made the landing even more risky. Today the story is told in many languages by guides on this bridge. For many visitors, it is the detail they remember longest — a reminder that even the most guarded places can have an open door.

The second story is darker, and it is more recent. On a cold night in February 2015, the opposition politician Boris Nemtsov was shot and killed while walking across this bridge. He was a former deputy prime minister of Russia. In the years after, his supporters left flowers, candles, and photographs at the spot where he fell. You may still see small bunches of flowers along the railing on the side facing the Kremlin. Many people stop here quietly, without speaking.

Now turn the other way, downstream toward the east. Just along the north bank is Zaryadye Park, a modern landscaped park built in the 2010s. Its most unusual feature is a concrete viewing platform that curves out over the river like a letter V. People call it the floating bridge, although it does not cross the water. From here you can often see small figures standing at its tip, looking back at you. It is one of the newest pieces of this old riverbank.

Walk to the southern end and the mood changes. Zamoskvorechye feels lower and calmer than the area around Red Square. This was once a district of merchants, craftsmen, and churches. Narrow streets run south from the river, and many old houses still stand among newer buildings. Straight ahead from the bridge, the road continues as Bolshaya Ordynka Street. Its name recalls the Horde, the Mongol power to which Moscow once paid tribute, and the old road that led toward it.

The river itself is worth a moment. In summer, long white tour boats pass slowly under the arches, and their decks fill with people waving toward the Kremlin. In winter, ice can form along the edges, and the water turns a dark steel grey. At night the bridge lamps light up, the Kremlin towers glow, and the red stars on top of the towers shine against the sky. The same view feels very different from season to season.

Before you leave, rest your hand on the granite parapet. Feel how cool and smooth it is, worn by many hands and many winters. Look once more at the Kremlin wall, then at the domes of Saint Basil's, and then at the quiet streets to the south. This bridge holds all of them together. As you walk away, listen to the wind over the water and the hum of traffic behind you. The city keeps crossing here, as it has for centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the western pavement for the clearest Kremlin view.
Come at dusk to see the Kremlin towers light up.
Cross fully to Zamoskvorechye for quieter streets and cafes.
Hold phones firmly; river wind gusts strongly across the bridge.
Combine with a walk through Zaryadye Park just downstream.
