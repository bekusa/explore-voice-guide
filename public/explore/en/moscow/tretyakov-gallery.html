Tretyakov Gallery

⏱ 6 min

The story

You step in from a quiet lane in an old Moscow district. The noise of the street fades behind you. In front of you hang the faces of a whole nation — its saints, its peasants, its dreamers. Landscapes glow on the walls. Battles and quiet evenings share the same rooms. This is Russian art gathered under one roof, and almost all of it is here. It began with one man and a simple idea.

That man was Pavel Tretyakov. He was a merchant, not a noble. He made his money in textiles and trade. But his real passion was painting. In 1856 he began to buy works by living Russian artists. At the time, few people thought Russian art was worth collecting. The great collectors chased European names. Tretyakov disagreed, and he spent the rest of his life proving them wrong.

He had a plan that was unusual for his day. He wanted to build a public collection of his country's art. He bought directly from the painters, often before the paint was fully dry. He visited their studios. He argued over prices. He took risks on young and unknown names. Slowly, room by room, his house filled with canvases until there was barely space left on the walls.

His home stood here, in the Zamoskvorechye district, south of the river. As the collection grew, he added gallery rooms onto the house, just to hold it all. Visitors began to come. In 1892 Tretyakov gave the whole collection to the city of Moscow. He handed over more than a thousand paintings, along with drawings and icons, as a gift to the public. He kept nothing back for himself.

The front you see today came later. The artist Viktor Vasnetsov designed this facade between 1902 and 1904. He drew on old Russian forms — the shape of a fairy-tale palace, with a carved crown above the door. The red and white front has become the gallery's signature. Many people know the building from this face alone.

Inside, the collection moves through the centuries. The oldest rooms hold icons painted on wood. Here is one of the most famous works in all of Russian art — the Trinity, painted by the monk Andrei Rublev in the early fifteenth century. Three angels sit around a table in silence. The colors are soft. The mood is calm. In a church that forms part of the gallery hangs an even older treasure, a tender image of mother and child, venerated for centuries.

Walk on, and the rooms open into the nineteenth century. This was a great age of Russian painting. You meet huge canvases full of people and feeling. One whole wall is given to a single enormous picture by Alexander Ivanov, which shows a crowd by a river at the moment a holy figure appears in the distance. He worked on it for twenty years. Ilya Repin fills another room with a religious procession winding through a crowd under a hot sky. You can almost feel the dust and the heat.

Vasily Surikov painted history on a grand scale. His scenes show crowds, snow, and hard choices from the Russian past. Ivan Shishkin painted forests so carefully that you can almost count the pine needles. His bears in a misty morning wood have become one of the best-loved images in the country, printed on walls and sweet wrappers alike.

Not every painting is loud. There are quiet portraits and landscapes too. Faces of writers, children, and strangers look back at you. Vasily Perov, Valentin Serov, and others caught the inner life of their sitters. Isaac Levitan filled his canvases with still rivers and autumn birch trees. Stand close to any of them, and the eyes or the light seem to shift as you move.

Tretyakov did something else unusual. He paid artists to paint the leading writers and composers of his day. He wanted faces to match the famous names. Because of him, we know how the writer Dostoevsky looked in life, and how Tolstoy appeared in his later years. The gallery became a portrait of a whole culture, not only its art.

The gallery you are in holds Russian art up to the early twentieth century. A second building, across the city on Krymsky Val, continues the story into modern and abstract times. Together they form one museum, split between two homes. Most visitors come here first, to the old house in the lane, where the collection began.

Think about how all this started. One merchant, buying pictures that others ignored, built a record of his country's soul. He did not keep it for himself. He gave it away while he was still alive. Almost every Russian schoolchild knows these paintings, and many meet them first in a classroom, long before they ever stand in these rooms.

So take your time here. Let the faces watch you for a while. Many of them have hung in this place longer than any of us have been alive. In the hush between the frames, a quiet collector is still doing what he loved most — sharing the art of his country with anyone who walks in.

Key facts

Pavel Tretyakov began collecting Russian art in 1856.
He donated the collection to Moscow in 1892.
Viktor Vasnetsov designed the facade between 1902 and 1904.
Andrei Rublev painted the Trinity in the early fifteenth century.
Tretyakov was a textile merchant, not a nobleman.

What to look for

Ivanov's huge canvas filling an entire wall.
Shishkin's bears in the misty morning pine forest.
Repin's crowded procession moving under a hot sky.
Levitan's still rivers and golden autumn birches.

Practical tips

Start in the icon rooms before the nineteenth-century halls.
Allow at least two hours for the main building.
Visit the New Tretyakov on Krymsky Val for modern art.
Buy tickets online in advance to skip the queue.
