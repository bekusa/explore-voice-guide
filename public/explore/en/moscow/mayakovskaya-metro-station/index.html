The guide

The story

Listen first. A train sighs to a stop, its doors fold open, and a wave of footsteps spreads across polished stone. Then, for a moment, the hall goes quiet. Above you, a row of small domes glows like a line of lanterns. Inside each one, a piece of sky looks down. This is Mayakovskaya, one of the deep stations on Moscow's green line. Many people consider it among the most beautiful stations of the Moscow Metro.

Before we look up, look around at the shape of the space. The station is a long central hall, framed by slender columns on both sides. Beyond them, the platforms run alongside the tracks. The columns are light and narrow, and they let air and light pass between the hall and the trains. That lightness was a technical achievement. Building a column station this deep underground, with supports this slim, was a bold engineering choice in the late 1930s.

The station opened in 1938, during the second stage of the metro's construction. Its architect was Alexey Dushkin, one of the leading designers of the early Moscow Metro. Dushkin wanted a hall that felt open and airy, not heavy and buried. Stand in the middle and you can feel what he was aiming for. The ceiling seems to float. The arches seem to lift rather than press down. Few visitors expect this feeling of space at the bottom of a long escalator.

Now move closer to one of the columns and touch it. The surface is cool and smooth — gleaming steel, bent into gentle curves. This stainless steel came from the same industrial world that built Soviet aircraft. In the 1930s, aviation was a symbol of progress and of the future. So the metal under your hand carries a message. This station was meant to feel modern, fast and confident, like a machine built for flight.

Look at the edges of the arches. Between the bright steel, there are bands of pink and deep rose stone. This is rhodonite, a mineral prized in Russia for its warm colour. Its soft red tone balances the cold shine of the metal. Then glance down at the floor. Marble in contrasting shades forms clean geometric patterns under your feet. Millions of commuters walk across this pattern every week, and most of them never notice it.

And now — look up. Along the centre of the hall runs a line of oval niches set into the ceiling. Each niche holds a mosaic, lit from its edges. Together they form a series that is often called a day of the Soviet sky. The artist was Alexander Deineka, a painter known for strong, clear images of athletes, workers and aviators. His idea was simple and beautiful. Underground, where no sky exists, he gave passengers a sky to look at.

Walk slowly down the hall and follow the series. The scenes show aircraft, parachutists, athletes and blossoming branches, all seen from below, as if you are lying on your back in a meadow. The colours change as you go, suggesting the movement of the day, from morning light toward evening. Here is a detail many people miss. The mosaics were not made in Moscow. They were assembled in Leningrad, now Saint Petersburg, in the workshop of the mosaic master Vladimir Frolov, and then brought to the capital.

The station quickly drew attention beyond the Soviet Union. A model of Dushkin's design received a Grand Prix at the 1939 World's Fair in New York. For an underground railway station to win such an award was unusual. It showed that the Moscow Metro was not just transport. It was also a showcase, built to demonstrate what the new state could create. That ambition is visible everywhere you look in this hall.

Only a few years later, the station played a very different role. During the Second World War, when German forces approached Moscow, the deep stations of the metro became air raid shelters. People came down here with children and bundles of belongings to wait out the bombings. The same mosaic sky that celebrated flight looked down on families hiding from aircraft. It is worth pausing for a moment to think about that contrast.

The most famous wartime event here took place in November 1941. On the eve of the anniversary of the October Revolution, a ceremonial meeting was held in this hall, underground and away from the bombing. Joseph Stalin spoke to the assembled officials. A train stood at the platform as part of the setting. The next day, troops marched through Red Square and then went straight to the front. For many people in the country, the meeting in this station was a sign that Moscow would hold.

The name of the station comes from Vladimir Mayakovsky, the poet of the revolutionary era. His poetry was loud, rhythmic and full of energy, and he loved the language of machines and speed. In a way, this steel and sky fit him well. Above your head, at street level, is Triumfalnaya Square, where a large statue of the poet stands. Concert halls and theatres line the streets around it, so the neighbourhood above has long been a place of performance and public life.

Remember that this is a working station, not a museum. Trains arrive every couple of minutes. Commuters move with purpose, and they may not slow down for visitors. The best way to enjoy the hall is to stand near the centre, out of the main flow, and let the crowds pass around you. Early or later hours are calmer. Then you can walk from one oval to the next and see the sky change.

Before you leave, stand still for one last look. Notice the soft light falling from the ovals onto the steel. Notice how the pink stone warms the arches. Listen to the rhythm of the trains, arriving and leaving, the same rhythm this hall has heard since the 1930s. When you ride the escalator up toward the real sky above Triumfalnaya Square, look up once more. You will be leaving one sky and stepping into another.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit outside rush hours for a calmer, clearer view.
Stand in the centre hall, away from the commuter flow.
Walk the full length to follow the mosaic sequence.
Exit to Triumfalnaya Square to see the poet's statue.
Keep photography quick and step aside for passengers.
