The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Traffic hums somewhere beyond the brick wall, but in here the loudest sounds are gravel under your shoes and crows calling from the lime trees. The air smells of damp earth and cut flowers. Fresh carnations lie on cold granite, red against grey. Paths run in straight lines between rows of headstones, and almost every stone carries a face, a sculpture, or a name you may already know.

This is Novodevichy Cemetery, on the southwestern edge of central Moscow. It sits directly beside the Novodevichy Convent, whose towers and golden domes rise over the wall to your north. The convent was founded in 1524 by Grand Prince Vasili the Third. He built it to mark the capture of the city of Smolensk from Lithuania. Today the convent is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the cemetery grew up in its shadow.

For centuries, burials happened inside the convent walls. Nuns, noble families, and wealthy merchants were laid to rest close to the churches. Around the turn of the twentieth century, the grounds became too crowded, and a new cemetery opened just outside the southern wall. That is the ground you are standing on now. Over the following decades, it became the most prestigious place in the country to be buried... second only, in Soviet times, to the Kremlin Wall itself.

That status shaped everything here. During the Soviet era, a place at Novodevichy was not something a family simply bought. It was granted. Writers, composers, scientists, generals, actors, and party officials were buried here as a kind of final honour from the state. So a walk through these rows is a walk through the twentieth century of Russia — its art, its science, its wars, and its politics, all side by side.

Start with the writers. Anton Chekhov, the playwright and short-story master, was buried here in 1904. His grave is modest, with a small white monument in a style that echoes old Russian wooden architecture. Nearby rests Nikolai Gogol, author of Dead Souls. He was not first buried here. In 1931, Soviet authorities moved his remains from the Danilov Monastery, which was being closed, and reburied him in this cemetery.

Here is the first surprise. When Gogol was moved, his original gravestone was left behind — a heavy, dark block of Crimean stone that admirers called Golgotha. Years later, the widow of the writer Mikhail Bulgakov found that stone and placed it on her husband's grave. Bulgakov had loved Gogol deeply. So today the author of The Master and Margarita lies beneath a stone that once marked the grave of his literary hero.

Music has its own corner of this place. Dmitri Shostakovich is buried here, and so is Sergei Prokofiev. Prokofiev died on the same day as Joseph Stalin in March 1953. Because the whole city was in mourning for Stalin, flowers were almost impossible to find, and very few people came to the composer's funeral. The great opera singer Feodor Chaliapin died in Paris, but his remains were brought back and reburied here in the 1980s.

Now look at the sculpture around you. Many monuments here are true works of art, and some are deeply personal. One of the most loved belongs to Yuri Nikulin, a famous circus clown and film actor. His bronze figure sits casually, a cigarette in hand, as if he has just stepped out for a break between shows. Visitors often leave flowers at his feet, and sometimes a small toy or a coin.

The political graves tell a stranger story. Nikita Khrushchev, who led the Soviet Union after Stalin, was removed from power in 1964. When he died, he was not given a place in the Kremlin Wall. Instead he came here. His tombstone was made by Ernst Neizvestny, a sculptor Khrushchev had once insulted in public. The monument is split into black and white stone, interlocking around his bronze head — a picture of a man with both dark and light in his record.

That is the second surprise. Khrushchev had attacked Neizvestny's modern art at an exhibition in the early 1960s. Yet the family chose him for the grave, and he accepted. Many people see the black and white design as a fair and honest judgement — not praise, not condemnation, but both at once. Stand in front of it and decide for yourself which half seems heavier.

The more recent leaders lie here too. Boris Yeltsin, the first president of the Russian Federation, was buried here in 2007. His grave has a wide monument shaped like a waving Russian flag, with white, blue, and red stone. Mikhail Gorbachev, the last leader of the Soviet Union, was buried in 2022 next to his wife, Raisa. Her grave had been here since 1999, and he chose to lie beside her rather than anywhere grander.

Not every story here is famous. Walk the quieter rows and you will see the faces of test pilots, engineers, ballet dancers, and soldiers, often carved with the tools of their work — a propeller, a violin, an open book. Along the inner wall, small plaques mark urns placed in niches. Old women tend some graves, wiping dust from the stone and changing the water in jars of flowers. This is still a working cemetery, and a place of real grief.

When you leave, walk around to the pond on the north side of the convent. Locals say that the view of the white walls reflected in this water helped inspire Tchaikovsky's Swan Lake, though that story is a legend rather than a proven fact. Either way, it is a calm place to sit. Behind you, the gravel paths and the quiet faces in stone remain, holding a century of the country's memory in a few green hectares.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pick up a cemetery map near the entrance before exploring.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel and stone paths.
Keep your voice low; families still visit and mourn here.
Combine your visit with a walk around the convent pond.
Allow at least an hour to find the main graves.
