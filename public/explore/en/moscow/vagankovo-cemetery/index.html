The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Moscow is still out there, a low hum behind the walls. But in here, the loudest sound is often gravel under your shoes, a crow in the old trees, and the soft scrape of someone sweeping leaves from a family plot. The air smells of damp earth, cut flowers, and sometimes candle wax. You are standing in Vagankovo Cemetery, one of the oldest working cemeteries in the city, and one of the most visited.

This place began in a crisis. In 1771, a plague epidemic swept through Moscow. Thousands died, and the city's old churchyards could not hold them. Worse, burying the dead inside the city spread fear of infection. Authorities ordered new cemeteries to be opened outside the city limits of the time. Vagankovo was one of them. So the first graves here belonged not to the famous, but to ordinary Muscovites taken by disease.

Where does the name come from? Nobody can say for certain. It comes from the old village of Vagankovo, which once stood on this land. One popular explanation links the word to an old Russian verb meaning to fool around or play tricks. Locals say jesters or entertainers once lived in the area. It is a charming story, but treat it as folklore rather than settled history. What is certain is that the village name stayed, long after the village itself disappeared into the growing city.

Near the main entrance, look for the church. It is the Church of the Resurrection of the Word, built in the early nineteenth century in the Empire style. Empire style was the formal, classical taste of that era, with columns, clean lines, and calm symmetry. The pale walls and the rounded dome give the entrance a quiet dignity. Funeral services still take place here. You may see families gathered on the steps, so walk past gently and keep your voice low.

The cemetery also holds the memory of war. Soldiers who were wounded at the Battle of Borodino in 1812, during Napoleon's invasion of Russia, were brought to Moscow. Many of them died and were buried here. A memorial to these men stands in the cemetery today. It is easy to walk past it on your way to more famous names. Pause for a moment. These were young men from all over the empire, and most of their names are not recorded on any stone.

Here is a story that surprises many visitors. In 1896, Moscow celebrated the coronation of Nicholas the Second, the last Russian emperor. Huge crowds gathered at Khodynka Field for a public festival, with promised gifts of food and souvenirs. Rumours spread, the crowd surged, and a terrible crush followed. More than a thousand people died. Many of the victims were buried here at Vagankovo, in a common grave. A memorial marks the spot. A celebration of a new reign ended in mourning, on the very first days.

Now to the names that bring most visitors through the gate. The poet Sergei Yesenin is buried here. Yesenin wrote lyrical, aching verse about the Russian countryside, love, and loss. He died in 1925, in a hotel room in Leningrad, at just thirty years old. His body was brought back to Moscow. Even now, people leave flowers, notes, and sometimes small glasses at his grave. Some stand and recite his lines quietly to themselves.

Near Yesenin lies Galina Benislavskaya. She was his close friend, his literary helper, and by many accounts deeply devoted to him. About a year after his death, in 1926, she took her own life at his grave. She was buried close to him. It is one of the saddest stories in this cemetery, and it is true. Visitors who know it often leave a flower for her as well, so that she is not forgotten beside the more famous name.

The grave that draws the largest crowds belongs to Vladimir Vysotsky. He was an actor, a poet, and above all a singer with a guitar and a rough, powerful voice. His songs about soldiers, prisoners, and ordinary people circulated across the Soviet Union on home-made tape recordings, often passed from hand to hand. He died in July 1980, during the Moscow Olympics. Despite little official announcement, enormous crowds came to say goodbye. His monument shows a figure straining against ropes, an image of a man pulling free.

Walk further, and you will find many other names from Russian culture. The painter Vasily Surikov, known for vast historical canvases, rests here. So does Alexei Savrasov, a landscape painter who helped teach Russians to see beauty in plain, muddy spring fields. The actor Andrei Mironov, much loved for his film comedies, is buried here too. So is the singer and poet Bulat Okudzhava, whose gentle songs about Moscow's old streets are still sung by friends at kitchen tables.

Notice how different the graves look from one another. Some are simple iron crosses, painted and repainted over the years. Some are heavy granite slabs with photographs etched into polished stone. Others are small fenced plots with a bench inside. This is a Russian custom. Families come to sit with their dead, to tidy the grave, and sometimes to share a quiet meal. The little fences and benches are not decoration. They are places for visiting.

This is still a working cemetery, not a museum. Burials continue, and families come every day. So the mood changes as you walk. One path is full of visitors searching for a famous grave. The next is empty except for an elderly woman wiping a photograph clean with her sleeve. The trees are tall and old, and in autumn the paths fill with yellow leaves. In winter, snow sits on the crosses and the whole place goes very quiet.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Look at the nearest stone. Read the name, even if it means nothing to you. Somebody chose those letters carefully, and somebody perhaps still brings flowers. As you walk back toward the gate and the noise of the city, listen for that change in sound, from gravel and birds to engines and voices. Vagankovo sits right in the middle of Moscow's daily life, and that is exactly why it feels so close.

Practical tips

Keep voices low near funerals at the church.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven gravel and root-lifted paths.
Visit on a weekday morning for quieter paths.
Bring a single flower if you wish to honour someone.
