The guide

The story

Step through the gate and the city noise drops away. Your shoes meet old paving, then soft earth and fallen leaves. Ahead, a tall red-brick cathedral rises between the trees. Its domes catch whatever light Moscow offers today. Crows call from the lime trees. Somewhere a bell rings once, then stops. Around you, rows of gravestones lean slightly, as if listening. This is Donskoy Monastery, one of the quietest places in this enormous city, and one of the most layered.

The story begins with an icon and an army. In 1591, a Crimean Tatar force rode toward Moscow. The Russian troops set up a fortified camp on this southern approach to the city. In a portable field church, they placed a revered icon of the Mother of God, known as Our Lady of the Don. The attackers turned back without a decisive battle. Many Muscovites saw the icon's protection behind that retreat.

The name of the icon carries an older legend. According to tradition, it travelled with Prince Dmitry of Moscow in 1380, when his army fought the forces of the Golden Horde, the Mongol-led state that dominated these lands, near the River Don. That victory gave Dmitry his nickname, Donskoy, meaning "of the Don". Whether this exact icon was really on that battlefield is a matter of faith and tradition rather than proof. But the link shaped how Russians saw this monastery for centuries.

After the Tatars withdrew, Tsar Feodor, the son of Ivan the Terrible, founded a monastery on the site of the camp. It was a thank-offering, built where the danger had been met. The first church here was modest. Look for a smaller, older church in the grounds, often called the Small Cathedral. It dates from the late sixteenth century, from the monastery's first years. Its simple white walls and compact shape feel very different from the grand red building nearby.

That grand building is the Great Cathedral, raised in the late seventeenth century. A royal woman funded much of it — Tsarevna Ekaterina, a sister of Peter the Great. Notice the shape. The cathedral stands in tiers, with five domes placed in a cross pattern. Around it, the lower galleries and the bright red brick with white stone trim follow the Moscow fashion of that time. Inside, the painted walls and the tall gilded iconostasis, the screen of icons before the altar, fill the space with dark gold.

Now turn and look at the walls themselves. The heavy brick enclosure with its towers went up at the end of the seventeenth century and the start of the eighteenth. By then, Moscow did not really need fortresses on this side. The walls were partly about defence and partly about prestige. Walk close and you will see arrow slits, decorative brick patterns, and towers with pointed tops. Above the main gate stands a church, a common Russian way of blessing everyone who enters.

For the first surprise, find the large sculpted panels set into the inner side of the walls. These carved figures did not start here. They came from the original Cathedral of Christ the Saviour, the huge church near the Kremlin that the Soviet authorities blew up in 1931. Workers saved some of its marble reliefs and brought them here. For decades, this monastery served as a store and museum for fragments of demolished Moscow. Each panel is a piece of a building that no longer exists.

That leads to the darker chapter. After the 1917 revolution, the new Soviet state closed monasteries across the country. Donskoy was shut by the late 1920s. Its buildings became, in turn, an anti-religious museum and later part of a museum of Russian architecture. The monks were gone. Yet the walls protected the churches from demolition. In a strange way, being turned into a museum helped this place survive while many other churches in Moscow were destroyed.

Here is the second surprise. In the early 1920s, Patriarch Tikhon, head of the Russian Orthodox Church, lived here under close watch by the Soviet authorities. He died in 1925 and was buried in the Small Cathedral. Over the following decades, the exact location of his remains became uncertain. In 1992, his relics were found inside that church, during restoration work. He is now honoured as a saint, and many visitors come specifically to pray at his shrine.

The monastery returned to the Church in the early 1990s, and monks live here again. Life inside the walls now follows the rhythm of services. You may hear chanting drift out of an open door. You may see women covering their heads before they enter a church. Visitors are welcome, but this is a working monastery, not a theme park. Speak quietly, keep phones low, and expect people to pause, cross themselves, and bow at doorways.

The cemetery is the part many travellers remember most. For centuries, this was a burial ground for nobles, scholars, and writers. The historian Vasily Klyuchevsky lies here, as does the thinker Pyotr Chaadaev. In more recent years, the grounds received figures returned from exile after death. The philosopher Ivan Ilyin and the White Army general Anton Denikin were reburied here in 2005. The writer Alexander Solzhenitsyn was buried here in 2008.

Take a slow walk among the older graves. Many tombstones are carved from dark granite or pale limestone. Some are shaped like small chapels or sarcophagi. Others carry Old Russian lettering, worn soft by rain and frost. Moss fills the cracks. Each one marks a family that once moved through the same city streets you crossed this morning. Beyond the walls, the nearby New Donskoy Cemetery holds a sadder memory — common graves linked to the political repressions of the Soviet era.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the Great Cathedral. Look up at the domes, then down at the paths, uneven from centuries of footsteps. Listen to the trees, the birds, a distant tram. This ground has been a battle camp, a royal project, a prison for a patriarch, a museum of broken buildings, and a monastery again. As you walk back toward the gate, notice how the city sound slowly returns, and how quiet you have become.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly; women may want a headscarf for churches.
Come on a weekday morning for the quietest cemetery walk.
Allow extra time to explore the graves along the walls.
Keep voices low and avoid photographing worshippers during services.
