The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement and tilt your head back. Above the traffic, a long frieze of glazed tiles catches the light. Blues, greens, and soft golds shine against the pale stone, almost like a painting hung outdoors. Cars hum along the square behind you, and the theatre district goes about its business. Up there, though, a pale princess looks down from a dream. This is the Hotel Metropol, and few buildings in Moscow carry their story so openly on the outside.

The hotel sits on Teatralny Proyezd, the Theatre Passage, facing the wide open space of Teatralnaya Square. The Bolshoi Theatre is a short walk away, and the Kremlin is not much farther. This placement was deliberate. At the turn of the twentieth century, this was where Moscow came to see and be seen. A grand hotel here would sit at the centre of the city's social and cultural life — and it still does.

The building belongs to the style that Russians call modern, known elsewhere as Art Nouveau. This movement rejected heavy imitation of older styles. Instead, it reached for flowing lines, natural forms, and the blending of art with architecture. Look at the rounded corners, the decorative ironwork, and the way the ornament seems to grow from the walls. Nothing here is a copy of a Greek temple or a Gothic church. It was meant to feel new, and in its time, it did.

The idea for the hotel came from Savva Mamontov, a railway industrialist and one of the great patrons of Russian art. Mamontov did not only collect paintings. He gathered artists around him, funded their work, and supported opera and theatre. He imagined something larger than a hotel here — a cultural complex that would bring art directly into public life. That ambition shaped every surface you see.

The design is linked to William Walcot, an architect of British descent who was born in the Russian Empire. The architect Lev Kekushev, a central figure of Moscow's Art Nouveau, also played an important role in the project. Their work combined the practical needs of a modern hotel with an almost theatrical sense of decoration. The result is a building that feels both solid and slightly dreamlike.

Now return to that great tiled panel at the top of the main facade. It is the work of Mikhail Vrubel, one of the most original Russian artists of his era. The panel is often called the Princess of Dreams. It draws on a play by the French writer Edmond Rostand about a troubadour who falls in love with a distant princess he has never met. Vrubel turned that story of longing into ceramic — and placed it high above a busy Moscow street.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The panels are majolica, a type of glazed, painted ceramic. Majolica holds colour for a very long time, so the blues and greens you see are not simply painted onto plaster. They are fired into the tile itself. Other panels along the facade were designed by Alexander Golovin, an artist also known for his work on theatre sets. Walk along the building slowly, and you will see that each section tells its own small scene.

The second surprise is written in words. Running along the facade is an inscription in Russian. It carries a thought attributed to the philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche — that only after you finish building your house do you realise how much you have learned. It is an unusual sentence to place on a luxury hotel. Perhaps it also reads as a quiet confession, because this project was far from smooth.

During construction, Mamontov's fortunes collapsed. He faced financial ruin and legal trouble connected to his railway business, and he lost control of the project. A fire also damaged the building while it was still being finished. Even so, the hotel was completed in the early years of the twentieth century and opened as one of the most modern in the city. It offered comforts that were still rare in Moscow at the time, from electric light to modern plumbing.

Then history arrived at the door. During the revolutionary fighting in Moscow in 1917, the area around the square saw heavy combat, and the hotel was damaged. Soon afterwards, the new Soviet government took over the building. For a period it became known as the Second House of Soviets. Officials lived and worked inside, and important political meetings were held under its roof. A palace for wealthy travellers became, for a time, an office of the revolution.

Over the decades of the Soviet era, the Metropol returned to its role as a hotel, often hosting foreign guests, writers, and diplomats. Through all of this, the building held on to much of its original decoration. A major restoration later in the twentieth century brought back many of its historic interiors. Inside, the main restaurant hall is known for its tall stained-glass ceiling, which fills the room with soft, coloured daylight.

If you step inside, notice how the decoration continues beyond the facade. Look for carved wood, patterned glass, and metalwork in the stairways and corridors. This is what the Art Nouveau architects wanted — a single artistic idea, carried from the street to the smallest door handle. Be respectful of guests, though. This is a working hotel, and not every space is open to casual visitors.

Back outside, take a moment on the square. In front of you, the Bolshoi rises with its columns. Behind the hotel, fragments of the old Kitay-gorod wall recall a much older Moscow. And above you, Vrubel's princess still looks down over the traffic. As you walk on, glance back once more — and watch the tiles change colour as the light moves across the facade.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross to Teatralnaya Square for a full view of the facade.
Visit on a bright day to see the majolica colours glow.
Combine your visit with a walk past the Bolshoi Theatre.
Ask staff politely before exploring the hotel's interior spaces.
Reserve ahead if you hope to dine in the stained-glass hall.
