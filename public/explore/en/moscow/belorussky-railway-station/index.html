The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on Tverskaya Zastava Square. Listen. There is the low rumble of traffic circling the square, the hiss of a train releasing its brakes somewhere behind the walls, and the quick footsteps of travellers who are always slightly late. In front of you, a long facade in pale green and white rises above the crowd. Its towers and rooflines look more like a small palace than a place to catch a train. This is Belorussky railway station, Moscow's gateway to the west.

The name tells you where the rails lead. Belorussky means "Belarusian", and for generations this has been the station for Minsk, Smolensk, and the long journey toward Poland and central Europe. Today, many visitors pass through for a shorter trip. The airport express train to Sheremetyevo leaves from here. So this building is a doorway twice over — to the western borderlands, and to the wider world beyond the runway.

The story begins in the nineteenth century. A railway line was laid west from Moscow toward Smolensk, and a first, more modest station opened here in 1870. In those years it was known as Smolensky station, after the city at the other end of the line. As the railway pushed further west, the name changed with it. Over the following decades the station carried several names, including Brestsky and Alexandrovsky. It became Belorussky in 1936, and that name has stayed.

The building you see now replaced the original station in the years just before the First World War. It was completed around 1912, and it is usually credited to the architect Ivan Strukov. Look at how the facade is broken into towers, gables, and steep roofs. The style mixes Russian Revival — a fashion that borrowed shapes from old Russian churches and wooden houses — with touches of Art Nouveau, the flowing, decorative style that was popular across Europe at the time.

Here is a detail many travellers miss. Notice how the station is not one flat wall but a series of separate blocks, each with its own roof. From a distance this gives the building a rhythm, like a row of small houses joined together. The white trim outlines windows and edges, so the green walls seem to sit inside a frame. On a grey Moscow day, that pale colour keeps the whole square from feeling heavy.

Now think about the name of this square — Tverskaya Zastava. A "zastava" was a gate or checkpoint at the edge of the old city. This was where the road from Moscow left for Tver and, further on, for Saint Petersburg. Travellers stopped here, papers were checked, and the city ended. So long before any railway arrived, this spot was already a threshold. The station simply took over a role the place had played for centuries.

The square once held another landmark. In the nineteenth century, a triumphal arch was raised here to celebrate Russia's victory over Napoleon. In the 1930s, as Moscow was rebuilt and its main streets widened, the arch was taken down. Years later, a new version was built far from here, on Kutuzovsky Prospekt. If you have seen that arch elsewhere in the city, you have seen a monument that first belonged to this crossroads.

Belorussky station holds a heavy place in Russian memory. During the Second World War, trains carrying soldiers to the western front left from these platforms. Families stood here to say goodbye, many for the last time. And in 1945, when the war in Europe ended, trains bringing soldiers home arrived here too. The station saw both the farewells and the returns, and for many Moscow families it became a place tied to that war.

That memory lives on in film and music. In 1970, a Soviet film called "Belorussky Station" told the story of former wartime comrades meeting again decades later. Its song, written by the poet and singer Bulat Okudzhava, became widely known across the country. Many older Russians still associate this building with that melody. When they say the name of the station, they are often thinking of the song as much as the trains.

There is another musical link worth knowing. A march called "Farewell of the Slavic Woman" was written in 1912 — the same period when this building was being finished. The tune became a classic accompaniment for troops leaving for war, played on platforms across Russia. Near the station you can find a sculpture inspired by that march, showing a woman seeing a soldier off. It is a quiet piece of bronze in a very busy place.

In front of the station you may also notice a statue of the writer Maxim Gorky. It stood on this square for decades in the Soviet period. During reconstruction of the area in the 2000s, it was removed and stored. Later it was returned to the square. Its journey away and back again fits this place well. At Belorussky, things leave, and things come home.

Step inside, and the mood changes from monument to working station. Departure boards flicker with destinations. Announcements echo off the walls. People carry suitcases, plastic bags, sometimes boxes tied with string. Underneath it all, the metro runs. Belorusskaya metro station sits right beside the square and connects two lines, including the Circle Line. So thousands of people move through this area every hour, most of them never looking up at the towers.

Before you leave, turn back and look at the facade once more. Try to see it as a traveller in 1912 would have seen it — new paint, new rails, the whole of Europe suddenly within reach. Then look at the people around you now, rushing for the airport train or a suburban line. The building has stayed. Only the journeys have changed. As you walk away, listen for one last train in the distance... another departure, another arrival, just like every day here for more than a century.
