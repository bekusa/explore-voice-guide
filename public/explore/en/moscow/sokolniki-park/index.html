The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Underfoot, the gravel crunches softly. Overhead, the wind moves through tall birches and old pines, and the sound rises and falls like distant surf. Somewhere nearby a bicycle bell rings, then fades. You are standing in Sokolniki Park, one of the oldest green spaces in Moscow. The city is still close, just beyond the trees, but here the air already smells of damp leaves and resin. Let's walk together and see how this forest became a park.

Start with the name, because it holds the first story. Sokolniki comes from the Russian word for falconers. Centuries ago this was wild forest on the edge of Moscow, and it was a royal hunting ground. In the seventeenth century, Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich was passionate about falconry. His falconers kept and trained their birds in this area. The hunters and their birds have been gone for centuries. The word they left behind still names the park, the district around it, and the metro station.

Now look at how the paths are laid out. From the main entrance, long straight alleys open in a fan shape, spreading out like the spokes of a wheel. Russians call these straight forest cuttings "proseki". The radial pattern is one of the park's most distinctive features. It came from cutting clear lines through the old forest. If you stand at the central circle and turn slowly, each alley pulls your eye deep into the trees. Many visitors walk straight through without noticing this geometry at all.

For a long time, Sokolniki was not a manicured park. It was a grove at the edge of the city. In the nineteenth century, it became a favourite escape for Muscovites. Families rented wooden summer houses, called dachas, among the trees. On holidays, crowds came out for picnics, music and tea served from steaming samovars, the large metal urns used to boil water. Toward the end of that century, the city took the grove into public ownership. Slowly, the wild forest turned into a park for everyone.

Here is a detail that surprises many visitors. A famous Russian painting was inspired by these alleys. In the late 1870s, the young artist Isaac Levitan painted a work called Autumn Day. Sokolniki. It shows a woman in dark clothes walking alone down a path lined with autumn trees. Levitan later became one of the great masters of the Russian landscape. The painting now hangs in the Tretyakov Gallery in central Moscow. In autumn, you can still find paths here that look very much like his canvas.

Take a breath and notice the trees around you. Sokolniki is not just a planted park. It still carries part of the old forest within its borders. Birch, pine, oak, maple and linden grow side by side. The deeper you walk, the wilder it becomes. Paths narrow, the light turns green and soft, and the city noise drops away. To the northeast, the park continues toward Losiny Ostrov, or Elk Island, a large protected forest. Together they form a long green belt across this part of Moscow.

Now for one of the most surprising chapters in the park's story. In the summer of 1959, at the height of the Cold War, Sokolniki hosted the American National Exhibition. For the first time, millions of Soviet citizens could see American cars, consumer goods and a model home. In that model kitchen, the American vice president Richard Nixon and the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev had a heated public argument. The exchange became known as the Kitchen Debate. It was about washing machines, but really about two competing ways of life.

That exhibition left a lasting mark. The area near the park became a place for trade fairs and exhibitions. Today, the Sokolniki exhibition centre still hosts events in its halls, close to the park. So, when you walk here, remember that this quiet forest once stood at the centre of world politics. Superpower leaders argued in a kitchen built a short walk from these birches. It is a strange and very human footnote to a tense era in world history.

Walking back toward the centre of the park, you reach the main circle and its fountain. On warm days, this is the social heart of Sokolniki. Children chase pigeons around the water, couples sit on the benches, and older Muscovites stroll slowly in pairs. In summer, you may hear music from a stage or a café. Look for the flower beds. The park is known for its seasonal planting, and there is a rose garden where you can stop and enjoy the scent.

Sokolniki also changes completely with the seasons. In spring, the birches turn a fresh pale green and the air smells of wet soil. Summer brings cyclists, roller skaters and long picnics on the grass. Autumn is perhaps its finest season, when the alleys fill with gold and copper leaves, just as in Levitan's day. In winter, snow covers the paths, and people come to skate and ski. The same alleys feel like four different places through the year.

Notice the people around you, because they are part of the park's character. Sokolniki is not mainly a tourist site. It is a neighbourhood park for Muscovites. You will see runners on early morning circuits and grandparents pushing prams. Dog walkers greet each other by name, and friends play chess or table tennis. Some come for a quick walk after work, others for a whole lazy Sunday. Spend a little time watching, and you will see an ordinary day in Moscow.

The park is also easy to reach, and that has shaped its story. Sokolniki station was part of the very first line of the Moscow Metro, which opened in 1935. For generations, this made the park an easy escape from the city centre. You could ride underground for a few stops, climb the stairs, and walk into the forest. Even today, that contrast is striking. In a few minutes you go from a busy street to the sound of wind in the pines.

If you have time, follow one of the radial alleys all the way to its end. The deeper sections of the park feel quieter and more like forest. Here you may hear woodpeckers, see squirrels running up the tree trunks, and smell mushrooms after rain. The light changes too. Near the entrance it is open and bright. Further in, it filters down in narrow beams between the trunks. This is the Sokolniki that falconers and nineteenth century picnickers would still recognise.

Before you move on, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Look down one of the long, straight alleys and let your eye follow it into the trees. Listen to the wind in the branches, the footsteps on the gravel, and the voices drifting from the fountain. A royal hunting forest became a place of summer dachas, a Cold War stage and a neighbourhood park. All those layers are here under the same trees. As you walk away, notice how slowly the city sounds return.
