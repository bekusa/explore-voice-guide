The guide

The story

Stand on the cobblestones of Red Square and feel how the ground slopes gently downhill toward the river. Just ahead, the striped domes of Saint Basil's Cathedral crowd the sky in red, green and gold. In front of them, on a tall block of polished granite, two bronze men share a single moment. One stands and points. The other sits, sword in hand, and listens. This is the Monument to Minin and Pozharsky. It has watched this square for more than two centuries.

Before we look closely, it helps to know why these two men matter. In the early seventeenth century, Russia went through a period that Russians call the Time of Troubles. It was a long crisis of famine, rival claimants to the throne, and foreign invasion. By 1610, Polish and Lithuanian forces held the Kremlin itself. The old ruling dynasty had ended, and the country had no tsar. For a while, it looked as if the Russian state might simply come apart.

The answer came from the Volga River, from the trading city of Nizhny Novgorod. A local merchant named Kuzma Minin called on his fellow townspeople to give money for a volunteer army. He was not a noble and not a soldier. He was a man of the market, and he asked ordinary people to pay for the defence of their country. To lead that army, the citizens turned to a prince and experienced commander, Dmitry Pozharsky, who was still recovering from wounds received in earlier fighting.

In 1612, the volunteer army reached Moscow and drove the occupying garrison out of the Kremlin. A few months later, an assembly chose a young Michael Romanov as tsar, and the Romanov dynasty began. So the event shown here is not only a military victory. It marks the moment when townspeople and nobility worked together to rebuild a state. Today, Russia marks the National Unity holiday on the fourth of November, linked to this liberation.

Now look at the figures themselves. Minin stands on the left. He wears a simple tunic, and his right arm stretches out toward the Kremlin walls. With his left hand, he offers a sword to the prince. Pozharsky sits beside him, half rising, holding the sword and leaning on a round shield. His posture is the first small surprise. The sculptor shows a wounded man, not a triumphant one. He is gathering his strength to answer the call.

The shield carries an image of the face of Christ, a religious icon known in Russian tradition as the Saviour. Armies of that era often carried such images into battle. Its presence here says that the struggle was understood as a defence of faith as much as of land. Notice, too, how the bodies and clothing are shaped. The folds of cloth, the bare arms, and the calm faces follow the style of classical Greek and Roman sculpture, which was the fashion of the time.

The man behind this work was Ivan Martos, one of the leading sculptors of the Russian Empire in the early nineteenth century. The idea for the monument appeared in the first years of that century. The money did not come from the state treasury alone. It was raised by public subscription, with people across the country giving donations. There is a quiet echo in that detail. A monument to a citizen who collected money from his neighbours was itself paid for by citizens.

Here is a second surprise. The figures were cast in bronze in Saint Petersburg, not in Moscow, and the casting was done in one piece, which was a difficult technical achievement for its day. The finished sculpture then had to travel to Moscow. It went largely by water, along rivers and canals, a slow journey for such a heavy load. The monument was finally unveiled on Red Square in 1818, just a few years after Napoleon's army had occupied and burned Moscow.

That timing is important. In 1812, the city had again faced a foreign army, and again it had recovered. When Muscovites gathered for the unveiling, the story of 1612 felt very close to their own experience. The monument became one of the first large sculptural memorials in the city. Read the inscription on the front of the pedestal. It dedicates the monument to citizen Minin and Prince Pozharsky, from a grateful Russia, and dates it to 1818.

Now step closer to the granite base. Bronze reliefs are set into its sides. On the front, you can see people of Nizhny Novgorod bringing their wealth for the cause. Look for families, older men and young people offering money and valuables. On the reverse side, a second relief shows the fighting and the expulsion of the enemy. These scenes are easy to miss when crowds gather in front. Take a slow walk around the whole base.

One more thing surprises many visitors. The monument did not always stand here. Originally, it was placed nearer the middle of Red Square, in front of the long trading arcades where the department store called GUM stands today. In 1931, Soviet authorities moved it to this spot near Saint Basil's Cathedral. The square was being cleared for parades and military vehicles, and the monument stood in the way. Since then, it has framed the view of the cathedral for countless photographs.

The move also changed what Minin points at. In the original position, his gesture pointed more directly across the square toward the Kremlin, the fortress his army was about to free. From here, the direction still reaches the Kremlin walls, but at a different angle. Try standing behind the monument and following his arm with your eyes. You will see the red brick towers rise beyond his fingers, with the Spasskaya Tower and its clock close by.

Before you move on, take a moment to listen. You may hear the chimes of that Kremlin clock, the scrape of shoes on stone, and many languages spoken at once. People still stop here to meet friends, to take pictures, and to rest. Look once more at the two bronze faces, darkened and polished by time and weather. One man asks. The other agrees. As you walk away across the square, the story they tell goes with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk fully around the pedestal to see both bronze reliefs.
Come early morning for fewer crowds around the base.
Stand behind Minin to follow his gesture toward the Kremlin.
Combine your visit with Saint Basil's Cathedral right behind.
Use a wide lens to frame the domes behind the figures.
