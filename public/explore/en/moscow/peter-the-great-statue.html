The guide

The story

Stand on the embankment for a moment and listen. Water slaps against the granite below. Traffic hums across the bridges. And on a clear day, the air here carries something unexpected, a faint sweetness of cocoa. It comes from the old chocolate factory just across the water. Now lift your eyes. Above the river, a giant bronze man stands on the deck of a ship, gripping the wheel and looking out over Moscow.

This is the monument to Peter the Great. It rises from a small artificial island where the Moskva River meets the Vodootvodny Canal, a channel dug to protect the city from floods. The statue was unveiled in 1997. It stands close to one hundred metres tall, measured from its base to the top of the figure. That makes it one of the tallest statues in the world, and easily the tallest in Moscow.

The sculptor was Zurab Tsereteli. He was born in Tbilisi, in Georgia, and became one of the most prolific monument makers of late Soviet and post-Soviet Russia. His work appears across Moscow, from Manezh Square to Victory Park. Tsereteli loved scale, colour and drama. This statue is perhaps the clearest example of that taste. Love it or not, nobody can say it is shy.

Why a ship? The monument marks three hundred years of the Russian Navy. Peter is credited with founding a regular Russian fleet at the end of the seventeenth century. Before him, Russia had almost no presence at sea. He studied shipbuilding himself, and he even worked in foreign shipyards in disguise as an ordinary carpenter. So the sculptor placed him exactly where Peter wanted to be... on a deck, at the helm, with water all around.

Look carefully at the column beneath the ship. It is not a plain pillar. It is built from the prows of smaller ships, stacked and jutting outward. This idea comes from the ancient Roman rostral column. Romans decorated such columns with the bows of captured enemy vessels as trophies of naval victory. Small flags fly from the ships as well. Many show a blue diagonal cross on white, the flag of Saint Andrew, which is still the ensign of the Russian Navy.

Now look at Peter's hand. He holds a golden scroll. Many viewers read it as a symbol of his reforms, or of a charter for the new fleet. Above him, the sails and rigging form a complex web of metal. Inside, a steel frame holds everything up. The surface is metal plating shaped to look like cast bronze. On a grey day the figure looks dark and heavy. In low evening sun, it warms to copper.

Here is the first surprise. Peter the Great did not love Moscow. He found the old capital closed, conservative and far from the sea. He built a new city on the Baltic coast, Saint Petersburg, and moved the capital there in the early eighteenth century. So this enormous tribute stands in the one city Peter chose to leave behind. Many Muscovites enjoy that irony. Some of them still mention it with a smile.

The second surprise is about a rumour. For years, a story has circulated that the statue was first designed as Christopher Columbus. According to this tale, the sculptor offered a Columbus monument to the United States for the five hundredth anniversary of his voyage. When no one accepted it, the story says, the head was simply changed. Tsereteli always denied this. Treat it as city folklore, not documented history, but it is part of how locals talk about the statue.

The monument has always divided opinion. When it appeared, many residents felt it was too large, too bold, and out of scale with the historic skyline. Petitions and protests followed. Polls of the time ranked it among the least loved buildings and monuments in the city. Around 2010, there was public talk of moving it elsewhere. Saint Petersburg reportedly declined the offer. And so the giant stayed exactly where it is.

Yet time softens many arguments. Today the statue has become a landmark that people use to find their way. Boat tours slow down beside it. Wedding couples pose with it in the background. Joggers along the embankment pass beneath it every morning. A monument that once seemed foreign to the city now appears in postcards and photographs of Moscow. Whatever people think of its taste, it has become part of the river view.

The neighbourhood around it adds to the experience. On the island behind you stands the red brick complex of the former Red October chocolate factory. Its workshops once filled the district with that sweet smell. Today the buildings hold galleries, studios, restaurants and bars. Nearby, the Strelka area at the tip of the island draws students and designers. A short walk away, the white walls and golden dome of the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour rise above the opposite bank.

The statue itself is not open to visitors. You cannot climb it or walk onto its little island. The best way to see it is from a distance. The pedestrian Patriarch's Bridge, near the cathedral, gives a wide view down the river with the statue against the sky. The embankments on both sides offer closer angles. A river cruise passes right beside the base, where you can feel its full height.

Before you leave, take one more look. Notice how the ship seems to sail against the current, its bow pointing toward the centre of the city. Notice the small flags, the golden scroll, the tiny figure of a man directing a vast fleet. Then turn and walk along the river. As the statue slowly shrinks behind you, the sounds of the city return... and perhaps that faint smell of chocolate, still in the air.
