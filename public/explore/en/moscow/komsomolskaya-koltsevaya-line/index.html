The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look up. Trains brake with a long metallic sigh, and suitcase wheels rattle across polished stone. Thousands of footsteps blur into one low hum. Then raise your eyes. Above the crowd, a warm yellow ceiling curves away from you, edged with white plaster ornament. Glittering mosaic panels sit inside it like framed paintings. Heavy bronze chandeliers hang in a long line down the hall. This is Komsomolskaya, on Moscow's brown Circle line, and few underground spaces anywhere feel this much like a palace.

Start with where you are standing. Directly above you is Komsomolskaya Square. Muscovites often call it the Square of Three Stations. Three of the city's great railway terminals face each other there: Leningradsky, Yaroslavsky and Kazansky. Trains leave them for Saint Petersburg, for the far north, for the Volga and beyond. So the people around you are not only commuters. Many of them have just arrived from far across the country, or they are about to leave. That explains the suitcases, the tired faces, and the steady flow of traffic.

The name itself tells a story. The Komsomol was the Communist youth league of the Soviet Union. Young Komsomol volunteers worked in large numbers on the construction of Moscow's first metro line in the 1930s. An earlier station on the red line, directly nearby, already carried the same name to honour them. This later station on the Circle line kept that tribute. The two stations are linked, so you can change between lines here without going up to the street.

This hall opened in 1952, during the final years of Stalin's rule. Its main designer was Alexey Shchusev, one of the most important architects of his age. He is also known for the Lenin Mausoleum on Red Square. Shchusev died in 1949, before the work was finished. Other architects completed the station from his design. So in a sense, this is the last major work of a man whose career covered both the old empire and the new Soviet state.

Notice the style. It looks almost nothing like a modern metro. The ceiling carries curling stucco shapes, the kind you might see in an eighteenth-century church or palace. Some writers compare it to Russian Baroque, a decorative style full of curves, gilding and rich colour. The pale columns are clad in stone and carry small decorative capitals. Everything is designed to look solid, permanent and grand. That was the point. The Soviet metro was meant to show ordinary people a palace that belonged to everyone.

Now look closely at the mosaic panels in the ceiling. They are the work of the painter Pavel Korin. They are made of smalt, which is coloured glass cut into small pieces. Smalt holds its colour for a very long time and catches the light from every angle. Gold backgrounds shine behind the figures. Stand under one panel and then step a few paces sideways. You will see the glitter shift as your angle changes.

Here is the idea behind the panels. In November 1941, German armies were close to Moscow. During a speech that autumn, Stalin called on Soviet soldiers to take strength from great military leaders of the Russian past. He named figures such as Alexander Nevsky, Dmitry Donskoy, Kuzma Minin, Dmitry Pozharsky, Alexander Suvorov and Mikhail Kutuzov. The ceiling of this station turns that speech into art. Several panels show exactly these heroes, each one from a different century.

Let us meet a few of them. Alexander Nevsky was a medieval prince who defeated invaders from the west in the thirteenth century. Dmitry Donskoy led Russian forces against the Golden Horde, the Mongol-led state that ruled these lands for generations. Minin and Pozharsky, a merchant and a prince, raised the army that freed Moscow from Polish occupation in the early seventeenth century. Suvorov and Kutuzov were generals of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Kutuzov is remembered for the war against Napoleon.

Other panels move closer to the time the station was built. They show scenes of the Second World War and the Soviet victory. This is where a detail most visitors miss comes in. When the station opened, Stalin himself appeared in the artwork. After his death, the Soviet leadership turned against his memory. Images of him were removed from many public places. Some mosaics here were changed or reworked during those years. So the ceiling you see today is not exactly the ceiling of 1952.

That makes this hall a quiet record of political change. The gold glass and the stucco look timeless. Yet the faces above you were chosen, edited and re-chosen according to who held power. Restorers have since worked on the mosaics to bring back their colour. As you walk the length of the platform, try to read the panels in order. You are walking through one version of history, written in glass, and later corrected.

Now come back down to eye level. The platform hall is unusually wide for a metro station. Designers expected heavy crowds from the three railway terminals above, and they gave people room to move. Watch how passengers flow around the columns. Notice how the arches frame each other in a long repeating line. If you stand at one end and look along the hall, the chandeliers seem to shrink into the distance. It is one of the best views in the whole metro.

A small surprise for the ears. Even in a hall this grand, the station is a working place first. Listen for the announcements, the hiss of train doors, and the warning tone before departure. Look at the station staff and the cleaners keeping the floor shining. Many Muscovites hurry past without a glance upward. For them, this is simply the way to the trains. For a visitor, the contrast between daily routine and palace decoration is part of the experience.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot near a column, out of the main stream of people. Look up one last time at the yellow vault and the glittering glass. Then look down at the crowd with its bags, its phones, its tired travellers. Those two layers have shared this space for more than seventy years. When you ride the escalator up toward the three railway stations, notice how the light changes. The gold slowly fades behind you, and the city begins again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit outside rush hours to see the hall without crowds.
Stand at one end for the long view down the hall.
Step aside behind a column before stopping to take photos.
Combine your visit with the three railway stations above.
Keep bags close; this is a very crowded transport hub.
