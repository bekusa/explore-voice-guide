The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the quiet. The traffic of the Kaluga highway fades behind you, and the ground under your feet turns soft with pine needles and fallen leaves. Tall trees close in overhead. The light comes down in thin, uneven bars. This could be any patch of forest outside Moscow, the kind of place where families once walked on a summer weekend. That ordinary feeling is part of what makes this place so hard to stand in.

You are at Kommunarka, one of the main execution and burial sites of the Great Terror. The Great Terror was the wave of arrests and killings ordered by Stalin's government in the late 1930s. During those years, the secret police, known as the NKVD, needed places to bury the people they shot. Kommunarka became one of those places. It took its name from a nearby collective farm called Kommunarka, a name that once suggested hope in a shared future.

Before it was a killing ground, this land was a country retreat. It is widely reported to have been the dacha, or summer house, of Genrikh Yagoda, one of the heads of the secret police. The estate lay behind fences, guarded and private. Then, in a turn that historians often point out, Yagoda himself fell from power. He was arrested, tried in one of the Moscow show trials, and shot in 1938. He is believed to be among those buried on his own former land.

The main period of executions here ran from 1937 to 1941. Researchers have identified more than six thousand people who were shot and buried in this forest. Many were brought from Moscow prisons. Most had been sentenced in brief closed hearings, with no real defence and no appeal. Their families were usually told nothing true. Some were told their relatives had received long prison terms, with no right to send letters.

Kommunarka was different from many other sites of the Terror in one important way. The people buried here were often prominent figures. They included senior Communist Party officials, government ministers, military commanders, diplomats, scientists, and writers. Nikolai Bukharin, once a leading Bolshevik thinker, is believed to lie here. So is the novelist Boris Pilnyak. The system that these men had helped to build, or had served, consumed them in the end.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Among the dead are many foreigners. In the 1930s, Moscow was a home for communists from abroad, people who had fled their own countries or come to serve the movement. Victims at Kommunarka include people from Latvia, Poland, Germany, Mongolia, and other nations. As you walk, you may find small memorials placed by foreign communities and governments. Each one is a quiet act of remembrance from a country far away.

For decades, this forest kept its secret. The area stayed closed and guarded through the Soviet years. Only in the 1990s, after the Soviet Union ended, did the truth begin to come out. Archives opened, at least in part. Researchers and families started to match names with places. In 1999, the land was handed over to the Russian Orthodox Church, which still cares for the site today.

Look for the wooden church on the grounds. It is dedicated to the New Martyrs and Confessors of Russia. In the Orthodox tradition, this title honours believers who were killed or persecuted for their faith during the Soviet period. The church was built in the early twenty-first century. Its warm timber and simple shape stand in sharp contrast to the forest around it. Many visitors stop here first, to light a candle or simply sit for a moment.

Much of the work of naming the dead was done by Memorial. Memorial was a Russian human rights and history society that spent decades collecting records of Soviet repression. Its volunteers searched archives, interviewed families, and compiled long lists of victims. Russian courts ordered the organisation to close in 2021 and 2022. Its work, however, remains visible in the names you can read here today.

Those names appear on a long memorial wall, installed in 2018. Panel after panel lists people with their dates of birth and death. Here is a second surprise. The wall caused real debate when it opened. The lists include not only innocent victims but also some former secret police officers who were themselves shot. Some people felt that executioners should not stand beside the people they killed. Others argued that every person shot here deserves a name. The wall carries both views.

If you walk slowly along the wall, you will see that the dates repeat. Many people died on the same day. Sometimes dozens of names share a single date. This detail tells you something the textbooks often cannot. It shows the pace of the killing — a schedule, a routine, carried out day after day. Families who come here often search for one date, one name, and leave flowers or a small photograph beside it.

The forest itself is part of the memorial. There are no grand statues or marble halls. The graves were mass pits, and their exact edges are not always marked. Instead, you see low mounds, wooden crosses, and small personal tributes. Some are handmade. Some are framed portraits, faded by rain and sun. This is a place that families and communities built up slowly, one gesture at a time, rather than a monument designed all at once.

If you visit at the end of October, you may notice more flowers and more people. Around that time each year, Russians mark a day of remembrance for victims of political repression. Readings of names have long been part of that tradition across the country. Even on a quiet weekday, though, you will usually find someone here, reading the wall in silence, or tidying a small corner of the forest.

Before you leave, stop and listen once more. Hear the wind moving in the tops of the pines, and the faint hum of the road beyond the trees. Look down at the soft ground under your feet. Thousands of people lie beneath this forest, most of them carried here in secret. Today, their names are spoken and written in the open. As you walk back toward the gate, you might choose one name from the wall to carry with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly and speak softly; this is an active burial site.
Allow extra time to read the memorial wall slowly.
Wear sturdy shoes for soft, uneven forest paths.
Bring a flower if you wish to leave a tribute.
Ask before photographing mourners or people at prayer.
