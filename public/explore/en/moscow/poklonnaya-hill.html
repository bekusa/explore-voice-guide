The guide

The story

Listen first. Water falls in long rows of fountains. Pigeons lift off the paving stones. Somewhere a child pedals a scooter across the wide granite square. Ahead of you, a tall pale needle rises into the Moscow sky, and at its very top a small bronze figure seems to float. This is Poklonnaya Hill, on the western edge of central Moscow. It is one of the city's most important places of memory.

The name itself carries a story. In Russian, Poklonnaya means something close to "the hill of bowing." For centuries, this rise of ground stood on the main road from the west. Travellers who reached it saw Moscow spread out before them for the first time. They saw golden domes and church towers. Tradition says many of them stopped here and bowed to the city. Others bowed again on the way out, saying goodbye.

The hill also has a darker moment. In September 1812, Napoleon's army marched on Moscow. The French emperor came to this hill and waited for city officials to bring him the keys to Moscow. Nobody came. The city had been largely abandoned, and soon it would burn. Leo Tolstoy later wrote this scene into his novel War and Peace. So this ground holds the memory of one great invasion... and the empty waiting that ended it.

The hill you stand on today does not look much like the one Napoleon saw. In the Soviet period, much of the original rise was dug away and levelled. Planners wanted a large memorial complex here. The project moved slowly for decades. The finished park, called Victory Park, opened in 1995. It marked fifty years since the end of the Second World War, which Russians call the Great Patriotic War.

Now look at the obelisk. It is one of the tallest monuments in Moscow, and its height is not random. It stands 141.8 metres tall. That works out to ten centimetres for every day of the war between the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany — 1,418 days in total. Many visitors photograph the column without knowing this. Once you know it, the height becomes a quiet count of days.

The obelisk was designed by Zurab Tsereteli, a sculptor born in Georgia who made many large monuments in Moscow. Its faces carry relief carvings of soldiers and battle scenes. Near the top, the bronze figure is Nike, the ancient Greek goddess of victory. Small winged figures sit around her. They hold trumpets, as if announcing the end of the war. From the ground she looks small. In reality she is very large.

At the foot of the obelisk is another sculpture. A rider on horseback drives a spear into a dragon. This is Saint George, the patron saint of Moscow. The dragon is cut into pieces under the horse's hooves. Look closely at its body. The scales carry symbols that refer to the enemy defeated in the war. It is a medieval image, used here to tell a twentieth century story.

Behind the obelisk stands a long, curved building with columns. This is the Central Museum of the Great Patriotic War. Inside you will find halls of photographs, letters, weapons and uniforms. There are large dioramas of major battles, built with painted backgrounds and real objects in the foreground. One hall is dedicated to memory and grief. Many visitors move through it in silence. A visit here needs time, so plan for it separately from your walk.

Now turn around and look back down the main avenue. The broad path runs gently downhill toward Kutuzovsky Prospekt. Stone terraces mark the way, and the fountains run along both sides. The terraces are linked to the years of the war, from 1941 to 1945. Walking up from the road, you move through those years toward the victory at the top. In summer, the spray from the fountains cools the air.

Here is something many visitors miss. Victory Park is not only one monument. It holds several houses of worship. There is an Orthodox church dedicated to Saint George. There is a memorial mosque. There is also a memorial synagogue, which houses a museum about the Holocaust. Together they remember people of many faiths who died in the war. Walk a few minutes from the main square to find them among the trees.

The park also has a large open-air display of military equipment. Tanks, artillery guns, trains and aircraft stand in long rows. Children climb around them, and older visitors often stop to read the labels. The machines look heavy and rough up close, with rivets and thick steel plates. Seeing them at full size gives a sense of the scale of the war that photographs cannot.

The atmosphere changes through the year. On Victory Day, the ninth of May, the hill fills with families, veterans' descendants and flowers. Carnations are laid at the memorials. On ordinary days the park feels like a neighbourhood space. People jog, walk dogs and sit on benches. In winter, snow covers the terraces and the obelisk stands sharp and grey against a white sky. The memory is always present, but daily life moves around it.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the top of the avenue. Look east, down toward the city. Long ago, travellers on this road had their first view of Moscow from near here. The skyline is very different now, with towers of glass and steel. But the gesture remains the same. You have arrived at the edge of a great city. Take a breath, listen to the fountains... and walk on.
