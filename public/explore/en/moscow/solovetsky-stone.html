The guide

The story

Start with the stone itself. Look at its surface: grey, rough, with no polish and no carving across most of its face. It sits low in a small garden, close to the traffic. Cars circle the square behind it and pedestrians hurry past toward the metro. This is a rock that was never meant to be pretty. It was meant to be carried a long way north to south, and then set down here, on purpose, on this exact spot.

You are standing on Lubyanka Square, in the centre of Moscow. The boulder came from the Solovetsky Islands, an archipelago in the White Sea, far to the north near the Arctic Circle. Russians often call those islands simply Solovki. The stone was taken from the ground where one of the earliest Soviet prison camps once stood. That journey, from the camp to this square, is the whole meaning of the monument.

Solovki has two lives in Russian memory. For centuries it was home to a great Orthodox monastery, founded in the fifteenth century, with thick stone walls built against the cold sea. Then, in the 1920s, the new Soviet state turned the islands into a prison camp. It held priests, nobles, scholars, peasants, and ordinary people caught up in political suspicion. Historians often describe Solovki as a testing ground for the larger system that followed.

That larger system is known as the Gulag. The word is a Soviet acronym for the state agency that ran the labour camps. Across several decades, millions of people passed through those camps, and many never came home. Some were sent for crimes. Many more were sent for words, for their background, or for no clear reason at all. Solovki was one of the first places where this machinery took shape.

Now turn and look across the square. The large building on the far side is the Lubyanka. It was the headquarters of the Soviet secret police, under its many changing names, including the KGB. Today it still houses the Russian security service. This is the first surprise for many visitors: the memorial was placed deliberately in sight of that building. The stone faces the institution that once ran the arrests.

The monument was installed in 1990 by Memorial, a human rights and historical society. Memorial was founded in the late Soviet years to document the victims of political repression and to return their names to public record. The stone was set in place at the end of October, on the eve of a date that political prisoners had already marked inside the camps themselves, quietly, since the 1970s.

That date, the thirtieth of October, later became an official Day of Remembrance for the Victims of Political Repression in Russia. So the stone is tied to a calendar as well as to a place. Each autumn, this small garden became the focus of the city's memory. People brought carnations and candles. They placed them along the base, against the cold granite, in the grey light of late October.

Here is the second surprise. When the stone first arrived, the square had a different centre. A tall bronze statue of Felix Dzerzhinsky, founder of the Soviet secret police, stood in the middle of the roundabout. In August 1991, after the failed coup against Gorbachev, crowds gathered here and the statue was lifted away by crane. For a short time, the boulder from Solovki and the empty plinth shared the same square.

The statue now stands in a park of Soviet monuments elsewhere in Moscow. The stone stayed. Look at the inscription on the pedestal. In plain words, it tells you where the boulder came from and who brought it. It says the stone honours the millions of victims of the totalitarian regime. There is no figure, no face, no heroic pose. The rock carries the message alone.

For many years, this spot hosted a ceremony called the Returning of the Names. On the day before the official remembrance date, people stood in a long line, sometimes for hours, in rain or early snow. One by one, each person stepped to a microphone and read aloud a few names of people shot in Moscow during the purges, with their age, their job, and the date of their death. Many readers added the name of a relative of their own.

The reading went on from morning into night. Strangers read the names of strangers. A schoolteacher, a factory worker, an engineer, a poet. In recent years, the city authorities have not allowed the public reading here, and Memorial itself was closed by a Russian court. Still, people have continued to come quietly, on their own, to lay flowers at the base. You may see fresh carnations here in late autumn.

The garden around the stone is small and modest. Behind it rises the long facade of the Polytechnic Museum, a nineteenth century building. Trees give some shade in summer. In winter the stone wears a cap of snow. Notice how ordinary the setting feels, with benches, paths, and passers-by. That ordinariness matters. Memory here is not fenced off in a museum hall. It sits beside daily city life.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the base and look at the texture of the rock up close: the pale flecks, the rough edges, the places where lichen once grew in the north. Then look up again, across the traffic, at the building on the other side. Between those two points lies a whole century. As you walk away toward the metro, you carry the stone's simple question with you — who do we remember, and how?

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit near late October to see flowers left by locals.
Pair with a walk past the Lubyanka building opposite.
Stand quietly; many visitors treat this as a mourning place.
Bring a single flower if you wish to pay respects.
Use Lubyanka metro station; the garden is a short walk.
