The guide

The story

Stand at the water's edge and tilt your head back. Pale stone and cream-coloured tile climb in stepped layers, terrace after terrace, until a slender spire catches the light far above you. Below, two rivers meet. The wide Moskva slides past, and the smaller Yauza joins it here, just beside your feet. Traffic hums along the embankment. And over it all rises a building that looks less like an apartment block and more like a wedding cake carved from a mountain.

This is the Kotelnicheskaya Embankment Building, one of the seven Stalinist skyscrapers that Muscovites call the Seven Sisters. They were planned in the years right after the Second World War, when the Soviet leadership wanted Moscow to look like the capital of a victorious superpower. The foundations of all seven were laid on the same day in 1947, the year the city marked its eight hundredth anniversary. It was a single, coordinated statement in stone. This tower, finished in the early 1950s, became one of the first of the sisters to be completed.

Now, here is something many visitors miss. Most of the Seven Sisters are ministries, a hotel, or a university. This one is different. It was built mainly as housing, a home for people the state chose to reward. The central tower rises to thirty-two storeys and roughly 176 metres, with the spire included. For years it ranked among the tallest buildings in Europe. Yet behind those grand walls are kitchens, bedrooms, and long corridors where ordinary family life went on.

The architects were Dmitry Chechulin and Andrei Rostkovsky. Chechulin was one of the most powerful architects in Soviet Moscow, and he shaped much of the city's postwar look. The pair did not start from an empty site. An earlier, lower wing along the Moskva had already gone up before the war. The skyscraper was designed to absorb it. So when you look along the river frontage, you are seeing two eras joined into one composition.

Look at the shape again. The building steps back as it rises, like a pyramid built from boxes. The central tower carries the eye upward, while lower wings spread out on either side, almost like arms. This stepped silhouette was common to all the Seven Sisters. It mixed several influences at once. There are echoes of the old Kremlin towers with their tapering tops. There are echoes, too, of the American skyscrapers of the 1920s and 1930s — a rival nation's style, quietly borrowed.

Let your gaze travel slowly up the facade. You will find sculptures standing on the ledges and corners. These are idealised figures of workers, soldiers, and farmers, the heroes of Soviet propaganda. From the street they look small, but they are large in reality. You will also see carved wreaths, sheaves of wheat, and stars. Each motif carried a message about abundance, labour, and victory. At the very top, the spire ends in a star, crowning the whole structure.

There is a darker side to this story, and it is worth hearing while you stand here. Historians have documented that prisoners from the Gulag labour camps were used on several of the Stalinist high-rises. The grand finish of buildings like this one sat on top of forced work by people who had no choice. Many visitors admire the stonework without knowing this. It does not make the building less impressive. But it adds a weight to the view that is good to carry with you.

Who actually lived here? The flats went to the Soviet elite of the time — scientists, engineers, officials, and especially artists. The ballerina Galina Ulanova lived in the building. So did the actress Faina Ranevskaya, famous across the Soviet Union for her sharp humour. The poet Andrei Voznesensky is also linked with these walls. For a family in a crowded postwar city, a flat here meant high ceilings, a lift, and windows looking over the rivers. It was a privilege few could imagine.

There is a well-loved story about Ranevskaya. According to the popular tale, her flat sat above a bakery and a cinema, and she joked that she lived above bread and circuses. Whether she said exactly those words is hard to confirm. But the joke works because the building really was designed as a small city. On the lower floors there were shops, services, and places of entertainment. Residents could buy food, post a letter, or watch a film without crossing a street.

That cinema still exists. It is called Illuzion, and it belongs to the national film archive of Russia. For decades it has screened classic and archival films, including old Soviet and foreign works that were hard to see elsewhere. For many Moscow film lovers, the building means Illuzion first and skyscraper second. If you walk around to the side facing the city, you can look for its entrance. The lobby has the same heavy, ornate feeling as the rest of the building.

Step back and think about the materials. From a distance the walls look like solid stone. Up close, you will see a mix of stone cladding on the lower levels and ceramic tiles higher up. Tiles were lighter and easier to fix at great heights. The cream and beige tones change with the weather. On a grey Moscow morning the tower can look almost white. At sunset it turns warm, nearly golden, and the details on the upper tiers become sharp and clear.

For the best overall view, cross to the opposite bank of the Moskva or walk onto one of the nearby bridges. From there you can see the full stepped outline, with the river in front and the city behind. Along the embankment itself, look for the archways and the long rows of windows on the lower wings. Notice how the building seems to grow as you move closer. Architects of this era planned these sightlines carefully, so the tower would dominate views from many parts of central Moscow.

Before you go, look once more at where you are standing. Two rivers meet here, and the tower stands right at that meeting point, like a marker on a map. People still live behind those windows. Someone may be making tea on the twentieth floor right now. As you walk away along the embankment, glance back over your shoulder a few times. Watch how the spire slowly sinks behind the rooftops, until only the star is left against the sky.
