The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on Novoslobodskaya Street and listen. Trams rattle past. Car tyres hiss on the asphalt. People walk quickly with shopping bags and phones. Yet behind the plain buildings and tall walls on this block stands one of the oldest working prisons in Russia. Very few passers-by look up. That ordinary noise, so close to a place of confinement, is the first thing to notice here.

This is Butyrka. The name comes from an old word for settlements that stood apart, beyond the edge of the city. Centuries ago this ground was outside Moscow, on the road leading north. The city slowly grew around it and swallowed it. Today the prison sits inside a busy central district, a short walk from Novoslobodskaya metro station. Its location tells a quiet story about how Moscow expanded.

The oldest part of the complex dates to the late eighteenth century, during the reign of Catherine the Great. The design is usually attributed to Matvey Kazakov, one of the great Moscow architects of that era. He built many of the city's elegant classical buildings. Here he worked on something very different — a fortress-like prison castle. Thick walls, a square plan, and round towers at the corners gave it the look of a medieval stronghold.

Those towers carry their own legends. The best known is called the Pugachev Tower. Legend has it that Yemelyan Pugachev, the leader of a huge peasant uprising, was held here in chains before his execution in 1775. Historians doubt this story, because the tower as we know it was likely finished later. But the name stuck. Locals and guidebooks still repeat the tale, and it shows how a building collects stories over time.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Butyrka is not a museum or a ruin. It is still a working pretrial detention centre. People awaiting trial are held behind these walls today. Families still queue to deliver parcels to relatives inside. So this is not a place to wander freely or peer through gates. It is a living institution, and that changes the way you should stand here and look.

Over more than two centuries, an extraordinary list of people passed through these cells. In the tsarist years, many revolutionaries were held here. Felix Dzerzhinsky, who later founded the Soviet secret police, was once a prisoner in Butyrka. So was the anarchist leader Nestor Makhno. The irony is sharp. Some men locked up here by the old regime later built a new system of prisons of their own.

The young poet Vladimir Mayakovsky was also held here as a teenager, after arrests for revolutionary activity. He later said that his time in a cell helped turn him toward poetry. It is a strange thought. Some of the bold, loud verse of early Soviet Russia may have had roots in a quiet, cramped room somewhere behind these walls.

After the revolution, the prison became even more crowded. During the Stalin era, and especially during the terror of the late 1930s, Butyrka served as a major transit point. Thousands of arrested people waited here for interrogation, sentencing, or transport to labour camps in the far north and east. Cells built for a few people often held many dozens. The air, survivors later wrote, was hot, stale, and heavy.

Alexander Solzhenitsyn passed through Butyrka after his arrest in 1945. He wrote about it in The Gulag Archipelago, his long history of the Soviet camp system. The writer Varlam Shalamov, famous for his Kolyma stories about the camps, was also held here. The rocket engineer Sergei Korolev, who later led the early Soviet space programme, spent time in Butyrka too. Few people looking at a rocket launch imagine its designer once waited in these cells.

The prison's story did not end with the Soviet Union. In 2009, the tax lawyer Sergei Magnitsky died in custody at Butyrka after being denied proper medical care. His case drew international attention. Several countries later passed laws named after him to sanction officials accused of human rights abuses. A name connected to this Moscow building now appears in legal systems far from Russia.

As you stand outside, notice how well the old castle hides. Later buildings, walls, and fences surround it, so you see only fragments from the street. Look for a glimpse of rounded masonry or an old pale facade above the newer structures. Notice the windows, the high walls, the security cameras. The contrast between classical eighteenth-century forms and modern prison hardware is quite striking.

There is a small museum inside the complex, focused on the prison's history and famous inmates. Access has been limited and usually requires arrangement in advance, so most visitors only see the outside. That is perfectly fine. Simply standing here, with this history in mind, is a meaningful experience. Please be respectful. Avoid photographing guards, entrances, or the families waiting nearby. They are not part of a tourist scene.

Before you walk on, look once more at the ordinary street around you. The cafés, the tram lines, the apartment windows with plants on the sills. Generations of Muscovites have lived their daily lives right beside these walls. Many of them had a relative inside at one time or another. As you head back toward the metro, listen again to the traffic — and remember the many voices that once waited, unheard, just a few metres away.
