The guide

The story

Listen first. A train sighs to a stop, the doors thud open, and a wave of footsteps rolls across polished stone. Then look up from the crowd. All around you, panels of coloured glass glow like lanterns set into the walls. Deep blues, ambers, ruby reds and soft greens shine from inside frames of gleaming brass. You are standing many metres beneath Moscow, and yet the light here feels almost like a church window at dusk.

This is Novoslobodskaya, a station on the Koltsevaya line. That is the Circle line, the ring that loops around the centre of the city and links the radial lines together. The ring opened in stages through the early 1950s. This station came into service in 1952, at the height of the postwar years. Moscow was building its underground as a kind of palace for ordinary people, and every station was meant to make a statement.

The name comes from the street above, Novoslobodskaya Street. A sloboda was an old Russian word for a settlement whose residents were freed from certain taxes or duties. Many such communities once ringed Moscow, and their names survive in the streets today. So the name roughly means the New Sloboda. A modest suburban past sits hidden inside the title of one of the most decorated rooms in the metro.

The lead architect was Alexey Dushkin. His name appears again and again in the story of the Moscow Metro. He also designed Mayakovskaya, with its slender steel arches, and Kropotkinskaya, with its glowing columns. Dushkin loved to work with light. At Novoslobodskaya he had an unusual idea for a station deep underground. He asked a simple question. What if there were windows here, even though there is no sky?

The answer is all around you. The station holds thirty-two stained glass panels, set into the pylons, the heavy pillars that line the central hall. Each panel is lit from behind, so the glass seems to shine with its own daylight. Here is something many visitors miss. The glass was made in Riga, in Latvia, by craftsmen with a long tradition of stained glass work. Moscow did not have enough of this skill at the time, so the work travelled hundreds of kilometres to reach these walls.

Walk slowly along the hall and study the figures inside the glass. Many panels show people at work in honoured professions. You may find an architect, an agronomist, an artist, a musician, a geographer. Around them run patterns of stars, flowers, leaves and geometric shapes. The style mixes old church window craft with the confident imagery of the Soviet state. Saints and kings have gone, and scholars and builders have taken their place.

Now notice the frames. Each panel sits inside a border of polished brass, and the brass curves into an elegant arch. These brass edges catch the light from the glass and from the lamps above, and they warm the whole hall. Run your eye down the pylon. The pale marble below and the bright glass above make a quiet contrast. The stone feels cool and heavy, while the glass feels light, almost weightless.

At the far end of the central hall, look for the large mosaic. It was designed by Pavel Korin, a painter and mosaic artist who also created decorations for other metro stations, including Komsomolskaya. The panel shows a mother lifting a child, with doves around them. It is often known by the title Peace Throughout the World. After a devastating war, the image of a mother and child offered a clear message. The station was a place of light, and the hope it carried was for peace.

Here is a second surprise. A station like this was not only decoration. It was also part of a working transport system that had to carry huge crowds every day. The deep stations of this period were built with sturdy pylons and thick walls. The beauty you see sits on top of serious engineering. Each panel had to survive vibration from trains, dust, and the constant rush of air through the tunnels for decades.

Take a moment to watch the people around you. Commuters hurry past the glass without a glance. They have seen it a thousand times. A student checks a phone. An older passenger pauses by one panel, perhaps a favourite. This mix is part of the station's character. It is a gallery, but it is also a daily corridor. The glass shines for everyone, whether they look up or not.

Novoslobodskaya also connects to another line. A passage leads to Mendeleyevskaya station, on the grey Serpukhovsko-Timiryazevskaya line. That station is named after Dmitri Mendeleev, the chemist who arranged the periodic table of the elements. If you have time, follow the signs and compare the two. The newer station has a very different, more modern mood. Moving between them feels like walking from one decade into another.

If you want the best view, try to visit outside the morning and evening rush hours. In quieter moments you can stand in the middle of the hall and see the line of glowing panels stretch away on both sides. The colours look richest when the platform is less crowded and fewer bodies block the light. Photographers often wait for the gap between two trains, when the hall goes still for a few seconds.

Pause once more before you leave. Look at the nearest panel, and notice how the light moves through the coloured glass. Small bubbles and lines inside the glass show that it was made by hand. Somewhere far above you, Moscow carries on with traffic, weather and noise. Down here, the glass keeps its own steady glow. As your train arrives and the doors open, take one last look over your shoulder... and carry a little of that coloured light with you.
