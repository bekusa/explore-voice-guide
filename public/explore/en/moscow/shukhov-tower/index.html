The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and tilt your head back. Above the rooftops of Shabolovka Street, a pale web of steel rises into the Moscow sky. It looks less like a building than a drawing in mid-air. On a windy day you can see the clouds slide behind it, and the whole lattice seems to shimmer as your eyes try to follow its lines. This is the Shukhov Tower, and it is one of the most daring structures of the early twentieth century.

Look closely at those lines. Each beam is perfectly straight. And yet the tower curves, narrowing at the waist of every section like an hourglass. This is the first quiet surprise of the place. The curve is an illusion made of straight pieces. Mathematicians call this shape a hyperboloid, a surface that bends while being built entirely from straight rods set at an angle to each other. Twist a bundle of straight sticks, and you get the same graceful pinch.

The man behind this idea was Vladimir Shukhov, a Russian engineer of remarkable range. He worked on oil pipelines, boilers, bridges, and the light steel roofs of railway stations and shops. Long before this tower, he had already shown a hyperboloid water tower at a large national exhibition in Nizhny Novgorod in 1896. He understood something simple and powerful. A lattice of straight steel, arranged in this way, is strong, cheap, and astonishingly light.

That lightness matters. Wind is the great enemy of any tall structure. A solid tower catches the wind like a sail. An open lattice lets it pass straight through. So the Shukhov Tower uses very little metal for its height. Engineers still admire it for exactly this reason: it does a great deal with very little. In a country short of almost everything, that was not just elegant. It was necessary.

And Russia was short of almost everything when this tower went up. Construction took place between 1920 and 1922, in the years of revolution and civil war. The new Soviet government wanted a powerful radio station in Moscow, a voice that could reach far beyond the capital. Lenin himself signed the decision to build it. Shukhov first imagined a tower far taller than the one you see now. But steel was scarce, and the plan had to shrink.

So the tower was built at roughly half that first ambition, about one hundred and sixty metres tall. Count the sections from the ground upward. There are six of them, stacked one on top of another. Each is its own hyperboloid, slimmer than the one below. Together they taper toward the sky like a telescope pulled out to its full length. That comparison is not an accident, as you are about to hear.

Here is the second surprise. The tower was raised without tall external scaffolding. Workers assembled each new section on the ground, inside the section beneath it. Then, using winches and pulleys, they lifted it upward through the structure and fixed it in place. Section by section, the tower grew out of itself — rising like a telescope. It was a clever answer to a hard question: how to build high, fast, and with almost no materials to spare.

The work was not smooth. During construction, a section fell and damaged parts of the structure below. Accounts of what happened next differ, and some of the darker stories may have grown in the retelling. What is clear is that the accident brought serious scrutiny on Shukhov in a dangerous political time. The work continued, the damage was repaired, and the tower was completed. It began radio broadcasts in 1922.

For many Muscovites, though, the tower means something else: television. In the following decades, the Shabolovka site became an early home of Soviet television broadcasting. For a long time, the word Shabolovka itself was linked in people's minds with the screen in their living room. Programmes were made and sent out from the studios nearby. So this lattice of steel carried both the first voices of Soviet radio and some of the first moving pictures.

As you look up, notice the rings. Horizontal steel hoops circle each section at regular intervals. They hold the diagonal beams together and stop them from twisting. Where the diagonals cross, they form small diamond shapes that repeat all the way to the top. From directly below, these diamonds stack into a dizzying pattern, almost like the inside of a woven basket. Give your eyes time. The geometry becomes clearer the longer you look.

The tower's survival was never guaranteed. Steel rusts, and time has not been gentle. In the 2010s, there was serious talk of taking the structure down, or of moving it. Architects, engineers, and ordinary residents from Russia and abroad raised their voices to protect it. The tower stayed. That campaign is a reminder that this structure is valued far beyond Moscow, as an important landmark in the story of modern engineering.

Its influence is easy to see once you know where to look. Lattice towers, cooling towers at power stations, and many modern skyscrapers and television towers use the same principle of light, curving steel shells. Some of the most celebrated contemporary towers in Asia and Europe follow in Shukhov's footsteps. Here, on an ordinary Moscow street, you are standing beside one of the early ancestors of that whole family of forms.

The base of the tower sits within a working broadcasting site, so most visitors see it from the streets around it. That is no loss. The best views come from a little distance, where the six sections line up and the full taper is visible. Walk slowly along Shabolovka and the side streets nearby. With every few steps, the lattice shifts, and new lines cross over old ones.

Before you leave, look up one last time. Notice how the sky shows through the steel, how little metal it takes to hold all that height. Listen to the traffic and the voices around you. People walk past it every day without looking up. Now you know what they are missing. As you walk away, glance back over your shoulder — the tower will still be there, a fine grey drawing against the clouds.
