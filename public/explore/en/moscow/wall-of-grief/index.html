The guide

The story

Start with the metal. Reach out, if you like, and touch the bronze nearest to you. It is cool and slightly rough, darker in the folds and polished in places where many hands have rested before yours. Behind you, traffic moves along one of Moscow's widest roads. In front of you, a crowd of figures rises from the ground, packed shoulder to shoulder. None of them has a face. This is the Wall of Grief, and it was made so that you would stand exactly where you are standing now.

The monument is dedicated to the victims of political repression in the Soviet Union. That phrase covers a great deal. It means people arrested at night and never seen again. It means families sent to labour camps in Siberia and the Far North. It means whole ethnic groups deported from their homelands. The worst period came under Stalin in the 1930s, but repression began earlier and continued later. Millions of lives were touched, and almost every Russian family carries some part of that story.

The wall was unveiled on 30 October 2017. The date matters. Since the early 1990s, the thirtieth of October has been marked in Russia as the Day of Remembrance for Victims of Political Repression. The sculptor was Georgy Frangulyan, an artist known in Moscow for large public works. He won a design competition for this site, and the city supported the project together with a foundation created to keep the memory of the victims alive.

Look at how the wall is built. From a distance it seems to be a single solid mass of bronze, roughly thirty metres long and taller than a person by several times. But step closer and you see that it is made of many separate figures. They lean, they twist, they press against one another. Some seem to be falling. Some seem to be holding each other up. The effect is of a huge number of people, all caught in the same moment.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The wall is not closed. There are openings between the figures — gaps shaped by missing bodies. You can walk into them and through them. The sculptor wanted visitors to pass inside the crowd, not only to look at it from outside. When you stand in one of these gaps, the bronze rises on both sides of you. For a moment, you take the place of someone who is no longer there.

Now think about the missing faces. The figures have the outline of heads and shoulders, arms and legs, but no features. This is a deliberate choice. It reflects how the system treated people: as numbers in a file, not as individuals. It also means that no single person is shown. Any visitor can look at these shapes and think of their own grandfather, a neighbour, a name read in a book.

Lower your eyes to the ground in front of the wall. You will see stones set into the paving area. These were brought from places connected with the camps and the repression, from many regions of the former Soviet Union. They are rough and uneven, very different from the smooth city stone around them. Each one carries a small piece of a distant landscape — a forest, a mine, a frozen river bank — into the centre of the capital.

Near the monument, look for the word Remember. It is written in many languages. This is the second quiet surprise of the site. The repression did not affect only Russians. Its victims included Ukrainians, Poles, Germans, Chechens, Kalmyks, Crimean Tatars, Georgians, Latvians and many others. The different alphabets placed side by side make that point without a single sentence of explanation.

The location also carries meaning. The wall stands on Academician Sakharov Avenue, close to where it meets the Garden Ring, the broad road that circles central Moscow. The avenue is named after Andrei Sakharov, the physicist who helped develop the Soviet hydrogen bomb and later became the country's best-known dissident. He was sent into internal exile for his views. A memorial to the persecuted, standing on a street named for a persecuted man, is a pairing that many Muscovites notice.

The monument has not been free of debate. When it opened, some former political prisoners and human rights activists signed a public letter. They argued that it was wrong for the state to honour past victims while, in their view, political pressure continued in the present. Others welcomed the wall as overdue national recognition. You do not need to choose a side to see that the argument itself is part of how a country deals with a painful past.

It helps to know that this is not the only memorial of its kind in the city. Near Lubyanka Square, outside the former headquarters of the Soviet secret police, stands the Solovetsky Stone. It is a single boulder brought from the Solovetsky Islands in the White Sea, where one of the first Soviet camps was set up. That stone was placed by civil society groups at the end of the Soviet era. The Wall of Grief is larger, newer, and official.

Take a moment to notice the sound around you. The traffic does not stop here. Buses pass, office workers walk by with coffee, someone checks a phone on the corner. The wall does not stand in a quiet park or a cemetery. It stands in an ordinary working part of the city. That may be the most honest thing about it. The events it remembers also happened in ordinary streets, to people who were going about their day.

Before you leave, walk once more along the full length of the bronze. Notice how the light catches the raised shoulders and leaves the gaps in shadow. Notice any flowers that people have left at the base, or small candles from an anniversary. Then step back towards the road and look at the whole crowd again. From here, the figures blend into one dark shape. Up close, you know, they were many — and each one was someone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk through the gaps between figures, as the sculptor intended.
Visit near 30 October to see flowers and memorial candles.
Pair your visit with the Solovetsky Stone near Lubyanka Square.
Keep voices low; many visitors come here to mourn relatives.
Step back toward the road for a view of the whole wall.
