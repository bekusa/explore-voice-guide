The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the traffic noise of central Moscow, there is a softer sound here: a low, steady hiss of gas feeding a flame. It burns inside a bronze star set into polished red stone. Behind it rises the dark red brick of the Kremlin wall. Under your feet, the paving of the Alexander Garden is cool and even. People lower their voices without being asked. Even children seem to slow down as they come close.

You are standing at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. It is Russia's national memorial to the millions who died in the Second World War, which people here call the Great Patriotic War. The memorial opened in May 1967, on the eve of Victory Day. Victory Day falls on the ninth of May, and it is the most emotional public holiday in the Russian calendar. This small stretch of garden carries a great deal of that feeling.

The story begins outside the city, in the winter of 1941. German forces advanced to within a short distance of Moscow. The fighting on the approaches to the capital was desperate, and many of the dead were buried in mass graves where they fell. In December 1966, on the twenty-fifth anniversary of that battle, the remains of one soldier were taken from such a grave. It lay beside the Leningrad highway, on the north-western edge of the city.

This soldier was chosen precisely because nobody knew who he was. There was no name, no rank to record, no family to claim him. That is the point of the memorial. He stands in for every soldier who never came home and was never identified. The remains were carried through Moscow on a gun carriage, and crowds lined the streets. He was buried here, below the Kremlin wall.

Now look down at the slab in front of the flame. The words carved there are often quoted across the former Soviet Union. In English they read: "Your name is unknown. Your deed is immortal." It is a short sentence, and that is its strength. Many families still have relatives who vanished in the war with no grave at all. For them, these words are personal.

Above the grave, there is a bronze sculpture lying on the stone. Look closely. A soldier's helmet rests on a folded battle banner, with a laurel branch beside it. There is no heroic figure, no raised rifle, no triumphant pose. The design is quiet and heavy, almost like objects left on a grave. On the wall behind it, you can read the dates 1941 and 1945, with the words "To those who fell for the Motherland".

The flame itself has a journey behind it. It was not lit from a match in Moscow. It was carried from Leningrad, today Saint Petersburg, from the eternal flame at the Field of Mars, a memorial to earlier revolutionary dead. Relay runners and vehicles brought the fire to the capital. Here is a detail many visitors miss: in 2009, during renovation work, the flame was moved temporarily to Victory Park on Poklonnaya Hill. It returned to this spot the following year.

Turn now toward the guards. Two soldiers stand motionless on either side of the flame. This is Post Number One, the most honoured guard post in the country. For decades, Post Number One stood at Lenin's Mausoleum on Red Square, just on the other side of this wall. In the late 1990s, it was moved here. That change tells you something about how the country chose to remember its past after the Soviet Union ended.

If you wait for the top of the hour, you will see the changing of the guard. A new pair marches in from the direction of the Kremlin, with high, sharp steps and a slow rhythm. The movements are exact, and the boots ring on the stone. Crowds gather quickly, so find your place a few minutes early. Watch the faces of older Russians in the crowd. For many of them, this is not a performance. It is a kind of greeting.

Now walk a little along the wall, away from the flame. You will see a row of dark red stone blocks. Each block carries the name of a Hero City, a title given to cities that endured especially fierce battles or sieges. Leningrad, Stalingrad, Kiev, Minsk, Odessa and others are here. Inside these blocks is soil brought from each of those places. So in a sense, earth from many battlefields rests in this one garden.

Further on, there is another block honouring the Brest Fortress, on the old western border, where Soviet soldiers held out in the first days of the invasion. Beyond that, an alley of stone markers lists the Cities of Military Glory, a later honour for other towns. The whole memorial grows quieter and plainer as you walk. It does not try to impress you with size. It asks you to read, name by name.

Here is something you may notice on a weekend: brides and grooms in wedding clothes, laying flowers by the flame. It is a long Russian tradition for newlyweds to visit a war memorial on their wedding day. They thank the generation that made their lives possible. You will also see red carnations on the stone. In Russia, carnations are the traditional flower for remembrance, usually given in even numbers at memorials.

The setting adds its own layer. The Alexander Garden was laid out in the early nineteenth century, after the war with Napoleon, over the course of a river that now runs underground. The Kremlin wall above you is far older. Its red brick was raised in the late fifteenth century. So you are standing where several centuries of Russian memory meet: medieval fortress, Napoleonic recovery, and the losses of the twentieth century.

Before you move on, step back a few paces. Look at the flame again, small against the long red wall and the trees of the garden. Listen for that soft hiss under the city noise. The guards have not moved. The flowers will be replaced tomorrow by other hands. As you walk away toward the gates of the garden, notice how the city's sound returns slowly — first the voices, then the traffic, then Moscow itself.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive a few minutes before the hour for the guard change.
Stand to the side to see the guards' full march.
Bring an even number of carnations to lay respectfully.
Keep voices low and avoid posing near the flame.
Walk the full length of the wall to read every block.
