The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere behind you, traffic hums along a city road. In front of you, the sound changes. Wind moves through birch leaves. A woodpecker knocks on a dead trunk. The path under your feet turns from asphalt to packed earth and pine needles. Within a few steps, a city of millions seems to fall away. This is Losiny Ostrov, one of the largest forests inside any major European city.

The name translates as Elk Island. In Russian, the word for elk means the large animal that North Americans call a moose. So when you hear elk here, picture a tall, dark animal with long legs and broad antlers. And the island? It is not surrounded by water. Long ago, Russians often used the word island for a patch of forest that stood alone in open land. This forest was an island of trees, and moose lived in it.

The park covers more than a hundred square kilometres. It stretches across the north-eastern edge of Moscow and continues beyond the city limits into the Moscow Region. Towns like Mytishchi, Korolev and Balashikha sit along its edges. The Moscow Ring Road, the great highway that circles the capital, cuts straight through the forest. So part of the park lies inside the city, and part lies outside it. Few visitors realise how far the trees go on.

This was protected land long before it became a park. For centuries, these woods were royal hunting grounds. Russian tsars came to the forests north-east of old Moscow to hunt with hounds and with falcons. The nearby district of Sokolniki takes its name from the falconers who served the court. Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich, who ruled in the seventeenth century, was known for his love of falconry. Because the land belonged to the crown, much of the forest stayed uncut.

That royal protection had a long effect. Moscow grew outward for hundreds of years. Villages became suburbs, and suburbs became city districts. Yet this forest survived, partly because of its old status and partly because of later efforts to guard it. In 1983, the area was declared a national park. It was among the very first national parks in the Soviet Union. Today it is still one of the oldest protected natural areas in Russia.

Now look around at the trees. The forest here is mixed. You will see pale birches with their white, peeling bark. You will see tall pines with orange-brown trunks near the top. Dark spruces stand close together in shaded places. In some parts, older broad-leaved trees like oak and linden grow as well. Each kind of tree changes the light. Under birches the ground is bright. Under spruces it is dim, cool and quiet.

Water shapes this landscape too. The park holds marshes, wet meadows and small streams. One surprise for many visitors is that the Yauza River begins in this area. The Yauza is the river that later flows through central Moscow and joins the Moskva River near the Kremlin. Here, near its source, it is a small, slow stream among reeds and wet ground. It is strange to think that the same water ends up passing city bridges and stone embankments.

And the moose are still here. They are shy animals, and most visitors never see one. They move mostly in the early morning and the evening. If you are lucky, you may notice a hoof print in soft mud, or chewed bark on a young tree. Wild boar also live in the forest. You might find patches of earth where they have dug for roots. Foxes, hares and many kinds of birds share these woods too.

Birdwatchers come here in every season. In spring, the forest fills with song. Woodpeckers are common, and their drumming carries a long way through the trunks. In winter, the woods become quiet and white. Then you can read the snow like a page. Tracks of animals cross the paths, and you can follow small stories of who passed by in the night. Many Muscovites come here on skis when the snow is deep.

For local people, this forest is part of ordinary life. On weekends you will see families with strollers, older couples walking slowly, runners and cyclists. In summer, people gather mushrooms and berries in permitted areas. Children walk along marked nature trails with their parents. Some trails have wooden boardwalks over wet ground, so you can cross marshy places without sinking. These walkways also protect fragile plants from thousands of feet.

The park is not only wild forest. It also has visitor centres and small educational sites, where you can learn about the animals and plants. Some areas focus on teaching children about nature. Other parts are kept quieter, with limited access, so animals can rest. This balance is the hard part of any city forest. Millions of people live nearby, and every one of them has a right to fresh air. The wildlife needs space as well.

Here is a second surprise. Standing deep in these trees, you are closer to the Kremlin than many suburbs are. The forest does not sit far out in the countryside. It presses right up against apartment blocks and metro lines. In some places, you can walk from a busy street into dense woods in a few minutes. That closeness is what makes this place unusual. It is wilderness at the edge of a capital city.

Before you leave, stop once more. Look up at the tops of the pines and notice how they move, even when the air feels still down here. Look down at the path and see what has passed before you. Listen again for the city behind the trees. It is still there, never far away. As you walk back toward the road, notice the moment the sounds change once more... and the forest closes quietly behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning for the best chance of wildlife.
Wear waterproof shoes; marshy trails stay wet after rain.
Stay on marked trails to protect resting animals.
Bring skis in winter for the snowy forest paths.
Download offline maps; mobile signal can weaken deep inside.
