The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the platform. Feel the warm draught that pushes ahead of every train, carrying the smell of iron, dust and machine oil. Then lift your eyes from the polished floor. Along the pylons, blue and white ceramic panels shine under the lamps. They look almost like fine porcelain plates hung in a grand dining room... except this is a working railway station, deep under Moscow.

This is Taganskaya, on the Ring line of the Moscow Metro. The station opened in 1950. It was part of the first stretch of the circle that now loops around the city centre. The Ring line has an important job. It links all the long lines that run out from the middle of Moscow, so passengers can change trains without crossing the centre. Taganskaya is one of the busiest of these transfer points.

First, a word about the name. Taganka is the old district above your head. One popular explanation says the name comes from the tagan, a small iron stand for a cooking pot. Locals say blacksmiths once lived and worked here and made these stands. It is a nice story, and it is widely repeated. But treat it as a traditional explanation rather than a proven fact. Place names in old cities rarely have one tidy origin.

Now look closely at the panels. They are made of majolica — glazed, painted ceramic, the same family of craft as Italian or Dutch tableware. Each panel shows a figure in white and soft blue. Many show soldiers, sailors and airmen. Around them run ornate frames with curls and leaves. The theme is the defence of the country in the Second World War. The station was built only a few years after the war ended, and the memory was very fresh.

Here is a detail many passengers miss. The figures are not shown in the middle of battle. They stand calm and upright, almost like statues in a park. Soviet designers of this period often preferred this style. The message was victory and order, not chaos. Walk along slowly and compare the panels. The uniforms, the weapons and the poses change from one to the next. Each one honours a different branch of the armed forces.

Think about what a passenger in 1950 saw here. Many Moscow families still lived in crowded, simple rooms. Then they came down the escalator into a hall of marble, bright lamps and decorated ceramics. The metro builders of this era wanted ordinary workers to feel that this beauty belonged to them. People often call these stations underground palaces. That phrase is a cliché now. Yet standing here, you can understand where it came from.

Notice also the shape of the hall. This is a deep station built with heavy pylons, the thick columns that carry the weight of the earth above. Between the pylons, short passages open onto the tracks. The design is strong and practical. It also creates a rhythm as you walk: open, closed, open, closed. Light falls across the floor in bands, and the ceramic panels catch it on their glazed surfaces.

Taganskaya is not one station, but a small knot of stations. Passages connect the Ring line with a second Taganskaya on another line, and with Marksistskaya nearby. The other platforms come from a later period. Their design is plainer and more modern. If you walk through the transfer tunnels, you can feel the change in style. The ornament of the early 1950s gives way to the simpler taste of the following decades.

Now let us go up to the surface. The escalators here are long, so take your time. Stand on the right if you are not walking. Above ground is Taganskaya Square, a wide and busy junction of roads and tram lines. Big roads cross here, and the traffic noise is constant. But behind the main streets, the old Taganka district keeps quieter lanes, small churches and older merchant houses.

Taganka has a strong place in Russian culture. In the nineteenth century and later, a well-known prison stood in this area. A famous Russian song carries the name Taganka, and many Russians still connect the word with that dark chapter. The prison no longer exists. Today the name brings other associations too, especially theatre.

A short walk from the station stands the Taganka Theatre. It was founded in 1964 under the director Yuri Lyubimov. In the Soviet years it became known for bold staging and for testing the limits of censorship. The singer, poet and actor Vladimir Vysotsky worked there and became one of its best-known performers. For many Muscovites, the word Taganka means this theatre first and the prison second.

There is one more surprise in the neighbourhood. Not far from the square, a former Cold War facility lies deep underground. It is often called Bunker 42, and today it operates as a museum with guided visits. So this district has two hidden worlds below the streets. One is the decorated metro, built for crowds and celebration. The other is a secret bunker, built to survive a war that never came.

Before you leave, go back to one of those blue and white panels, or picture it in your mind. Notice the small cracks in the glaze, the faint wear on the edges, the light on the curved frame. Thousands of people pass it every hour without looking. Trains arrive, doors open, the warm wind returns. And for one quiet moment, you are part of the long line of travellers who have stood exactly here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit outside rush hours to study the panels calmly.
Stand on the right side of the long escalators.
Keep clear of platform edges when trains approach.
Walk the transfer tunnels to compare station styles.
Book Bunker 42 visits in advance as tours are guided.
