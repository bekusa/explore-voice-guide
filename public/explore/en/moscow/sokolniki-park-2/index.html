The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the tall trees, the city's roar drops to a low hum. Gravel crunches under someone's bicycle tyres. Somewhere a crow calls, and the air smells of damp bark and cut grass. You are standing in Sokolniki, one of Moscow's oldest green spaces. Just behind you, traffic still runs along the boulevards. Ahead, long straight alleys run away into the woods, and they all seem to point toward one place.

Start with the name, because it holds the whole story. In Russian, a sokolnik is a falconer. Several centuries ago, this forest on the north-eastern edge of Moscow was a royal hunting ground. The tsars kept falcons here, along with the men who trained them. Falconry was a serious court passion in the seventeenth century, and Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich, the father of Peter the Great, was especially devoted to it. The trees around you once echoed with hawking parties and the bells tied to the birds' legs.

Those falconers lived in a settlement near the forest. Over time their trade gave its name to the woods, then to the surrounding district, and later to the metro station you may have arrived at. That station opened in 1935 on the very first line of the Moscow Metro. So a word for a medieval hunting job is now printed on modern underground maps. It is a small surprise many visitors walk past without noticing.

Now look down the alleys again. The layout here is not random woodland. In the nineteenth century, straight radial paths were cut through the forest. Locals call them the prosekí, which roughly means cleared lanes. They spread out from a central circle like the spokes of a wheel. Stand at the start of one and you will see the path narrow toward a vanishing point, framed on both sides by tall trunks. It is a very deliberate kind of perspective, made from living trees.

That geometry gives Sokolniki its particular feeling. Wherever you walk, you are rarely lost for long. Each lane eventually leads back to the circle near the heart of the park. Moscow families have used this simple map for generations. Children run ahead down one spoke, parents follow, and everyone meets again at the centre. It works like a forest that someone tidied, but did not tame completely.

As Moscow grew, the old royal forest slowly became a place for ordinary people. By the late nineteenth century, city residents came here for summer walks, tea, and fresh air. Wooden country houses, the dachas, rose nearby, and wealthier Muscovites spent their warm months close to these trees. In the early Soviet period, the woods were formally organised as a public park of culture and rest, the Soviet name for a leisure park. The falcons were gone. The crowds had arrived.

Here is a detail that art lovers often miss. In 1879, the young painter Isaac Levitan made a picture called Autumn Day, Sokolniki. It shows a woman in dark clothes walking alone down a park lane, with yellow leaves around her and grey sky above. Levitan later became one of Russia's best-loved landscape painters, and that canvas now hangs in the Tretyakov Gallery in the city centre. If you come here in October, you may find almost the same scene in front of you.

And now the second surprise, which belongs to the history of the Cold War. In the summer of 1959, Sokolniki hosted the American National Exhibition. It was a rare moment, when the United States showed its products and way of life directly to Soviet citizens. Visitors queued to see cars, televisions, and a model American kitchen. Many tasted Pepsi-Cola for the first time.

It was beside that model kitchen that Vice President Richard Nixon and Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev began a sharp, public argument. They debated washing machines, rockets, and which system offered people a better life. The exchange became known as the Kitchen Debate, and it travelled around the world in newspapers and on television. Today the spot looks peaceful. But for a few days, this quiet park was a stage for the two most powerful countries on Earth.

The exhibition tradition did not disappear. Part of the park is still used as an exhibition centre, with large halls that host trade fairs and events. So you may notice modern buildings and service roads near the edges. They sit beside older features, such as ponds where ducks gather and the formal flower beds near the main entrance. Near that entrance, a fountain square marks the place where most visitors begin, especially on warm weekends.

The park also changes completely with the seasons. In summer, people rent bikes, lie on the grass, and read under the trees. In autumn, the birches and maples turn yellow and orange, and the light becomes low and golden in the afternoon. In winter, snow covers the lanes, and parts of the park are used for skating and cross-country skiing. Moscow winters are long, and Sokolniki gives many people a reason to go outside anyway.

It helps to know what lies beyond the edges. To the north-east, the park borders Losiny Ostrov, which means Elk Island. This is a very large protected forest, partly inside the city limits. In a way, Sokolniki is the soft, cultivated edge of a much wilder woodland. If you walk far enough along the outer paths, the benches thin out and the trees close in. The old hunting forest still feels close.

On the western side, the small Yauza River runs near the park, one of the rivers that shaped old Moscow. Water, forest, and city meet here in a way that explains why the tsars liked this place. It was near enough to the Kremlin for a day's ride, yet far enough to feel like open country. That same balance is what draws Muscovites today, on a lunch break or a long Sunday walk.

So take one more look down the lane in front of you. Notice how the trunks line up, and how the path narrows toward a point you cannot quite see. Falconers walked here, Levitan painted here, and two world leaders argued here about kitchens. Now it is your turn. As you walk away, listen for the gravel under your feet, and for the moment when the city's noise returns.

Practical tips
