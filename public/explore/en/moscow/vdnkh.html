The guide

The story

Listen first. Water falls onto granite, a steady roar that grows louder as you walk. Gold flashes at the edge of your vision. Under your feet, a wide avenue of pale stone runs straight ahead, almost too wide for the people on it. This is VDNKh, in the north of Moscow. It is part park, part museum, and part stage set, built to show a whole country what its future was supposed to look like.

The story begins in 1939. The site opened as the All-Union Agricultural Exhibition, a showcase for Soviet farming. Each region and republic built its own pavilion, and each tried to look richer and more confident than its neighbours. Wheat, cotton, cattle, machines... everything was displayed as proof of progress. The exhibition closed during the Second World War. It reopened in the 1950s, larger and more ornate than before.

In 1959 the site received the name it carries today. VDNKh is a Russian abbreviation. In English it means the Exhibition of Achievements of the National Economy. The focus moved beyond farming to industry, science, and later space. So the pavilions you pass were not built for one purpose. Many of them changed their exhibits, and sometimes their whole identity, several times across the Soviet decades.

Start with the main entrance arch. On top stands a pair of figures, a tractor driver and a collective farm woman. Together they raise a sheaf of wheat toward the sky. Pay attention to their scale and their confident stride. Arches like this were common in Stalin-era design. They tell the visitor, before any word is spoken, that they are entering a special territory with its own rules.

Walk forward and the avenue leads you to the Central Pavilion. Its tall spire carries a star at the top, and the building rises in tiers like a wedding cake. This is the architecture often called Stalinist Empire style, a Soviet blend of classical columns and heavy decoration. It was meant to feel permanent. Stand at its steps and turn around. The whole avenue lines up behind you, exactly as the planners intended.

Now the sound of water returns. The Friendship of Nations fountain is the image most people carry home. Sixteen gilded women stand in a ring around a central golden sheaf. Each figure represents a Soviet republic of the 1950s, dressed in a stylised version of its traditional costume. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Today there are fifteen former Soviet republics, not sixteen. The extra figure stands for a republic that was later folded into Russia.

The fountain carries a message about unity, of course. But it also carries a quiet irony. Every republic shown in that golden circle is now an independent country, except the one that disappeared. Watch how visitors from different countries look for their own figure. Some take photographs with pride. Others simply stand and study the faces. The fountain means different things to different people, and that is part of its power now.

Further along stands the Stone Flower fountain, built around the same time. Its centrepiece is a huge open bloom covered in coloured stone, inspired by the decorative stonework of the Ural Mountains. Around it, bunches of metal fruit and grain spill out like a harvest. Look closely at the surface. The colour comes from many small pieces of stone set side by side, not from paint. On a sunny day the wet stone glows.

As you wander between the pavilions, notice how each one tries to speak in a regional voice. Some carry carved ornaments, some carry ceramic tiles, some carry patterns borrowed from carpets or embroidery. Armenia, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Georgia, Belarus, and others all had pavilions here. The details are often more interesting than the facades. Look for wheat, grapes, and cotton bolls worked into the plaster above doorways.

In the 1990s, after the Soviet Union ended, the site lost its purpose. It became the All-Russian Exhibition Centre. Many pavilions were rented out as shops and markets, selling electronics, clothes, and garden seedlings. Paint peeled. Fountains sometimes stood dry. Older Muscovites still remember buying a television or a cheap phone inside a building once dedicated to a Soviet republic. That chapter is a real part of the place, even if little sign of it remains.

The old name, VDNKh, returned in 2014, and a long restoration followed. Fountains were repaired, gilding was renewed, and many pavilions became museums and exhibition halls again. The Space pavilion is one of the most striking results. Its huge interior space holds Soviet spacecraft and models, under a vaulted glass roof. In front of it stands a full-size replica of the Vostok rocket, the type that carried the first human into orbit.

Space is a theme that extends beyond the grounds. Near the southern entrance, a tall titanium monument rises in a single curve, like the trail of a launching rocket. It honours the conquerors of space, and the Museum of Cosmonautics sits beneath it. Nearby, on its own high base, stands Vera Mukhina's steel sculpture of a worker and a collective farm woman, lifting a hammer and a sickle. It was first made for the 1937 World Exhibition in Paris.

The grounds cover more than two hundred hectares, so do not try to see everything. VDNKh today is also simply where Moscow comes to relax. In summer, families cycle and eat ice cream beside the ponds. In winter, the avenues become an enormous skating rink, lit up after dark. To the west, the slim Ostankino television tower rises above the trees. It makes a clean, modern contrast to all this gold.

Before you leave, stop for one more moment. Feel the width of this avenue again, and the distance between you and the next building. Everything here was built to make a single person feel small inside a grand idea. That idea has gone, but the stone, the water, and the gold remain. Listen once more to the fountains as you walk away... and notice how the city slowly takes their place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan a route because the grounds cover over two hundred hectares.
Rent a bicycle or scooter to cover the long avenues.
Visit the Space pavilion for a large indoor break.
Come in winter evenings for the lit skating avenues.
Use VDNKh metro station to reach the main entrance arch.
