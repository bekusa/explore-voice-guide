The guide

The story

Stand on the paving stones of Cathedral Square and tilt your head back. White walls rise in clean, stacked tiers, each one a little narrower than the last. At the very top, a golden dome catches the light, even on a grey Moscow afternoon. Around you, pigeons scatter across the stones, and somewhere a guide's voice carries between the cathedrals. This is the Ivan the Great Bell Tower, and for centuries it was the tallest structure in all of Moscow.

The tower stands at the heart of the Kremlin, the walled fortress that has been the seat of Russian power for most of the last seven hundred years. Look around and you will see why this spot matters. To one side stands the Cathedral of the Assumption, where tsars were crowned. Nearby are the Archangel Cathedral, where many of them were buried, and the Annunciation Cathedral. The bell tower rises above them all and ties the square together.

The story of this place begins earlier than the tower itself. In the fourteenth century, a small stone church stood here, dedicated to Saint John Climacus. He was a monk from Sinai, and his name means John of the Ladder, after a famous book he wrote about climbing towards heaven, step by step. Many historians link the name Ivan the Great to that old church. Ivan is simply the Russian form of John.

The tower you see was begun in the early 1500s. Its builder was an Italian architect, known in Russian records as Bon Fryazin. That surname was not really a family name — it was a label Russians gave to Italians and other Western Europeans. Italian masters shaped much of the Kremlin in this period. They brought Renaissance engineering skills to a city that wanted stone buildings as solid as its ambitions.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. The tower was not always this tall. The original structure was lower. Around the year 1600, under Tsar Boris Godunov, builders added the upper tier and the gilded dome. The finished tower reached roughly eighty-one metres. Lift your eyes to the band just below the dome. There is a line of large gold letters running around the drum. That inscription names Boris Godunov and his son as the men who raised the tower to its full height.

Boris Godunov is a complicated figure in Russian history. He was not born into the royal line. He rose through court politics and became tsar after the old dynasty died out. His reign ended in famine and turmoil, and his family did not hold the throne for long. Yet his name is still written high above the Kremlin in gold. It is one of the most visible signatures any ruler left on Moscow.

Now look at the shape of the walls. The tower is built as a series of octagons, one stacked on another, with open arches for the bells. The walls at the base are very thick. That thickness is the reason the tower has survived so much. Moscow has burned many times over the centuries, and wooden buildings disappeared again and again. This stone shaft stayed standing through all of it.

The tower is not alone. Attached to its side is a wider, lower building — the Assumption Belfry — and beyond it an annex with a pointed tent-shaped roof, added in the 1620s under Patriarch Filaret. Together they form a single long ensemble along the edge of the square. Inside these openings hang around twenty bells, large and small. On important church holidays, the bells still ring, and the sound rolls out over the river and the city below.

The second surprise comes from 1812. That year, Napoleon's army occupied Moscow. When the French retreated, they set explosives in the Kremlin. The blasts destroyed the belfry and the Filaret annex. But the Ivan the Great tower itself did not fall. Its thick walls held. Later, the ruined buildings beside it were rebuilt in their old form. Locals like to tell a story that French soldiers tried to take the cross from the top, believing it was solid gold. Treat that one as a popular tale.

Now turn your attention to the ground near the base of the tower. On a stone pedestal sits the Tsar Bell, one of the largest bells ever cast. It was made in the 1730s, but it never rang. A fire broke out while the bell was still in its casting pit. Water from the firefighting hit the hot metal, and a huge piece cracked away. That broken slab now rests beside the bell. Walk around it and look at the decorations cast into its bronze surface.

For much of its life, this tower had a practical job as well as a sacred one. From the top, watchmen could see far across the city and the surrounding countryside. They could spot fires or approaching armies long before anyone at street level. Today, part of the tower and belfry is used for museum exhibitions. In some seasons, visitors can climb to an upper gallery for a view over the Kremlin and the Moscow River, usually with a separate ticket.

It is worth pausing to think about scale. When the upper tier was finished, almost every house in Moscow was built of wood and stood only one or two storeys high. A white tower of this height, crowned with gold, would have been visible from far outside the city. For travellers arriving on the roads into Moscow, it was the first sign that they were close. It worked as a landmark, a lookout, and a statement of power all at once.

Before you leave, step back across the square until you can see the whole ensemble in one view. Notice how the white walls change colour as clouds move — cool grey in shadow, warm cream in sun. Listen for the bells, or for the silence where they would be. Then look once more at the gold letters under the dome. Five centuries of Moscow have passed beneath this tower, and today you are part of that long line of people looking up.
