The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel. It crunches under every step along the wide alleys, the same pale crunch that carriage wheels made here more than two centuries ago. In front of you lies a long, still pond. Behind you, a low pink-and-white palace sits on its bank. On a calm morning, the water holds a perfect copy of the facade, and for a moment you see two palaces, one standing and one upside down.

This is Kuskovo, the summer estate of the Sheremetev family. In the eighteenth century, the Sheremetevs were among the richest landowners in the Russian Empire. They owned vast lands and many thousands of serfs. Kuskovo was not a working country home. It was a stage. Its owners built it for one purpose — to receive guests, to impress them, and to entertain them on a scale few families could afford.

The man behind most of what you see was Count Pyotr Borisovich Sheremetev. His father, Field Marshal Boris Sheremetev, had been a close companion of Peter the Great. Pyotr inherited wealth, and he spent it with confidence. Through the middle and later decades of the eighteenth century, he turned a modest property into a full ensemble. It included a palace, pavilions, a church, a formal garden and a theatre. People in his own time sometimes called it the Moscow Versailles.

Now look closely at the palace itself. It looks like stone, but it is mostly wood. The timber walls are plastered and painted to look like masonry. This was a common and practical choice in Russia at the time. Wood was cheaper, faster to build, and warmer in winter. Many visitors walk right past without guessing. Once you know, you start to see the softer lines at the corners and edges.

Here is a second surprise. Much of Kuskovo was designed and built by serfs. Serfs were peasants legally bound to the land and to their owner. The Sheremetevs trained some of their own serfs as architects, painters and craftsmen. Fyodor Argunov and Alexei Mironov are two names linked with the estate's buildings. Their talent shaped these walls, yet for most of their lives they were not free people.

Inside the palace, the rooms run in a long line, one door opening into the next. This layout is called an enfilade, and it lets you look straight through the whole building. The interiors carry silk wall coverings, parquet floors in patterned woods, painted ceilings and portraits of the family. Notice how the rooms change in mood. Formal halls for receptions give way to smaller, quieter rooms for private life.

Walk out into the formal garden behind the palace. This is a French-style regular park. Paths run in straight lines. Hedges are clipped into clean shapes. Marble statues stand along the alleys like guests waiting for a party to begin. The geometry is the point. In the eighteenth century, a garden like this showed that the owner could bring order to nature itself.

Scattered through the park are small pavilions, each in a different style. The Dutch House is built of red brick with a stepped gable, a nod to Peter the Great's love of Holland. Inside, the walls are lined with blue-and-white tiles. The Italian House recalls a villa from the south. The Grotto is a pavilion decorated inside with shells and stone, meant to feel like a cool sea cave on a hot day.

One of the most charming buildings is the Hermitage, a small pavilion in the park. Its name comes from the idea of a quiet retreat. The upper floor was used for intimate dinners. According to accounts of the estate, the tables were raised from the floor below by a lifting device. Guests could eat and talk with no servants in the room at all. Privacy, in this house, was a luxury built into the machinery.

Now turn toward the Great Stone Orangery. In the eighteenth century, an orangery was a heated greenhouse for citrus trees and other plants that could not survive a Russian winter. Growing oranges near Moscow was a quiet boast of wealth. Today, this building and the estate form part of the State Museum of Ceramics. The collections hold porcelain, faience and glass from Russia, Europe and Asia across several centuries.

Kuskovo was also a place of music and theatre. The Sheremetevs kept a serf theatre company. Its most famous member was the singer and actress Praskovia Zhemchugova. Pyotr's son, Count Nikolai Sheremetev, fell in love with her. Against every rule of their society, he later married her. Their story still moves visitors, and it shows how the theatre at Kuskovo shaped real lives, not only evenings of entertainment.

The estate's great festivals were famous in their day. Records describe celebrations with fireworks over the pond, music from the gardens and boats on the water. Empress Catherine the Great visited Kuskovo during one of these occasions. Picture the pond at night, lit by lanterns and sparks, crowded with guests in silk and powdered wigs. Then look at the same water now, quiet, with ducks moving slowly across it.

Today, Kuskovo sits inside the city of Moscow, on its eastern side. Apartment blocks rise beyond the park, and the hum of traffic is never far away. Yet inside the gates, the scale is still human. Families walk the alleys. People sit on benches by the pond. In winter, snow covers the parterres, and the pink palace stands bright against the white.

Before you leave, stand once more at the edge of the pond and look back at the palace. Listen to the gravel, the birds, the distant city. Everything here was built to be seen and admired, and it still works. As you walk out, notice the statues one last time... still holding their poses, still waiting for the next guests to arrive.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early on a calm day for mirror reflections on the pond.
Buy separate tickets if you plan to enter several pavilions.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel alleys across the park.
Save the Orangery's ceramics for a rainy or cold afternoon.
Visit in winter for the pink palace against fresh snow.
