The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the platform and look up. Above you, a single wide vault curves overhead, pale and smooth, crossed by a pattern of raised ribs. Trains arrive with a rush of warm air and a low electric hum. Then the doors close, the carriages pull away, and the hall goes quiet again. The light is soft and even. It falls on pale stone walls and on a polished floor worn smooth by decades of footsteps.

This is Aeroport, a station on the Zamoskvoretskaya line, the green line of the Moscow Metro. The name means simply airport. Yet there are no runways here, no departure boards, no suitcases rolling through the hall. The name points to a time and a place that have mostly vanished. To understand the station, you need to know what once lay just to the west of where you are standing.

Not far from here stretched Khodynka Field, a wide open space on the northwest edge of old Moscow. In the early twentieth century it became the city's first airfield. Pilots tested new machines there, and for a time it served as Moscow's Central Airport. Passengers flew from this field when air travel was still new and a little dangerous. When the metro line arrived nearby, the planners named the station after this airport. The name has stayed, long after the planes moved away.

The station opened in 1938, during the second stage of metro construction. That was a period when Soviet planners saw the metro as much more than transport. Stations were meant to be underground palaces for ordinary people. Each one received its own theme, its own materials, its own character. For a station named after an airport, the theme was clear from the start. The designers turned to aviation, which was a source of enormous national pride in the 1930s.

Now look again at the ceiling. This is the detail most commuters never notice, because they look at their phones or at the train. The vault is covered with a grid of ribs that form repeating diamond shapes. Many visitors compare this pattern to the inner frame of an airship, or to the skeleton of an early aircraft wing before the fabric was stretched across it. Once you see it this way, it is hard to see anything else.

This connection to airships is worth a moment. In the 1930s, airships were not yet a curiosity of the past. Several countries, including the Soviet Union, still built them and flew them. They promised slow, grand journeys across huge distances. So a ceiling that recalls an airship frame was not nostalgic when it was built. It was modern. It pointed toward the future that people of that decade believed they were about to enter.

Notice also the shape of the hall itself. Aeroport is a shallow station, built close to the surface rather than deep underground. Instead of rows of heavy columns, it has one broad, open vault. This gives the platform a feeling of lightness and space, a little like standing inside a hangar. The effect is quiet and calm. There is no gold, no mosaic crowd of heroes, none of the heavy decoration you find at some central stations. The beauty here comes from line and proportion.

That restraint is part of the surprise. Many people arrive in Moscow expecting every metro station to look like a ballroom. Some do. But Aeroport shows a different side of the same era — a cooler, more engineered elegance. The designers let the structure carry the theme. The ribs are not added ornament; they are the main idea. It is a station that rewards a slow look rather than a quick photograph.

When you climb back up to the street, you arrive on Leningradsky Prospekt. This wide avenue runs northwest out of the city toward the airport at Sheremetyevo and, much further on, toward Saint Petersburg. The road has carried travellers out of Moscow for centuries. So in a way, the name still fits. You are standing on one of the main routes that people take when they leave the city by air.

The neighbourhood around the station also carries the name. The whole district is called Aeroport. Its streets are lined with large residential blocks from the middle of the twentieth century, with tall windows, heavy entrances, and quiet inner courtyards full of trees. In the decades after the Second World War, this area became home to many people from the worlds of literature, science, and the arts. Several cooperative apartment buildings here were associated with writers, and the district gained a reputation as a thoughtful, bookish corner of the city.

It is worth walking a few blocks away from the main avenue to feel that atmosphere. The traffic noise drops quickly. You hear children in the courtyards, the scrape of a shovel in winter, the rustle of poplars and lime trees in summer. Older residents sit on benches near the doorways. Small shops and cafes sit on the ground floors. This is everyday Moscow, far from the souvenir stalls of Red Square, and it moves at its own pace.

If you head west toward the old airfield, you will see how much the area has changed. The open field of the early aviators is now largely covered by new apartment towers, parks, and shopping centres. Little of the original airport remains visible. That makes the station below your feet even more interesting. Underground, the airport is still remembered in stone and plaster, while on the surface the city has moved on and built over it.

Before you leave, take the escalator or the stairs slowly and glance back at the hall one last time. Watch how the light runs along the ribs of the vault. Listen to the next train approaching through the tunnel, the rising whine of the motors, the rush of air across the platform. Every few minutes thousands of people pass through here, heading to work, to school, to the airport. Most of them never look up... but now, you know what to look for.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit outside rush hour to study the ceiling calmly.
Stand mid-platform for the clearest view along the vault.
Walk into side streets to see the postwar residential courtyards.
Combine with a walk toward the former Khodynka Field area.
Avoid blocking escalators; stand on the right side.
