The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look up. Under the great curved roof, the sound behaves differently. Laughter, music and the rumble of a passing ride all fold together into one warm, steady hum. The light is soft and even, filtered through the glass overhead, so the afternoon feels the same whether the sky outside is grey or blue. You are standing inside one of the largest covered theme parks in Europe. It sits on a quiet bend of the Moskva River, in the south of the city.

This is Dream Island, known in Russian as Ostrov Mechty. It opened in early 2020. That timing is part of its story. The park welcomed its first visitors only weeks before the pandemic closed public spaces across Moscow. A place built for crowds went silent almost as soon as it opened. When the doors opened again, Muscovites came back. For many of them, an indoor world with its own weather suddenly made a lot of sense.

That is really the key idea behind this place. Moscow winters are long, dark and cold. Snow can stay on the ground for months, and daylight is short. Most outdoor amusement parks in the region spend half the year waiting for spring. Dream Island does not need to wait. Under its roof, a family can ride a roller coaster in January wearing a light jumper. The building is a practical answer to a northern climate, wrapped in fairytale clothing.

And the clothing is deliberate. Look back at the main entrance and you will see a castle facade, with towers, turrets and pale walls. It looks like a storybook palace. It is not a historical building, and it does not pretend to be one. It is a stage set on a huge scale. Its job is to tell you, before you even step inside, that the ordinary rules of the city stop here.

Now look at the land around the building. This whole district sits on the Nagatino floodplain, a low peninsula wrapped by a loop of the river. For a long time, this was open, marshy ground. The river flooded it, and it was hard to build on. For most of Moscow's history, it stayed on the edge of things. Turning it into a place for entertainment was a large engineering project. It also shows how far the city has spread outward in recent decades.

Here is something many visitors miss. Just across the water lies Kolomenskoye, a former royal estate of the Moscow tsars. Its white stone Church of the Ascension, from the sixteenth century, is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. So on one bank of the river stands one of Russia's oldest stone churches. On the other bank stands a twenty-first century glass-roofed fantasy castle. Few places in the city put the very old and the very new so close together.

Inside, the park is divided into themed zones. Each zone has its own architecture, colour palette and soundtrack. Some draw on fairy tales and animated films. Others build whole little worlds around a single story or character. As you walk between them, notice how the paving changes, how the music shifts, and how the shop fronts take on a new style. The designers wanted you to feel that you have crossed a border, even though you only walked a few metres.

The rides range widely. Gentle carousels and slow boat trips suit the smallest children. There are also proper roller coasters, with drops and turns that make adults grip the bar. Several attractions use screens and moving seats together, so the room seems to fly or fall around you. Queues build quickly at weekends and during school holidays. A little planning about which rides matter most to your group will save a lot of standing around.

Here is a second surprise. The theme park is only one part of the complex. Dream Island also includes a long covered shopping and dining area. It is built to look like a row of European city streets, with painted facades, balconies and lamp posts. You can wander there without a ride ticket at all. Many locals come only for this part, for a meal or a walk on a rainy evening. The complex also has a cinema, a concert hall and a hotel.

Outside the building, a landscaped park stretches across the peninsula. There are paths, lawns, ponds and open views toward the river. In summer, this outdoor space becomes a reason to visit by itself. People cycle, walk dogs and sit on the grass. It is a useful reminder that Dream Island is not only a sealed box of attractions. It was planned as a whole district for leisure, both indoors and out.

It is fair to say that the place divides opinion. Some Muscovites see it as a welcome gift for families. They value a safe, warm and clean day out in a city that did not have anything quite like it before. Others find it too commercial, or feel it belongs to a global style rather than to Moscow. Both views make sense. Standing here, you can decide for yourself what you think of a fairytale built from steel, glass and careful marketing.

Whatever you decide, watch the people around you. That is often the most honest part of any theme park. Notice the grandparents with phones raised, filming a ride they would never board themselves. Notice the teenagers comparing photos, and the toddler who has fallen asleep in a pushchair halfway through the day. The castle and the coasters are the setting. But the real content of this place is these small family moments, repeated thousands of times every weekend.

Before you leave, stop once more under the glass roof and look up. Depending on the hour, you may see clouds moving past, or the early dark of a Moscow evening pressing against the panes. Inside, the light stays gentle and the music keeps playing. As you walk back out toward the river, the air will feel sharper and the city will sound louder. Take a moment there. Look across the water toward Kolomenskoye, and notice how two very different Moscows sit side by side.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come on a weekday to avoid the longest queues.
Pick your top rides before entering a themed zone.
Visit the covered shopping streets without a ride ticket.
Walk the outdoor park in summer for river views.
Dress in layers; indoors stays warm all year.
