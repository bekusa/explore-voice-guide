The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the hill, the Pacific is breaking on the shore. The wind comes in off the water, carrying salt and the green smell of pine. Under your feet, the path is smooth and wide, made for strollers, bicycles and slow walkers. Then you come over a small rise, and the land ahead turns to colour. Whole slopes are planted with a single flower. For a moment, it can look less like a garden and more like weather.

Welcome to Hitachi Seaside Park. Most visitors arrive with Mito in mind, the capital of Ibaraki Prefecture. In fact, the park sits just beyond Mito, in the neighbouring coastal city of Hitachinaka. It is a short trip from the capital, and many people combine the two in one day. But this park lives in a different world from Mito's formal gardens. It belongs to the sea, the dunes and the open sky.

The ground you are standing on has a surprising past. For years after the Second World War, this coastline was used by the United States military as a firing and training range. Planes practised over these dunes. The land was returned to Japan in the early 1970s. Planners then faced a question: what do you do with a former range beside the ocean? Their answer, years later, was this national government park, which opened its first areas in 1991.

That is worth holding in your mind as you walk. The quiet paths, the picnic lawns, the families on bicycles... all of it grew on ground that once held a very different purpose. The park is still large, and parts of it feel wild. Pine forest and coastal grass cover much of the land. You can spend hours here and still not see every corner.

The heart of the park is Miharashi Hill. The name means, roughly, a hill with a wide view. It is the highest point in the park, but it is gentle, not steep. A winding path leads to the top. From the summit, you can see the Pacific stretch away to the east. On a clear day, the line between the sea and the sky seems to disappear. This hill is where the park became known around the world.

In spring, usually from late April into May, Miharashi Hill turns blue. The flower is nemophila, known in English as baby blue eyes. Millions of these tiny plants cover the slope. Each flower is small, pale blue with a white centre. On its own, it is easy to miss. Together, they form a soft blue blanket that blends into the sky above. Photographs of this hill spread across the internet and brought visitors from many countries.

Here is the first surprise. Nemophila is not a Japanese flower. It comes from western North America, from places like California. The gardeners here chose it for its colour and its low, even growth. So the scene that many people now picture as a symbol of Japanese springtime is built from an American wildflower, planted on land that an American military once used. History in this place has some unexpected turns.

Now look again at the hill in autumn. If you are here in October, you will see something completely different. The slope is covered in round, soft bushes called kochia. In summer, they are bright green, almost like small fluffy trees. As the nights cool, they turn red. By the peak of the season, the hill glows crimson. Later, the colour fades to a warm brown, and the bushes dry in the wind.

And here is the second surprise. Kochia is not only decorative. In parts of northern Japan, especially Akita Prefecture, the seeds of this plant are eaten. The dish is called tonburi. The tiny seeds pop gently in the mouth, and people sometimes call it land caviar. So the red bushes in front of you are related to a traditional rural food. Many visitors take their photos here without knowing that.

The park changes with every season, not only in spring and autumn. Early in the year, daffodils bloom under the pine trees in a quiet woodland garden. Tulips follow in planted beds. Summer brings other flowers and long green lawns. The gardeners plan the seasons carefully, so one display follows another. If you come back in a different month, you will meet a different park on the same ground.

This place is not only about flowers. There is a small amusement area with rides and a large Ferris wheel. From the top of the wheel, you can see the shape of the park below: the forest, the hill, the coastline. Cycling paths run through the grounds, and many visitors rent bicycles to cover the long distances. For many years, the park also hosted one of Japan's largest summer rock festivals, filling these lawns with music.

For local families, this is an everyday place. You will see children running across the grass and grandparents resting on benches. Groups unpack lunches under the pines. In peak flower weeks, the crowds can be heavy, and the paths fill with cameras. On quieter weekdays, the park feels calm and open. Come early in the morning, and you may share the hill with only the wind and a few gardeners at work.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the light on the hill, whichever colour it carries today. Listen for the sea behind the dunes. Feel the wind that shapes the pines and moves the flowers in long waves. As you walk back toward the gate, look at the ground at your feet. It once held targets. Now it holds seeds, and every season it grows something new.

Practical tips

Visit on a weekday during peak bloom weeks.
Bring a windproof layer for breezes off the Pacific.
