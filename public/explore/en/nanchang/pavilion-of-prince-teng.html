The guide

The story

Stand at the railing and feel the wind come off the water. The Gan River is wide here, broad and brown and slow, and it carries the faint smell of wet stone and river mud. Below you, boats move without hurry. Above you, layers of curved eaves step up toward the sky, each corner lifting like the tip of a brush stroke. This is the Pavilion of Prince Teng, on the east bank of the river in Nanchang.

The story begins in the seventh century, during the Tang dynasty, one of the great golden ages of Chinese culture. A prince named Li Yuanying, a son of the dynasty's founding emperor, held the title Prince Teng. In the year 653, while he served as governor in this region, he built a pavilion on the riverbank. It was a place for banquets, for music, for looking out over the water. The building took its name from his title, and the name has stayed for more than thirteen centuries.

Here is the first surprise. The prince is not the person who made this place famous. Tradition also remembers him as a painter, especially of butterflies, and a style of butterfly painting carries his name. But his pavilion might have faded into the long list of forgotten buildings... if not for a young poet who arrived some twenty years later.

His name was Wang Bo. He was in his twenties, already known as one of the brightest literary talents of his generation. Around the year 675, he was travelling south to visit his father, and he passed through this city. At that time, the local governor held a grand autumn banquet in the pavilion, and the guests were invited to compose a piece of writing for the occasion.

The story that people tell about that day is part history, part legend. According to the popular tale, the governor had quietly arranged for his own son-in-law to write the honoured piece in advance. The other guests politely declined the brush. Wang Bo, young and perhaps unaware of the plan, accepted it. Legend adds that a river spirit sent a favourable wind to carry his boat here just in time. Whatever the truth, what he wrote that day changed this building forever.

The text is known as the Preface to the Pavilion of Prince Teng. It is written in a formal, balanced style, where lines are paired in rhythm and meaning, like two halves of a mirror. Chinese schoolchildren still memorise parts of it. And one couplet stands above all the rest. In English, it runs something like this: the evening clouds fly together with a lone wild duck, and the autumn water shares one colour with the endless sky.

Pause for a moment and look out over the river. On a clear evening, you can still see the scene he described. The water turns the same pale gold as the clouds. Birds cross the horizon. The line between river and sky becomes hard to find. That is the second surprise of this place. The most famous view in Chinese literature is not a painting in a museum. It is right in front of you, and it changes every day.

Wang Bo did not live long enough to enjoy his fame. Not long after that banquet, during the same journey, he drowned at sea. He was still a young man. His preface became his most lasting work, and through it, the pavilion entered Chinese memory. Over the centuries, it came to be counted among the Three Great Towers south of the Yangtze River, together with the Yellow Crane Tower in Wuhan and the Yueyang Tower in Hunan.

Now a confession about the building you are looking at. It is not the Tang pavilion. Fire, war, and flood destroyed the structure again and again, and each time it was rebuilt. Records count the reconstructions in the dozens. Later writers, including the Tang essayist Han Yu, also wrote about the pavilion and its restoration. The building you see today was completed in 1989, after the earlier one was lost to fire during fighting in the 1920s.

The modern pavilion is built mainly of reinforced concrete, but it follows the look of classical Song dynasty architecture. Its design drew on sketches by Liang Sicheng, a pioneering scholar of traditional Chinese building. Notice how the structure is arranged. From the outside, it appears to have three main storeys. Inside, there are more hidden levels tucked between them. This layering is an old technique, and it gives the building its tall, stepped profile.

As you walk through, look at the colours. The roofs are covered in glazed tiles, and the columns and beams carry deep reds, greens, and golds. The upper floors hold displays of calligraphy, paintings, and scenes connected with the pavilion's long literary life. On some floors, you may hear traditional music, or see small performances that recall the old banquets. Take your time on the staircases. Each level opens a slightly different view of the river and the city.

From the highest balconies, Nanchang spreads in every direction. To one side, glass towers and bridges stretch across the Gan. To the other, the older streets press close to the riverbank. It is a strange and honest contrast. A Song-style roof against a modern skyline. A poem written in the seventh century, read today by visitors holding phones. The pavilion has always been rebuilt for its own time, and this version is no exception.

Before you leave, go back to the railing one more time. Let the wind reach you again. Watch a single bird cross the water, or a cloud move slowly over the far bank. Somewhere in that view are the clouds and the lone wild duck that a young poet saw on an autumn evening long ago. The building has burned and risen many times. The river and the sky are the parts that stayed.
