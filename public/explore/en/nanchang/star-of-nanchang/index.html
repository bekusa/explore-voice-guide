The guide

The story

Listen first. Above you, there is a low, steady hum, the sound of a very large machine moving very slowly. A breeze comes off the Gan River, carrying the smell of damp stone and water. Tip your head back and follow the white steel spokes as they climb into the sky. The wheel turns so gently that at first it seems to stand still. Then you notice that one cabin, near the top a moment ago, has quietly slipped a little lower.

This is the Star of Nanchang, a giant observation wheel on the bank of the river in the capital of Jiangxi Province. It stands about 160 metres tall, roughly the height of a fifty-storey tower. When it opened in 2006, it was the tallest Ferris wheel in the world. It took that title from the London Eye, which is shorter by about twenty-five metres. For a short time, this city in southeastern China held a record that most people in the world never heard about.

That record did not last long. In 2008, the Singapore Flyer opened and claimed first place. Since then, taller wheels have appeared in other countries, and the list keeps changing. Here is something many visitors miss, though. Engineers draw a difference between a classic Ferris wheel and an observation wheel. A Ferris wheel is held up by supports on both sides, while some newer designs lean on one side only. By some counts, this structure stayed among the tallest of its own type long after it lost the overall title.

Now look closely at the rim. Sixty enclosed cabins hang from it, spaced evenly like beads on a bracelet. Each cabin holds a small group, usually up to about eight people. The cabins are closed and air-conditioned, which matters a great deal here. Nanchang has a reputation for hot, heavy summers. In Chinese, people sometimes call it one of the country's furnace cities. Without that cool air inside, a long slow ride in July would feel very different.

And the ride is long. One full turn takes around half an hour. That slow pace is the point. The wheel does not stop for each group to get on. Instead, it keeps moving at a walking pace, and staff help you step in while the cabin glides past the platform. Then the doors close, the noise of the ground falls away, and for thirty minutes your only task is to look.

So what will you see? As your cabin rises, the Gan River opens out below. This is the largest river in Jiangxi. It runs north through the province and finally empties into Poyang Lake, the biggest freshwater lake in China. On a clear day, the water looks wide and slow, and long barges move along it. On a hazy day, which is common here, the far bank softens into grey and the bridges seem to float.

Across the river, you may see the towers of the Honggutan district. This is the newer side of the city, built up quickly in recent decades. Its glass buildings rise in neat lines along the waterfront. In the evening, the facades of many of these towers carry large light displays. Whole buildings change colour together, and moving images run across them. From the wheel, you see the entire show at once, like a screen laid along the horizon.

The older story of Nanchang is also within view, if you know where to look. Somewhere along the riverbank stands the Pavilion of Prince Teng, a tall, many-roofed tower. The first pavilion on this site dates back to the Tang dynasty, more than a thousand years ago. The building you see today is a modern reconstruction. Over the centuries, the pavilion was destroyed and rebuilt many times, which is part of its long story.

The pavilion became famous because of a young poet named Wang Bo. In the Tang dynasty, he wrote a preface for a gathering there. One of his lines is still learned by schoolchildren across China. It describes evening clouds flying together with a lone wild duck, and autumn water sharing one colour with the endless sky. Here is the second surprise. That poem describes the same river and the same sky you are looking at now, from a much higher seat.

Nanchang carries a more recent history as well. On the first of August, 1927, an armed uprising took place in this city. Chinese history remembers it as the founding moment of the army that later became the People's Liberation Army. Because of this, Nanchang is often called a heroic city. You may notice the date, the first of August, on street names, bridges, and squares. In Chinese, people say it as Bayi, which simply means eight-one.

So the wheel sits in an interesting place. Behind you is a city of old poems and revolutionary memory. In front of you is a symbol of the 2000s, when China was building quickly and boldly. Many of the giant wheels in China were built in this same period. They gave fast-growing cities a new way to show themselves to their own people. A family from the countryside could ride up and see the whole city at once.

A few practical thoughts before you board. The view changes a lot with the time of day. Late afternoon gives you soft side light on the river. Just after sunset, you get both the last colour in the sky and the first city lights. Weekends and public holidays can bring long queues, so a weekday visit is usually calmer. If you are sensitive to heights, choose a seat facing the river rather than looking straight down.

Inside the cabin, take a moment near the very top. This is where the wheel feels quietest. The river sounds are gone, and the city traffic becomes a faint line of moving lights. Glass on all sides makes the cabin feel like a small floating room. Notice how the bridges connect the old bank with the new one. From up here, the whole layout of Nanchang suddenly makes sense.

When you are back on the ground, turn around once more. The cabin you just left is already climbing again, carrying someone else toward the same view. The hum continues above you, steady and patient. Walk a little way along the riverbank and look back as the light changes. The white spokes will catch the evening sky, and the wheel will keep turning, slowly, as it has done here every day since 2006.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive just before sunset for daylight and city lights.
Visit on a weekday to avoid long holiday queues.
Board carefully, since the wheel keeps moving while loading.
Choose a river-facing seat if you dislike looking down.
Bring a cloth to wipe cabin glass for clearer photos.
