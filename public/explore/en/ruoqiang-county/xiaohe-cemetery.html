The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the loudest sound is often the wind. It moves across the sand in long, dry breaths, and it carries a fine grit that settles on your sleeves and in the corners of your eyes. Ahead of you rises a low, oval hill of sand. On top of it stands a crowd of wooden poles, grey and cracked, leaning at odd angles against a pale sky. This is the Xiaohe Cemetery. Its name means Small River, and for about four thousand years, it has kept its dead.

You are deep in the Tarim Basin, in the eastern reaches of the Taklamakan Desert, within the wide territory of Ruoqiang County in Xinjiang. Not far away lies Lop Nur, once a great salt lake, now mostly a dry and cracked basin. It is hard to picture this land as green. Yet the people buried here lived beside a river, with grass, cattle, and reeds. The desert you see today is the end of that story, not the beginning.

The modern story of the site begins in the 1930s. A Swedish archaeologist named Folke Bergman was working in the region. A local hunter called Ördek told him about a hill covered with a thousand coffins. Ördek guided Bergman across the dunes, and in 1934 Bergman reached the mound and recorded what he saw. He named the place after a small river nearby. Then war came, the region closed, and the location was lost for decades. For more than sixty years, no outsider could find it again.

It was only around the year 2000 that a team from the Xinjiang Institute of Archaeology relocated the hill, using satellite positioning and Bergman's old notes. Systematic excavation followed in the early 2000s. What the archaeologists found changed how scholars think about the Bronze Age in Central Asia. The dry air, the salty soil, and the sealed graves had preserved the dead with remarkable care — skin, hair, eyelashes, and clothing were all still there.

Now look again at the poles. They are not random. Many of them mark individual burials, and they come in two main shapes. Some are broad and flat at the top, a little like the blade of an oar. Others are rounded and pillar-like, and archaeologists widely read them as male symbols. The two shapes appear to correspond to the burials of women and men. In other words, this hill is covered with symbols of life and fertility, placed over the dead. It is a surprising thing to realise while standing among them.

Beneath the sand, the coffins were just as unusual. Many were shaped like small upturned boats, made of wooden planks with no bottom. The body lay on the sand, and the boat-shaped frame covered it. Over the top, the builders stretched fresh cattle hide. As the hide dried, it shrank and tightened like a drumskin, sealing the coffin almost airtight. That single choice — wet hide on wood in a dry land — is a large part of why the bodies survived so well.

The people inside were dressed for a long journey. They wore wool cloaks wrapped around their shoulders and felt hats, some decorated with feathers. Small bundles of ephedra twigs, a desert shrub, were often placed with them. Woven baskets sat beside many bodies, made from grasses with real skill. Some of the most famous finds are faces that still look calm, with long lashes and fine features. One woman's face is so well preserved that she is often called the Beauty of Xiaohe.

Here is a detail most visitors never hear. In some baskets and on some bodies, researchers found yellowish lumps of an old dairy product. Scientific analysis later identified it as a kind of fermented cheese, similar in method to kefir. It is among the oldest cheese ever found anywhere in the world. Imagine that — a snack, packed for the afterlife, still recognisable after four thousand years in the sand.

For a long time, these mummies sat at the centre of a heated debate. Their features, their wool, and their felt seemed to point far to the west. Many people assumed they were migrants from the steppe or from even farther away. Then, in 2021, a genetic study published in the journal Nature looked at their DNA. The result surprised almost everyone. The earliest Xiaohe people appear to have been a local population, isolated for a very long time, descended from ancient hunter-gatherer groups of inner Asia.

So their genes were local, but their culture was connected. They raised cattle, sheep, and goats. They used wheat and millet. They made dairy products in ways known from the steppe. Ideas and goods clearly travelled to them, even if people mostly did not. It is a quiet reminder that cultures can share far more than bloodlines. A recipe, a hat, a way of herding — these crossed the desert on their own.

If you are here today, you are one of very few people who have stood on this mound. The site is extremely remote, protected, and closely managed. Reaching it means hours of desert driving and official permission. Most of the finds are no longer here. Many of the mummies and objects are kept in museums, including the Xinjiang Museum in Ürümqi. What remains on the hill is the setting itself — the sand, the poles, and the shape of the ground.

Take a moment to notice the surface under your feet. The wind has worked on this mound for thousands of years, and it is still working. Some poles stand tall, some have fallen, some are broken into stumps. The wood has turned silver and deeply grooved, like old rope. In the low light of morning or evening, the poles throw long shadows across the dune. The whole hill looks less like a graveyard and more like a forest that forgot to grow leaves.

Before you turn away, look out at the horizon. Somewhere out there was the river that gave this place its name. The people who lived along it are gone, and so is the water. But their hats, their baskets, and even their cheese have travelled forward to us. As you walk back across the sand, listen once more to the wind. It is the same wind that dried their cattle hides, and it is still shaping this quiet hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Travel only with an authorised operator holding the required permits.
Carry ample water; the drive crosses long empty desert.
Wear sunglasses and a scarf against blowing sand.
Visit the Xinjiang Museum in Ürümqi to see the mummies.
Stay on marked paths and never touch the wooden poles.
