The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Out here it runs low across the gravel and the sand, and it does not stop. Under your feet the ground is hard, pale and scattered with small dark stones. Ahead of you, a long wall of packed earth rises out of the desert. It is the colour of dry bread, and it holds sand dunes against its sides like snow banked against a fence. This is Khara-Khoto, the Black City, at the far western edge of Inner Mongolia.

The name is Mongolian, and it means exactly that, Black City. Nobody is quite sure why. The walls are not black, and neither is the earth. One idea is that the name came later, from the Mongol herders who grazed animals here long after the city was empty. To them, a ruined town in the desert may simply have looked dark against the pale ground. Whatever the reason, the name stayed.

The people who built much of what you see were the Tangut. They ruled a kingdom that Chinese histories call Western Xia. From roughly the eleventh century to the early thirteenth century, it covered a wide stretch of what is now northwest China. The Tangut were Buddhist. They had their own written language, with thousands of complex characters. For a long time, almost no one in the modern world could read it.

Why build a city here, of all places? The answer is water. The Ejin River, which locals also call the Black River, flows north out of the mountains toward the desert lakes. In the past, its channels came close to this spot. Water meant fields, trees, and grazing land. It also meant control of a route between the Mongolian steppe and the Hexi Corridor, the narrow strip of land that carried Silk Road traffic toward Central Asia.

In the early thirteenth century, the armies of Genghis Khan came south. The Mongols attacked Western Xia several times, and around the 1220s they destroyed the kingdom. Khara-Khoto survived the change of rulers. Under the Mongol Yuan dynasty, the city was rebuilt and enlarged. Much of the wall you see today belongs to that later phase. Many scholars believe the town Marco Polo called Etzina was this same place.

Now look at the shape of the walls. They form a large rectangle, thick at the base and narrowing as they rise. They are made of rammed earth, which means soil packed hard in layers inside wooden frames. If you look closely, you can often see the faint horizontal bands of each layer. On some sections, small holes run in rows. These are probably the gaps left by the wooden beams that held the frames.

Along the top of the northwest corner, a row of white stupas stands against the sky. A stupa is a Buddhist shrine, usually a rounded or bell-shaped mound that holds relics or sacred objects. These shapes are the clearest sign of the city's Buddhist past. On a bright day they glow almost white above the brown wall. At sunset they turn gold, then grey.

Here is the first surprise. The most important thing ever found at Khara-Khoto was not gold or weapons. It was paper. In the early twentieth century, the Russian explorer Pyotr Kozlov arrived with an expedition. Outside the walls, his team opened a stupa and found it packed with books, manuscripts, printed texts and Buddhist paintings. It was one of the largest finds of medieval documents in Asia. Most of that collection went to Saint Petersburg, where much of it remains today.

And here is the second surprise. Among those papers was a bilingual glossary, a small book that matched Tangut words with Chinese words. Scholars often call it the Pearl in the Palm. It became a key tool for reading the Tangut script again. In other words, a forgotten language came back partly because of a box of papers left in the desert near where you are standing. Later, the British explorer Aurel Stein also visited and collected material here.

Now, the legend. Locals tell a story about a general called Khara Bator, the Black Hero. According to the tale, an enemy army surrounded the city and cut off the river. With no water left, the general hid his treasure in a well, and then broke out through a hole in the northwest wall. Legend has it the treasure is still down there. It is a good story, but it is only a story. Still, look for that gap in the wall near the stupas.

The real end of the city was probably slower and less dramatic. Traditional accounts say that Ming dynasty forces captured Khara-Khoto in the late fourteenth century. But the deeper problem was the river. Over time, the water channels shifted away, and the farmland around the walls dried out. Without water, the people left. The desert moved in, and sand began to pile up against the walls, as it still does today.

As you walk, notice the ground inside the walls. It is scattered with broken pottery, pieces of glazed ceramic, and small fragments of brick. Please leave them where they lie. They are part of the site. Outside the southwest corner, look for a small domed building. It is often described as a mosque or a tomb from the Yuan period. It shows that Muslim communities also lived and traded here, beside the Buddhist Tangut and the Mongols.

Take a moment before you leave. Turn slowly in a full circle. On one side, the walls and the white stupas. On every other side, gravel, dunes and an open sky. Somewhere to the east, the river still flows, and the desert poplars still grow along it. Listen again to the wind across the sand. It is the same wind that moved over this place when people lived here. As you walk back, look behind you once. The Black City will be quiet, and still standing.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit near sunset for warm light on the walls.
Bring plenty of water; the site has little shade.
Wear closed shoes for walking on loose sand.
Combine your trip with the Ejin desert poplar forests.
Check road conditions before driving out from Ejin Banner.
