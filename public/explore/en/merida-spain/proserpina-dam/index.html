The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves tap against old stone, and somewhere a duck lifts off the water with a clatter. The air smells of wet granite, pine resin and warm dust from the path. Run your hand along the top of the wall beside you. The blocks are rough, a little cool, and fitted together by people who lived nearly two thousand years ago. You are standing on a Roman dam, and it is still holding back a lake.

This is Proserpina, a few kilometres north of the city of Mérida, in the Extremadura region of western Spain. Most Roman dams are gone. Floods broke them, silt filled them, or later builders took their stone. This one survived. It is one of the very few Roman dams in the world that still does its original job, and that alone makes the walk out here worth it.

To understand why it exists, picture the city behind you as it looked in Roman times. Mérida was founded in 25 BCE under the emperor Augustus. It was a settlement for retired soldiers, veterans of the wars in northern Spain, and its Latin name was Augusta Emerita. Emerita means, roughly, the honourably discharged. The city grew quickly. It became the capital of the province of Lusitania, which covered much of what is now Portugal and western Spain.

A Roman capital needed water — a great deal of it. There were public baths, street fountains, private houses with pools, and a theatre and amphitheatre that drew crowds. The river Guadiana runs past the city, but river water was not ideal for drinking. So Roman engineers looked to the hills. They dammed a small valley here, collected the rain and stream water, and sent it towards the city through a channel and an aqueduct.

That aqueduct is the one Mérida now calls Los Milagros, the Miracles. If you visit it later, you will see tall piers of granite and red brick striding across a valley on the edge of town. Locals say the name came from people who thought it was a miracle that the arches stayed standing for so many centuries. Whatever the origin, the water that once flowed along those arches started here, in this lake.

Scholars still debate exactly when the dam was built. Most place it in the first or second century of the Common Era, during the early imperial period. Later repairs and changes make precise dating difficult. What is certain is that it has been altered over time, especially in the modern era, when the wall and the reservoir were restored and reinforced. The core of the structure, though, is Roman work.

Now look carefully at the shape of the wall, because here is the first surprise. On the side facing the water, you can see a row of buttresses — solid stone supports that push out into the lake. That is unusual. Engineers today normally put buttresses on the dry side of a dam, where they brace the wall against the weight of the water. At Proserpina, the dry side is backed instead by a large bank of earth.

Why build it this way? The likely reason is simple. When the reservoir was low, the heavy earth bank behind the wall could push the stone forward, towards the empty lake bed. The buttresses on the water side held the wall in place against that push. In other words, the Romans were worried less about the water and more about the soil. It is a quiet piece of engineering logic, and many visitors walk past without noticing it.

The second surprise sits in the water itself. Along the wall stand two stone towers, rising from the lake like small square keeps. These were intake towers. Through them, water could be drawn off and sent into the channel that led to the city. Their openings allowed the engineers to control how much water left the reservoir. Look at how the stone changes colour at the waterline, darker below and pale above.

And the name? Proserpina is the Roman goddess of spring and of the underworld. She was the daughter of the harvest goddess, and in myth she spent part of each year below the earth. A stone inscription found in this area mentions her alongside Ataecina, a goddess worshipped by the local Iberian peoples before the Romans arrived. The Romans often joined local gods to their own, and that blend of names gave the lake its modern title.

Since 1993, the dam has formed part of the Archaeological Ensemble of Mérida, which UNESCO lists as a World Heritage Site. The same listing includes the Roman theatre, the amphitheatre, bridges, temples and the aqueducts. Most visitors stay in the city centre and never come this far. That is a pity, because without this lake, much of what they admire in town would never have had running water.

Today the reservoir has a second life. In summer, families from Mérida come here to swim, picnic under the trees and rent pedal boats. There is a sandy beach area, cafés open in the warm months, and on hot afternoons the shore fills with the sound of children and radios. In winter it is much quieter. Walkers and cyclists follow the paths, and herons stand very still in the shallows.

It is worth thinking about that contrast for a moment. Roman engineers designed this wall for a practical purpose — clean water for baths and fountains. They could not have imagined that people would still be sitting on its stones, eating sandwiches and watching the sunset, long after their empire was gone. Few structures from the ancient world are used this casually, and that ordinary use is part of what keeps the place alive.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute on the wall. Look along its length to the far end, and follow the line of the buttresses into the water. Notice the towers, the darker stone below the surface, and the green hills that feed the lake. As you walk back towards the car or the path, the sound of the small waves will fade behind you... the same sound the Romans heard, still going.

Practical tips

Pair this visit with the Los Milagros aqueduct.
Come on a summer weekday to avoid beach crowds.
Bring water and a hat for the exposed wall.
Visit at late afternoon for softer light on stone.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven granite walkway.
