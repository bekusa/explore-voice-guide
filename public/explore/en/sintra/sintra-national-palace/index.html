The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water runs somewhere nearby, cool air drifts down from the wooded hills, and above the square two great white cones rise into the sky. They look like something from a dream, or from a giant's kitchen. In fact, they are chimneys. You are standing in front of Sintra National Palace, in the old heart of the town, and those chimneys are the first clue that this building has served royals for a very long time.

The palace sits low in the town, not high on a ridge like its famous neighbours. That placement tells you something. Pena Palace and the Moorish Castle look down from the peaks above, but this building stays among the streets, the cafés and the shops. For centuries, Portuguese kings and queens came here to escape the summer heat of Lisbon. The palace was not built to impress from afar. It was built for living.

The story begins before Portugal existed as a kingdom. In the medieval period, Muslim rulers governed much of the Iberian Peninsula, and a residence stood on this site under their rule. In 1147, Christian forces under Afonso Henriques captured Lisbon, and Sintra passed into the hands of the new Portuguese crown. From then on, this ground belonged to kings. The building you see today grew in layers over many generations, each ruler adding rooms, courtyards and decoration.

Two kings shaped most of it. King João the First rebuilt and expanded the palace around the turn of the fifteenth century. About a hundred years later, King Manuel the First added a new wing and filled the rooms with decoration. Manuel ruled during Portugal's great age of sea voyages, when ships returned with wealth from Africa, India and Brazil. His style mixes Gothic shapes with twisted ropes, sea creatures and other maritime motifs. Portuguese historians call it the Manueline style.

Here is a surprise that many visitors miss. This is often described as the best-preserved medieval royal palace in Portugal, and it was used by the royal family almost without a break for hundreds of years. Kings were born, married and died within these walls. Royal life continued here until the end of the monarchy in the early twentieth century. Few palaces anywhere in Europe stayed in such long, continuous use by one crown.

Inside, one room holds a famous legend. It is called the Magpie Room. Its ceiling is painted with dozens of magpies, each holding a rose and a small ribbon with the words por bem, which means for good. Legend has it that King João was caught kissing a lady of the court by his wife, Queen Philippa. The king supposedly said that he meant it only for good. The court gossiped, and he answered by painting a magpie for every chattering lady.

Whether or not the tale is true, Queen Philippa herself was very real. She was English, the daughter of John of Gaunt, a powerful English duke. Her marriage to João helped seal one of the oldest alliances in Europe, between Portugal and England. Her children became famous, including Prince Henry, often called Henry the Navigator, who supported early Portuguese voyages along the coast of Africa. So this quiet palace connects to the start of a global age.

Not far away is the Swan Room, one of the largest spaces in the palace. Its wooden ceiling is divided into panels, and in each panel a white swan sits with a golden crown around its neck. The room was used for receptions and grand occasions. Look at the swans closely. Each one turns slightly, holding its own pose, and together they create a calm, graceful rhythm above your head.

Then comes the Coats of Arms Room, and this one often stops visitors in silence. King Manuel commissioned a high domed ceiling covered in gilded wood. In the centre are the royal arms. Around them, in rings, are the coats of arms of Portugal's leading noble families, each painted on its own shield with a stag. Below, the walls are covered in blue and white tiles from a later century, showing hunting scenes, country life and the landscape around Sintra.

Tiles appear everywhere in this building. Portugal is famous for its glazed ceramic tiles, called azulejos. The palace holds some of the oldest examples in the country. Many show geometric patterns that came from Islamic and Spanish craft traditions. The colours are deep green, white, honey and blue. Some tiles have raised edges that keep the coloured glazes apart. Run your eyes along a wall and you will see patterns that seem to repeat forever.

A darker story lives here too. In the seventeenth century, King Afonso the Sixth was removed from power by his brother. Afonso spent his final years held in this palace, and he died here in 1683. Locals say that he paced his small room so many times that he wore down the floor tiles. Historians cannot confirm that detail, but the room remains, and its simplicity stands in sharp contrast to the gilded halls nearby.

And those chimneys? They belong to the palace kitchens. The enormous cones pulled smoke and heat up and away from the cooks working below. Inside, you can stand underneath and look straight up into the tapering white space. The kitchens had to feed a full royal court during hunting seasons and long summer stays. The chimneys are pure function, yet they became the shape that everyone remembers about Sintra.

When you step back outside, pause in the square for a moment. Notice how the palace walls are white and plain from here, with only the arched Manueline windows hinting at what lies within. Behind you, the town climbs up the green slope toward the castle walls and the painted towers of Pena. Before you leave, look once more at those two white chimneys rising above the rooftops, the same shape that has stood over this town for centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see the rooms before tour groups fill them.
Take the train from Lisbon instead of driving into Sintra.
Wear comfortable shoes for the steep cobbled town streets.
Visit this palace first, then climb toward Pena and the castle.
Book a separate ticket for each Sintra monument you plan to visit.
