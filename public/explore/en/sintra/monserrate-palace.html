The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the trees the air is cool and damp, and somewhere below you water is running over stone. The path is soft with fallen leaves. Then the green opens, and a pale palace appears on the slope. Its walls are carved like lace, and a reddish dome rises over the roofline. It does not look Portuguese, or English, or Indian. It looks like a dream that borrowed a little from each.

This is Monserrate Palace. It sits a few kilometres west of Sintra's old town, on the shaded, moist side of the Sintra hills. Mist from the Atlantic often settles here, and that moisture matters. It explains almost everything you will see today, from the moss on the stone to the tree ferns in the valley below. Before it was a palace, this hillside was a place of quiet devotion.

In the sixteenth century, a small chapel stood here. It was dedicated to Our Lady of Monserrate, named after the famous mountain monastery near Barcelona in Catalonia. Pilgrims and local people climbed up through the woods to pray. Over time the chapel fell into ruin. The great earthquake of 1755, which destroyed much of Lisbon, also brought damage to buildings across this region. The name of the old chapel survived, and it passed to everything built here later.

In the late eighteenth century, the land passed to Gerard de Visme, an English merchant who made his fortune in Lisbon trade. He built a house in the Gothic revival style — the fashion of that time for pointed arches and medieval details. Soon afterwards, another Englishman arrived. William Beckford was a writer and collector, and one of the richest men in England. He rented the estate for a time and began to shape the gardens. Then he left, and the house slowly decayed.

Here is the first surprise. Monserrate became famous partly as a ruin. In 1809 the young poet Lord Byron rode through Sintra and saw the abandoned house. He wrote about it in his long poem Childe Harold's Pilgrimage, and he described the empty mansion as a lonely, broken place. His readers across Europe came to imagine Monserrate as a romantic ruin. For several decades, travellers came here to look at its decay, not its beauty.

That changed in the 1850s. Francis Cook, a very wealthy English textile merchant, acquired the estate. He hired the English architect James Knowles to build something completely new on the old foundations. The result is the building in front of you. King Ferdinand the Second of Portugal, the same king who created the nearby Pena Palace, gave Cook the title Viscount of Monserrate. So an English merchant became a Portuguese nobleman, named after this hill.

Now look closely at the walls. The style is often called eclectic, because it mixes several traditions. There are Gothic shapes from medieval Europe. There are also patterns inspired by Islamic architecture and by the palaces of India, which many British travellers admired in that period. Some writers compare it to the Royal Pavilion in Brighton, another English fantasy of the East. The carved stone looks almost like fabric. It is fine and repeated, with leaves, flowers and geometric shapes.

Inside, the house is organised along a long gallery. At its centre is an open octagonal space with a marble fountain. Light falls from above onto the white stone. At one end of the gallery is the music room, under the dome. Its shape carries sound well, and Cook and his family held musical evenings here. When you stand in this room, notice how your own footsteps sound. Many visitors speak more quietly here without knowing why.

Here is the second surprise. The palace is only half of Monserrate. Many people would say the gardens are the true work of art. Cook created one of the most important botanical gardens in Portugal. His team included the landscape painter William Stockdale, the botanist William Nevill, and the head gardener James Burt. Together they brought plants from many continents and arranged them by region. You can walk from one climate to another in a few minutes.

Follow the paths downhill and the plants change around you. In the valley there are tall tree ferns from Australia and New Zealand. Their trunks are rough and hairy, and their leaves open high above your head. On a drier, sunnier slope there is a Mexican garden, with agaves and palms. There are bamboos, camellias, rhododendrons and huge conifers from the Americas. The damp Sintra climate allows plants from very different places to grow side by side.

Keep an eye out for the ruined chapel in the gardens. It looks like a medieval church that has fallen apart. But it was designed to look this way. In the nineteenth century, many romantic gardens included false ruins, built to create a feeling of age and sadness. Here, a large tree has grown around the stones, and its roots now hold the walls. It is hard to say where architecture ends and nature begins.

After the Cook family, the estate had a quieter story. The Portuguese state acquired Monserrate in 1949, but for many years the house stood empty again, and parts of it decayed. In 1995 UNESCO recognised the Cultural Landscape of Sintra as a World Heritage Site, and Monserrate is part of it. In recent decades, the public company that manages the Sintra parks restored the palace and the gardens. Much of what you see today is the result of that careful work.

As you walk, notice the sounds of the estate. Water is everywhere. Small streams, a lake, and a waterfall were part of the original design, and they keep the valley cool and green. Birds call from the high trees. On misty mornings, the dome can disappear into the cloud and then return a few minutes later. The light changes quickly here. The same carved wall can look grey at one moment and warm gold the next.

Before you leave, stand at the lawn below the palace and look back up. Notice the dome, the lace of the stone, and the dark trees around it. A chapel, a ruin, a poet's lament, a merchant's dream... all of them sit on this one hillside. As you walk away along the shaded path, listen again for the water. It was running here before any of these buildings, and it is running still.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes; the garden paths are steep and often damp.
Bring a light layer; this side of the hills stays cool.
Visit the gardens first, then the palace as you climb back.
Arrive early in the day for quieter paths and softer light.
Allow at least two hours for both house and gardens.
