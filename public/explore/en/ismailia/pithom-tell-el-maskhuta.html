The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the wind there is a dry crunch underfoot, the sound of broken pottery and crumbled mudbrick mixed into the sand. The light out here is flat and wide. Green fields press in from one side, and the pale edge of the desert waits on the other. This low, uneven mound does not look like much at first. Yet for more than a century, people have come to this spot because of a single word in an ancient text... Pithom.

You are standing at a site that archaeologists call Tell el-Maskhuta. It lies in the Wadi Tumilat, a long, narrow valley that runs west from the area of modern Ismailia toward the Nile Delta. A "tell" is an Arabic word for a mound made by people, not by nature. It grows slowly as one settlement is built on the ruins of the last. So the ground under your feet is not natural earth. It is layer upon layer of human life.

The name Pithom comes from the ancient Egyptian Per-Atum, which means "House of Atum". Atum was a creator god, closely linked with the setting sun. A temple to him would have given the town its identity. Most visitors know the name from another source, though. In the Book of Exodus, Pithom appears as one of two store cities built for Pharaoh by the enslaved Israelites. That one line turned a quiet mound in the eastern Delta into a place of enormous curiosity.

That curiosity brought the Swiss Egyptologist Édouard Naville here in 1883. He was working for the newly founded Egypt Exploration Fund, and this was among its very first excavations. Naville uncovered inscriptions that mentioned Atum and the region's ancient name. He also found a grid of thick mudbrick chambers. He believed these were the storehouses described in the Bible. For a time, the identification seemed settled, and the site became known around the world.

Here is the first surprise. Later scholars looked again at those brick chambers. Many now think they were not granaries at all. They were more likely the foundations of a fortified building, a kind of solid platform with hollow compartments built to support heavy walls above. The idea of storehouses was attractive, because it matched the story so well. But the bricks themselves point to a different purpose. It is a good reminder that archaeologists, like all of us, sometimes see what they hope to find.

And here is the second surprise. Scholars still debate whether this mound is Pithom at all. Some researchers argue that this site matches an Egyptian town called Tjeku, which some link with the biblical Succoth. They suggest that the true Per-Atum may have stood at another mound further west in the same valley, a site called Tell el-Retaba. The question is not closed. So as you walk here, you are walking through one of the most interesting open arguments in the archaeology of Egypt.

From the 1970s onward, a team led by the Canadian archaeologist John Holladay returned to the Wadi Tumilat. Their work changed the picture of this mound. They found evidence of a much earlier settlement, from the period when people from the Levant, known in Egyptian history as the Hyksos, lived in the eastern Delta. Then the site seems to have been empty for a long time. The main town rose again much later, around the late seventh century before Christ.

That later rebirth is closely tied to water. Ancient writers describe a canal that once linked the Nile to the Red Sea, running through this very valley. Work on it is usually associated with the pharaoh Necho the Second, and later rulers, including the Persian king Darius, are said to have improved it. A town here would have guarded and served that route. In a sense, this mound is an early ancestor of the Suez Canal, which now runs only a short distance to the east.

One of the most important objects from this site is a large inscribed stone known as the Pithom Stele. It dates to the reign of Ptolemy the Second, one of the Greek-speaking kings who ruled Egypt after Alexander the Great. The text describes royal works in the region, including activity connected with the canal and with the eastern trade routes. The stele no longer stands here. Like many finds from the mound, it was moved to a museum for protection.

That is worth keeping in mind as you look around. What remains on site is mostly the raw material of the past. You will see eroded mudbrick, scattered potsherds, and the uneven shape of old walls under the sand. Mudbrick is simply Nile mud mixed with straw and dried in the sun. It is cheap, cool, and practical. But it melts back into the earth over centuries, which is why towns like this one look so modest today.

Take a slow look at the landscape too. The Wadi Tumilat has always been a corridor. Traders, soldiers, herders, and travellers moved through it between Egypt and the lands to the east. Today the same strip of land carries farms, irrigation channels, roads, and a railway. The line between green fields and bare desert here is sharp. It shows exactly how far the water reaches, just as it did in antiquity.

If the site holds your interest, the Ismailia Museum in the city is the natural next step. Many objects from the Wadi Tumilat are kept there, and seeing them helps you imagine what once stood on this mound. Visiting the site itself is a quieter experience. There are few signs and little shade. Access may need to be arranged in advance through local antiquities officials, so it is wise to ask before you travel.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Feel the heat rising from the ground, and notice the small fragments of red and brown pottery at your feet. Each one was handled by someone who lived here, whether this was Pithom, Tjeku, or a town with both names at different times. The argument about its identity will go on. But the people who built these walls were real. And as you walk back toward the road, the fields and the desert are still meeting at the same quiet line.

Practical tips

Arrange site access in advance with local antiquities officials.
Bring a hat, water, and sun protection; shade is scarce.
Visit the Ismailia Museum to see finds from this valley.
Come early in the morning to avoid the strongest heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose sand and uneven mudbrick.
