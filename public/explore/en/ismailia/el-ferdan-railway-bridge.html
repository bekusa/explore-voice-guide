The guide

The story

Listen first. On a calm day, the Suez Canal is quieter than you might expect. There is the low hum of a ship's engine somewhere far off, a breeze off the desert, and the flat, steady light that comes off the water. Then, rising out of that wide landscape, you see the steel. Two long arms of grey metal stand on either bank, pointing the same way as the canal, like a pair of open gates.

This is the El Ferdan Railway Bridge, a short distance north of the city of Ismailia. It is a swing bridge. That means it does not lift and it does not fold. Each half turns on a pivot on its own bank. When the two halves swing out over the water and meet in the middle, a railway line crosses one of the busiest shipping routes on Earth. When they swing back, the canal is open again.

It is worth pausing on that idea. Most of the time, the bridge stands open, lying parallel to the canal. Ships have priority here. Tankers, container vessels and bulk carriers pass between Europe and Asia in long, slow convoys. The bridge waits for gaps in that traffic. So on most visits, you see it in its resting position, as two separate structures, and not as one complete crossing.

The scale is the first surprise. When the two arms close, they form one of the longest swing spans ever built anywhere in the world. Engineers often describe it in that category, alongside only a handful of other bridges. Standing on the bank, the length is hard to judge. The desert gives you very little to measure it against. A passing ship is the best ruler you will get, and even a large ship looks modest beside these arms.

Why build a bridge that moves at all? The answer is the canal itself. A fixed bridge would need to be extremely high to let the tallest ships pass underneath. A railway cannot climb that easily. Trains need gentle slopes. So the designers chose a crossing that stays low, close to the level of the land, and simply turns out of the way. It is an old solution, used on rivers for a long time, applied here at a much larger size.

The present structure is not the first crossing at this spot. Earlier railway bridges stood at El Ferdan in the twentieth century. One of them was destroyed during the war of 1967, when the canal became a front line between Egypt and Israel. For years after that, the canal was closed to shipping, and this stretch of bank was a military zone. The steel you see today belongs to a later chapter, after peace returned to the region.

The current bridge was completed around the start of this century. It was an international engineering project, and its purpose was clear: to link the railway network of the Nile Valley with the Sinai Peninsula on the far bank. For Egypt, that link carries real meaning. The Sinai was occupied for years and returned through the peace process. A railway reaching into it was a statement about connection and about one country, joined across the water.

Here is the second surprise. For long stretches of its life, this enormous machine has seen very little rail traffic. In 2015, Egypt opened a major expansion of the canal, adding a new parallel channel along part of its length. That changed the geography around the bridge, and its role had to be reconsidered. So you may be looking at a structure that is more often still than moving. That quiet is part of its character.

Let your eyes move to the pivots. Each arm rests on a heavy base on its own bank. Much of the weight sits there, balanced so that the long part of the arm can reach out over the water. Look at the lattice of the steel. The open triangles keep the structure strong while keeping it lighter than solid plate. In the strong Egyptian sun, those triangles throw sharp, changing shadows across the ground beneath.

Now think about the city behind you. Ismailia was born with the canal in the nineteenth century. It took its name from Khedive Ismail, the ruler of Egypt at that time. It grew as a working town for the canal, with leafy streets, villas, and the shore of Lake Timsah, whose name means crocodile lake. The bridge belongs to that same story. It is one more piece of engineering built to serve this narrow ribbon of water.

A practical note. This is not a museum or a park. The canal zone is sensitive, and security is taken seriously along its banks. Access to the bridge itself may be limited, and photography near military or infrastructure sites can be restricted. Many visitors see the bridge from a respectful distance, from a road nearby, or from the water. Ask locally before you raise a camera, and follow any instructions you are given.

If you are lucky enough to see a ship pass, watch carefully. The vessel seems to slide across the sand, because the canal is narrow and the banks are low. From some angles, you cannot see the water at all. A tall steel hull simply moves through the desert, past the open arms of the bridge. It is a strange, quiet sight, and it explains, better than any number, why this crossing has to step aside.

Before you go, look once more at the two arms. They point the same way, side by side across the water, never touching while the canal is open. Feel the warmth coming off the ground and hear the wind across the flat land. Somewhere to the north, the Mediterranean waits. To the south lie the Red Sea and the long route to Asia. And here, between those seas, two halves of a bridge stand ready to meet.
