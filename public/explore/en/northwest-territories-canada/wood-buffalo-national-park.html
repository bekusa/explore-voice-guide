The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The wind moves through spruce and aspen, and somewhere far off a raven calls. The ground under your boots may be soft and spongy, or crusted white with salt. The air smells of resin, wet moss and cold water. Here, on the border between the Northwest Territories and northern Alberta, the land runs out in every direction. There are no city lights and no motorway hum. There is mostly forest, wetland and sky, and very few people.

This is Wood Buffalo National Park, and its size is hard to picture. It is the largest national park in Canada, covering roughly 44,800 square kilometres. That is larger than Switzerland. Most of the park lies in northern Alberta. A northern slice crosses into the Northwest Territories. The main gateway is the small town of Fort Smith, on the territorial side. Some visitors also arrive through Fort Chipewyan, one of the oldest communities in Alberta, which sits near the park's southern edge.

The park takes its name from one animal — the wood bison. It is the largest land animal in North America, taller and darker than its cousin, the plains bison. By the late nineteenth century, hunting and settlement had pushed wood bison close to extinction. Only a small herd survived in this remote northern country. Canada created the park in 1922, largely to protect those last animals. Today the park holds one of the largest free-roaming herds of wood bison in the world.

The story of the herd is not a simple success. In the 1920s, the government shipped thousands of plains bison north by rail and river barge from a park in central Alberta. Those animals carried diseases, including bovine tuberculosis and brucellosis. They also bred with the wood bison. Scientists and local communities have debated the consequences ever since. So the animals you may see here carry two stories at once. One is about survival. The other is about how well-meant decisions can leave long shadows.

The park also has a human history much older than its boundaries. Cree, Dene and Métis peoples have lived, hunted, trapped and travelled across this land for generations. Rivers were their roads, and the seasons set the rhythm of life. When the park was established, outside rules limited how some families could use land they had always relied on. That history is still felt in the region today. Many local guides come from these communities, and they bring knowledge that no map or sign can give you.

Now for a surprise most visitors never expect. In 1954, a forest worker spotted whooping cranes in a remote part of the park. Until then, nobody knew where these birds nested. The whooping crane is one of the rarest birds in North America. It is tall and white, with black wingtips and a red crown. Wood Buffalo turned out to be the only natural nesting area left for the wild flock. Each spring the cranes fly here from the Texas coast. The nesting grounds are closed to visitors, to protect the birds.

Look at the water around you, because water shapes everything here. In the south of the park lies the Peace-Athabasca Delta. Two large rivers, the Peace and the Athabasca, meet and spread into a maze of channels, lakes and marshes. It is one of the largest freshwater inland deltas in the world. Millions of migrating birds rest and feed here in spring and autumn. In 1983, UNESCO listed the park as a World Heritage Site. The delta, the bison and the cranes were all part of the reason.

Then there are the salt plains, which feel like a different planet. Under the park lie layers of salt left by an ancient sea. Groundwater pushes up through those layers and brings the salt to the surface. As the water dries, it leaves white crusts, small salt mounds and pale, open flats. Bison and other animals come here to lick the minerals. If you walk out onto the plains, look closely at the mud. You may find the tracks of bison, wolves, or birds pressed into it.

The ground itself holds another secret. Much of the park sits on gypsum and limestone, rock that dissolves slowly in water. Over thousands of years, this has created what geologists call karst — a landscape full of sinkholes, caves and underground streams. In some places the forest floor simply drops away into a round, water-filled hollow. Pine Lake, a popular spot for swimming and camping, formed from sinkholes like these. Its clear, green-blue water looks almost tropical on a calm summer day.

Here is a detail that sounds like a tall tale, but it is true. Somewhere in the park's southern wetlands stands what is believed to be the largest beaver dam ever recorded. It stretches for around 850 metres. Researchers first noticed it on satellite images, not from the ground, because it sits in such remote, boggy country. Generations of beavers have likely worked on it over many decades. Very few people have ever reached it on foot. It is a fine reminder of how much of this park remains unseen.

When the sun goes down, the park shows its other face. In 2013, Wood Buffalo was named a dark sky preserve, the largest in the world at the time of its designation. With almost no artificial light for hundreds of kilometres, the stars look thick and close. The Milky Way stretches clearly across the sky. From late summer into winter, the northern lights often appear. They move in green ribbons, sometimes with edges of pink or violet. Fort Smith holds a dark sky festival each year that draws stargazers from far away.

A visit here asks for some planning and some patience. Roads are few, distances are long, and services are limited. Weather can change quickly, and in summer the mosquitoes and black flies are serious company. Wildlife is wild. Bison may look calm, but they are large and fast, so keep a wide distance. The reward is quiet. You may walk a trail for an hour without hearing an engine, and that silence becomes part of the experience.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. Notice the colours — dark spruce, pale aspen, perhaps a flash of white salt or open water. Notice how far you can see, and how little of it was built by people. This land was here long before the park was drawn on a map. With care, it will be here long after. As you walk away, keep listening. The wind, the birds and the water carry on, whether anyone is watching or not.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at Fort Smith, the park's main gateway town.
Bring strong insect repellent for summer visits.
Stay well back from bison, even if they seem calm.
Plan fuel and supplies before driving into the park.
Visit from late summer for northern lights viewing.
