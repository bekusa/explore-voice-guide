The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the colour. A deep, saturated cobalt blue covers the outer walls, so bright it seems to hold the sunlight. Along the quiet streets of Coyoacán, the walls rise plain and solid. Then you step through the gate, and the city noise drops away. In its place come birdsong, rustling leaves, and the soft crunch of footsteps on garden paths. Welcome to the Casa Azul, the Blue House, where Frida Kahlo was born, lived, worked, and died.

This was a family home long before it was a museum. Frida's father, Guillermo Kahlo, was a photographer. He built the house in the early twentieth century, and Frida was born here in 1907. Here is a small surprise. For much of her life, Frida said she was born in 1910. That was the year the Mexican Revolution began. She wanted her own story to begin with the story of modern Mexico.

Coyoacán itself is part of that story. Today it sits inside the huge sprawl of Mexico City. A century ago, it felt more like a separate village, with cobbled lanes, old churches, and leafy plazas. When you leave the museum, it is worth walking a few blocks. The neighbourhood still has a slower rhythm. You will find markets, coffee stalls, and families gathered around the fountains in the central gardens.

Frida's life changed in 1925. She was a teenager, riding a bus, when it collided with a tram. Her injuries were terrible. Her spine, pelvis, and leg were badly damaged, and she spent long months in bed. During that time she began to paint seriously. Her mother arranged a mirror above her bed, so Frida could study her own face. That is one reason self-portraits fill so much of her work. She was often the only model she had.

In 1929, Frida married the muralist Diego Rivera. He was already one of the most famous artists in Mexico — older, larger, and very public. Their marriage was passionate and difficult. They divorced in 1939 and married again the following year. Through all of it, this blue house kept pulling them back. After her father's death, it became their shared home and the centre of their life together.

As you move through the rooms, notice how the house mixes the personal with the political. Frida and Diego collected folk art from across Mexico. You will see painted clay pots, carved masks, wooden toys, and papier-mâché figures called Judas figures. These large, colourful effigies were traditionally burned in festivals. For the couple, this collecting was a statement. They believed the art of ordinary Mexican people deserved the same respect as European painting.

The kitchen is one of the warmest spaces in the house. Look at the bright yellow surfaces and the rows of clay cooking pots. On one wall, small clay jugs are arranged to spell out the names Frida and Diego. It is a playful, domestic detail. It reminds you that this was a working home, full of meals, guests, and conversation — not only a gallery of masterpieces.

And there were remarkable guests. In 1937, the exiled Russian revolutionary Leon Trotsky arrived in Mexico. He and his wife lived in this house for a time, under the couple's protection. Trotsky later moved to another house a short walk away in Coyoacán. He was murdered there in 1940. So these quiet streets were once at the centre of world politics.

Now find Frida's studio upstairs. Large windows let in clear light from the garden. Her easel still stands there, with her wheelchair placed in front of it. Her brushes and paints remain close by. Many visitors stop here in silence. In her final years, Frida's health was very poor. She sometimes painted sitting down, or even lying in bed. Standing in this room, you can picture the effort behind each canvas.

The bedrooms are perhaps the most personal rooms of all. Here you may see the plaster corsets Frida wore to support her damaged spine. She painted on some of them, turning a medical object into a work of art. Frida died in this house in 1954. Her ashes rest here too, inside a pre-Columbian vessel — an object from the cultures that lived in Mexico before the Spanish arrived.

Here is a second surprise. After Frida's death, Diego Rivera asked that some rooms be locked. A bathroom and storage spaces stayed closed for decades. When they were finally opened in the early 2000s, they held thousands of objects. There were letters, photographs, medicines, and many of her dresses. Those clothes, including the embroidered garments of the Tehuana tradition from southern Mexico, have since been shown in special exhibitions.

Diego Rivera gave the house to the Mexican people, and it opened as a museum in 1958. He did not live to see it open; he died the year before. Since then, interest in Frida has grown enormously. Her face appears on posters, bags, and murals around the world. Inside these rooms, though, she becomes a person again. The paintings, the spectacles, the jewellery, and the paintbrushes all belong to one specific life.

Before you leave, spend a few minutes in the garden. Tall plants, cacti, and trees shade the paths. Look for the stepped stone pyramid, where ancient carved figures are displayed. Listen to the birds, and look back at the blue walls framed by green leaves. Then walk out through the gate into Coyoacán, and notice how ordinary the street looks. That ordinary street was Frida's view every day.
