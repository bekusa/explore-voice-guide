The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the trees, traffic hums along the edges of Chapultepec. In here, the sound softens. Gravel shifts under your shoes. Pigeons settle on stone. The air smells of cut grass and resin from the tall trees overhead. For most of the twentieth century, very few Mexicans ever heard this quiet. Now anyone can walk in, and that change is the heart of this place.

You are standing in Los Pinos, which served for decades as the official residence of the presidents of Mexico. It sits inside the Bosque de Chapultepec, the large forest park on the western side of the city. The castle on the hill nearby once housed emperors and presidents too. Los Pinos took over that role in the 1930s. From then on, the nation's leaders lived and worked behind these gates, out of public view.

Before it carried this name, the estate was known as Rancho La Hormiga, which means "the ant ranch." It was a modest property on the edge of the forest. Then, in 1934, President Lázaro Cárdenas chose to live here instead of in Chapultepec Castle. Cárdenas was a reformer, remembered for land reform and for nationalising the oil industry. He reportedly found the castle too grand. A smaller house among the trees suited him better.

The name has a personal story behind it. According to the version most often told, Cárdenas named the estate after a pine orchard in his home state of Michoacán. That orchard was where he had courted Amalia Solórzano, the woman who became his wife. So the name of Mexico's seat of power began as a private memory. It is a tender detail, and many visitors walk past the sign without knowing it.

Over the following decades, the complex grew. New buildings appeared for offices, staff, security and official receptions. The largest of them is the house named after President Miguel Alemán, who governed from the late 1940s into the early 1950s. It became the main residence for later presidents. Its formal rooms, wide staircases and polished floors were designed to receive heads of state. Walking through them now, you feel the scale of official life.

Here is the first surprise. For most of its history, Los Pinos was one of the most closely guarded addresses in the country. Ordinary citizens knew it from news photographs and from the gates. Then, at the end of 2018, President Andrés Manuel López Obrador refused to live here. He chose an apartment in the National Palace instead. On the first day of his term, the gates of Los Pinos opened to the public.

Picture that day. Families who had never been closer than the fence walked straight up the drive. People photographed themselves in rooms where presidents had slept and argued and signed decrees. Some people came out of curiosity. Others came with a kind of quiet satisfaction. The residence had become a cultural centre, run for the public. The point was symbolic, and the symbol was simple. This house belongs to everyone.

As you move through the rooms, notice how much is empty. Many spaces were handed over without the furniture and personal belongings of their former occupants. That emptiness is part of the experience. Bare walls show the outlines where paintings once hung. Light falls across floors that once held carpets and desks. You are left to imagine the meetings, the dinners and the ordinary family moments that happened here.

The second surprise is how domestic some corners feel. Alongside the grand reception halls, there are bedrooms, bathrooms, kitchens and service corridors. Presidents raised children here. Staff cooked, cleaned and kept the household running. In some rooms the tiles, fittings and colours belong clearly to particular decades, a little like a time capsule of changing tastes. Look closely and the mood shifts from state ceremony to family home.

Step outside again, and the gardens take over. Lawns run between the buildings, shaded by mature trees. Paths link the houses, the offices and the open spaces used for events. Along one route stands a series of statues of former presidents, cast in bronze. Walking past them feels like moving through a timeline of the country's leadership. Some faces will be familiar to Mexican visitors. Others may be new to you.

Today the complex hosts exhibitions, concerts, workshops and public gatherings. The programme changes, so what you find inside depends on the day. Some rooms tell the story of the residence itself. Others show art, photography or themes from Mexican history and culture. The shift is clear. A building made for a few people now holds activities for many. Spaces once reserved for diplomats now fill with schoolchildren and weekend crowds.

It helps to remember where you are. Chapultepec is one of the largest urban parks in Latin America, and Los Pinos is only one part of it. Nearby are museums, a lake, the castle on the hill and long shaded avenues. Many visitors combine a walk here with a visit to the castle. Seeing both places together shows how presidential power moved from the hilltop palace down into the trees.

Before you leave, pause on the drive and look back at the main house. Notice the guards' posts, the fences and the wide lawns that once kept the public at a distance. Now people stroll across them, eat snacks on benches and take photographs. Listen again to the gravel, the birds and the voices of strangers. Then walk out through the gate. It is the same gate that once stayed closed, and it is open behind you.
