The guide

The story

Stand on the stone plaza for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along Calle Tacuba, a vendor calls out, and pigeons scatter across the paving. Above you rises a pale grey palace, heavy with columns, balconies and carved stone. This is the Museo Nacional de Arte, known to most locals by its short name, MUNAL. From the outside it looks more like a government ministry than a gallery. That is not an accident. For most of its life, that is exactly what it was.

The building began as the Palace of Communications. It was raised in the early twentieth century, during the final years of Porfirio Díaz's long rule. Díaz wanted Mexico City to look like a modern European capital. Railways, telegraph lines and post offices were the symbols of that modern country. So the ministry that managed them was given a home designed to impress. Its architect was the Italian Silvio Contri, and you can feel his training in every line of the façade.

Look closely at the style. Contri mixed elements from the European Renaissance with the grand, decorated taste of the late nineteenth century. The result is often described as eclectic, which simply means it borrows freely from several traditions at once. Notice the symmetry of the front, the tall arched windows and the crowning details along the roofline. Even the stone has a quiet weight to it. Nothing here was meant to look temporary.

Now turn around. In the centre of the small plaza behind you stands a bronze horse and rider. Locals call it El Caballito, the little horse, although there is nothing little about it. It shows the Spanish king Charles the Fourth, and it was cast in the first years of the nineteenth century by the sculptor and architect Manuel Tolsá. The square itself carries his name. Here is a surprise most visitors miss: this statue has been moved around the city several times before settling here.

Across the street, the long, elegant building facing the museum is the Palace of Mining, also designed by Tolsá. So in one glance you see two eras of ambition. On one side, the late colonial confidence of New Spain. On the other side, the modernising dream of the Díaz years. The plaza between them works like a hinge between two centuries. It is worth standing still for a minute just to take in that conversation of stone.

The ministry eventually moved out. In 1982 the palace opened its doors as the National Museum of Art. The idea was ambitious and clear: one building that would tell the story of Mexican art across several centuries. The collection runs roughly from the colonial period, beginning in the sixteenth century, through to the middle of the twentieth century. In other words, it covers the long stretch before Mexican muralism became famous around the world, and the years when it began.

When you walk inside, let your eyes rise. The great staircase is the heart of the building. Its metal railings and lamps were made with real care, and much of this decorative ironwork was produced in Italy and shipped across the ocean. The steps curve upward under painted ceilings and soft light from above. Many visitors spend so long looking at the paintings that they never notice the building itself is one of the finest objects in the collection.

Here is a second detail that surprises people. Mexico City sits on the bed of an old lake. The soil underneath is soft and wet, and many historic buildings in the centre lean or sink over time. Heavy stone palaces like this one need careful engineering to stay upright. As you walk through the rooms, you may notice how solid the floors feel. That steadiness is the result of thinking deeply about the ground below.

In the colonial galleries, the mood changes. The light is lower, and the walls fill with religious paintings in deep reds, golds and dark browns. These works were made for churches and convents in New Spain, the name for Mexico under Spanish rule. Look for the names Cristóbal de Villalpando and Miguel Cabrera, two of the most admired painters of that period. Their saints and angels carry European techniques, but the faces and details often feel distinctly local.

Move forward in time and the walls open up into landscape. The nineteenth-century painter José María Velasco is one of the great reasons to come here. He painted the Valley of Mexico again and again, with volcanoes on the horizon and wide skies above the lakes. Stand close and you can see tiny figures, trains and roads lost in all that space. These paintings show the valley before the city spread across it. Today, much of that open land is covered by streets.

In the later rooms, you meet artists who were searching for a new Mexican identity in the early twentieth century. The painter known as Doctor Atl, for example, was fascinated by volcanoes and painted them with a sweeping, almost aerial view. Other works from this period show village life, local festivals and everyday people. You can feel the country asking itself a question: what does Mexican art look like when it stops copying Europe? The answer slowly forms on these walls.

A practical word as you plan your time. The building is large, with several floors and many rooms, so it helps to choose a direction before you begin. Some people start at the top and work down through time. Others go straight to Velasco and then wander. Take breaks on the benches by the staircase. The views across the inner spaces are part of the experience, and the building rewards people who slow down.

Before you leave, step back out onto the plaza. Look once more at the grey façade, the bronze horse, and the Palace of Mining across the street. Notice how the late afternoon light softens the stone and how the noise of the city returns around you. You have just walked through several centuries of Mexican painting inside one former ministry. As you head toward the Palace of Fine Arts or deeper into the old centre, carry that long view with you.
