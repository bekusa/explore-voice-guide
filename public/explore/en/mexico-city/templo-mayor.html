The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the traffic of the Zócalo hums and honks. Somewhere nearby, a drum is beating for the dancers who perform in the square. Below you, behind the railings, lies a field of grey and reddish volcanic stone, cut into steps and walls that seem to fold into one another. This is the Templo Mayor, the Great Temple of the Aztec city of Tenochtitlan. You are standing at what its builders considered the centre of the world.

To understand this place, picture water. Five centuries ago, this ground was an island in Lake Texcoco, surrounded by canals, causeways, and floating gardens. According to the founding legend, the Mexica people, often called the Aztecs, were told to settle where they saw an eagle perched on a cactus. Tradition places the founding of their city in the early fourteenth century. That eagle still appears today on the Mexican flag.

The temple rose as a stepped pyramid with two shrines on its summit, side by side. One shrine honoured Huitzilopochtli, the god of the sun and of war. The other honoured Tlaloc, the god of rain and fertility. War and water, conquest and harvest. These were the two forces that the Mexica believed kept their empire alive. Two staircases climbed the western face, one to each god, so the whole pyramid carried a kind of double message.

Now here is the first thing most visitors miss. What you see below is not one temple. It is several temples, nested inside one another like layers of an onion. Archaeologists identified seven main building phases. Each new ruler enlarged the pyramid by building a bigger shell over the old one. So as you walk the raised paths, you are moving through time. The innermost, oldest stages sit deepest, while the later stages are the outer walls you see first.

Why did the inner layers survive at all? Because of what happened in 1521. After the Spanish and their Indigenous allies conquered Tenochtitlan, the temple was torn down. Its stones were taken to build the new colonial city, including churches and houses around this very square. The outer layers were stripped away. But the older pyramids hidden inside were buried and forgotten under streets and buildings for more than four centuries.

The rediscovery came by accident. In 1978, workers from the electric company were digging near this corner when they struck a large carved stone. It turned out to be an enormous round disc showing Coyolxauhqui, the moon goddess. Her body is shown broken apart, with her head, arms, and legs separated. That find led to a major excavation, directed by the archaeologist Eduardo Matos Moctezuma. Whole city blocks of colonial buildings were removed to reach the ruins.

The story behind that disc explains the whole temple. In Mexica myth, the goddess Coatlicue became pregnant on a sacred hill called Coatepec, the Serpent Mountain. Her daughter Coyolxauhqui and her many sons were angry and plotted to kill her. At that moment, Huitzilopochtli was born, fully armed. He defeated his sister and threw her body down the hill, where it broke apart. The Templo Mayor was built as a copy of that mountain. The broken goddess lay at the foot of the stairs.

As you follow the walkway, look at the base of the platforms. You will see carved serpent heads, some with curling fangs and open mouths. Serpents appear again and again here, because the pyramid stood for Serpent Mountain. On the Tlaloc side, look for a reclining figure holding a bowl on its belly, known as a Chac Mool. Experts believe offerings were placed in that bowl. Traces of paint survive on some surfaces too. Faint reds, blues, and ochres hint at how colourful the original temple must have been.

Here is the second surprise. Look closely at how some walls tilt and lean. The ground beneath Mexico City is old lakebed, soft and full of water. Heavy stone sinks into it unevenly. The Aztec builders fought this problem, and so do modern engineers. The cathedral right next to you has struggled with the same sinking for centuries. When you notice a slanting step or a cracked wall, you are seeing the lake that once covered this valley, still shaping the city from below.

The excavation did not stop in the 1970s. Discoveries continue. In 2006, archaeologists found a huge carved stone slab showing Tlaltecuhtli, an earth deity, now displayed in the museum. More recently, work in the streets nearby uncovered part of a great skull rack, a structure where the skulls of sacrificed people were displayed. Human sacrifice was part of religious life here. The Mexica believed the gods needed this offering so that the sun would keep moving and the rains would keep falling.

When you leave the open-air ruins, the site museum continues the story. It is organised around the same two gods. One part of the building focuses on Huitzilopochtli and the world of warfare, tribute, and empire. Another part focuses on Tlaloc and the world of water, farming, and daily life. Inside, you can see the Coyolxauhqui disc up close, along with masks, ceramics, shells, and objects brought here as offerings from distant regions. Those offerings show how far Mexica power and trade reached.

Take a moment to look up from the ruins to the city around them. The cathedral towers rise to one side. Colonial palaces with dark volcanic stone line the streets. Vendors call out, and office workers hurry past without a glance. This mix is the real lesson of the site. The Spanish city was built directly on top of the Aztec one, often from the same stones. Here, one of the oldest layers of Mexico City lies open, right beside the newer layers that replaced it.

Before you go, stand still at the railing once more. Find the base of the twin staircases, where the two gods once met the ground. Notice the dark, porous stone, the small plants pushing between the blocks, the dust on the paths. Five centuries ago, priests climbed those steps while canoes moved along the canals below. Today the drums in the square are still beating. As you walk back into the Zócalo, remember that the pavement under your feet covers the rest of the island city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the outdoor ruins first, then visit the site museum.
Bring sun protection, since the raised walkways have little shade.
Combine your visit with the cathedral and the Zócalo next door.
Arrive early in the day to avoid larger tour groups.
Allow extra time for the museum's two themed wings.
