The guide

The story

Start with your hand. If you can, rest it for a moment on the pale stone of the outer walls. It feels cool, smooth, almost soapy under the fingers. This is marble, carried across an ocean from Italy, and it glows differently with every hour. In the morning it looks like bone. At sunset it turns the colour of honey. Above you, a dome of orange and gold tiles catches the light over Mexico City.

You are standing at the Palacio de Bellas Artes, the Palace of Fine Arts. It sits at the eastern edge of the Alameda Central, the old green park in the heart of the capital. Behind you, traffic moves along the avenues. Pigeons gather on the plaza. Street vendors call out. And in front of you rises a building that took three decades and one revolution to finish.

The story begins in the first years of the twentieth century. Mexico was ruled by Porfirio Díaz, a president who held power for more than thirty years. Díaz wanted Mexico City to look like Paris or Rome. He wanted grand avenues, monuments, and a national theatre to show the world that Mexico was modern. To design it, the government chose an Italian architect named Adamo Boari. Work began in the early 1900s.

Boari imagined a palace in the European style of his day. Look at the outside and you will see it. There are swirling plant forms, sculpted figures, and curving lines, all typical of Art Nouveau, a style that loved natural shapes and flowing ornament. But look a little closer. Mixed into the European decoration are details from ancient Mexico. Watch for the heads of serpents and stylised masks. Boari wanted a building that belonged to both worlds.

Then history intervened. In 1910 the Mexican Revolution began, and the old order of Díaz collapsed. For years, the country was at war with itself. Money vanished. Boari eventually left Mexico. The half-finished palace stood here, a shell of marble and steel, for a very long time. Imagine walking past it in those years — a grand building from a regime that no longer existed, waiting for someone to decide what it should be.

Here is the first surprise. The building is heavy, and the ground beneath it is soft. Mexico City stands on the bed of an ancient lake, drained over centuries. The soil underneath is wet clay. From the start, this enormous marble structure began to sink. Over the decades it has settled several metres into the ground. If you look at how the palace meets the plaza, you may notice that you step down toward it, not up.

The work was finally completed in the 1930s, under the Mexican architect Federico Mariscal. The palace opened in 1934. But by then the world had changed, and so had taste. Mariscal finished the interior in Art Deco, the sleek style of that decade, with sharp geometry, polished surfaces, and bold lines. So this one building has two personalities. Outside, it belongs to the early twentieth century. Inside, it belongs to the age of jazz and skyscrapers.

If you go inside, the main hall opens upward through several floors. The walls are dark and glossy, the light falls from above, and the sound of footsteps echoes softly. The upper levels hold some of the most important murals in Mexico. In the decades after the Revolution, painters covered public walls with huge scenes of history, labour, and struggle. The idea was simple. Art should belong to everyone, not only to the rich.

The second surprise hangs on one of these walls. Diego Rivera, the most famous of the muralists, was invited to paint a mural at Rockefeller Center in New York. He included a portrait of Lenin. His patrons objected, and the mural was destroyed. Rivera did not give up. He painted a new version of the same composition here, in this palace. You can stand in front of the image that New York refused to keep.

Rivera is not alone. Murals by José Clemente Orozco, David Alfaro Siqueiros, and Rufino Tamayo also fill these walls. Each painter had a different voice. Orozco's work often feels violent and dark. Siqueiros painted with dramatic movement and depth. Tamayo used softer, deeper colours, drawn from ancient Mexican art. Walking from one to the next feels like listening to an argument about what Mexico is, and what it should become.

The theatre itself is the heart of the building. It holds one of the most unusual stage curtains in the world. Instead of fabric, it is made of glass, assembled by Tiffany Studios in New York. Its design shows the Valley of Mexico with its two great volcanoes, Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl. Above the seats, a glass ceiling designed by the Hungarian artist Géza Maróti spreads light over the hall. Today the theatre hosts opera, concerts, and the Ballet Folklórico de México, a company that performs regional dances from across the country.

The palace is also a place of public farewell. When important artists die, Mexico sometimes honours them here. In 1954, the painter Frida Kahlo lay in state inside this building, and crowds came to say goodbye. Other writers, singers, and artists have received the same honour since then. So this is not only a theatre or a museum. For many Mexicans, it is the place where the nation thanks its artists.

Now look up again at the dome. It reflects the sky over the city. Across the street, the Torre Latinoamericana, one of the city's first great skyscrapers, rises above the trees. Behind you, the Alameda fills with families, couples, and people resting on benches. Many visitors cross the street to the upper floors of a nearby department store, where a terrace looks straight down onto the dome. From there, the palace looks like a jewel box set into the city.

Before you walk on, take one more look at the marble. It started as a dream of a dictator who wanted Mexico to look like Europe. It stood unfinished through a revolution. It was completed by a new generation with new ideas. And now it slowly settles into the old lakebed, carrying all of that history with it. Notice the people on the steps around you, the light on the stone... and the sound of the city moving past this quiet, heavy palace.

Practical tips

Allow extra time to walk the upper mural floors.
Combine your visit with a stroll through the Alameda Central.
