The guide

The story

Stand on the old brick bridge for a moment and listen. Rooks call from the tall trees, and the grass in the dry moat below moves in the wind. Ahead of you, a pale stone tower rises above the canopy. It is square and solid. It has very few windows. This is the great keep of Hedingham Castle, and it has watched over this corner of Essex for nearly nine hundred years.

The bridge under your feet is a story in itself. It is made of soft red Tudor brick, and it was built in the late fifteenth century to replace an earlier wooden crossing. Before it existed, anyone who wanted to reach the inner bailey crossed a bridge that defenders could remove in a crisis. By the time the bricks were laid, the castle was less a fortress and more a statement of wealth and family pride.

That family was the de Veres. They came to England with William the Conqueror after 1066, and they received wide lands across eastern England. The keep you are looking at was raised in the twelfth century, around the 1140s, by Aubrey de Vere or his son of the same name. The younger Aubrey became the first Earl of Oxford. His descendants held the title, and this castle, for more than five centuries.

Now look closely at the walls. The facing stone is not local. Essex has almost no good building stone, so the masons brought fine limestone from Barnack in Northamptonshire, far to the north and west. Moving that stone by river, cart and muscle was very expensive. The pale ashlar blocks were a message to every traveller on the road. The de Veres had money, connections and the favour of kings.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Look up at the corners of the keep. The builders designed four turrets, one at each corner. Only two survive today. The others were lost over the centuries, and the gaps change the outline of the tower from almost every angle. Walk slowly around the base and you will see how the shape shifts as the missing turrets come in and out of view.

The keep was never meant to be a comfortable home. It was a strong room, a treasury, and a place of ceremony. The entrance sits above ground level, reached by an external stair, so that attackers could not simply walk in or batter the door at street height. The walls are so thick that small passages and chambers are cut into them. Inside, the floors stack one above another like rooms in a stone chimney.

When you climb inside, you reach the banqueting hall, and this is the moment most people stop speaking. A single round arch spans the room from wall to wall. It is often described as one of the largest Norman arches in England. Norman builders loved this rounded style, which came from the Romanesque tradition — the round-arched architecture of Europe before the pointed Gothic arch arrived. The arch carries the weight of the floor above, and it still does its job today.

High above the hall, a narrow gallery runs inside the thickness of the wall. Musicians may have played from up here during feasts, though its full purpose is still discussed. Stand beneath it, look up, and imagine the room lit by fire and candles. Imagine smoke rising into the dark, voices echoing off bare stone, and servants hurrying up the stairs with food from distant kitchens.

The castle also has a royal connection that surprises many visitors. Matilda of Boulogne, the wife of King Stephen, died here in 1152. Her husband fought a long civil war against his cousin, also called Matilda, for the English crown. The English later called those years the Anarchy. The de Veres moved carefully between the rival sides, and Hedingham was one of the places where that dangerous politics played out.

Peace did not last. In 1216, during the war between King John and his rebellious barons, royal forces besieged the castle and took it. The earl of the time had stood with the barons who forced the king to accept Magna Carta, the charter that limited royal power. Hedingham changed hands more than once in those troubled years, but the de Veres eventually recovered their home and their lands.

By the Tudor period, the thirteenth Earl of Oxford was one of the most powerful men in England. He had fought for Henry Tudor at Bosworth, the battle that made Henry king. According to a well-known story, Henry later visited Hedingham and saw the earl's many servants lined up in his livery, the uniform of a private retinue. The king was not pleased. Private armies were exactly what he wanted to control, and the story says the earl paid a heavy fine.

One later de Vere is famous for a very different reason. Edward de Vere, the seventeenth Earl of Oxford, was a courtier and poet in the age of Elizabeth the First. A small group of writers argue that he wrote the plays of Shakespeare. Most historians reject this idea, but it has kept his name alive. He also sold off much of the family's land, and the long de Vere story at Hedingham slowly came to an end.

The castle passed to other families, and in the eighteenth century a red brick house was built in the grounds. That house stands as a quiet contrast to the stone tower: one built for war and ceremony, the other built for tea, books and long summer evenings. The estate is still in private family hands today, which is unusual for a Norman keep of this age and quality.

Before you leave, walk back to the bridge and turn around once more. Notice how the keep rises above the trees, and how the village roofs gather below it. The Norman church of Saint Nicholas in the village is worth a short walk too. Listen again to the rooks. They have probably nested here for as long as people have climbed these stairs. The stone stays. Everything else, including us, simply passes by.
