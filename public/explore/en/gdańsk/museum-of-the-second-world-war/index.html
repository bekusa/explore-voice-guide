The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the open square and look up. A single tower leans out of the ground at a steep angle. Its walls are glass and concrete, washed in a deep rust red that seems to darken as clouds pass. The wind comes off the water nearby, and you can hear gulls over the old shipyards and the hum of traffic on the road. There is very little noise here, given how large this building is.

This is the Museum of the Second World War in Gdańsk. What you see above ground is only a small part of it. The main exhibition lies deep beneath your feet, many metres below the square, in a long series of dark halls. The tower is a marker. The real weight of the museum is underground, and that is a deliberate choice.

The architects have described the design in three layers of time. The underground halls hold the past. The flat square where you stand belongs to the present. The tilted tower, rising and leaning forward, points toward the future. You do not need to accept the idea. But the building feels unsettled, as if it might tip at any moment. Many visitors say that matches what lies below.

Why build this museum here, in Gdańsk? Because the war in Europe began in this city's harbour. In the early hours of 1 September 1939, a German battleship opened fire on the Polish military depot at Westerplatte. That narrow peninsula is only a few kilometres from here, at the mouth of the port. Between the two world wars, this city was not part of Germany or Poland. It was the Free City of Danzig, a small state under the League of Nations.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Just a short walk away stands the Polish Post Office building. On that same first morning of the war, Polish postal workers barricaded themselves inside and fought back for most of the day. They were clerks and postmen, not soldiers. After they surrendered, many of them were sentenced by a German military court and shot. So the first shots of the war landed close to where you stand.

The museum opened to the public in 2017, after almost a decade of planning and construction. From the start, its creators wanted a different kind of war museum. They did not want to tell the story mainly through generals, battles and maps. Instead, they placed ordinary people at the centre. The exhibition asks what war did to families, to towns, to children, and to the everyday rhythm of life.

As you go down into the main exhibition, the light falls and the ceilings press closer. The route moves through the years before the war, the rise of totalitarian states, the invasion of Poland, and the long years of occupation. The halls cover terror against civilians, forced labour, and resistance. They also give careful space to the Holocaust, the murder of Europe's Jews. Expect to spend several hours here. Many people find it hard to see everything in one visit.

One of the most remembered rooms is a recreated street from a small Polish town before the war. You see shop windows, a tram line, signs, and the quiet ordinary order of life. Later in the route, you meet a version of that street again, now marked by occupation and fear. The change hits hard, because you have already walked through the peaceful version. It is a simple idea, and it stays with people long after they leave.

The museum's collection is huge, and much of it is small and personal. You will find letters, identity papers, worn clothing, photographs, toys, and household objects. Many came from families across Poland and beyond, who donated things their parents and grandparents had kept. Look closely at the labels. Often the most powerful object in a case is the least dramatic one. A child's shoe or a handwritten note can say more than any weapon.

There is heavy equipment too. Further along the route, you will find large military vehicles, including tanks, standing in the dim halls. They show the industrial scale of this war. Next to the personal objects, they feel cold and enormous. The contrast is part of the design. The machines show how the war was fought, while the small objects show who paid for it.

The museum has also lived through its own arguments. Not long after it opened, a new government changed the museum's leadership, and some parts of the exhibition were later altered. Debate followed in Poland about how the national story of the war should be told. It is worth knowing this as you walk through. A museum about memory is never only about the past. It is also shaped by the country that builds it today.

If you are visiting with children, there is a separate family exhibition. It follows one home and one family across the years before, during and after the war. It is gentler than the main halls and made for younger visitors. The main exhibition contains images and stories that many parents will find too difficult for small children. Staff can advise you on which spaces suit your family best.

When you come back up to the square, give your eyes a moment to adjust to the daylight. Look again at the red tower leaning over the water. Turn toward the harbour, and remember that Westerplatte lies just beyond, at the edge of the bay. Across the river, the cranes of the shipyard still stand against the sky. Behind you, the old city carries on with ordinary life. That ordinary life is exactly what the halls beneath you are about.

Practical tips

Allow at least three to four hours for the main exhibition.
Book tickets ahead during summer and holiday weekends.

Walk to the nearby Polish Post Office building afterwards.
Combine your visit with a boat trip to Westerplatte.
