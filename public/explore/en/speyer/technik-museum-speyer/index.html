The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the high roof there is a low, steady hum of voices, footsteps on concrete, and somewhere a child calling out to a parent. The air smells faintly of machine oil and old metal. Light falls through tall windows onto propellers, polished chrome, and riveted steel plates. Then you look up, and above you a jumbo jet sits on its steel supports, its nose pointing out over the town as if it were waiting for clearance.

This is the Technik Museum Speyer, a museum of transport and engineering on the edge of one of Germany's oldest cities. It opened in 1991. A private association runs it, the same group behind the Auto and Technik Museum in Sinsheim, about an hour's drive away. Both museums grew from collectors who wanted to keep large machines from going to the scrapyard. That spirit is still here. Many objects are not behind glass. You can walk around them, under them, and sometimes inside them.

Start with the building itself. The main exhibition space is a large historic industrial hall, known locally as the Liller Halle. It has a long connection to aircraft. In the early twentieth century, Speyer had an aircraft factory, and the site carries that memory. The iron framework overhead, the long sightlines, and the sheer volume of the space all come from an age when factories were built like cathedrals of work. Take a moment to notice the roof structure before the exhibits pull your eye away.

Now back to that aircraft high above the ground. It is a Boeing 747, the wide-bodied airliner that made long-haul travel possible for ordinary people from the 1970s onward. Here it is mounted well above the ground, and visitors can climb up and walk through the cabin. The surprise most people miss comes next — you can step out onto the wing itself. Standing there, you feel how vast these machines really are. The wing alone is wider than many houses.

Not far away sits another giant from the other side of the Cold War. The Antonov An-22 is a Soviet heavy transport aircraft with four engines and contra-rotating propellers. That means two sets of blades on each engine, turning in opposite directions. It was designed to carry very large military and civilian cargo across the Soviet Union. Look at its huge tail and the wide, rounded body. Next to the 747, it shows two very different answers to the same question: how do you move heavy things through the sky?

The museum's most unusual object is in its space hall. Here stands a Buran test vehicle, part of the Soviet Union's answer to the American Space Shuttle. The real Buran programme flew only once into orbit, without a crew, in 1988, and then the programme ended. This vehicle was used for testing in the atmosphere. Its journey to Speyer is a story in itself. It arrived in 2008, after a long trip by sea and then along the Rhine, and crowds came out to watch it pass.

The space hall around the Buran tells a wider story of human spaceflight. You will find rockets, capsules, spacesuits, and models from both the Soviet and Western programmes. It is a rare place where the two rival space efforts stand side by side under one roof. The tone is not about winners and losers. Instead, it shows how engineers on both sides solved the same hard problems — heat, weight, air, and the human body far from Earth.

Now leave the sky and go under the water. Outside, the museum displays a submarine called U9, a small boat that once served in the West German navy. You can climb down inside. The space is tight. Pipes, valves, and gauges cover every wall, and the bunks look almost too short for an adult. Many visitors come out quieter than they went in. It is one thing to read about life on a submarine. It is another to stand in that narrow corridor and imagine weeks at sea.

Around the grounds, the collection moves between land, water, and air. There are steam locomotives with heavy iron wheels, vintage fire engines in deep red, classic cars, motorcycles, and boats. Some objects are polished and restored. Others still show their working life — scratches, dents, faded paint. That contrast is worth noticing. The polished pieces show how a machine looked when it was new. The worn ones show how it was actually used, year after year.

There is also a quieter side to the museum. In a separate historic building called the Wilhelmsbau, you will find a large collection of mechanical musical instruments. Think of fairground organs, music boxes, and self-playing pianos. These were the jukeboxes of their day. Before records and radio, a machine with punched paper or pinned cylinders could fill a room with music. If you hear one playing, stop and listen. The sound is bright, slightly mechanical, and full of small clicks.

The museum is also part of a larger day out. There is a large dome cinema on site, and the museum has its own hotel and a campsite nearby. Families often stay for many hours, because the outdoor areas and climbable exhibits suit children well. If you came with kids, the slides and walkways near the aircraft will probably catch their attention first. Plan your route, because this place is larger than it first appears.

Finally, remember where you are. Speyer is an old city on the Rhine, and its great Romanesque cathedral is only a short walk from here. Romanesque is the style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with round arches and thick, heavy walls. The cathedral is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. So in one afternoon, you can move from a medieval imperial church to a Soviet spaceplane. Few towns put nine centuries of human building this close together.

Before you leave, look up one last time at the jumbo jet on its steel supports. Then look down at the ground under your feet — flat, ordinary pavement. Every machine around you began as a sketch, a problem, and a team of people who were not sure it would work. As you walk back towards the old town, listen for the river and the church bells. They belong to the same long story of people building things.

Look closer

What to look for

The iron roof framework of the historic Liller Halle.
Contra-rotating double propellers on the Antonov An-22.
The short, narrow bunks inside the U9 submarine.
Black heat-shield tiles on the underside of the Buran.
Punched paper rolls on the mechanical organs in Wilhelmsbau.
Before you go

Practical tips

Climb up to the Boeing 747 and walk onto the wing.
Wear flat shoes for ladders, stairs and submarine hatches.
Allow at least half a day for indoor and outdoor areas.
Check whether the dome cinema needs a separate ticket.
Combine your visit with a walk to Speyer Cathedral.
