The guide

The story

The air up here is cooler than in the valleys below. Wind moves through the pine forest, and on many days a thin mist sits over the slope. Underfoot, gravel crunches on the terraced paths. Ahead of you, a double row of barbed-wire fence runs down the hillside, broken by wooden watchtowers. It is quiet, and that quiet is the first thing most visitors notice. This place asks you to slow down, to listen, and to remember.

You are standing at Natzweiler-Struthof, in the Vosges mountains of Alsace, close to the village of Natzwiller. Before the war, this ridge drew skiers and walkers. Struthof was the name of a mountain inn. In 1940, Nazi Germany annexed Alsace in practice, though never under international law. The following spring, in 1941, the SS opened a concentration camp here. It became the only one of its kind built on what is today French territory.

Why this remote hillside? The answer lies in the rock. Nearby there was a quarry of red granite, a stone prized by the Nazi regime for its planned monumental buildings. The SS wanted that stone, and it wanted prisoners to cut it. So the camp was placed within walking distance of the quarry. Prisoners marched there each day in every season, through snow and rain, to haul and break stone under constant threat of violence.

Look at the shape of the ground. The camp was built on a steep slope, so the barracks stood on a series of stepped terraces. That design made daily life even harder. Prisoners climbed and descended these steps many times a day, often in wooden clogs, often starving. The roll-call square sat near the top. Here men stood for hours in the mountain cold while guards counted and recounted them.

Who were the prisoners? They came from across occupied Europe. Many were political prisoners and members of resistance movements — French, Polish, Dutch, Norwegian, Soviet, and many others. A large group were classed as Night and Fog prisoners. This was a Nazi policy of making resistance fighters simply vanish, so their families never learned where they were taken or whether they still lived. Jewish, Roma, and homosexual prisoners were also held here, along with people the regime labelled criminal or asocial.

The main camp was only part of the story. Natzweiler became the centre of a large network of satellite camps, spread across both sides of the Rhine. Many thousands of prisoners passed through this system, and many thousands died — from hunger, disease, exhaustion, beatings, and execution. Historians still refine the exact numbers. What is certain is that the toll was enormous for a camp that many people, even in France, have never heard of.

As you move through the site, you will find a few original buildings still standing. One holds the prison block, with its tiny cells. Another holds the crematorium, where the bodies of the dead were burned. There is also a dissection room, a reminder that the camp was linked to medical crimes. Inside, the spaces are small and plain. Take your time here. Many visitors find they speak in whispers without deciding to.

One of the darkest chapters took place a short walk below the camp, in a building that was once part of the old inn. The SS turned a room there into a gas chamber. In August 1943, eighty-six Jewish men and women were brought from Auschwitz and murdered in it. Their bodies were sent to the Reich University in Strasbourg, where an anatomist named August Hirt wanted them for a so-called skeleton collection. It was racist pseudo-science, carried out as murder.

Those victims were nameless to the world for decades. Then, in the early twenty-first century, a researcher worked to identify every one of them, and their names were recovered. Today they are remembered individually, not as a number. This is one of the details many visitors miss: memory here is still being built, name by name, long after the war ended.

In 1944, four women agents of the British Special Operations Executive were brought to the camp and killed here. They had been working with the French resistance before their capture. A memorial plaque honours them. Their story is a reminder that people from many countries crossed paths on this mountain, and that the camp held women as well as men, even though most prisoners were male.

As Allied armies advanced in 1944, the SS evacuated the prisoners, moving them east to other camps. In November of that year, American forces reached Natzweiler and found it empty. It is often described as the first concentration camp discovered by the western Allies in Europe. The empty barracks and the crematorium gave them an early, chilling glimpse of the wider system they would later uncover.

After the war, France made the site a national memorial. Below the camp lies a national cemetery, with rows of graves for deported people who died. Above it rises a tall white monument, curved like a flame, with the thin carved figure of a prisoner on its surface. Close to the entrance stands the European Centre of Deported Resistance Members. Its exhibitions explain resistance and deportation across the whole continent, not only in France.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the fence and look out. On a clear day, the view across the Vosges is wide and green — ridges, forest, open sky. Prisoners saw this same view every day. Notice the wind again, the scent of pine, the sound of your own steps on the gravel. As you walk back toward the entrance, you carry something of this place with you. That is the purpose of a memorial like this one.
