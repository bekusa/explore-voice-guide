The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of these towers and tilt your head back. The brick climbs and climbs, and the tiles catch the light in flashes of blue, white and gold. Swifts cut across the open sky between the two pylons. Underfoot, the paving is warm and pale. And in front of you is a gap: a vast, empty space where an arch once joined the towers together. You are standing at the gate of Ak-Saray, the White Palace of Shahrisabz.

This is a ruin, and that matters. What survives is only the entrance, the two great piers of the main portal. The courtyards, the halls and the private rooms have gone. Yet the fragment alone is enough to stop people in their tracks. The surviving towers rise well over thirty metres. Try to picture the full gateway, complete and roofed with its arch. Then picture an entire palace behind it.

The palace was built by Amir Timur, known in Europe as Tamerlane. He was the fourteenth-century conqueror whose empire stretched across Central Asia, Persia and beyond. Shahrisabz was his home ground. He was born close to this town, which in his time was called Kesh. The modern name, Shahrisabz, means green city. That fits the orchards and fields that still surround it. Timur ruled from Samarkand, but he chose to build his grandest residence here, in the place he came from.

Work began around 1380. Timur had recently conquered Khwarezm, a rich region to the north-west with old traditions of building and tilework. Historical accounts say he brought skilled craftsmen from there to work on this site. Builders and artisans from other conquered lands followed. The work continued for many years, roughly two decades or more. The palace was still being decorated near the end of Timur's life, in the early fifteenth century.

We have a rare eyewitness to that moment. In 1404, an ambassador from the Spanish kingdom of Castile, Ruy González de Clavijo, travelled to Timur's court. He passed through Kesh and saw the palace while craftsmen were still working. In his written account, he described great halls, rich tilework and decoration in blue and gold. His report is one of the few descriptions of Ak-Saray from someone who saw it standing. It gives us a glimpse of what the ruins cannot show.

Now look closely at the surface of the towers. The decoration is not painted. It is built into the wall itself. Glazed bricks in turquoise, deep blue and white are set among plain baked bricks. Together they form geometric patterns and large blocks of angular script. This technique is called banna'i, a Persian word for builder's work. From a distance, the patterns look like woven cloth stretched over the brick.

Here is something many visitors miss. Some of that angular lettering spells out religious phrases and the names of God, repeated across the facade. The script is so large and so stylised that it reads as pure geometry. Look for straight lines that turn at right angles and repeat in a rhythm. In other places, the tilework is finer, with small pieces of mosaic and touches of gold. Up close, you can see where original tiles survive and where restorers have filled the gaps.

There is also a famous phrase linked to this palace. Popular tradition says the gateway carried the words: if you doubt our power, look at our buildings. Guides repeat it often, and it captures how Timur used architecture as a message. Scholars are more careful about whether the exact wording appeared here. But the idea is clear when you stand beneath the towers. This building was meant to make visitors feel small before the ruler inside.

So what happened to the rest? The palace fell into decline in the centuries after Timur's dynasty lost power. Locals tell a dramatic story about its end. According to legend, a sixteenth-century ruler of Bukhara, Abdullah Khan, saw the towers on the horizon and rode towards them. The journey took him far longer than he expected. In anger, the tale says, he ordered the palace destroyed. It is a good story, but treat it as folklore rather than recorded fact.

Whatever the cause, the result is what you see today. Look at the inner faces of the two piers. The broken edges show the sheer thickness of the walls. Here you can read the structure honestly: a core of brick, a skin of glazed decoration, and the scars where the arch pulled away. The arch was once very wide. Many writers describe it as one of the largest spans in Central Asia. Its absence makes the towers feel even taller.

In 2000, the historic centre of Shahrisabz was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list. The listing covers Ak-Saray along with other buildings from Timur's era in the town. In the 2010s, a large redevelopment cleared older houses around the monuments and created the open park you see now. UNESCO expressed concern about these changes, and the site was placed on its list of heritage in danger. That debate is part of the modern story of this place.

Turn around and you will likely see a statue of Timur himself, standing in the open space in front of the palace. It was put up after Uzbekistan's independence in the 1990s, when Timur became a national symbol. Families come here to take photographs, and wedding parties often stop in front of the towers. Beyond the park, along the axis of the old town, you can reach other monuments from the same era, including mosques and mausoleums linked to Timur's family.

Before you walk away, take one more look up. Notice how the colour shifts as the sun moves: the blues deepen, the gold flickers, and the plain brick turns from pale to honey. Listen to the birds nesting in the high gaps of the masonry. These two towers are all that remain of a palace built to show power. Today they show something quieter... the patience of the craftsmen who set each glazed brick, more than six hundred years ago.

What to look for

Small mosaic panels with touches of gold leaf.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon when low sun warms the brick.
Stand between the two piers to grasp the lost arch.
Bring binoculars to study the high tile patterns.
Wear a hat; the open park has little shade.
Combine your visit with Timur-era monuments along the old town axis.
