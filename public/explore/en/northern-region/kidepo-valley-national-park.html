The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the tall grass with a dry, steady hiss. Somewhere far off, a ground hornbill booms, low and hollow, like someone blowing across the mouth of a bottle. Under your boots the earth is red and crumbly, baked hard by the sun. All around you the land opens out into a wide bowl of savanna, with blue mountains on every side. This is Kidepo Valley National Park, in the far northeast corner of Uganda.

The first thing most visitors notice is the sense of distance. Kidepo sits in Karamoja, close to the borders with South Sudan and Kenya. The drive from Kampala is long, and many travellers arrive by small plane instead. That remoteness shapes everything here. You may share a whole valley with a herd of buffalo and no other vehicle at all. In many East African parks, a lion sighting draws a crowd of jeeps. Here, it can feel like a private meeting.

Uganda declared this land a protected area in the colonial period, and it became a national park in 1962, the same year the country gained independence. The decision had a cost. Pastoral families who grazed cattle here, and the Ik people who hunted and gathered in these hills, lost access to land they had used for generations. That history is still part of the story. When you look across these empty plains, it helps to remember that they were not always empty of people.

The park has two main valleys, and they feel very different. The Narus Valley, in the southwest, holds most of the water through the dry season. Because of that, it holds most of the animals too. Pools and swamps here draw herds in from the surrounding plains. Most game drives start in the Narus, and for good reason. If you have limited time, this is the valley where your guide will probably take you first.

The Kidepo Valley itself lies further north, and it is drier and more austere. The Kidepo River runs through it — but for much of the year, it does not run at all. What you see instead is a broad bed of pale sand, lined with tall borassus palms. These are fan palms, with wide leaves shaped like open hands. Elephants sometimes dig into the sand here, searching for water hidden below the surface.

Now, here is a detail many visitors miss. Kidepo shelters animals you will struggle to find anywhere else in Uganda. The dry, open country suits species from the arid north. Ostriches stalk across the plains here, tall and slightly comical. Cheetahs hunt in the grasslands, built for speed in open ground. You may also see the bat-eared fox, a small animal with enormous ears that it uses to hear insects moving underground.

The larger animals are here too. Elephants move through the valleys in family groups. Big herds of buffalo gather near the water in the Narus, sometimes hundreds strong. Zebra, eland, and Jackson's hartebeest graze the plains. The hartebeest is a long-faced antelope with oddly shaped horns, and locals call it kongoni. Giraffes browse among the acacia trees, their heads rising above the canopy. Lions hunt all of these animals, and leopards keep to the rocky outcrops.

Those rocky outcrops are worth watching closely. Granite boulders, called kopjes, rise out of the grass across the park. Kopje is an old Afrikaans word for a small hill. Lions sometimes rest on top of them, using the height to watch the plains below. Your guide may scan every large rock you pass. Stay patient — a tawny shape on grey stone is easy to miss until it lifts its head.

Take a moment to look up at the mountains. To the east, the Morungole range rises over the park. These are the highlands of the Ik, a small community with their own language and customs. In the 1970s, an outside anthropologist wrote a famous and very harsh book about them. Many people now consider that portrait unfair and badly out of date. Today, some lodges arrange guided community visits, which give the Ik a chance to tell their own story.

The Karamojong are the other neighbours of this park. They are a pastoral people, and cattle sit at the centre of their life and culture. On the road into Kidepo, you may pass herders walking with long sticks, wrapped in bright checked blankets. You may see homesteads ringed by thorn fences, built to keep livestock safe at night. Cattle here carry wealth, status, and family history all at once.

The birds deserve a full morning of their own. Kidepo has one of the longest bird lists of any park in Uganda, and many species here are hard to find elsewhere in the country. Look for the Abyssinian ground hornbill, a large black bird with a red throat. It walks through the grass hunting lizards and insects. Raptors circle overhead in the warm air, and in the dry season you may count several kinds of eagle in a single drive.

The rhythm of a day here follows the light. Early morning brings soft gold across the grass, and animals move while the air is still cool. By midday the heat presses down, and most creatures retreat into shade. Then, in the late afternoon, the valley wakes again. The mountains turn pink and purple, and dust from moving herds hangs in the low sun. At night, the sky fills with stars, because there is almost no artificial light for many kilometres around.

So pause here, wherever you are standing. Feel the warm wind on your face, and notice how far your eye can travel before it meets anything made by people. Listen again for the hornbill, or the sudden call of a francolin in the grass. As you move on, keep watching the shade under the trees and the tops of the rocks. In Kidepo, the most remarkable things are often lying very still, waiting for you to notice them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Consider flying in to avoid the very long road trip.
Start game drives early while animals are most active.
Ask your guide to scan granite kopjes for resting lions.
Bring binoculars for birds and distant herds.
Arrange Ik or Karamojong visits through a lodge or ranger.
