The guide

The story

Before you see it, you feel it. A low, steady roar moves through the ground and into your chest. The air turns damp and cool, and fine spray settles on your arms. Somewhere just ahead, the longest river in Africa is being forced through a gap so narrow it hardly seems possible. Welcome to Murchison Falls National Park, in northwestern Uganda, where the Victoria Nile makes its loudest statement.

This is Uganda's largest national park. It covers several thousand square kilometres of rolling savannah, riverine woodland, and swamp. The Nile cuts straight through the middle, running west toward Lake Albert. North of the river lie wide grasslands dotted with borassus palms, tall fan-leaved trees that give the plains their distinctive look. South of the river, the land grows thicker and greener. The river is the spine of the whole park, and almost everything here, animals and people alike, moves in relation to it.

Now, the falls themselves. Here is the first surprise. Murchison Falls is not famous for its height. The drop is roughly forty-three metres, impressive but not extreme. What makes it extraordinary is the squeeze. The entire Victoria Nile, a broad river upstream, is funnelled through a rock cleft only about seven metres wide. Imagine the force of all that water fitting through a gap you could almost throw a stone across. The result is less a waterfall than an explosion.

Stand at the viewpoint known as the Top of the Falls, and you can see this for yourself. The water turns white and furious as it hits the gorge. The noise makes conversation difficult. Rainbows often hang in the spray when the sun is out. On a hot afternoon the mist feels like relief. Watch your footing on the paths, which can be slick, and take a moment simply to listen. The sound never stops, day or night.

The falls carry a British name, and that name has a story. In 1864, the explorer Samuel Baker and his partner Florence travelled through this region searching for the sources of the Nile. Baker named the falls after Sir Roderick Murchison, a geologist who led the Royal Geographical Society in London. Murchison himself never saw them. It is a reminder that many places in Africa were named by outsiders for people who lived thousands of kilometres away.

The name did not always stay. In the 1970s, under Idi Amin, the falls and the park were renamed Kabalega, after a nineteenth-century king of Bunyoro. Bunyoro is the old kingdom that ruled this region, and King Kabalega is remembered for resisting British colonial control. After Amin's government fell, the British name returned. But some Ugandans still use Kabalega today, and you may hear both names on your visit.

Here is the second surprise. Look a short distance from the main falls and you may notice a second, wider cascade. These are Uhuru Falls. They did not exist in Baker's time. They formed after severe floods in the early 1960s pushed water over a new channel. The timing was fitting. Uganda gained independence in 1962, and Uhuru means freedom in Swahili. So the river created a new waterfall at the same moment the country began a new chapter.

One more twentieth-century story belongs to this place. In 1954, the American writer Ernest Hemingway was flying over the falls with his wife Mary on a sightseeing trip. Their small plane crashed near the river. They survived, and were then injured again in a second crash while being rescued. For a time, newspapers around the world reported that Hemingway had died. He lived to read his own obituaries.

Below the falls, the river changes character completely. It slows, widens, and turns calm and brown. This is where many visitors take a boat trip upstream from the Paraa area toward the base of the falls. From the water, you see the park from a different angle. Hippos crowd the shallows, grunting and blowing. Nile crocodiles lie motionless on the sandbanks, mouths open in the heat. Elephants sometimes come down to drink at the banks.

The birdlife along the river is just as rich. Kingfishers hover and dive. Fish eagles call from the treetops, a high, ringing cry that many people associate with African rivers. Further west, where the Nile meets Lake Albert, the papyrus swamps are one of the better places in Uganda to look for the shoebill. This is a tall, grey, prehistoric-looking bird with a huge bill shaped like a wooden clog. Sightings are never guaranteed, which makes each one feel earned.

On the northern bank, game drives take you across the open savannah. This is home to elephants, buffalo, lions, and many kinds of antelope, including the Uganda kob. The park is also an important home for Rothschild's giraffe, sometimes called the Nubian giraffe, a subspecies that has become rare across its range. Their pale legs look as if they are wearing white stockings. Watching them move slowly between the palms in morning light is one of the quiet pleasures of this place.

The park has also lived through hard times. During years of conflict and instability in the late twentieth century, poaching hit wildlife populations badly. Elephant and other animal numbers fell sharply. In recent decades, conservation work has helped many species recover. Today the park also faces new questions, including oil development in the surrounding region. Visitors here are seeing a landscape that people are still actively working to protect, and its future is still being decided.

So before you leave, return your attention to what is in front of you. Notice the colour of the water, the smell of wet rock, the steady thunder under your feet. Notice how small the gap really is. The river has been pushing through this stone for a very long time, and it will keep doing so after you walk away. As you head back along the path, listen to the roar slowly fade behind you... until only the birds remain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the boat trip upstream to see the falls from below.
Hike to the Top of the Falls viewpoint for the full roar.
Wear shoes with good grip; viewpoint paths get slippery.
Book an early morning game drive north of the river.
Bring binoculars for shoebills and fish eagles near Lake Albert.
