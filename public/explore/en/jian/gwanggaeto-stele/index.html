The guide

The story

Stand close to the glass and look at the surface of the stone. It is not smooth. It is rough and uneven, the colour of dark ash, with shallow pits and small ridges where the weather worked on it for centuries. The carved characters follow the natural face of the rock. Some are sharp. Others have faded into the grain. Outside, the air of the Yalu River valley is still, and the low green hills of Ji'an rise all around. This is one of the oldest long texts written in East Asia, and it stands here in the open countryside.

You are looking at the Gwanggaeto Stele. It is a memorial to one king, set up by his son. The kingdom was Goguryeo, one of the three ancient kingdoms of the Korean peninsula. At its height it reached far into what is now northeast China. For several centuries, the land under your feet sat at the centre of that kingdom. The town of Ji'an grew on the site of its old capital, a walled city known as Gungnae. Goguryeo moved its capital south to Pyongyang in the year 427.

The king honoured here is known in Korea as Gwanggaeto the Great. His name roughly means "broad expander of territory". He ruled from the late fourth century into the early fifth century. He was still young when he died. His son, King Jangsu, raised this stone in 414, two years after his father's death. Jangsu would rule for a very long time, and he was the king who moved the capital to Pyongyang. So this monument stands at a turning point — the last great years of Ji'an as a royal city.

Now take in the shape. The stele is a single block of stone, more than six metres tall. It is roughly square in section, with four inscribed faces. It does not have the carved turtle base or the dragon crown you might see on later Chinese steles. It looks closer to a natural pillar that has been lightly shaped. That plain form is part of its power. It feels less like a sign and more like a piece of the mountain, pulled upright and given a voice.

The inscription runs around all four sides. It contains roughly 1,800 characters, written in classical Chinese, which was the written language of state across the region at that time. The characters are large and square, cut in a style close to the clerical script of the period. Clerical script is a formal, slightly flattened hand, used for official records. Over the centuries, some characters have been lost entirely. Scholars still disagree about how to read certain gaps.

The text opens with a founding story. It tells of Chumo, also known as Jumong, the legendary first king of Goguryeo. According to the legend, he was the son of a heavenly ruler and the daughter of a river god, and he hatched from an egg. When he came to a river with no bridge, turtles and fish rose to the surface so he could cross. This is myth, not history. But it shows how the royal family wanted their line to be seen: as descended from heaven itself.

The middle section changes tone. It becomes a record of campaigns. Year by year, it lists the king's military expeditions — against the neighbouring kingdom of Baekje, against northern peoples, and against forces from the Wa, the name used then for people from the Japanese islands. It also describes Goguryeo sending troops to help the kingdom of Silla. The text counts captured fortresses and villages. It reads like a royal report card, carved in stone for everyone to see.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The final section is not about battles at all. It is about tomb care. It sets out which households must guard and maintain the royal tombs, and it forbids buying and selling these guardian families. The king wanted his grave looked after forever, and he wrote the rules into stone. Not far from here is a large stepped tomb that many scholars link to Gwanggaeto himself. The stele and the tomb were meant to work together.

The second surprise concerns the stone's modern life. For centuries, it stood almost forgotten, covered in moss and vines. In the late nineteenth century, local farmers cleared the region and found it again. Rubbings soon travelled to scholars, and one copy was taken to Japan by an army officer in the 1880s. Very quickly, one short passage about the Wa became the subject of argument. Japanese, Korean and Chinese historians have read it in very different ways ever since.

The debate is still not fully settled. In the twentieth century, some scholars argued that lime had been applied to the surface to sharpen the characters for rubbings, and that this could have changed their shapes. Others disagreed strongly. Later study of older rubbings helped to clarify many points, but not all. So when you look at these worn marks, you are looking at a text that modern nations have read through their own histories. A few broken strokes still carry real political weight.

Today the stele stands inside a glass-walled pavilion. The glass protects the stone from rain, frost and touch, all of which damaged it in the past. It also means you see the monument through a soft reflection of the sky and the trees outside. Walk slowly around all four sides. Notice how the light changes the depth of the carving. On a bright afternoon, side light can make the characters stand out more clearly.

The stele is part of a larger World Heritage listing for the capital cities and tombs of the ancient Goguryeo kingdom. Around Ji'an, you can find stepped pyramid tombs, old city wall foundations, and thousands of smaller burial mounds spread across the valley. A large granite tomb known as the Tomb of the General is a short drive away. Seen together, these sites show a royal city of stone, built by people who expected their memory to last.

Before you leave, look once more at the grey surface. Think of the hands that cut these characters more than 1,600 years ago, working with simple tools on a stone this hard. Then look past the glass to the hills and the river valley. This was the view of a capital. Kings rode out from here, and families came here to tend the graves. As you walk back along the path, listen for the wind in the trees... it is the same valley, under the same sky.
