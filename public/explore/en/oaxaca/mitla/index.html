The guide

The story

Run your eyes along the wall in front of you. Up close, it is not one surface at all. It is thousands of small, cut stones, each one shaped by hand and fitted against its neighbours. They form zigzags, steps, and spirals that repeat across the whole façade. In the dry air of the Oaxaca valley, the stone is pale and warm. When the sun moves, the patterns throw sharp little shadows. You are standing at Mitla, one of the great ceremonial centres of the Zapotec people.

First, a word about the name. Mitla comes from the Nahuatl word Mictlan. Nahuatl was the language of the Aztecs, and Mictlan was their name for the underworld, the place of the dead. The Zapotecs, who built this place, had their own name for it: Lyobaa. It is usually translated as place of rest, or place of burial. Both names point to the same idea. This was a sacred site connected to death, to ancestors, and to the world below.

People lived in this part of the valley for a very long time. But most of what you see today belongs to the later centuries before the Spanish arrived. Archaeologists call this the Postclassic period, roughly from the tenth century to the early sixteenth century. By then, the older mountaintop city of Monte Albán, near Oaxaca city, had lost its power. Mitla rose as a major religious centre in its place. Spanish friars later wrote that a powerful high priest lived here.

Now look again at the stonework, because this is what makes Mitla different from almost every other site in Mexico. These geometric panels are often called grecas, or stone fretwork. The builders did not carve the patterns into one big block. Instead, they cut many small pieces of stone and set them, one by one, into a backing of mortar. It is a kind of stone mosaic. Most visitors see the pattern first. Few stop to think about the patience behind every single piece.

The patterns are not random. They repeat in bands and panels, and they change from room to room. You will see step shapes, zigzag lines, and hooked spirals. Many scholars link the stepped spiral to ideas of movement, water, or the sky, but nobody can be fully certain what each design meant to the Zapotecs. What we do know is the effect. A plain wall becomes a field of light and shadow, and the building seems to move as you walk past it.

The site is arranged in several groups of buildings around open courtyards. The best known is the Group of the Columns. Here, you walk up into a long hall with a row of thick stone columns down the middle. Each column is a single piece of stone. Long ago, they probably held up a heavy roof. Today the sky is the ceiling. From this hall, a narrow, low passage leads into an inner courtyard. Notice how you must lower your head to pass through.

That inner space is one of the quietest corners of Mitla. The walls are covered almost completely with fretwork, from low down to high up. The rooms around it are small and closed. It feels private, and that is probably the point. This part of the complex may have been reserved for priests or rulers. Stand still here for a moment. Listen to how the sound changes when the walls close in around you.

Now for the part of Mitla that many visitors miss. Beneath some of these buildings are tombs, built in the shape of a cross. You go down steep steps into dark, cool chambers. Their walls carry the same fine fretwork you saw above ground. So the decoration was not only for the living. It continued down into the world of the dead, which fits the meaning of the name. If the tombs are open during your visit, the change in temperature alone is worth the short climb down.

One of these tombs holds a stone pillar that locals call the Column of Life. There is a popular tradition attached to it. Locals say that if you wrap your arms around the column, the space left between your fingertips tells you how many years you still have to live. It is folklore, not history, and access to the column is often restricted to protect it. But the story shows how this site is still part of local life and imagination.

Turn toward the church with its red domes. This is the Church of San Pablo, built by the Spanish after the conquest, in the sixteenth century. It was not placed here by accident. The church stands on top of a pre-Hispanic platform, and its builders used stones taken from the older Zapotec buildings. Look closely at the church walls and you can find cut blocks that clearly came from somewhere else. One faith was literally built over another.

In the group of buildings close to the church, look for traces of colour. On some lintels, the stone beams above doorways, you can still see faint red paint and painted figures. These paintings are in a style shared across many peoples of southern Mexico at that time. Once, many surfaces here were probably bright red. The pale stone we admire today is only part of what the builders intended. Try to picture these walls in strong red, with the white fretwork standing out against it.

Mitla is not frozen in the past. The town around it, San Pablo Villa de Mitla, is a living Zapotec community. As you walk from the entrance, you pass stalls selling woven textiles, embroidered blouses, and shawls. Many families in this valley have worked with cloth for generations. The wider valley is also known for mezcal, a spirit made from roasted agave plants. You may notice the smoky smell of it in the shops nearby. The patterns on some textiles echo the fretwork on the walls.

Before you leave, go back to one wall and stand very close to it. Pick a single small stone. Look at its edges, its colour, the tiny gap where it meets the next one. Then step back slowly until the whole pattern returns. Someone placed that stone more than five hundred years ago. It is still where they put it. As you walk out past the church and the market, notice how often those same stepped lines appear around you, on cloth, on doorways, on signs... still part of the valley today.
