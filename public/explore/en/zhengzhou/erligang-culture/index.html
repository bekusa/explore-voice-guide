The guide

The story

Run your hand along the old earthen wall, and the surface feels almost like compressed sand. Look closely at a cut face, and you will see thin horizontal bands, one stacked on another, each only a few fingers deep. Traffic hums nearby. Scooters pass. Yet these layers of pressed soil were laid down by human hands more than three thousand years ago, in the heart of what is now modern Zhengzhou.

You are standing among the traces of the Erligang culture. Archaeologists use this name for a Bronze Age society that flourished in the middle of the second millennium BCE. The name comes from Erligang, a low rise of land in Zhengzhou where key finds were first made. So the word does not describe a kingdom or a people's own name for themselves. It describes a place where the ground gave up its evidence.

That evidence began to surface in the early 1950s. Pottery fragments turned up in the soil around the city, and archaeologists soon followed. What they found was far larger than anyone expected. Beneath the streets and fields lay the outline of an ancient walled city. Excavation has continued in phases ever since, and much of the old settlement still lies under homes, shops and roads.

The walls are the clearest thing you can see today. They were built with a method called rammed earth. Workers set up wooden frames, poured in loose soil, and pounded it hard with tools, layer after layer. The result was a wall as dense as soft stone. The full circuit of the city walls runs to roughly seven kilometres. That is a long walk, even today, and it tells you how many people this city once needed to protect.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. If you look at an exposed section, you may notice small round dents in some layers. These are often marks left by the pounding tools. Each one records a single blow from a worker. Think about the scale of that effort for a moment... thousands of people, many seasons, and millions of those blows, all to raise a wall from the earth around them.

Who built this city? Most historians link the Erligang culture with the early Shang dynasty. The Shang are the first Chinese dynasty known from written records of their own time, though those records come from a later capital. Some scholars believe Zhengzhou was an early Shang capital, mentioned in later texts by names such as Ao or Bo. Others are more cautious. The debate is still open, and honest guides should say so.

What is not in doubt is the skill of the people here. Outside the walls, archaeologists found workshops. Some made pottery. Some carved bone into tools and pins. Others cast bronze. The workshops sat in separate zones, which suggests an organised city with specialised crafts. This was not a village that grew by accident. It was planned, managed and supplied by people who understood how to run a large community.

Bronze is the heart of the Erligang story. Craftsmen here used a technique called piece-mould casting. They shaped clay moulds in several sections, fitted them around a core, and poured molten bronze into the gap. This method let them produce vessels with sharp, even decoration. Many vessels carry a pattern known as taotie, a stylised face of eyes and curling lines. Its exact meaning is still uncertain, but it appears again and again.

Now for the second surprise. In the 1970s, large square bronze cauldrons were found buried near the old city. These vessels are called ding. They were used in rituals, probably for offerings of food to ancestors and spirits. The largest ones are heavy enough that several adults would struggle to lift them. Some of these finds are now displayed in museums in Zhengzhou, and they are worth seeking out if you have the time.

The reach of the Erligang culture is just as striking. Pottery and bronzes in the same style have been found far from Zhengzhou. One well-known site is Panlongcheng, in today's Hubei province, hundreds of kilometres to the south. Archaeologists debate what this spread means. It may show military control, trade networks, or the movement of skilled craftsmen. Whatever the reason, Erligang styles travelled across a very wide part of ancient China.

It helps to place Erligang in a sequence. Before it came the Erlitou culture, centred to the west of here, near the city of Luoyang. After it came the later Shang capital at Anyang, famous for oracle bones carved with early Chinese writing. Erligang sits between them. It is a bridge between an earlier world and a later, better-documented one, and Zhengzhou holds one of its most important sites.

So much of this past is hidden that it takes some imagination. Picture the city as it might have been. Smoke rising from the bronze furnaces outside the walls. The dull, steady thud of rammers on new layers of earth. The smell of damp clay drying in the sun. People carrying grain, firewood and copper ore through the gates. None of these sounds remain, but the walls they surrounded are still here.

Before you move on, look once more at the earthen wall beside you. Notice how modern Zhengzhou presses right up against it, with apartment blocks, trees and the steady movement of the street. The two cities share the same ground. As you walk away, watch the soil in parks and building sites along your route. Somewhere beneath your feet, more of this old city is still waiting to be found.
