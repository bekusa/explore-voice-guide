The guide

The story

Listen first. The air here is dry and very still. A crow calls somewhere above the rock, and the sound comes back off the stone a moment later. Under your boots the ground is red grit, fine as crushed brick. When the sun lands on the canyon wall, the sandstone turns orange, then deep red. You are in Watarrka National Park, in the heart of Australia's Northern Territory. Kings Canyon, its best-known feature, is right in front of you.

Let's begin with the name. Watarrka is a word from the Luritja language. The Luritja are the Aboriginal people whose country this is, and they have lived in this landscape for many thousands of years. The name is usually linked to the umbrella bush, a tough acacia shrub that grows across this dry country. So the park carries a plant's name, not a person's. That small detail says a lot about how the traditional owners see the land: as something alive and growing.

The English name came much later. In the 1870s, the explorer Ernest Giles travelled through this region, and he named the canyon after one of his supporters. For the Luritja, of course, this place already had names, stories, and meaning long before any European arrived. Today the traditional owners and park rangers manage the land together. Some sites here have deep cultural importance, and visitors are asked to stay on marked tracks and treat the place with care.

Now look up at the canyon walls. They are sheer, and in places they rise more than one hundred metres above the creek bed. The rock is sandstone, laid down in layers over an enormous span of time. You can often see the bands clearly — paler stone, then darker stone, stacked like pages. Over ages, water and wind found cracks in this rock. Slowly they cut down through it, and the canyon you see today is the result.

Here is something most visitors walk straight past. Up on the rim, the flat rock surface carries ripple marks. They look like the patterns that small waves leave in wet sand at the beach. That is exactly what they are. Long ago, this was the edge of shallow water. The sand hardened into stone, and the ripples stayed. So in the middle of the desert, hundreds of kilometres from any sea, you can stand on an old shoreline.

The most popular way to see all this is the Rim Walk. It is a loop of roughly six kilometres, and most people take three to four hours. The start is the hard part. A steep set of rough stone steps climbs straight up from the car park. Locals have a nickname for it: Heart Attack Hill. Take it slowly. Once you reach the top, the path levels out, and the hardest work is behind you.

On the rim, the landscape changes completely. All around you stand rounded domes of weathered sandstone. They look like giant beehives, or the ruins of an old town. This area is often called the Lost City. The domes formed as water ran through cracks in the rock and wore the edges away, block by block. Walk between them and the sounds change. Your footsteps go quiet, and the wind moves through the narrow gaps between the stone.

Then the path drops down into a surprise. Deep in a side gorge sits a permanent waterhole, shaded and cool. It is known as the Garden of Eden. After hours of bare red rock, the green here is almost a shock. Ferns grow from the damp walls. Tall palm-like plants called cycads stand along the water. Cycads are an ancient plant group, older than the dinosaurs. Pockets of moisture like this one let them survive in a desert that would otherwise kill them.

This is the second surprise of Watarrka. It is not only dry country. The canyon walls trap shade and water, and that creates small shelters for life. Rock wallabies can sometimes be seen on ledges in the early morning. Birds gather near the water. Lizards rest on warm rock as the day heats up. Move quietly and keep your eyes on the cliffs, and you will notice far more than you expect.

If the Rim Walk is too much, there are gentler options. The Kings Creek Walk follows the canyon floor. It is short and mostly flat, and it leads to a viewing platform below the towering south wall. From down here, you get a sense of scale that the rim cannot give you. Elsewhere in the park, Kathleen Springs offers another easy walk to a quiet waterhole. Serious hikers can take on the Giles Track, a longer route across the range.

A few words on heat, because it matters here. Central Australia can be extremely hot, especially in the summer months. On very hot days, rangers may close the Rim Walk early in the morning for safety. Start as early as you can, carry plenty of water, and wear a hat. There is little shade on the rim. Every year, people underestimate this place. Respect the sun, and the walk becomes a pleasure rather than a struggle.

The light is part of the experience too. In the early morning, the walls glow a soft orange, and shadows fill the canyon below. Around midday, the colours flatten and the rock looks pale and hard. Late in the afternoon, the red returns, stronger than before. If you have time, come back at different hours. The canyon does not change its shape, but it changes its colour, and each version feels like a different place.

Before you leave, stop for a moment. Look at the line where red rock meets blue sky. Feel the warmth coming off the stone. Listen for the crow, or the wind, or simply the silence. This land was shaped by water over vast stretches of time, and it has been cared for by its people for generations. As you walk away, look down at the red dust on your boots. You are carrying a little of Watarrka with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the Rim Walk at dawn to avoid the heat.
Carry plenty of water; the rim has little shade.
Take the steep opening climb slowly and rest often.
Choose the flat Kings Creek Walk for an easier option.
Return in late afternoon to see the walls turn red.
