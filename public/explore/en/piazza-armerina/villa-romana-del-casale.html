The guide

The story

Listen first. Your footsteps ring softly on the raised walkway, and below you there is only stillness. The light comes down through the modern roofs and settles on a floor of tiny stone cubes. Some are red, some ochre, some the deep green of olive leaves. Look closely and the floor is full of faces, animals, ships and running feet. It is as if a whole Roman world has been pressed flat into the ground and left here for you to read.

You are standing in the Villa Romana del Casale, in the green hills just outside Piazza Armerina, in the heart of Sicily. This was a grand country residence of the late Roman Empire, built mostly in the early fourth century. That was a time when Rome was changing fast. Emperors were moving away from the old capital, and wealthy landowners were building private palaces on their estates. This villa is one of the finest examples of that world that has survived anywhere.

What makes it so rare is the floor beneath your feet. The villa holds one of the largest collections of Roman mosaics still in their original place. Experts usually estimate the decorated surface at around 3,500 square metres. That is roughly half a football pitch of pictures made from stone and glass. Many scholars believe the work was done by skilled mosaic workshops from North Africa, which was a centre of this art in late Roman times.

So who lived here? The honest answer is that nobody knows for certain. No inscription names the owner. For many years, one popular theory said the villa belonged to Maximian, a co-emperor who ruled alongside Diocletian. Today many historians lean towards a different idea — a very rich senator or high official, perhaps someone responsible for supplying games in Rome. Keep that idea in mind. The floors will give you clues.

As you move through the rooms, notice the layout. The villa was not built as one neat block. It spreads across the slope in several groups of rooms. There is a monumental entrance, a bath complex, a large garden courtyard with columns and a fountain, private apartments, and a grand hall for receiving guests. The walkways follow these spaces from above, so you see each floor the way the owner might never have seen it — all at once, from the air.

The great surprise for most visitors is the long corridor of the Great Hunt. It runs for more than sixty metres along one side of the courtyard. Along its length, hunters capture wild animals in distant lands and load them onto ships. You will see an elephant walking up a gangway, antelopes being carried, a tiger, and leopards. This is not a sport scene. It shows the business of bringing exotic beasts to the amphitheatres of Rome.

Now look for the strangest moment in the corridor. Near one end, a man sits inside a wooden cage. Above him hovers a griffin, a mythical creature with the body of a lion and the head of an eagle. The man is bait. It is a dreamlike detail in a scene that is otherwise practical and busy. At each end of the corridor, large female figures stand for distant lands, surrounded by animals from those regions.

This is where the clue about the owner comes in. A man who paid to see this enormous scene every day had a reason to be proud of it. That is why many scholars think he was connected to the trade in animals for the games. In the late Roman world, organising spectacular shows was one way for the rich to win fame and political favour. The floor may be a kind of advertisement for his own power.

Then there is the room that many people remember best. Ten young women in two-piece outfits are running, throwing a discus, lifting weights and playing with a ball. Visitors often call it the room of the bikini girls. One woman holds a crown and a palm branch, the prizes of victory. Interestingly, this mosaic was laid on top of an older floor. In a few places you can glimpse the earlier geometric pattern underneath.

Keep an eye out for the children, too. In one set of rooms, children hunt hares and ducks, and sometimes the animals fight back. In another, children race small chariots pulled by birds. These playful scenes copy the adult world in miniature. Elsewhere, a mosaic shows a chariot race in a great circus, likely inspired by the Circus Maximus in Rome, with crowds, turning posts and racing teams.

Mythology fills the most formal spaces. In the great three-lobed dining hall, giant figures struggle in scenes from the labours of Hercules, the Greek hero famous for his impossible tasks. In another room, the giant Polyphemus receives a cup of wine from Ulysses. Look at his face. The artist has given this one-eyed Cyclops three eyes. Nobody is quite sure why, and it remains one of the quiet puzzles of the villa.

The villa survived because it was lost. After the Roman period, people continued to live on the site for centuries. Then, sometime in the Middle Ages, the area was abandoned. Over time, a landslide and layers of mud from the hillside covered much of the complex. That earth protected the floors from weather and from builders looking for stone. Serious excavation began in the twentieth century, with major campaigns in the 1950s.

Take a moment to think about the people who made all this. Each tiny cube was cut and set by hand, one beside another, often in curved lines that follow a muscle or a wave. Craftsmen knelt where you now look down. Servants carried water to the baths and food to the dining hall. Guests walked barefoot or in sandals across these same pictures. The images were meant to be lived on, not hung on a wall.

Before you leave, pause at the edge of the walkway once more. Let your eyes drift away from the famous scenes to the borders, the leaves, the small birds and baskets of fruit. Then step outside and look at the wooded hills around you. This quiet valley once held a palace full of noise and colour. As you walk back towards the gate, listen to the wind in the trees, and imagine the floors still waiting under the earth.
