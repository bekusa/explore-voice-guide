The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, the scooters of Bari hum along the edge of the old town. In front of you, there is silence: a wide, dry moat, and above it, walls of pale limestone that turn honey-coloured as the sun moves across them. The stone is rough under your hand. It feels heavy, patient, and very old. This is the Castello Normanno-Svevo, the Norman-Swabian Castle. Its name tells you, in two words, about the two dynasties that shaped it.

The first part of the name, Norman, takes us back to the twelfth century. Norman knights had come south from northern France, and they had made themselves masters of southern Italy. In the 1130s, the Norman king Roger the Second, the first king of Sicily, built a fortress here. It had a clear purpose. It was a castle meant to keep watch over the city as much as to defend it. Bari was a proud and restless port, and it did not always welcome its new rulers.

That restlessness had a heavy cost. In 1156, after a revolt, the Norman king William the First, remembered in history as William the Bad, took his revenge on the city. According to the chroniclers, Bari was largely torn down. The castle fell with it. Only one great church, the Basilica of Saint Nicholas, was spared. So the walls in front of you are not the first walls on this ground. They are a second life, built on the memory of a destroyed one.

The second part of the name, Swabian, refers to the Hohenstaufen family, a German dynasty from the region of Swabia. Its most famous member was Frederick the Second, Holy Roman Emperor and king of Sicily. In the 1230s, Frederick rebuilt the castle. He was a restless, curious ruler. He wrote a book on falconry, he kept scholars at his court, and he filled the Apulian countryside with castles. Here in Bari, he raised a strong square fortress around an inner courtyard.

Now here is something many visitors miss. The castle you see is really two castles, one inside the other. The tall, compact core, with its square towers, is the medieval fortress of Frederick's time. Around it, much lower and wider, run the sloping bastions with their sharp angled corners. Those outer walls came centuries later. They were built for a different kind of war — a war of cannons, when tall, thin walls became targets rather than protection.

Those later changes belong to the sixteenth century and to two remarkable women. Isabella of Aragon, Duchess of Bari, and then her daughter Bona Sforza, held the city as their own duchy. Bona Sforza married the king of Poland and became Queen of Poland. Yet she kept strong ties to Bari, and she eventually returned here. Under these two duchesses, the grim fortress became something closer to a Renaissance court, with refined rooms, music, and guests from across Europe.

Think about that journey for a moment. A girl raised in the Italian courts became a queen in Krakow, ruled over cold northern lands, and then came home to this sunny Adriatic coast. Bona Sforza is buried in the Basilica of Saint Nicholas, only a short walk from here. In Poland, she is still remembered, and Polish visitors often come to Bari partly because of her. It is an unexpected thread, tying this southern port to central Europe.

As you look at the moat, imagine water and waves nearby. In earlier centuries, the northern side of the castle stood much closer to the sea. The coastline has changed, and the city has grown outward, so the shore now feels further away. The moat today is dry and grassy. Still, the defensive logic is easy to read. Water on one side, a ditch on the others, and thick stone walls rising straight up from the ground.

When you cross into the castle, take a slow look at the entrance areas and the stonework around the openings. Frederick's builders worked with fine, carefully cut limestone, and some carved decoration survives. Look for sculpted details around doorways and window frames — foliage, figures, and small creatures that seem to peer out from the stone. Many medieval carvings in Apulia mix Christian symbols with older images, and these are worth reading closely.

After the duchesses, the castle's story becomes less elegant. Over the following centuries, it served practical and often grim purposes. At different times, it was used as a prison and as a military barracks. Soldiers and prisoners left their own marks, though most are now gone. Restoration work in the twentieth century removed many of the later additions. It brought the medieval and Renaissance structure back into view, and it opened the castle to the public.

Inside, one of the most interesting collections is the gipsoteca. A gipsoteca is a collection of plaster casts — exact copies of sculptures and architectural carvings. Here, the casts reproduce details from churches and monuments across Apulia, the region around Bari. You can stand close to capitals, portals, and carved figures that would normally sit high on a cathedral wall. It is a rare chance to see medieval stonework at eye level, almost like a sketchbook of the region.

The castle also sits at a meeting point of two worlds. On one side lies Bari Vecchia, the old town, with its narrow alleys, washing hung between houses, and women making orecchiette, the small ear-shaped pasta, at tables in the street. On the other side, the modern city opens out in straighter, wider streets. The castle stands between them like a border post. It marks the edge where the medieval town ends and the newer Bari begins.

Before you leave, walk part of the way around the outside of the moat. Watch how the light changes on the different faces of the walls. The angled bastions throw sharp shadows, while the older towers rise behind them, simpler and more severe. Listen again. The traffic, the voices from the old town, perhaps a church bell from the cathedral nearby. Around these walls, people have kept on living for nearly nine centuries.

As you turn away, take one last look at the pale stone. Two dynasties built it, one king destroyed it, an emperor raised it again, and two duchesses softened it. Now it simply stands here, quiet in the middle of a busy city. Carry that layered picture with you as you walk into the old town — each street there holds its own small piece of the same long story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full moat perimeter before going inside.
Visit late afternoon when the limestone turns warm gold.
Combine with the nearby Basilica of Saint Nicholas.
Continue into Bari Vecchia to see street pasta makers.
Allow extra time for the plaster cast collection.
