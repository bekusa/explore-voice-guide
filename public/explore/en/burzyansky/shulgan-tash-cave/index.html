The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead of you, water is moving. It runs cold and clear out of the rock, gathers into a still pool, and slips away between the trees. The air near the cave mouth feels cooler than the forest around you, and it carries the smell of wet stone. Under your feet the ground is damp and uneven. Look up, and a great arch of pale limestone opens into the hillside, wide enough to swallow a house.

This is Shulgan-Tash, also known in Russian as Kapova Cave. It lies in the Burzyansky District of Bashkortostan, in the southern Ural Mountains. The forests here are thick with lime and oak, and the hills are cut by rivers. The cave is a karst cave. That means water has dissolved the soft limestone over a very long time and carved hollow passages through the mountain. The river you can hear is still doing that work today.

The pool at the entrance has a striking colour. On a clear day it can look deep blue or green, almost unreal against the grey rock. This is the outlet of the Shulgan River, which travels underground through the cave system before it reaches the daylight here. A short distance away it joins a larger river, the Belaya, known in the Bashkir language as the Agidel. Stand still for a moment and watch the surface. You may see it stir where the water rises from below.

Now for the reason scientists from around the world come to this remote valley. Deep inside the cave, on the walls of its inner halls, there are paintings made by people of the Ice Age. They show animals in red ochre, a natural pigment made from iron-rich earth. Researchers have identified mammoths, horses, a woolly rhinoceros and bison among the figures. There are also abstract shapes, lines and patterns whose meaning we can only guess at.

Here is the first surprise. For a long time, many experts believed that this kind of Paleolithic cave art belonged mainly to Western Europe — to famous sites in France and Spain. The paintings at Shulgan-Tash changed that picture. They showed that people far to the east, at the edge of Europe and Asia, were also painting on cave walls in the deep past. Specialists have proposed various ages for the images, but they are generally placed many thousands of years ago, in the Upper Paleolithic, the late Old Stone Age.

The paintings came to scientific attention in 1959, when the zoologist Alexander Ryumin, who worked in the local nature reserve, reported seeing images on the walls. Archaeologists soon followed. In the following decades, excavations on the cave floor uncovered traces of the people who visited here. These included stone tools, pieces of pigment and remains of fires. Each find helped researchers understand that the cave was not only decorated. It was also a place people actually came to, again and again.

The Russian name, Kapova, is often linked to the verb for dripping. Inside, you can understand why. Water seeps through the ceiling and falls in slow drops onto the stone below. Over time, those drops build up mineral formations — stalactites hanging down and flowstone coating the walls. The cave has several levels, stacked one above another, with galleries, halls and narrow passages. Some sections are flooded, and divers have explored parts of the underground river.

The Bashkir name tells a different story. In the Bashkir epic called Ural-batyr, a long traditional poem about the hero Ural, there is a character named Shulgan. He is Ural's brother, and he chooses the side of darkness and evil. According to local tradition, this cave and its waters are connected with Shulgan and with the underground world. This is legend, not history. But it shows how deeply the place is woven into the culture of the people who live here.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a quiet one. The paintings are fragile. Changes in temperature, humidity and even the breath of visitors can damage them. For this reason, access to the painted halls is tightly limited, and parts of the cave may be closed to the public. Many visitors see the images through replicas and exhibits in the museum nearby. It can feel disappointing at first. Yet this restraint is the reason the paintings have survived for us at all.

The cave sits inside a protected nature reserve, and the forest around you is part of the story. These woods are home to a special local honey bee, the Burzyan bee, a dark wild bee adapted to the harsh Ural winters. For centuries, Bashkir beekeepers have practised an old craft of keeping bees in hollows carved high in living trees. They climb the trunks with ropes to collect the honey. The reserve was created in part to protect this bee and this tradition.

As you walk the paths, watch the trees. In some places you may notice marked hollows or wooden structures high on the trunks. These are signs of the tree-hive craft. The honey from this region has a strong, dark flavour, and locals take real pride in it. If you find it sold nearby, it connects you directly with the forest you are standing in, and with families who have worked these trees for generations.

Think for a moment about the people who made the paintings. They came here in a much colder world, with mammoths on the open land and ice not far away. They carried fire into the dark. They ground red earth, mixed it, and pressed it onto the stone with their hands or simple tools. We do not know their names or their language. We do not fully know why they painted. But they chose this cave, beside this same river.

Before you leave, return to the water at the entrance. Look at the arch of rock above you, streaked with grey and cream. Listen to the river as it comes out of the mountain. That sound would have been familiar to the painters, thousands of years ago. As you walk back through the trees, notice the cool air fading behind you... and the forest smells returning. The cave stays here, quiet and protected, holding its oldest images in the dark.
