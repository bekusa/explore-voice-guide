The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps softly against a pebbled shore. Behind you, a steep wall of green rises straight up from the beach, and somewhere inside it a branch cracks. Then a call starts, low and breathy, climbing to a shriek that echoes down the valley. That sound is a chimpanzee. You have arrived at Gombe, on the eastern shore of Lake Tanganyika in western Tanzania, and the forest in front of you is one of the most closely watched forests on Earth.

Most visitors reach Gombe the way you probably did, by boat. There is no road along this shore. The park lies north of Kigoma town, and the journey runs along a coastline of fishing villages, small beaches and steep hills. That separation matters. Gombe feels remote because it is remote. The lake is the main road here, and it has shaped the life of this place for as long as people have lived beside it.

The lake itself deserves a moment. Lake Tanganyika sits in the Great Rift Valley, a long crack in the African continent where the land is slowly pulling apart. It is one of the oldest lakes in the world and the second deepest. Its water is famously clear, and its fish include many species found nowhere else. At night, you may see small lights scattered across the dark water. Those are fishing boats, using lanterns to draw small silver fish toward their nets.

Now turn back to the forest. Gombe is tiny compared with the great savanna parks of Tanzania. It is one of the smallest national parks in the country. Yet its landscape is dramatic. Narrow valleys cut down from the rift escarpment to the lake, each carrying its own stream. Between the valleys run sharp ridges. The lower slopes hold thick forest, while the higher ground opens into grassland and woodland. Walking here means climbing, almost always.

The story that made Gombe known around the world begins in 1960. That year, a young Englishwoman named Jane Goodall arrived on this shore to study wild chimpanzees. She had no university degree at the time. Her work was supported by the paleoanthropologist Louis Leakey, a scientist who studied human origins and believed that our closest living relatives could teach us about our own deep past. At first, the chimpanzees fled whenever she came near.

Goodall was patient. She sat for long hours on the ridges with binoculars, and slowly the animals accepted her presence. Then came the observation that changed science. She watched a male chimpanzee, whom she named David Greybeard, strip leaves from a twig and push it into a termite mound to fish out insects. He was making a tool. Until then, many scientists believed that making tools was something only humans did. Here is the first surprise: one patient woman on a quiet ridge forced science to rethink what makes us human.

That was only the beginning. Goodall gave the chimpanzees names instead of numbers, which was unusual at the time and criticised by some scientists. She described their personalities, their friendships and their rivalries. She saw them hunt and eat meat. Later, researchers here recorded something darker: violent conflict between chimpanzee groups. The picture of a peaceful ape gave way to something more complex, and in some ways more familiar to us.

Here is the second surprise. The research Goodall started did not stop. The study of chimpanzees at Gombe has continued for more than six decades, making it one of the longest-running studies of any wild animal. Researchers have followed mothers, their children, and their grandchildren. Famous individuals like the old mother Flo, her daughter Fifi, and Fifi's son Frodo are part of one continuous family record. Goodall herself died in 2025, but the work here continues.

The area became a national park in 1968, and today chimpanzee trekking is the main reason people come. A guide leads you up into the valleys, following the sounds and signs of the chimpanzees. Sometimes they are close to the shore. Sometimes the trek is long and steep. When you find them, the rules are strict: keep your distance, stay quiet, and stay with your guide. Chimpanzees can catch human illnesses, so visitors who feel sick should not join a trek.

Chimpanzees are not the only primates here. Olive baboons often move along the beach and the forest edge, and they have also been studied at Gombe for many years. Look up into the canopy and you may see red colobus monkeys, or red-tailed monkeys with their bright tails hanging down. Birds call constantly from the trees. Keep your belongings closed and food out of sight, because the baboons are bold and quick.

As you walk, notice the forest floor. Chimpanzees leave signs everywhere. Look for broken branches folded together high in the trees. These are nests, and a chimpanzee builds a fresh one almost every night. Look also for cracked fruit, scattered leaves, and termite mounds with small holes in their sides. Each of these is a small clue to the daily life you have come to see.

It is also worth thinking about the land around the park. From the lake, you can see how the hills outside the boundary look different. Much of that land has been cleared for farming and villages over the years. Gombe's forest now stands almost like an island. Conservation work here focuses not only on the chimpanzees, but on the communities nearby, because the future of the forest depends on the people who live next to it.

Before you leave, walk back to the shore. Feel the smooth stones under your feet and the warm water at the edge of the lake. Look up at the ridges one last time, where a young researcher once sat for hours, waiting for a chimpanzee to trust her. Listen again. Perhaps a call will carry down the valley, the same kind of sound that greeted her more than sixty years ago. The forest is still here, and so are its families.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book your chimpanzee trekking permit well before arriving.
Wear sturdy shoes with grip for steep, slippery trails.
Skip the trek if you feel unwell to protect chimpanzees.
Keep food hidden and bags closed around the baboons.
Pack a light rain jacket for sudden forest showers.
