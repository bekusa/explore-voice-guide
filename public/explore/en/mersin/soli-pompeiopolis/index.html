The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, traffic hums along the Mersin coast road. In front of you, a line of pale stone columns rises from the grass. The sea is close enough to smell, salt and warm air coming in off the Mediterranean. Modern apartment blocks look down on all of it from every side. This is Soli, later called Pompeiopolis, and it is one of the strangest places in Turkey to meet the ancient world. It sits right inside an ordinary neighbourhood.

Locals have long called this area Viranşehir, which means roughly "the ruined city" in Turkish. The name tells you how people saw these stones for centuries. They were simply the old ruins at the edge of town, a place for goats and grass. Today the district is called Mezitli, and the city of Mersin has grown all the way around them. That contrast is part of the experience here. A woman hangs laundry on a balcony while Roman columns stand in the street below her.

The story of Soli begins long before Rome. Ancient writers said that settlers from the island of Rhodes founded a colony here, around 700 years before Christ. This coast was part of a region the Greeks called Cilicia, the fertile strip between the Taurus Mountains and the sea. But the Greeks were not the first people on this spot. Archaeologists working on the large mound nearby have found layers far older than any Greek colony, reaching back into the Bronze Age.

That mound is called Soli Höyük, and it is worth finding during your visit. A höyük is a hill made not by nature, but by people. Each generation built on the remains of the one before, until the ground itself rose. Excavations here have uncovered remains from the Hittite period, when a powerful empire ruled much of Anatolia. So the ground under your feet holds not one city, but many. They are stacked like pages in a very old book.

Now here is a surprise most visitors never hear. Soli may have given the English language a word. According to an ancient tradition, the Greeks of Soli spoke a broken, unusual form of Greek. Other Greeks mocked their mistakes in grammar. From the name Soli came the Greek word soloikismos, and from that came the English word "solecism" — a mistake in grammar or manners. Whether the people of Soli really spoke badly, we cannot know. But the word has survived for more than two thousand years.

This small city also produced some remarkable minds. The Stoic philosopher Chrysippus was born here. Stoicism is the school of thought that teaches calm, reason, and acceptance of what we cannot control. Chrysippus became one of its most important teachers in Athens, and he wrote a huge number of works. Ancient writers said that without Chrysippus, there would have been no Stoic school at all. A great deal of Stoic logic is traced back to him.

The poet Aratus also came from Soli. He wrote a long poem called the Phaenomena, which describes the constellations and the signs of the weather. It became one of the most widely read poems of the ancient world. And here is a second surprise. In the Bible, in the Book of Acts, Saint Paul speaks to the people of Athens. He quotes a line from a Greek poet: "For we are also his offspring." That line is generally traced to Aratus, the poet of this town.

The city's fortunes did not always run smoothly. In the first century before Christ, the Armenian king Tigranes the Great took control of this region. He moved much of Soli's population away to his new royal city, Tigranocerta. Soli was left nearly empty. For a time, it seemed this old Greek colony might simply fade from history. Then Rome arrived.

The Roman general Pompey came to this coast to fight the Cilician pirates. These pirates had become a serious threat to shipping across the Mediterranean. Pompey defeated them quickly. Then he made an unusual choice. Instead of executing many of the captured pirates, he resettled some of them here, in the half-empty city, and gave them land to farm. The city took his name. From then on, it was Pompeiopolis — the city of Pompey.

Under Roman rule, the city grew rich again. Its greatest monument is the colonnaded street in front of you. It once ran from the harbour toward the heart of the town, lined on both sides with columns. Originally there were around two hundred of them. Today only a couple of dozen still stand. Walk slowly along the line and look up at the tops. Many columns carry Corinthian capitals — decorated with carved acanthus leaves, a spiky Mediterranean plant the Romans loved.

Look carefully at the shafts of the columns, too. Some have small projecting stone brackets, about halfway up. These brackets once held statues — probably of emperors, local benefactors, and important citizens. Imagine walking this street in Roman times. Shops would have filled the covered walkways behind the columns. Painted statues would have watched from above. And at the far end, the harbour, full of ships, with the smell of tar and fish in the air.

That harbour still exists, though in a quieter form. The Romans built stone breakwaters to protect the ships from the open sea. Parts of these old harbour walls can still be traced near the shoreline. Over the centuries, sand and silt filled much of the basin. Earthquakes also struck this coast hard, and one in the sixth century caused great damage. Slowly, the city lost its importance. People left, and the columns stood alone.

Today, Soli is a place of patient work. Archaeologists still return to the mound and the street, uncovering mosaics, walls, and small objects from daily life. Some finds have gone to museums in the region. What remains here is a site that feels unfinished. You may see fenced trenches, protective covers, or fragments of carved stone resting on the ground. Each one is a small piece of the city's long story, still being read.

Before you leave, stand at one end of the colonnade and look down its length toward the sea. Notice how the old columns and the modern towers share the same sky. Listen again to the traffic, the voices, perhaps children playing nearby. Rhodians, Hittites, philosophers, and pirates all lived on this same strip of coast. Now it is your turn to walk here. As you go, look back once at the columns... and remember the word solecism, born on this shore.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid the midday heat.
Walk the full colonnade from the harbour end inland.
Bring water and a hat; the site has little shade.
Combine your visit with a walk along the nearby seafront.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground around the mound.
