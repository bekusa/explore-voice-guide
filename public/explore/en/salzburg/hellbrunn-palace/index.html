The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere close, water is running. It trickles through stone channels, drips from moss, and hisses softly behind walls you cannot see. The air near the gardens feels cooler than the road you came from. It smells of wet gravel and old limestone. At Hellbrunn, water is never only decoration. It is the main performer, and it has been on stage here for more than four hundred years.

The palace sits at the southern edge of Salzburg, at the foot of a small wooded hill. A long, straight avenue lined with trees once connected it to the old town. That avenue still runs through fields and villas today. It tells you something about the purpose of this place. Hellbrunn was a retreat, close enough for a short carriage ride, but far enough from the duties and the crowds of the city.

The man behind it was Markus Sittikus, the Prince-Archbishop of Salzburg in the early seventeenth century. In Salzburg, the archbishop was both a church leader and a ruling prince, with real political power. Work on the palace began in 1612 and moved quickly. The architect most often named is Santino Solari, an Italian who also worked on Salzburg Cathedral. You can see the Italian influence in the calm, light-coloured facade and the balanced lines.

Here is the first surprise. Hellbrunn was built as a day palace. The archbishop came here for pleasure, for feasts, for music and for walks in the gardens. Then, as a rule, he returned to the city for the night. Italian nobles called this kind of country house a villa suburbana — a villa just outside the city. Hellbrunn is one of the clearest examples of that idea north of the Alps.

Inside, the rooms carry painted decoration from the palace's first years. The festive hall is the most dramatic space. Its walls and ceiling are covered with painted architecture, columns and open skies that make the room seem taller than it really is. Look closely and you will see that much of this depth is an illusion. Flat plaster is painted to look like galleries and balconies. The whole building plays this kind of game with the visitor.

And that game reaches its peak outside, in the trick fountains. In German they are called Wasserspiele, which means water games. Markus Sittikus clearly had a strong sense of humour. His gardens are full of hidden jets that spray guests without warning. The fountains still work on the original principle. They use water from springs on the hill and gravity alone. No modern pumps are needed for the main effects.

The most famous joke is a stone dining table set outdoors, with stone stools around it. Guests sat down, the wine was poured, and then water shot up from the seats. Every seat was wet — except one. The archbishop's own chair stayed dry. Guests were not allowed to stand up before the host did, so they simply had to sit there and laugh along. On a guided walk today, the same table still catches people by surprise.

Further along, the grottoes take you into cool, dim spaces decorated with shells, stones and sculpted figures. In one grotto, a jet of water lifts a crown into the air and lowers it again. In another, a grotesque mask rolls its eyes and sticks out its tongue. These effects were the high technology of their age. For a visitor in the seventeenth century, they would have felt close to magic.

The second surprise is the Mechanical Theatre. It was added in the mid-eighteenth century, under a later archbishop, long after Markus Sittikus had died. It shows a small baroque town in miniature, full of tiny figures. There are craftsmen, soldiers, dancers and street sellers. When the water starts to flow, more than a hundred of them move at once. A water-driven organ plays music while the whole town comes to life.

Not everything at Hellbrunn is so playful. Up the hill, inside the woods, you can find the Stone Theatre. It is a natural stage cut into an old quarry, with rough rock walls rising around it. Many people believe it is one of the earliest open-air theatres for opera in the German-speaking world. Local tradition links it to some of the first opera performances north of the Alps. Historians still discuss the details, so treat the exact story with some caution.

Also on the hill stands a small building with a famous tale. It is called the Monthly Palace. According to a popular story, Markus Sittikus built it within a single month to impress a visiting guest. Whether it was really that fast is hard to prove. Today it houses a museum of folk culture, with traditional costumes, masks and objects from everyday life in the Salzburg region.

One more detail draws many visitors to the park. A white glass gazebo stands among the trees. It appears in the 1965 film The Sound of Music, which was filmed in and around Salzburg. The pavilion was moved here from its original filming location. Fans come to photograph it, and some still hum the song from the film as they walk past. It is a modern addition, but it fits the spirit of a garden built for entertainment.

Now, look around you once more. The ponds hold a still reflection of the palace front. The paths are straight, then suddenly open into wide views of the hill and the sky. Somewhere behind you, the water keeps running in its stone channels, as it has for four centuries. When you walk away, notice how quiet the sound becomes. Then notice how much you still want to look back.
