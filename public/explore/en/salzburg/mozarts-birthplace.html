The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on Getreidegasse and listen. Footsteps echo between tall, narrow houses. Wrought-iron signs hang above your head, and a café door opens onto the street. Now look up at the yellow facade in front of you. Painted letters across it spell out a simple message in German: this is the house where Mozart was born. Thousands of people pass this spot every day, and most of them tilt their heads back exactly the way you are doing now.

The address is Getreidegasse number 9. In the eighteenth century, this building belonged to a merchant named Johann Lorenz Hagenauer. He owned the house and rented the third floor to a young couple, Leopold and Anna Maria Mozart. Leopold was a violinist and composer at the court of the Prince-Archbishop of Salzburg. At that time, Salzburg was not part of Austria at all. It was an independent church state, ruled by archbishops who were both religious leaders and princes.

On 27 January 1756, in one of the rooms upstairs, Anna Maria gave birth to a son. The next day he was baptised in Salzburg Cathedral, just a few minutes' walk from here. His baptismal names were long and formal: Joannes Chrysostomus Wolfgangus Theophilus. Theophilus is Greek for 'loved by God'. Later in life he often used the Latin version of that name, Amadeus. The world still knows him by it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Mozarts had seven children, but only two survived childhood. One was Wolfgang. The other was his older sister, Maria Anna, whom the family called Nannerl. She was also a gifted keyboard player. As children, the two of them toured the courts of Europe together, and audiences were amazed by them. That sad family history makes the small rooms upstairs feel even more personal.

It is worth noticing what kind of building this really is. It is not a palace. It is a typical tall burgher house, the home of a prosperous townsperson. Shops are on the ground floor, apartments are stacked above, and a narrow passage leads through to the back. The Mozart family apartment was modest — a kitchen, a living room, a bedroom and a small study. When you walk through it, you see how close together everyone lived. Music, conversation and the noise of the street all shared the same few rooms.

The family lived here from 1747 until 1773. That means Wolfgang spent his whole childhood and early youth in this house. He wrote some of his first pieces as a small boy, and his father carefully wrote many of them down. When the apartment became too crowded, the family moved across the river. They took a larger home on what is now Makartplatz. Today that second house is also a museum, so the Mozart story in Salzburg has two homes.

The birthplace became a museum in 1880. Today it is run by the International Mozarteum Foundation, a Salzburg organisation that looks after Mozart's legacy. Inside, the rooms hold real objects from his life. You can see letters, family portraits and documents. You can also see instruments that are connected to the composer. Seeing them in the rooms where he grew up connects the music to an ordinary family life.

One of the most moving objects is a small violin that Mozart played as a child. It is surprisingly small. Look at it closely and imagine a boy's hands on that neck. There is also a clavichord, a quiet keyboard instrument with a soft, delicate sound. Families often used one at home, because it would not disturb the neighbours. Many visitors stop in front of these instruments longer than in front of anything else.

Look out for a portrait of the adult Mozart painted by his brother-in-law, Joseph Lange. It was never finished. The lower part of the canvas is incomplete, yet many people find this picture more honest than the polished official portraits. It shows a thoughtful, slightly tired face rather than a powdered genius. Mozart's wife, Constanze, is said to have thought it was one of the best likenesses of him.

Now step back outside and look at the street itself. Getreidegasse means roughly 'grain lane'. It has been a busy shopping street for centuries. The hanging iron signs are a tradition from a time when many people could not read. A shop showed its trade with a picture, such as a key, a boot or a pretzel. Even modern shops here keep this custom. Their brand names are worked into the same elegant ironwork.

If you have time, walk through one of the narrow covered passages that cut between the houses. These little lanes connect Getreidegasse with the squares and streets behind it. The Mozart house has a back side too. It faces Universitätsplatz, a wide square where a market often sets up its stalls. From there you can see the rear of the building and the busy daily life around it. The contrast is striking: a famous birth room above, and fresh bread and flowers being sold below.

Think, too, about how Mozart felt about Salzburg. As a young man he worked for the archbishop, but he grew frustrated. He wanted more freedom and a bigger audience. In 1781 he left the archbishop's service and settled in Vienna, where he spent the rest of his short life. He died there in 1791, aged only thirty-five. Salzburg came to celebrate him fully only later. The city that once felt too small for him now carries his name on almost every corner.

Before you move on, look up once more at the yellow facade and the windows of the third floor. Behind one of them, a child first heard the sounds of this street. He heard bells from nearby churches, voices from the market and his father's violin in the next room. Those sounds still drift through Getreidegasse today. As you walk away, listen for them — the footsteps, the chatter, a bell in the distance. It is the same everyday music that surrounded the young Mozart.
