The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves through the cypress trees and the dry grass. Somewhere beyond the fence, a tractor works its way between rows of vines. The ground under your feet is pale and dusty, and in summer the light here is almost white. This is a quiet valley in the hills of the northeastern Peloponnese. Yet for centuries it drew athletes and pilgrims from across the Greek world.

You are standing in the sanctuary of Zeus at Nemea. In ancient times, this was not a town. It was a sacred place, set apart from ordinary life. People came here for one reason above all: the Nemean Games. Together with the games at Olympia, Delphi, and Isthmia, they formed a circuit of four great festivals. An athlete who won at all four earned a special title. It was one of the highest honours a Greek athlete could hold.

Before the history, there is the myth. And Nemea has two of them. The first belongs to Heracles, the hero of superhuman strength. Legend has it that a lion lived in these hills. Its hide could not be cut by any weapon. For the first of his famous Labours, Heracles strangled the beast with his bare hands. Afterwards he wore its skin as a cloak. In ancient art, that lion-skin is how you recognise him.

The second myth is darker, and it is the one the ancient Greeks linked to the games themselves. According to the story, an army of seven champions marched past Nemea on its way to attack the city of Thebes. A nurse set down the infant prince Opheltes on a bed of wild celery while she helped the soldiers find water. A serpent killed the child. The warriors held funeral games in his honour — and so, the tale says, the Nemean Games began.

That little detail of the celery is worth remembering. Victors at Nemea did not receive gold. They received a crown of wild celery. It sounds modest, almost comic, to modern ears. But the prize carried enormous prestige. A winner returned home a hero. Cities sometimes honoured champions with statues, songs, and free meals for life. The plant itself was a reminder of the dead child at the heart of the story.

Now turn toward the temple. The tall grey columns rise in the Doric style — the plain, sturdy Greek order with simple, cushion-like tops. This Temple of Zeus was built in the fourth century before Christ, replacing an earlier one. For a very long time, only three of its columns stood upright. Travellers sketched them and painted them. Those three lonely columns became the image of Nemea for generations of visitors.

Here is the first surprise. Many of the columns you see today were lying in pieces on the ground for centuries. In recent decades, archaeologists and conservators have been raising fallen drums back into place, one by one. This careful method is called anastylosis — rebuilding with the original stones. Look closely and you can often tell the ancient blocks from the newer fill. The temple is slowly standing up again in front of you.

Inside, the temple held another unusual feature. Below the floor at the back, there was a sunken chamber, a kind of crypt. Scholars still discuss what happened there. It may have been linked to an oracle, or to some ritual we no longer fully understand. Around the temple, you can trace the low foundations of other buildings: a long altar, houses for priests or officials, and a bath building for the athletes.

The sanctuary did not always hold its own games. Historical sources show that at one point the festival was moved to the nearby city of Argos. Later it returned here, and then left again. Each move followed the politics of the region. When the games departed, the sanctuary emptied. Centuries later, early Christians built a small basilica among the ruins. Its remains still sit here, reusing stones from the pagan buildings around it.

A short walk down the road takes you to the stadium. This is where the second great surprise waits. The athletes entered through a vaulted stone tunnel. Its walls still carry ancient graffiti, scratched by young men waiting for their race. One famous line reads, in Greek, that a certain Akrotatos is beautiful. More than two thousand years later, you can still read the boasts and names of nervous competitors.

On the track itself, look for the long stone starting line. Its grooves held the runners' toes at the start. Spectators did not sit on grand marble seats here. Most of them sat or stood on the earthen slopes around the track. Picture the noise — a crowd shouting in the heat, the dust rising, the runners barefoot. Since the 1990s, this scene has returned. Modern Nemean Games are held here, and anyone can run, barefoot and in a simple tunic.

Much of what you see exists because of long excavation work led by the University of California, Berkeley, together with Greek archaeologists. The small museum near the temple shows what they found: coins, pottery, athletic equipment, and objects from daily life. It helps you picture the people behind the stones. Around the site, the valley is still farmland. This region is known for its red wine, made mostly from a local grape called Agiorgitiko.

Before you leave, stand still once more near the temple. Look at the line where old stone meets new stone in the columns. Notice the cypress trees, the vineyards, and the low hills that held the lion of legend. As you walk back to the road, listen again to the wind in the grass. For a few days every few years, this same quiet valley still fills with runners and cheering voices.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strongest summer heat.
Bring a hat and water; shade is very limited.
Walk the short road between sanctuary and stadium.
Visit the site museum before exploring the temple ruins.
Try local Agiorgitiko wine at a nearby winery.
