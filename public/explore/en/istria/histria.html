The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through dry grass, and somewhere out on the water a bird calls. Under your feet the ground crunches with broken stone and old pottery. Ahead of you, low walls run in lines across the plain, and beyond them lies a wide, flat sheet of water. This is Histria, on the Black Sea coast of Romania. For more than a thousand years, people lived, traded and prayed in this place.

The story begins with sailors from Miletus, a powerful Greek city on the western coast of what is now Turkey. Around the seventh century before Christ, Milesian colonists settled here. Histria is usually described as the oldest urban settlement on Romanian soil. The settlers chose this spot for its sheltered bay and for its position near the mouth of a great river. In fact, the city took its name from that river. The Greeks called the Danube the Istros.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. When the Greeks arrived, this was a seaside city. Ships sailed right up to its harbour. The calm water you see today, Lake Sinoe, was once an open bay of the Black Sea. Over the centuries, sand and sediment from the Danube slowly closed it off. The sea moved away from the city, and with it went much of the trade. Histria did not fall in a single day. Its coastline left it, a little every year.

In its best years, the city lived from the sea and from the land behind it. Greek merchants traded olive oil, wine and fine pottery with the peoples of the interior. In return, they received grain, fish, honey and other goods. Histria minted its own silver coins. A well-known design shows a sea eagle gripping a dolphin in its talons. It is a simple picture of a city that looked both to the sky and to the water.

As you walk, notice that the ruins sit in layers. Greek foundations lie beneath Roman ones, and later buildings stand on top of both. Rome took control of this coast around the first century before Christ. Histria then became part of a Roman frontier province on the lower Danube. The city changed its shape many times. Each new period built over the last one, often reusing the older stones.

Look along the main defensive walls. In late Roman times, the city shrank behind a strong wall of stone. Its builders took whatever material they could find. You may see carved blocks, column pieces or inscribed stones set into the masonry at odd angles. These pieces once belonged to temples and public buildings. In a time of danger, safety mattered more than beauty, and old monuments became building blocks.

Inside the walls, the paths lead you through the remains of streets, houses and public spaces. Archaeologists have uncovered Roman baths here. You can still see the low brick supports that once held up heated floors. Hot air from a furnace moved through the space beneath, and the floor above became warm. Imagine the steam, the voices and the slap of wet feet on stone. For a frontier town, these baths were a piece of everyday Roman comfort.

The city also had Christian basilicas, built in its later centuries. Their outlines remain as low walls and rows of column bases. Stand inside one of these outlines and look east, the direction early churches usually faced. The roof and the painted walls are gone. Still, the floor plan shows you where the community gathered. Here, the old Greek town and the Christian world of the late empire meet in one place.

In the older part of the site, closer to the lake, lies the sacred area of the Greek city. Excavations here found the remains of temples. Scholars connect them with Greek gods, including Zeus and Aphrodite. Only foundations and scattered blocks survive. But this ground was the religious heart of the first settlers. Think of the smoke of offerings, the sound of prayers, and the sea just a short walk away.

The second surprise is how long Histria survived. Raids and wars struck the city again and again. Goths and other peoples crossed the Danube and attacked the coast. Each time, the people rebuilt. The city finally emptied around the seventh century after Christ, during a period of invasions and upheaval across the region. By then, the harbour had closed and the old trade had ended. The people left, and the grass slowly covered the stones.

Modern excavation began in 1914 under the Romanian historian and archaeologist Vasile Pârvan. Work has continued over many decades since then. Generations of archaeologists have studied this ground, and much of the site is still not fully understood. The museum on site holds many of their finds. It displays pottery, sculpture, inscriptions and coins. If you can, visit it before or after your walk. The objects give faces and names to the empty streets outside.

Now turn toward the water and the reeds. Histria sits at the edge of the Danube Delta Biosphere Reserve, one of Europe's great wetlands. The lagoons around the site attract many birds, especially in spring and autumn. You may see herons or cormorants, or hear frogs in the warm months. The wildlife and the ruins share this landscape. Nature has reclaimed the bay that once made the city rich.

The light here changes the place. At midday, the sun is strong, the stones turn pale, and there is very little shade. Late in the afternoon, the light becomes softer and lower. The walls throw long shadows across the grass, and the lines of the old streets become easier to read. The flat land and the wide sky make every season feel different. This is a quiet site, and it rewards slow walking.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the line where the old walls meet the grass, and then at the line where the grass meets the water. Once, ships came to rest just there. Now there is only wind, reeds and the call of birds. As you walk back, listen to the stones under your shoes. Each small piece was once part of a city, and you are walking through what it left behind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a hat and water; the site has little shade.
Visit the on-site museum to see the excavated finds.
Come late afternoon when low light shows the wall lines.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and gravel paths.
Bring binoculars for birds on the surrounding lagoons.
