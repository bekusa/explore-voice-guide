The guide

The story

Listen first. On a still day the only sound here is the wind coming off the water and the crunch of gravel, or snow, under your shoes. Then lift your eyes. A white triangle rises straight up from the slope, sharp as a folded sheet of paper. Its pale panels catch whatever light the Arctic sky offers. Behind it, steep mountains climb towards the sky. In front of it, the island city of Tromsø sits low across the strait.

This is the Arctic Cathedral, known in Norwegian as Ishavskatedralen. The name roughly means the Cathedral of the Arctic Ocean. Here is the first surprise. It is not actually a cathedral. Officially, it is Tromsdalen Church, a parish church of the Church of Norway, the country's Lutheran church. Tromsø's real cathedral is a wooden building back in the city centre. The grand nickname came from the public and stayed.

You are standing on the mainland side of Tromsø, in the district of Tromsdalen. The city centre is on an island across the water, connected by a long, arching bridge. Many visitors walk that bridge to get here. It takes a while, and the wind can be fierce. But the reward is the slow approach, watching this white shape grow larger and sharper with every step. From the bridge, the church looks almost like a mountain peak that has come down to the shore.

The church was designed by the Norwegian architect Jan Inge Hovig. It was completed and consecrated in 1965. At that time, Tromsdalen was growing quickly, and the new parish needed a church of its own. Hovig gave it something unexpected for northern Norway. Instead of a traditional steeple and wooden walls, he built a modern form from concrete. The building became one of the best-known modern churches in the country.

Look closely at the structure. The roof and walls are the same thing. A series of large triangular panels lean against each other, stepping down in size from front to back. They are made of concrete, and the outside is clad in aluminium. That metal skin is the reason the church seems to glow. On grey days it is soft and silver. On clear days it is almost blinding white against the blue.

What does the shape mean? Hovig's design has invited many readings, and people still debate them. Some see icebergs or the snowy peaks of the surrounding mountains. Some see the racks used along this coast to dry fish in the open air. Others see the shape of a traditional Sami tent. The Sami are the Indigenous people of northern Scandinavia. Perhaps the best answer is that the building lets each visitor bring their own picture of the North.

Now turn to the front, the side facing the water. Here, a tall wall of glass fills the gap between the panels. A large cross is set into it. At night, when the lights are on inside, this glass wall shines out across the strait towards the city. For people on the island, the church becomes a lantern on the opposite shore. In the long winter darkness, that matters more than you might think.

When you step inside, the mood changes. The space is tall and narrow, rising to a point high above you. The ceiling follows the stepped panels you saw outside, so you can read the structure from within. The walls are plain. Rows of simple pews face the altar. The air is quiet. After the wind outside, many people simply stop and stand for a moment before they walk forward.

At the far end, behind the altar, comes the second surprise. When the church opened, the east wall was mostly bare. A few years later, in the early 1970s, a huge glass mosaic was added. It was made by the Norwegian artist Victor Sparre. It shows Christ returning to earth, with rays of light falling from above. The colours are deep blues, warm golds and touches of red.

That window works with the Arctic light in a special way. Tromsø lies far north of the Arctic Circle. In midwinter, there is a long period called the polar night, when the sun does not rise above the horizon. In summer, there is the midnight sun, when it never sets. So the light through this glass is never quite the same. Sometimes it is a faint blue glow. Sometimes the colours burn bright late into the evening.

Look up as you walk, and you may notice the lights hanging in the nave. They are made of glass prisms, and they hang in long lines from the ceiling. Some people say they look like icicles. Then turn back towards the entrance. Above the door you will find the church organ. Music is a big part of life here. Besides regular services, the church hosts concerts, especially in the bright summer nights and the dark winter months.

Those concerts are part of the church's double life. On Sundays, it is a local parish church, with baptisms, weddings and funerals for the people of Tromsdalen. On many other days, it is a destination for travellers from around the world. Please remember both lives as you visit. Keep voices low inside, and step aside if a service or ceremony is under way. You are a guest in a working church.

Before you leave, walk a few steps away and look back. Notice how the white triangles echo the mountains behind them. Notice how small the building seems against the slopes, and how strong it seems against the sky. Listen again to the wind and the water. When you cross back towards the island, turn around once more. The church will still be there, pale and sharp on the shore, holding the northern light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk across the Tromsø Bridge for the best approach views.
Dress for strong wind on the bridge and the open slope.
Check the church calendar for midnight sun or winter concerts.
Stay quiet and step aside during services or ceremonies.
Return after dark to see the glass front glowing.
