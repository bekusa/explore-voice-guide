The guide

The story

Put your hand on the parapet. The brick is rough and warm, worn smooth in places by centuries of elbows. Below you, the Adige moves fast and green, cold from the Alps. Above you, the battlements rise in sharp notched shapes against the sky. This is the Castelvecchio Bridge, also called the Scaliger Bridge. You are standing on a structure that was never meant for strolling. It was built as an escape route.

The bridge dates to the mid fourteenth century, the 1350s. Verona was then ruled by the della Scala family, known in English as the Scaligeri. The man behind the bridge was Cangrande the Second. He was not a popular lord. His city resented him, and his own family was a danger to him. So he built a fortress on the riverbank, and he built this bridge to go with it.

Think about the logic of that. The castle behind you, Castelvecchio, sits on the right bank. The bridge links it to the far bank of the Adige. From there, roads led north toward the Alps and the lands of powerful allies. If the city rose against him, Cangrande could cross the river without passing through a single Veronese street. The bridge was a private door out of his own city.

In the end, the door did not save him. In 1359, only a few years after the bridge was finished, Cangrande the Second was murdered. The killer was his own brother, Cansignorio, who then took power. There is a dark irony in it. The lord built walls and bridges against his subjects. The threat came from inside his own family, close enough to touch.

Now look at the bridge itself. It crosses the river in three arches, and they are not the same size. The arches grow wider as they approach the far bank. The largest span is remarkably long for its time. It is often described as one of the widest bridge arches of the medieval world. Medieval builders had no steel and no concrete. They had brick, stone, timber scaffolding, and a deep practical knowledge of how weight travels through an arch.

Look down at the piers, the thick supports that stand in the water. Their lower parts are faced with pale stone, while the structure above is red brick. The pale stone is there for a reason. It faces the river, where water, ice and floating debris strike hardest. The brick above is lighter and quicker to build. The two materials together give the bridge its familiar look — white feet, red body.

Now raise your eyes to the battlements along the top. Each one ends in a V-shaped notch, like the tail of a swallow. In Italy these are often called Ghibelline merlons. The Ghibellines were the faction that supported the Holy Roman Emperor in medieval Italian politics. The Scaligeri belonged to that side. So these notches are more than decoration. They are a political badge, built in brick, telling every traveller which side Verona stood on.

Walk slowly and notice the towers that rise along the bridge. The walkway is narrow, with high walls on both sides. Soldiers could move along it under cover. Small openings in the walls let defenders watch the river and the banks. Today, couples lean in those openings to take photographs. Six hundred years ago, the same gaps were for archers. The bridge was a weapon first and a crossing second.

Here is something many visitors walk past without knowing. The bridge you are standing on was destroyed during the Second World War. In April 1945, retreating German troops blew up the bridges of Verona, and this one fell into the Adige. For a few years after the war, there was only a gap between the banks. The towers and arches you see today are a reconstruction, finished in the early 1950s.

But this reconstruction is unusual. The rebuilders did not simply copy the old bridge in new materials. They recovered original bricks and stones from the riverbed, where they had fallen in the explosion. Where they could, they put those pieces back into the structure. They also looked for new bricks that matched the old ones in colour and size. So much of what you touch here truly is medieval. It simply spent a few years at the bottom of the river.

Turn back toward the castle at the end of the bridge. Castelvecchio means old castle, though in its own time it was the new fortress of the Scaligeri. Today it holds the Castelvecchio Museum, with medieval sculpture, paintings and arms. In the twentieth century, the architect Carlo Scarpa redesigned the museum's interior. His work combines modern concrete, steel and glass with the ancient walls. Architects travel to Verona just to see how he did it.

If you go into the museum, look for the way Scarpa treated old and new. He left gaps between them, so you can always see where history ends and his own design begins. The bridge and the museum share a similar honesty. Both are old places that were broken and then repaired. Neither one hides the repair completely. If you look closely at the bridge bricks, you can sometimes notice slight changes in tone.

Before you leave, walk to the middle of the bridge and stop. Look upstream and downstream along the Adige. The river curves through Verona, and the hills rise to the north. The water is loud here, especially after rain in the mountains. Listen to it under your feet. The same current ran here when Cangrande stood on this spot, planning for a day he might need to run.

As you walk on, let your hand rest on the parapet one more time. Some of those bricks fell into the river and came back out again. Some have stood here, untouched, since the fourteenth century. You cannot always tell which is which... and perhaps that is the point. The bridge carries its whole story at once — the fear of a lord, the skill of its builders, the damage of war, and the patience of the people who rebuilt it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to cross before tour groups arrive.
Pair your walk with the Castelvecchio Museum next door.
Walk to the far bank for a full side view.
Return at sunset when the brick turns deep orange.
Wear grippy shoes for the worn, uneven walkway.
