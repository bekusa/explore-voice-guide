The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Below you, the Adige river runs fast and green, and its sound rises up the stone steps. The seats under your feet are cool and smooth, worn down by centuries of weather. Across the water, the bell towers and red roofs of Verona's old centre catch the light. You are sitting where Roman audiences once sat, looking at almost the same view.

This is the Roman Theatre of Verona. It sits on the left bank of the river, cut into the slope of the San Pietro hill. Most visitors to Verona go first to the Arena, the great amphitheatre in Piazza Bra. Fewer cross the river to this side. That is a small piece of luck for you. Here it is often quiet, and the setting feels more intimate.

The theatre was built at the end of the first century before Christ, in the age of the emperor Augustus. This was the moment when Verona was growing into an important Roman town. It stood on major roads that linked Italy with the Alpine passes and the lands to the north. A town of that rank needed public buildings. A theatre was one of the clearest signs of Roman city life.

It helps to understand the difference between this place and the Arena. An amphitheatre, like the Arena, is a full oval. It was built for gladiator fights and spectacles with animals. A theatre is a half circle. It was built for plays, music, and recitation. Here, Veronese citizens came to watch comedies and tragedies, and to hear the spoken word carry across the stone.

The Roman builders used the hill itself. Instead of raising the whole structure on arches, they set the curved rows of seats into the slope. Above the seats, terraces climbed even higher up the hillside. From the river, the effect must have been dramatic. The whole hill became a single stage set of stone, with the stage building facing the water and the city beyond.

Now for the first surprise. For many centuries, this theatre almost disappeared. After the Roman world faded, people took its stone for other buildings. Floods from the Adige covered parts of it. Houses, gardens, and small streets were built directly over the ruins. By the early modern period, a person could walk through this neighbourhood and not know a Roman theatre lay beneath their feet.

The theatre came back to light in the nineteenth century. A local citizen named Andrea Monga bought up many of the houses that covered the site. He then had them cleared, and excavation began. It was a private passion that became a public treasure. Later work by the city continued what he started. What you see today is the result of that long effort of uncovering.

Look up and to one side of the seating. You will see a small church sitting right on top of the ancient structure. This is the church of Saints Siro and Libera. Its origins go back to the early medieval period, and it was changed and decorated in later centuries. It is a strange and lovely sight. A Christian church rests on the bones of a pagan theatre, and neither seems out of place.

Here is the second surprise. Above the theatre, reached by a lift and by stairs, there is a museum. It occupies a former monastery, once home to a religious community. Today it holds the city's archaeological collection. You can find Roman sculpture, mosaic floors, inscriptions, and everyday objects there. Many visitors leave without ever going up. That is a mistake, because the climb also brings you to some of the best views in Verona.

From the museum's small cloister and terraces, the city opens below you. You can follow the line of the river as it bends around the old centre. You can pick out church towers and the shape of the medieval streets. Just downstream sits the Ponte Pietra, the old stone bridge. It is the river crossing that Romans used to reach this side of town.

That bridge has its own story. Retreating German troops blew it up at the end of the Second World War, in 1945. Afterwards, people recovered many of the original stones from the riverbed. In the 1950s, the bridge was rebuilt using those same pieces wherever possible. When you look at it, you are looking at ancient stone put back into place by careful hands.

The theatre is not only a ruin. It is still used for performance. In summer, the city holds a theatre festival here, and the steps fill with people once again. Plays by Shakespeare appear often in the programme. That is fitting in a city he chose as the setting for Romeo and Juliet and The Two Gentlemen of Verona. Watching a play here after dark, with the river below, connects you directly to the theatre's first purpose.

As you walk around the site, pay attention to the stone. Some blocks are large, square, and clearly Roman. Others are smaller and rougher, from later repairs. In places, you can see where medieval and modern walls meet the ancient ones. The site is a layered thing. Every century has left something, and every century has taken something away.

Before you leave, sit for one more minute on the steps. Look across the river at the city, and down at the curve of the seating around you. Notice the voices of other visitors, how they carry, even when people speak softly. That is the theatre still doing its work. As you walk back toward the bridge, turn once and look up at the hillside... the church, the terraces, the old stone, all stacked together above the water.
