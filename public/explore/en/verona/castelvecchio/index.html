The guide

The story

Run your hand along the brick at the river gate. It is rough, warm in the afternoon sun, and the colour of dried clay. Below you, the Adige moves fast and green, pulling past the piers of the bridge. The water is loud here. It fills the arches with a steady rushing sound, and over it you may hear footsteps on the bridge above. This is Castelvecchio, the old castle of Verona, and it was built to watch this river.

The castle was raised in the mid-fourteenth century by Cangrande the Second, a lord of the della Scala family. The Scaligeri, as Italians call them, ruled Verona for much of that century. Their power was real, but it was never fully secure. Rivals plotted outside the city, and enemies plotted inside it. So this fortress did two jobs. It defended Verona from attack, and it protected the ruling family from the people they ruled.

That second job explains the bridge. The Scaliger Bridge runs from the castle straight across the Adige, and it belonged to the fortress alone. Historians often describe it as an escape route. If the city rose against its lord, he could cross the river and ride north toward the Alps, toward allies beyond the mountains. Look at the walls of the bridge as you walk it. They are high and topped with battlements, built for soldiers, not for strolling families.

Now look up at those battlements. Each one ends in a split shape, like the tail of a swallow. This form is often linked to the Ghibellines — the medieval faction that supported the Holy Roman Emperor against the Pope. Square tops are often linked to their rivals, the Guelphs. The rule was not strict everywhere, but here the forked shapes are a clear signature. They tell you which side of that old argument Verona's lords chose.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The name Castelvecchio means simply "old castle". It was not always old. At first it carried the name of a nearby church, San Martino in Aquaro. Later, after the Scaligeri fell and the Visconti family of Milan took the city, a new fortress rose on the hill of San Pietro across the river. Only then did this one become the old castle. The name is a comparison, frozen in time.

The centuries that followed were not gentle. Venice ruled Verona for a long period and used the castle for military purposes. At the end of the eighteenth century, French troops under Napoleon arrived, and the fortress became a barracks. The French built a long, plain wing across the courtyard to house soldiers. You can still see it today. Its regular windows and calm façade look very different from the rough medieval towers around it.

Then came the darkest moment. In 1945, near the end of the Second World War, retreating German forces blew up the bridges of Verona, and the Scaliger Bridge fell into the river. After the war, the city decided to rebuild it. Workers recovered bricks and stones from the riverbed and put them back where they could. So when you cross the bridge now, much of what you touch is original material, lifted out of the Adige and set back in place.

In the twentieth century, the castle became a museum. Its most important transformation came in the late 1950s and 1960s, under the Venetian architect Carlo Scarpa. Scarpa did not try to make the building look new, and he did not pretend it was untouched. Instead, he peeled back layers. He removed some later additions, exposed old walls, and placed modern materials beside medieval ones — steel, concrete, polished plaster, and dark wood — so the different eras stay visible.

The best example waits in the courtyard area, near the junction of the old walls. Here stands the equestrian statue of Cangrande the First, the most famous of the Scaligeri. The original statue once crowned his tomb in the centre of the city. Scarpa placed it high on a concrete support, so it seems to float in open air. The knight wears a strange grin under his helmet, and his horse wears a draped cloth. Walk around it and see it from several levels — Scarpa designed those views on purpose.

Inside, the museum rooms follow one another through the old structure. You will find medieval sculpture, much of it from Verona's churches, with saints carved in soft stone. Further on, the paintings begin. The collection includes works by artists of the Veneto region, and by famous names such as Pisanello, Mantegna, Giovanni Bellini, Paolo Veronese, and Tintoretto. Notice how Scarpa hung many paintings on easels or brackets, away from the walls, so you can approach them almost like objects.

The museum has its own modern drama too. In November 2015, armed thieves stole seventeen paintings from these galleries in a single evening. The theft shocked Italy. The following year, the paintings were found in Ukraine and later returned to Verona. Today they hang here again. As you pass the canvases, it is worth remembering that some of them made a long and secret journey before they came home.

Take a moment on the walkways along the walls, if they are open during your visit. From up here you see the river curve, the red roofs of the city, and the hills to the north. The towers around you are made of brick laid in bands, sometimes mixed with river stone. Some patches are clearly repairs from different centuries. The walls carry many hands — medieval builders, Venetian engineers, French soldiers, and post-war masons.

Outside the castle, the neighbourhood is calm compared with the crowds near the Roman arena. Corso Cavour leads away toward the centre, lined with old palaces. Along the river, locals walk dogs, cyclists pass, and in the evening the light turns the brick from orange to deep rose. The bridge is one of the best places in Verona to watch that change. Stand at the middle and look back at the castle as the colour slowly fades.

Before you leave, pause once more by the water. Listen to the Adige under the arches, the same sound the guards heard almost seven centuries ago. Look at the forked battlements against the sky, and at the bricks that were pulled from the river and put back. This place has been fortress, barracks, ruin, and museum. Right now, it is simply a quiet stretch of old wall, with the river running beside it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross the Scaliger Bridge at sunset for the warmest brick colours.
Walk around Cangrande's statue to see it from several levels.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven stone floors and wall walkways.
Photograph the castle from the opposite riverbank for the full view.
Combine your visit with a stroll along Corso Cavour.
