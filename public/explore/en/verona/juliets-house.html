The guide

The story

A brick archway on Via Cappello leads into a small courtyard. Voices drop. Cameras rise. Ivy climbs the old walls, and on one of them a stone balcony waits, just high enough for a scene. Below it stands a bronze girl with a bright polish on her chest. Welcome to Juliet's House, the most visited courtyard in Verona.

The house itself is real and old. It dates from the thirteenth century, and for generations it belonged to the Dal Cappello family. Their emblem, a carved stone hat, still marks the inner side of the courtyard arch. The family name sounds close to Capuleti, the Italian name of Shakespeare's Capulets. That echo was enough to tie the house to the story forever.

The story came before Shakespeare. In the 1520s the Italian writer Luigi da Porto set down the tale of two young lovers from feuding Verona families, Romeo and Giulietta. He placed the events in the days of the city's medieval lords, around the year 1300. Other writers retold the tale across Europe. In the 1590s Shakespeare turned it into the play the world knows. He never saw Verona. He did not need to.

For centuries the house was simply a house, and for a time an inn. In 1905 the city of Verona bought it. In the 1930s the museum director Antonio Avena restored it with a romantic hand, giving the facade its medieval look of brick and stone. The famous balcony was added in those years, built from carved stone pieces, some of them taken from an old sarcophagus. It is honest to say it plainly: Juliet's balcony is a twentieth-century creation.

And yet, stand in the courtyard when someone steps out onto it. Friends call up from below. Strangers smile and lift their phones. The scene repeats hundreds of times a day, in every language, and it never quite loses its charm. Fiction built this stage, and real feelings perform on it from morning to night.

The bronze statue of Juliet was made by the sculptor Nereo Costantini and placed in the courtyard in 1972. A tradition grew around her: touch her right breast, and you will be lucky in love. Millions of hands followed the advice. The original statue, worn thin by all that affection, moved inside the museum in 2014. The Juliet standing in the courtyard today is a faithful copy, and she is already polished bright in the same spot.

Look at the walls of the entrance passage. For decades, lovers covered them with notes, names, and letters to Juliet. The city now protects the walls and asks visitors to leave their messages in other ways. Real letters still arrive from around the world, some addressed simply to Juliet, Verona. A group of volunteers known as the Juliet Club reads them and answers every one.

Inside, the museum spreads over several floors of the old house. You walk through rooms with Renaissance-era furniture, ceramics, and frescoes that suggest how a wealthy Veronese family once lived. Film lovers pause at two pieces above all: the bed and the costumes from Franco Zeffirelli's film of Romeo and Juliet, made in 1968.

From the upper floors you can step onto the balcony yourself. It takes a ticket, and often a queue, but many visitors find the moment worth the wait. You look down on the crowd, someone waves, someone laughs, and for a few seconds the old play casts you in the leading role.

Was there ever a real Juliet? Historians find no trace of her. Yet the quarrels of Verona's medieval clans were real enough. Dante, writing in the early fourteenth century, already named the Montecchi and the Cappelletti as feuding families. From such seeds, over three centuries, the legend slowly grew.

The courtyard is free to enter, and it is busiest from late morning until evening. The quietest hour is early morning, just after opening, when the bronze Juliet stands alone, the stones are freshly washed, and the balcony hangs empty over the silence. That is the moment when the little courtyard feels closest to a stage before the play begins.

Perhaps that is the truest thing about this place. People know the story is invented, and they come anyway, because the feelings are not. Somewhere between a made-up balcony and a real letter answered by a stranger, Verona keeps a small flame lit for everyone who has ever loved.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive at opening time to see the courtyard uncrowded
Buy a museum ticket to step onto the balcony
Expect queues at midday; allow about one hour
Leave letters for the Juliet Club instead of marking walls
