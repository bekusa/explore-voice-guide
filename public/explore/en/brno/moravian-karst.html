The guide

The story

Listen for the drip. Somewhere in the dark, water falls from the tip of a stone icicle onto a waiting stone below. It has done this for thousands of years. The air around you is cool and damp, and it smells faintly of wet rock and earth. Above ground, the beech forests rustle. Below, the Moravian Karst keeps its own slow time.

This landscape lies just north of Brno, less than an hour away by car or train. The small town of Blansko is the usual gateway. From the road, you see wooded hills, narrow valleys, and pale grey cliffs. Nothing on the surface hints at what lies underneath. Beneath your feet runs a maze of more than a thousand caves, shafts, and hidden channels. It is one of the largest cave systems in Central Europe.

The word karst describes a special kind of landscape. It forms when rainwater slowly dissolves limestone. Here, that limestone is very old. It began as layers of shells and coral on the floor of a warm, shallow sea, more than three hundred and fifty million years ago. Over time, the sea disappeared and the rock rose. Then water went to work. Drop by drop, it opened cracks, widened them into tunnels, and hollowed the tunnels into great halls.

One of the strangest things here is that rivers vanish. Streams flow across the surface and then simply disappear into the ground. Locals call these sinkholes ponors. The water travels for kilometres in total darkness before it returns to daylight. The Punkva River is the best-known example. It is born underground, from waters that sank into the rock elsewhere, and it only sees the sky near the end of its hidden journey.

That brings us to the most famous sight in the region — the Macocha Abyss. Imagine a giant hole in the forest floor, with sheer walls dropping straight down. It is commonly given as about one hundred and thirty-eight metres deep. At the bottom lie two small green lakes, fed by the Punkva. Moss and ferns cling to the walls. On cool mornings, mist rises from the depths like breath from a great mouth.

The name Macocha means stepmother in Czech, and it comes from a dark folk tale. Legend has it that a cruel stepmother pushed her stepson over the edge. By some miracle, a tree caught him, and woodcutters heard his cries. When the villagers learned the truth, they threw the stepmother into the abyss instead. It is a story, not history. But locals still tell it, and the name has stayed for centuries.

The real exploration of Macocha is a story of courage. In the early twentieth century, the Czech geographer and cave explorer Karel Absolon led expeditions into the Punkva system. His teams crawled, climbed, and waded through freezing water with simple lamps. They mapped passages that no one had seen before. Their work opened the way for the cave tours that visitors enjoy today. Much of what you can walk through now was once a dangerous unknown.

If you visit the Punkva Caves, you will experience something few caves can offer. After walking through chambers full of stalactites and stalagmites, you reach the floor of the Macocha Abyss itself. You look up at a circle of sky far above. Then you step into a small boat. A guide pushes the boat along the underground river, under low ceilings and through quiet, glowing halls. Keep your head down. The rock passes very close.

Punkva is not the only cave open to the public. There are five in total. Catherine's Cave has one huge main hall with remarkable acoustics, and it sometimes hosts concerts. Locals say it is named after a shepherd girl who went looking for a lost sheep and never came back. Balcarka Cave is known for its colourful decorations. The Sloupsko-šošůvské Caves have deep shafts and wide halls. Výpustek has a long human story, including use during the twentieth century.

Here is something many visitors miss. People lived in this landscape long before written history. Caves in the Moravian Karst have produced finds from the Stone Age, including tools and the bones of animals that hunters brought inside. Cave bears also used these spaces. Their remains have been found in several caves here. Standing in the cool dark, it is worth remembering that you are not the first to shelter here. Far from it.

Another cave, Býčí skála, which means Bull Rock, holds one of the region's great mysteries. In the nineteenth century, the physician and archaeologist Heinrich Wankel excavated it. He found evidence of a prehistoric burial site, with many human remains, objects of bronze and iron, and signs of ritual. Scholars still debate exactly what happened there. Today the cave is not part of the regular tourist route, but its finds have shaped how we understand ancient Moravia.

The Moravian Karst is a protected landscape area, and the protection matters. The caves are fragile. A single touch can leave oils on a formation and stop its growth. Some stalactites grow only a tiny fraction of a millimetre each year. Inside, the temperature stays cool all year, so bring a warm layer even in summer. Above ground, marked trails lead through the forests, past the rim of Macocha, and along quiet valleys where you can walk for hours.

When you come back up into the daylight, pause for a moment. Notice how bright the green looks, and how warm the air feels on your skin. Listen to the birds and the wind in the beech trees. Somewhere beneath you, the Punkva is still moving through the dark, and water is still dripping, slowly building the next stone column. The landscape is not finished. It is still being made, one drop at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book Punkva Caves tickets in advance during busy seasons.
Bring a warm layer; caves stay cool all year.
Wear shoes with good grip for wet cave floors.
Visit the upper Macocha viewpoint after your cave tour.
Use Blansko as your base for reaching the caves.
