The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the gates, a kookaburra starts its rolling laugh, and another answers it. The air smells of eucalyptus, warm earth, and sunscreen. Under your feet, the paths are wide and the light comes down through tall gum trees in pale green patches. Families in hats move past you, and a keeper in a khaki shirt walks by with a bucket. Welcome to Australia Zoo, in Beerwah, in the green hinterland of Queensland's Sunshine Coast.

Before we go further, look at the shirt that keeper is wearing. That khaki colour is not just a uniform. Around the world it became the signature of one man, Steve Irwin, known to millions as the Crocodile Hunter. He was famous for his enthusiasm, his shorts, and one word shouted with delight... Crikey. But the story of this place starts before Steve was famous, with his parents, and with a much smaller dream.

In 1970, Bob and Lyn Irwin opened a small wildlife park here, known as the Beerwah Reptile Park. It was a modest operation on a modest block of land. Bob was a passionate herpetologist, which simply means a person who studies reptiles and amphibians. Lyn had a deep gift for caring for injured and orphaned animals. Their son Steve grew up among snakes, lizards, and crocodiles. For him, this was not a workplace. It was his childhood backyard.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. As a young man, Steve worked with his father catching problem crocodiles in north Queensland. These were animals that lived too close to people and might otherwise have been shot. The Irwins captured them and relocated them instead. That simple idea, that a dangerous animal could be moved rather than killed, sits at the heart of everything you will see today. Conservation here began as practical, muddy, hands-on work.

In the early 1990s, Steve and his wife Terri took over the park. Terri had come from the United States, and the two met here. Soon the park took a new name — Australia Zoo. At around the same time, their documentary series, The Crocodile Hunter, began to reach television screens across the world. Suddenly a small reptile park in the Queensland bush had an audience in dozens of countries. Visitors began to arrive from very far away.

Now turn your attention to the biggest structure on site, the Crocoseum. It is a purpose-built stadium, and its name blends the words crocodile and Colosseum. Tiered seating rises around a central arena and a pool. Here, during the Wildlife Warriors show, keepers demonstrate how crocodiles strike and feed. Birds often swoop low over the crowd. Pay attention to the moment the keepers fall quiet. A crocodile can stay still for a very long time... and then move with frightening speed.

The zoo has grown far beyond reptiles. As you walk, you will pass koalas sleeping in the forks of branches, and kangaroos resting in the shade. You may see wombats, echidnas, and Tasmanian devils, animals many overseas visitors have never seen before. Beyond the Australian sections, there are animals from other continents too, including tigers and an African savannah area. Signs along the paths name each animal. Read them slowly, because many carry stories of individual animals.

In 2006, the story took a heartbreaking turn. Steve Irwin was killed by a stingray while filming on the Great Barrier Reef. The news shocked people around the world. Many expected the zoo to fade without him. It did not. Terri, together with their children Bindi and Robert, chose to carry the work forward. Today, both children are well known in their own right, and they still appear at the zoo and in its shows.

Here is the second surprise. Not every animal here is on display. Close to the zoo stands the Australia Zoo Wildlife Hospital, which treats sick, injured, and orphaned native wildlife. Koalas hit by cars, birds tangled in fishing line, turtles, and possums all arrive here. The hospital treats thousands of animals each year. The goal is always the same — to heal them and release them back into the wild. It is Lyn Irwin's gift for care, grown into a modern clinic.

In some areas you can watch part of this work through viewing windows. Vets and nurses move quietly between treatment rooms. It is a calmer space than the stadium, and it carries a different kind of drama. A tiny joey wrapped in a cloth pouch. A koala with a bandaged paw. Small moments like these show why so many people support the conservation charity that the family founded, called Wildlife Warriors.

Now lift your eyes beyond the trees. On a clear day, you may catch sight of the Glass House Mountains rising from the plain nearby. These are steep volcanic peaks, the remains of ancient volcanoes, and one of the tallest is called Mount Beerwah. They are deeply important to the local Aboriginal peoples, the traditional custodians of this land. The road that brought you here also carries a name worth noticing. It is called Steve Irwin Way.

As you continue, take your time. The zoo is large, and the Queensland sun can be strong, even in the cooler months. Stop at the shaded benches. Watch how the keepers talk with visitors. They answer questions with patience, and many of them have worked here for years. Children often stand with their faces close to the glass of the reptile houses, waiting for a snake to move. Sometimes the waiting is the best part.

Before you leave, pause for a moment. Listen again to the birds in the gum trees, the splash from a pool, the laughter from a nearby path. What began as a small reptile park run by one family has become a place where millions of people meet animals face to face. As you walk on, notice the khaki shirts around you. Each one carries a small part of that first idea — that wild animals are worth protecting, one at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the daily show schedule as soon as you arrive.
Reach the Crocoseum early for good seats near the arena.
Bring a hat, water, and sunscreen for the open paths.
Wear comfortable shoes; the grounds cover a large area.
Plan extra time for the wildlife hospital viewing areas.
