The guide

The story

The first thing many visitors notice is the quiet. You walk down a long, curving ramp, and the sounds of the city above slowly fall away. The light softens. Your footsteps echo a little on the hard floor. By the time you reach the bottom, the traffic and the birds and the voices outside feel very far off. This is how the Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Museum asks you to arrive. It does not ask you to hurry.

You are standing in the Urakami district, in the northern part of the city. On the morning of August 9, 1945, this valley was a working neighbourhood. It had factories, schools, homes, and one of the largest Catholic communities in Japan. At 11:02 that morning, an American bomber released a single plutonium bomb over this area. It exploded in the air, a short walk from where you are now.

Here is something many visitors do not know. Nagasaki was not the first choice for that day. The original target was the city of Kokura, further to the north. Clouds and smoke covered Kokura, and the crew could not see their aiming point. So the plane turned toward Nagasaki. Even here, the bomb did not fall on the city centre. It fell over Urakami, a valley away from the old harbour district.

The current museum opened in 1996, around the fiftieth anniversary of the bombing. It replaced an older hall that had held the collection for decades before. The building sits on a slope above the hypocenter, the point on the ground directly below the explosion. Today that point is marked by a simple black monolith in a small park. You can walk there in a few minutes after your visit.

Inside, one of the first objects is a clock. Its hands stopped at 11:02. It is a small, ordinary object, the kind that hung in many homes. That is exactly why it holds people in place. Nearby are photographs of the valley before the bombing — rooftops, streets, trees on the hillsides. Then come the photographs taken after. The contrast does not need any explanation.

Take your time with the objects in the cases. You will see glass bottles that melted and fused together in the heat. You will see roof tiles with a rough, bubbled surface, because the heat boiled the top layer of the clay. There are coins stuck together, scorched clothing, and twisted metal from factory frames. Each piece is small. Together, they show what happened to an entire neighbourhood in a few seconds.

One large display recreates part of a wall from Urakami Cathedral. The cathedral stood on a hill not far from the hypocenter. It was destroyed in the blast, and many people inside were killed. The original church was built by the local Catholic community, whose ancestors had practised their faith in secret during centuries of persecution. The cathedral you can visit today was rebuilt after the war, but stone statues damaged by the blast still stand outside it.

The museum does not only show buildings and objects. It also tells the stories of people. Survivors in Japan are called hibakusha, a word that means people affected by the bomb. Their testimonies appear in text, in photographs, and in recorded video. Some describe the flash and the heat. Others describe the weeks and years afterwards — the illness, the loss of family, and the long effort to rebuild ordinary life.

The human cost was enormous. By the end of 1945, the number of dead in Nagasaki is commonly estimated at around seventy thousand people. Many more were injured. Radiation caused illnesses that appeared months and even years later. The museum explains this carefully, with diagrams of the heat, the blast wave, and the radiation. It is clear and direct, and it does not soften the facts.

Later rooms widen the view. They explain the path that led to the bombing, the course of the war, and the development of nuclear weapons. The final sections look at the world after 1945 — nuclear testing, the arms race, and the long international effort toward disarmament. Nagasaki has made a clear wish for itself: it hopes to be the last city ever to suffer an atomic bombing. That idea runs quietly through the whole museum.

Next door is a separate building, the Nagasaki National Peace Memorial Hall for the Atomic Bomb Victims. Many visitors miss it. It is calm and very simple. Inside, there is a space dedicated to the victims, with the names of those who died recorded and kept there. A pool of water sits above it. In the evening, small lights appear in the water. The hall is a place for remembrance rather than explanation.

When you step outside, look around at the city. The valley that was flattened is full of life again. There are apartment blocks, schools, tram lines, and small shops. A short walk north is the Peace Park, with its large seated statue. One raised hand points toward the sky, toward the threat from above. The other arm stretches out to the side, a gesture of peace. Every year on August 9, a ceremony takes place there.

You will probably see strings of folded paper cranes around these sites. School groups from across Japan bring them, and so do visitors from abroad. In Japan, the crane is a symbol of long life and hope, and folding a thousand of them is linked to a wish for recovery. The colours are bright against the grey stone. They are a small sign that people still come here, and still care.

Before you leave, pause for a moment where you are. Listen to the city around you — a tram bell, a car passing, perhaps children on their way home from school. All of this exists in the same valley where the clock stopped. As you walk back up toward the street, notice the ordinary things: a window, a tree, a person waiting at a crossing. Here, ordinary life has a particular weight.
