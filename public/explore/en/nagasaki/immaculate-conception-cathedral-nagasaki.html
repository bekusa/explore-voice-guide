The guide

The story

Stand still at the foot of the hill and listen. Traffic hums along the valley road below, but up here the sound softens. Red brick rises in front of you, warm in tone and finished with pale stone trim. Two square towers frame the entrance. On a quiet morning you may hear a bell, a deep and steady note that moves across the rooftops of Urakami. That sound carries more weight here than almost anywhere else on Earth.

This is the Immaculate Conception Cathedral, known locally as Urakami Cathedral, or Urakami Tenshudo. It is the seat of the Catholic Archdiocese of Nagasaki. To understand why it stands here, in this particular valley, you need to go back several centuries. You need to begin with people who kept a faith in secret for more than two hundred years.

Christianity reached this part of Japan in the sixteenth century, carried by Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries. For a time it spread quickly around Nagasaki. Then the rulers of Japan turned against it. In the early seventeenth century the faith was banned. Churches were destroyed, priests were expelled or killed, and believers were forced to renounce their religion or face death.

Here is the first thing many visitors never hear. In villages like Urakami, families did not give up. They became what historians call Hidden Christians. They prayed in private, passed prayers down by memory, and disguised images of Mary as Buddhist figures. For generations they lived under a system that registered every household at a Buddhist temple. Officials sometimes made villagers step on sacred images to prove they were not Christian. Many stepped, and quietly asked forgiveness afterwards.

In the 1860s, after Japan reopened to the outside world, a group of Urakami villagers revealed their faith to a French priest in Nagasaki. The response from the government was harsh. Thousands of Urakami Christians were arrested and sent into exile across Japan, and many died far from home. Only in 1873, under foreign pressure, did the government lift the ban. The survivors returned to their burned fields and empty houses.

Those same families decided to build a church. Construction began in the 1890s. It was slow, patient work, carried out largely by the parishioners themselves, who gave money and labour over many years. The building was dedicated in 1914, and the two bell towers were added in the 1920s. When it was finished, it was described as the largest Christian church in East Asia. Fittingly, it stood on land connected to the old village headman's office — the place where suspected Christians had once been interrogated.

Then came the morning of 9 August 1945. At 11:02, an atomic bomb exploded over the Urakami valley. The hypocentre lay only about half a kilometre from the cathedral. The building collapsed and burned. The priests and parishioners inside at the time were killed. Around the valley, a very large part of the Catholic community of Urakami died that day or in the months that followed. A faith that had survived centuries of persecution was nearly erased in a single moment.

Walk around the building now, to its north side, and look down the slope toward the small stream. Here is the second surprise. A huge piece of one of the original bell towers still lies where it fell in 1945. It is a dark, heavy mass of brick and stone, half buried in the bank. Nobody moved it. It remains as a silent record of the force that struck this place. Many visitors walk past the front steps and never see it.

Back at the front, look closely at the stone statues near the entrance. Some are saints and angels from the old cathedral. Their surfaces are blackened and pitted by heat. Some have lost hands, or noses, or parts of their faces. They were salvaged from the ruins and placed here deliberately. They are not decoration. They are witnesses.

One survivor matters more than the rest. In the rubble, a wooden statue of the Virgin Mary was found, or rather what was left of it — only the head. The face is scorched dark, and the glass eyes are gone, leaving two empty hollows. Today it is known as the Bombed Mary, or Hibaku Maria. It is kept in a small side chapel. People come from around the world to stand in front of it in silence.

The bell you may hear also has a story. One of the cathedral bells was dug out of the ruins after the war. Local parishioners hung it again, and it rang at Christmas in 1945, over a city still in ashes. A Catholic doctor from this neighbourhood, Takashi Nagai, wrote about that sound in his book The Bells of Nagasaki. He was himself injured by the bomb and spent his final years in a tiny hut nearby, writing about peace.

The church you see now is not the original. A new cathedral was built in concrete in the late 1950s. Around 1980, before Pope John Paul II visited Nagasaki, the outside was covered in red brick to echo the lost building. Inside, the space is calm and simple. Light falls through coloured glass onto rows of wooden pews. The congregation here includes descendants of the Hidden Christians, and Mass is still held regularly. Please remember that this is a working church, not a museum.

Before you leave, turn and look back down the valley. From this hill you can trace the route toward the Hypocentre Park and the Peace Park beyond. A fragment of the old cathedral wall was moved to stand near the hypocentre, so the two places remain linked. As you walk away, listen once more for the bell. Notice the red brick behind you, and the quiet people climbing the steps. This valley has seen exile, return, destruction, and rebuilding. Today, it simply carries on.
