The guide

The story

Before you see anything, you smell it. Sandalwood smoke drifts down the stone steps, thick and sweet, catching in the back of your throat. Somewhere above you, a bell rings once. Under your feet, the paving is worn smooth by centuries of shoes and sandals. Pause here at the gate for a moment. Let your eyes follow the grey granite up the slope of the hill, where small roofs with curved eaves sit one above another among the trees.

This is A-Ma Temple, one of the oldest temples in Macau. It sits at the foot of Barra Hill, on the southwestern edge of the peninsula, facing the water. For centuries, this shoreline was the first place many sailors touched after a long voyage. The temple is dedicated to Mazu, the goddess of seafarers and fishermen, known here by the affectionate name A-Ma. People across southern China and Taiwan still pray to her for safe passage at sea.

The story of the goddess begins with a real person, according to tradition. Mazu is said to have been a young woman from Fujian province, on China's southeastern coast. Stories say she could predict storms and rescue sailors in trouble. After her death, coastal communities began to worship her. Over many generations, her cult travelled with Chinese fishermen and merchants along the coast. Wherever they settled, they built a shrine to her near the harbour. This hillside became one of those places.

Locals tell a famous legend about this exact spot. A poor girl once asked the large fishing junks for passage across the sea. Every wealthy captain refused her. Only one humble fisherman agreed to take her aboard. On the way, a terrible storm destroyed the other boats. His small boat, however, arrived safely here. The girl stepped ashore, climbed the hill, and vanished in a glow of light. The fishermen understood that she was A-Ma herself, and they built a temple on the place where she landed.

Now comes a surprise that many visitors miss. Look at the name of the city itself. When Portuguese traders arrived in the sixteenth century, they came ashore near this temple. According to a popular account, they asked local people for the name of the place. The answer was something like A-Ma-Gau, meaning the bay of A-Ma. The Portuguese heard Macau. Historians debate the details, but the link between the goddess and the city's name is widely accepted.

So the temple almost certainly existed before the Portuguese arrived. That makes it older than the churches, forts and pastel colonial buildings that most people associate with Macau. When you walk the rest of the old city later, keep this in mind. The Chinese fishing community and its goddess were here first. The European city grew up around a place that already had a sacred centre on this hill.

As you climb, you will notice that the temple is not one single building. It is a group of small halls and pavilions set at different heights on the hillside. They are connected by narrow paths, stone stairs and terraces. Each hall was added at a different time. The result feels less like a grand monument and more like a village of shrines. You discover it slowly, one level at a time, with new views of the harbour opening behind you.

The halls do not all serve the same faith. Some honour A-Ma. One is dedicated to Guanyin, the Buddhist figure of compassion, often shown as a calm woman in white robes. Higher up, a Buddhist pavilion stands among the rocks. This mixture is normal in Chinese popular religion. Taoist, Buddhist and folk traditions often share one sacred space. Worshippers move from altar to altar, lighting incense at each one, without any sense of contradiction.

Look up as you enter the halls. Large spirals of incense hang from the ceilings like coiled springs. Each coil can burn for many hours, even days. Many carry a small red tag with a family's name or a prayer written on it. The smoke rises slowly and darkens the wooden beams above. Below, people kneel on cushions and bow three times with sticks of incense held at the forehead. Please move quietly around them. This is a working temple, not a museum.

Next, find the rocks. Large granite boulders rise out of the hillside between the buildings. Many are carved with Chinese characters, painted red. These are poems and inscriptions left by visitors and scholars over many generations. One rock near the entrance carries a carving of a traditional sailing junk. It recalls the fishing boat from the legend. Sailors once saw it as a symbol of protection. For you, it is a small, easy detail to miss.

In 2005, this temple became part of the Historic Centre of Macau, listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. The listing covers a chain of buildings across the old city, both Chinese and Portuguese. A-Ma Temple sits at one end of that chain. The listing recognises Macau as a place where Chinese and European cultures lived side by side for more than four centuries. This hillside is where that long meeting began.

The temple is busiest during festivals. At Lunar New Year, crowds arrive at midnight to place the first incense of the year. Firecrackers echo off the rocks, and the air fills with red paper and smoke. The goddess's birthday, celebrated in spring on the lunar calendar, brings Cantonese opera and offerings of fruit and roast pork. On an ordinary weekday morning, though, the mood is gentle. You may share the halls with only a few elderly worshippers.

When you are ready, walk back down the steps to the square below. Notice the Portuguese paving there, laid in black and white stone waves that echo the sea. Across the square, the low hills meet the water, the same water the fishermen once crossed. Turn around once more. The smoke is still rising from the roofs above you. Listen for the bell. It has been calling sailors home to this shore for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early on weekdays for a quieter visit.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven stone hillside steps.
Ask permission before photographing people at prayer.
Combine your visit with a walk around Barra Square.
Expect large crowds and smoke during Lunar New Year.
