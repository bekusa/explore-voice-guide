The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your feet, and somewhere in the trees a rook calls across the parkland. Ahead of you, at the end of a long straight avenue, a cluster of pink-grey towers rises out of the green. Conical roofs. Small round turrets. Tall chimneys. From a distance it looks less like a fortress and more like a castle from a children's book. Take a moment here. That first view is part of the design, and the people who built it wanted you to feel exactly this.

This is Glamis Castle, a short drive southwest of Forfar, in the Scottish county of Angus. Glamis is pronounced "Glahms", with a single soft syllable. Around you lies the broad valley known as Strathmore. It is good farming country, with rich red earth, wide fields and the hills rising to the north. That landscape matters. It is the reason a powerful family settled here, and the reason they stayed for more than six hundred years.

That family is the Lyons, later the Bowes-Lyons. In the late fourteenth century, King Robert the Second of Scotland granted the lands of Glamis to Sir John Lyon. Sir John was a trusted royal official, and he married into the royal family itself. His descendants became Lords Glamis, and later the Earls of Strathmore and Kinghorne. The remarkable thing is continuity. The same family still owns the castle today. Very few houses in Britain can say that.

Now look at the building itself. At its heart is a tall medieval tower house. That was the standard Scottish home for a powerful family in uncertain times: thick stone walls, narrow windows, and a living space stacked vertically for defence. But the castle you see is not purely medieval. In the seventeenth century, Patrick Lyon, the first Earl of Strathmore, remodelled the old tower. He added the turrets, the decorative rooflines and the grand approach. He turned a defensive stronghold into a statement of status and taste.

This is the first surprise for many visitors. Those fairy-tale turrets were never really about war. They carry the influence of French chateaux, the great country houses of France, which Scotland admired through its long alliance with that country. By the time the Earl was building, gunpowder had made tall stone towers far less useful in battle. So the turrets were fashion, not defence. They were a way of saying: this family is old, rich and cultured.

And then there is Shakespeare. In the play Macbeth, the witches greet Macbeth as "Thane of Glamis", a thane being a local lord in early Scotland. Because of that line, Glamis is forever linked with the Scottish king and his bloody ambition. But here is the second surprise. The real Macbeth lived in the eleventh century, long before this castle existed, and there is no evidence he ever had anything to do with this spot. The connection comes from the play, not from history.

The castle does lean into the story, though. Inside, one of the oldest rooms is known as Duncan's Hall. It is named for King Duncan, whose murder drives the plot of Macbeth. Tradition likes to place that killing here. Historians, however, believe the real Duncan died in battle elsewhere in Scotland. So when you stand in that hall, you are standing inside a legend rather than a crime scene. Still, the stone, the low light and the heavy walls make it easy to understand why the story stuck.

Glamis has collected more ghost stories than almost any house in Scotland. Locals tell of a Grey Lady who walks the chapel. She is often linked to Janet Douglas, Lady Glamis, who was burned as a witch in Edinburgh in 1537. Her conviction is generally seen as the result of King James the Fifth's hostility towards her family, not any real crime. There is also the tale of Earl Beardie. According to legend, he played cards with the devil late one night, and he still plays on, somewhere behind the walls.

The most famous legend is the secret room. One popular tale says that a hidden chamber exists within the walls, and that only the heir was told its secret. Some versions add a monster, others a family tragedy. None of this has ever been proven. But the thick medieval masonry certainly leaves space for the imagination. A story often told is that guests once hung towels from every window to find a window they could not account for.

Now to the twentieth century, and a quieter kind of history. Elizabeth Bowes-Lyon, a daughter of the Earl of Strathmore, spent much of her childhood here. During the First World War, the castle served as a convalescent home for wounded soldiers, and the young Elizabeth helped to look after them. Years later she married the future King George the Sixth, and the British public came to know her as the Queen Mother. Her younger daughter, Princess Margaret, was born here at Glamis in 1930.

That royal link explains much of what you will see on a tour. The rooms are not museum spaces stripped bare. They carry family portraits, furniture, armour and personal objects collected over centuries. Look out for the chapel, with its painted ceiling and wall panels from the seventeenth century, showing saints and scenes from the Bible. It is a small, intimate room, and the colours feel surprisingly warm after the grey stone of the stairs.

When you step back outside, give the grounds some time. The gardens and parkland stretch around the house, with old trees, walled planting and long walks. Near the castle stands a tall carved stone sundial from the seventeenth century, covered in many small faces that catch the sun at different angles. It is easy to walk past. Stop and look closely. It is one of the finest pieces of craftsmanship on the estate, and a reminder that the Earl's ambitions reached far beyond the walls.

The village of Glamis sits just beyond the estate gates. It is small and quiet, with stone cottages and a parish church. If you have time, a short wander there gives you a sense of how the castle and the community have lived side by side for centuries. The farms, the woods and the houses all grew up around this one family seat.

Before you leave, walk a little way back down the avenue and turn around. Watch how the towers line up at the end of the trees. Notice the colour of the sandstone as the clouds move, shifting from pink to grey and back again. Somewhere behind those windows, the family still lives. The story here is not finished. You are simply visiting one chapter of it.
