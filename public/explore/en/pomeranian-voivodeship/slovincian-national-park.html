The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wind there is a soft hiss, almost like rain. It is sand, millions of grains sliding over each other, moving across the ridge in front of you. Feel how the ground gives way under each step, warm on top and cool just below. On a bright day the dunes are so pale that you may need to squint. Ahead, beyond the ridge, the Baltic Sea is a strip of grey-blue. Behind you, a forest is slowly being swallowed.

This is Slovincian National Park, on the Baltic coast of northern Poland, between the small resort town of Łeba and the village of Smołdzino. It was created in 1967 to protect a landscape that has almost no match in Europe. Later it was named a UNESCO biosphere reserve. The park covers a long strip of coastline, wide shallow lakes, peat bogs, meadows and pine woods. But most visitors come for one thing. They come for the sand that does not stay still.

These are moving dunes, sometimes called wandering dunes. The sea brings sand to the shore. The wind, mostly from the west, lifts the dry grains and pushes them inland. Over many years the sand piles up into great ridges and crescents, and each ridge creeps forward. The dunes here can move several metres in a single year. That is not fast enough to see in one afternoon. But it is fast enough to change the map within a single human lifetime.

Now look at the edge of the sand, where it meets the trees. You may see the tops of pine trees sticking out of the dune, as if they were bushes. They are not bushes. They are full-grown trees, buried up to their crowns. On the far side of some dunes, the opposite happens. The sand moves on and leaves behind grey, dead trunks. Here is the first surprise for many visitors — this desert is not the result of drought. It sits right next to lakes, bogs and the open sea.

The best-known of these dunes is Łącka Góra, which means Łącka Mountain. It rises around forty metres above the shore. From the top, on a clear day, you can turn slowly in a full circle. To one side lies the sea. To the other side lies Lake Łebsko, flat and silver. Between them is a thin band of sand that the wind keeps reshaping. Climb slowly. Loose sand makes even a short slope feel twice as long.

The lakes tell their own story. Lake Łebsko and its smaller neighbour, Lake Gardno, were once open bays of the Baltic Sea. Over thousands of years, the waves and currents built a sandbar across their mouths and cut them off from the sea. Today they are shallow lakes of brackish water, a mix of fresh and salt. Lake Łebsko is one of the largest lakes in Poland. Its reedy shores are a resting place for huge numbers of migrating birds.

That brings us to a sound you may hear in spring and autumn. It is the calling of geese, cranes and ducks moving along the coast. The park sits on an important bird migration route. The wetlands here are protected under the Ramsar Convention, an international agreement for the protection of wetlands. Sea eagles nest in the area. If you see a large bird with a pale tail circling over the lake, stop and watch for a while. Many people walk past without looking up.

And now the second surprise. At Rąbka, near the main path from Łeba toward the dunes, the forest hides the remains of a military testing site. In the final years of the Second World War, the German army used this remote stretch of coast to test rockets. The empty sand and the open sea made it easy to fire and to track them. Today you can see concrete foundations and an outdoor exhibition about this period. It is a strange thing to find on the way to a nature walk... a reminder that empty places have often attracted secret work.

The park takes its name from the Slovincians. They were a small Slavic community related to the Kashubians, another Slavic people of the Pomeranian coast. They lived among these lakes and marshes for centuries. They fished, cut reeds, and farmed the poor, sandy soil. Their language and way of life slowly faded under outside pressure, and by the twentieth century very little remained. The name of the park keeps their memory on the map.

You can still see how they lived. In the village of Kluki, on the shore of Lake Łebsko, there is an open-air museum of Slovincian rural life. It is a collection of old farmhouses with low roofs, wooden interiors, and simple gardens. The rooms show how a family lived at the edge of the marsh. If you have time after the dunes, it gives a human face to the landscape you have just crossed.

On the coast to the west stands the Czołpino lighthouse. It rises out of a forested dune, so the tower sits higher than its own height would suggest. For generations it guided ships along a coast with shifting shallows and few safe harbours. On the beach below, the sand stretches on for kilometres with no buildings and no cafés. Only footprints, seaweed, and sometimes small pieces of amber washed up after a storm.

A practical word about getting around. Private cars cannot drive into the heart of the park. Most people start at the entrance near Łeba and walk, cycle, or ride small electric shuttles toward the dunes. In the warmer months, boats also cross Lake Łebsko. Bring water, a hat, and sunglasses. The sand reflects the light, and there is very little shade once you leave the forest. Please stay on marked paths. The plants that hold the dunes in place are fragile.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Look at the ridge in front of you, and remember its shape. The next time someone stands here, it will not be exactly the same. A little sand will have moved forward. A little more of a tree will be hidden, or a little more will be free. As you walk back toward the trees, listen again for that soft hiss under the wind. The landscape is still being made, quietly, around you.
