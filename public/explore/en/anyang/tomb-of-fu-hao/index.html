The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look down. Beneath the low hall in front of you, the earth drops away into a narrow, rectangular pit. The soil here is a fine, pale yellow-brown. It is the loess of the north China plain, and it is soft enough to crumble in your fingers. For more than three thousand years, that quiet ground kept one of the great secrets of early China. Listen to the wind in the trees around the site. Then imagine the silence that lay over this place for thirty centuries.

You are standing at Yinxu, the ruins of the last capital of the Shang dynasty. The site lies on the edge of the modern city of Anyang, in Henan province. The Shang ruled much of the Yellow River valley in the second millennium before the common era. They are the earliest Chinese dynasty confirmed by written records and by archaeology together. Yinxu was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2006. This small tomb is the reason many visitors come here first.

The woman buried here was Fu Hao. She was one of the consorts of King Wu Ding, one of the most powerful Shang rulers. He reigned in the thirteenth century before the common era. Wu Ding had several consorts, but Fu Hao is the one history remembers most clearly. She was not famous only as a royal wife. She was a general, a priestess, and a landholder in her own right. That combination is rare in any ancient record.

How do we know this? The answer comes from oracle bones. The Shang kings asked questions of their ancestors and gods by heating turtle shells and ox shoulder blades until they cracked. Diviners then carved the question and sometimes the answer into the bone. Fu Hao's name appears in many of these inscriptions. The king asks whether she will win a battle. He asks whether her childbirth will go well. He asks whether her illness will pass. Through these short, practical questions, a real person comes into view.

Some of those inscriptions describe Fu Hao leading armies against neighbouring peoples. One is often cited as recording a force of thirteen thousand troops under her command. That would make it one of the largest armies mentioned in the Shang records. She also led rituals and offered sacrifices, a role usually reserved for the highest members of the court. So the woman in this pit was not a quiet figure behind the throne. She stood in the front line, both on the battlefield and at the altar.

Now the first surprise. For decades, archaeologists dug at Yinxu and found the great royal tombs to the north already robbed. Grave robbers had emptied them, some perhaps in ancient times. Then, in 1976, a team led by the archaeologist Zheng Zhenxiang opened this modest pit. It sat not in the main royal cemetery, but close to the palace and temple area. And it was untouched. It remains the only Shang royal tomb ever found fully intact.

The pit itself is surprisingly compact. You might expect a vast underground chamber. Instead, you see a deep, narrow shaft, cut straight down into the loess. A wooden chamber once stood at the bottom, holding a lacquered coffin. The wood has long since decayed. But the objects packed around the coffin survived. Archaeologists recovered close to two thousand of them from this small space.

The numbers are worth hearing slowly. There were more than four hundred bronze objects. There were around seven hundred and fifty pieces of jade. There were objects of bone, ivory, and stone. And there were nearly seven thousand cowrie shells. In Shang times, cowries came from distant coasts and served as a form of wealth. To bury so many with one person was a clear statement of rank.

Many of the bronzes carry Fu Hao's name, cast directly into the metal. Among them are heavy ritual axes. In Shang culture, an axe of this kind was a symbol of military command. Finding them in her grave matches what the oracle bones say about her as a general. Here is the second surprise. Text and object, written record and buried evidence, agree with each other. In ancient history, that kind of match is rare and precious.

The tomb also holds a darker truth. The excavators found the remains of sixteen people buried with Fu Hao, along with several dogs. Human sacrifice was part of Shang royal ritual. Those who served a ruler in life could be sent to serve them in death. It is uncomfortable to think about while standing here. But it is part of the honest picture of this world, and the archaeology does not hide it.

The original treasures are no longer in the pit. Most are now held in museums, including the museum here at Yinxu and the National Museum of China in Beijing. In the pit and the hall above it, you will see careful replicas. Look for the pair of owl-shaped bronze vessels. They stand on two legs and a tail, with round eyes and folded wings. They are among the most loved objects from the tomb. Look too for the jade figures, some of them small kneeling people and animals.

The hall built over the tomb follows the style of Shang architecture, as scholars have reconstructed it. Notice the raised earthen base, the timber posts, and the steep, thatched-looking roof. Shang builders pounded layers of earth into hard platforms, a method still used in rural parts of northern China. Nearby, a statue of Fu Hao stands in the grounds. It is a modern interpretation, of course. No portrait of her survives. But it gives a face to a name that was once only a few carved characters on bone.

Before you move on, look down into the pit once more. Notice how small it is against the open sky above Yinxu. Inside that narrow shaft lay a general, a priestess, and a queen. Her questions to the gods were carved in bone. Her name was cast in bronze. And the soft yellow earth under your feet kept her safe for three thousand years. As you walk away, listen for the crunch of gravel on the paths. It is the same ground she once knew.

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes; the Yinxu grounds are spread out.
