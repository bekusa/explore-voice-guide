The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water moves somewhere close by, slow and heavy. Jelgava Palace stands on low, flat ground between rivers, and the air here often feels damp, even on a bright day. In front of you, long ranks of tall windows run across warm-toned walls. The building does not tower over you. It spreads out wide, calm and confident, like it has always been here. In a way, something has.

This ground has held power for centuries. Long before the palace, a medieval castle stood here. The Livonian Order built it. The Order was a branch of German crusading knights who ruled much of what is now Latvia in the Middle Ages. Their fortress guarded the river crossings, and over time it became the seat of the Dukes of Courland. Courland was a small but ambitious duchy in western Latvia, and Jelgava, then known by its German name, Mitau, was its capital.

The palace you see now begins with one man — Ernst Johann von Biron. His story sounds almost like a novel. Biron rose from modest beginnings to become the favourite of Anna, Empress of Russia. With her support, he became Duke of Courland. A new duke wanted a new home. So he did something bold. He had the old castle of the knights torn down, and he ordered a grand Baroque residence in its place.

For the design, Biron turned to Francesco Bartolomeo Rastrelli. Rastrelli was an Italian-born architect working in Russia. Later he became famous for buildings in Saint Petersburg, including the Winter Palace. Baroque, in simple terms, is a style of drama and grandeur — rhythm, symmetry, and rich decoration meant to impress. Construction here started in the late 1730s. Here is the first surprise. Rastrelli also designed Rundāle Palace, not far from here, for the same duke. Biron was building two palaces at once.

Then everything stopped. In 1740, Empress Anna died. Without her protection, Biron fell almost overnight. He was arrested and sent into exile deep inside Russia. The half-built palace stood empty and unfinished. For more than two decades, scaffolding and silence replaced the plans for ballrooms and grand halls. It is a reminder that in eighteenth-century courts, a fortune could vanish with a single death.

Biron's luck turned again. In the 1760s, under a new Russian ruler, he was allowed to return to Courland as duke. Work on the palace began once more. Rastrelli came back to continue the project, and the building was finally completed around the early 1770s. By then, tastes had started to shift. Some of the interiors were finished in a lighter, more restrained style than the first designs had planned.

Now look at the shape of the building. It is arranged around a large courtyard, with long wings framing the space. The design gives a strong sense of order. Windows repeat in steady rows. White details stand out against the coloured walls, and the eye moves easily from one end to the other. This palace is often described as the largest Baroque palace in the Baltic states. Standing here, that size feels less like height and more like width.

The second surprise is a French one. In the late 1790s, the duchy had already become part of the Russian Empire. The Russian emperor offered this palace as a refuge to an exiled French royal. He was the man who would later become King Louis the Eighteenth. He was the brother of Louis the Sixteenth, who died in the French Revolution. For several years, a small court in exile lived inside these walls, far from Paris, waiting for history to change.

The twentieth century was hard on the palace. In 1919, during the fighting that followed the First World War, the building was set on fire, and the interiors were badly damaged. It was restored, and then the Second World War brought new destruction in 1944. Much of the original decoration inside was lost. What you see from the outside today is the result of careful rebuilding after the war.

And here is a detail many visitors never expect. This grand ducal residence is a working university building. The palace is home to the Latvia University of Life Sciences and Technologies. Students study agriculture, forestry, food science and related subjects here. So on a weekday, you may see young people with backpacks walking through the gates of a Baroque palace. The building is still lived in, in its own way.

Below the palace lies one of its most moving spaces — the ducal crypt. It holds the tombs of the Dukes of Courland and their families. These include members of the Kettler family, the dynasty that ruled the duchy for much of its history, and members of the Biron family too. Rows of metal and wooden coffins rest in the cool, quiet vaults. The crypt is usually visited as part of the palace museum, and it often requires a separate ticket.

As you walk around the outside, notice the setting. The palace sits beside the Lielupe River, wide and slow, with green banks and open sky. Walk a little along the water, and you can see the long main facade from a distance. From there, the full scale of Rastrelli's plan becomes clear. In the evening, the light falls low across the river, and the windows catch it one by one.

Take a final look before you leave. These walls began as a dream of a favourite, stood empty through his exile, sheltered a king without a crown, and burned twice. Today they hold lecture halls and quiet dukes in the vaults below. Listen again to the water. The river was here before the knights, before Biron, before the students. As you walk away, it keeps running past the palace, just as it always has.
