The guide

The story

Dry wind moves across the desert here. It carries fine grey dust and the faint salt of the Pacific, somewhere to the west beyond the low hills. Underfoot the ground is loose sand and broken stone. The light is hard and clear, and every shadow has a sharp edge. On a ridge in front of you, a row of small stone towers stands against the sky... like the teeth of a comb.

This is Chanquillo, in the Casma Valley on the northern coast of Peru. It is one of the oldest known solar observatories in the Americas. Archaeologists date it to around the fourth century before Christ. That makes it far older than the Inca, by close to two thousand years. In 2021, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List as the Chankillo Archaeoastronomical Complex.

The row of towers is the heart of the site. There are thirteen of them, running roughly north to south along the crest of a low hill. They are built of stone set in mortar, and each one is a squat, rectangular block. From a distance they look like a simple wall with gaps. But the gaps are the point. Each space between two towers frames a small piece of the eastern and western horizon.

For a long time, nobody was sure what the towers were for. Travellers and scholars noticed them for generations and offered many guesses. Some thought they were defensive. Others suggested storage or ritual platforms. The answer came in 2007. Archaeologist Ivan Ghezzi and astronomer Clive Ruggles published a study in the journal Science. They showed that the towers work as a calendar. The tool is the horizon, and the clock hand is the sun.

Here is how it works. On each side of the ridge, the builders left an observation point. One stands to the east, one to the west. If you stand at the western point and watch the sunrise, the sun comes up behind the ridge of towers. Over the course of a year, the place where it rises moves slowly along the row. In June it rises near one end. In December it rises near the other.

This is the first surprise most visitors miss. The towers do not mark only the solstices — the two turning points of the year. They mark the whole year in between. An observer could count the days by watching which gap the sun appeared in. At each end of the row, the sun seems to pause for several days before turning back. That pause was likely a moment of great importance to the people who lived here.

Remember that the seasons run the other way in the southern hemisphere. So the June sunrise here marks the shortest day of the year, the start of winter. The December sunrise marks the longest day and the height of summer. For farmers in a river valley at the edge of the desert, knowing these moments mattered. Planting, water, and festivals could all follow the movement of the sun along the ridge.

Now turn and look toward the higher hill nearby. On its summit sits the other great structure of Chanquillo. Archaeologists often call it the fortified temple. It is a ring of massive walls, built in three concentric loops, one inside the other. The walls are thick and tall, and the gateways through them are narrow. From the plain below, the whole thing looks like a stone crown on the hilltop.

The purpose of this hilltop building is still debated. For many years it was described as a fortress. The thick walls and the tight gateways certainly suggest defence. But excavations have turned up evidence of ritual activity as well. Many researchers now see it as a temple that was also protected. It is possible that it served both roles at different times. The honest answer is that the question is still open.

Here is the second surprise. The temple and the towers seem to belong to one plan. The observation points, the ridge of towers, and the hilltop walls form a single landscape. The people who built Chanquillo did not just watch the sky. They shaped the ground itself to match it. The desert hills became part of the instrument, and the horizon became a line of measurement.

We do not know the name these builders used for themselves. They lived long before writing reached this coast, and they left no inscriptions. What they did leave is this careful arrangement of stone, along with pottery, offerings, and the traces of daily life. The Casma Valley was already an old centre of culture when Chanquillo was built. Older monuments such as Cerro Sechín stand in the same valley, a reminder of how long people have lived here.

As you walk, notice how quiet it is. There are no trees to hold the sound of the wind. You may hear only the crunch of your own steps on the gravel and perhaps a distant truck on the highway. The colours are few: the pale brown of the sand, the darker brown of the stone, and the deep blue of the sky. In this plain setting, the sun is impossible to ignore. Perhaps that was part of the design.

Before you leave, take a moment to look along the line of towers one more time. Pick a gap and imagine the sun rising inside it on a single morning, more than two thousand years ago. Someone stood near where you are standing and watched that same light. As you walk back across the sand, look at your own shadow. It is pointing away from the sun, just as theirs did — part of the same old calendar, still running.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring sun protection; the desert site has almost no shade.
Carry plenty of water for walking between the structures.
Visit near sunrise to see the towers against the light.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose sand and broken stone.
Combine your visit with nearby Cerro Sechín in the valley.
