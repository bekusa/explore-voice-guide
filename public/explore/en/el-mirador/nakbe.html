The guide

The story

Listen first. A howler monkey roars somewhere in the canopy, a deep sound that rolls through the trees. Leaf litter crunches under your boots. The air is heavy, green, and warm. Ahead of you the forest floor rises into a slope that looks natural at first glance. Look again. Under the roots and the soil is cut stone. You are standing at Nakbe, one of the oldest known cities of the Maya world.

Nakbe lies in the Mirador Basin, in the far north of Guatemala's Petén region. The basin is a low, forested area close to the Mexican border. Today it is one of the most remote corners of Central America. There are no paved roads here. Most visitors arrive on foot after days of trekking through the jungle, or by helicopter. That isolation has protected the ruins. It also makes every step here feel earned.

The name Nakbe comes from Yucatec Maya, and it is usually translated as something like near the road. It is a modern name, given by researchers. Nobody knows for certain what the city's own people called it. That is a theme you will meet again and again at Nakbe. This place is so old that much of its story comes not from written words, but from stone, plaster, pottery, and the layers of earth that archaeologists study.

Here is the first surprise. Many visitors imagine the Maya at their peak in the Classic period, the age of famous cities like Tikal. But Nakbe is far older. Archaeologists date its first settlement to roughly a thousand years before Christ, in what they call the Middle Preclassic period. By several centuries later, people here were already building large platforms and temples. That makes Nakbe one of the earliest places in the Maya lowlands with monumental architecture.

Think about what that means. Long before the great Classic kingdoms, communities in this swampy basin organised labour on a large scale. They quarried limestone, carried it, shaped it, and raised it into platforms. Someone planned the layout. Someone fed the workers. These are signs of a society with leaders, shared beliefs, and a sense of public space. Nakbe helps archaeologists understand how Maya civilisation began, not only how it flourished.

The city is arranged in two main groups, usually called the East Group and the West Group. A raised causeway links them across the centre of the site. The Maya word for such a road is sacbe, which means white road. These causeways were built up from stone and finished with pale lime plaster. In bright sunlight, they would have shone across the landscape. Walk the line of the causeway today and you can still feel how it lifts slightly above the forest floor.

Here is the second surprise. Nakbe was not alone. Another causeway runs from this area towards El Mirador, the giant city to the northwest, roughly a dozen kilometres away. El Mirador grew to enormous size in the Late Preclassic period, and Nakbe was part of the same network of connected settlements. Researchers see the basin as a linked landscape of cities, towns, and roads, built long before most of the famous Maya sites existed.

Now look at the mounds around you. Each one is a building, wrapped in soil and roots after many centuries. The tallest structures in the West Group rise many storeys above the forest floor. From the top of the largest temple, the canopy spreads out in every direction, a dark green surface broken only by other forested mounds. On a clear day you can understand why the builders wanted height. Height meant visibility, power, and closeness to the sky.

The surfaces of these buildings were once covered in thick lime plaster. Some were decorated with large modelled masks of gods and mythical creatures, painted in deep reds and other colours. Making lime plaster required burning great amounts of limestone, and that burning needed enormous quantities of wood. Some researchers have argued that this demand cleared large areas of forest and changed the local environment. It is a debate still under study, but it is a powerful idea to consider here.

One of the best known carved monuments from the site is a stela, an upright stone slab, now known as Stela 1. It shows two standing figures facing each other. Some scholars link the scene to the Hero Twins, the divine brothers of Maya myth who play a central role in the later sacred book known as the Popol Vuh. That interpretation is not certain. Still, it suggests that some of the great Maya stories may already have had roots here.

Serious modern study of Nakbe began in the late twentieth century. American archaeologist Richard Hansen has led long-term research across the Mirador Basin, working with Guatemalan colleagues and institutions. Their excavations have mapped buildings, dated pottery, and traced how the city grew and changed. Much of the site is still unexcavated. What you see is only a fraction of what lies under the trees. That is part of the experience. You are walking through a city that is still being discovered.

Eventually, Nakbe and many of its neighbours declined, around the end of the Preclassic period. The reasons are still debated. Environmental change, soil erosion, and shifting political power are among the explanations researchers discuss. People returned to parts of the basin in later centuries, but the great Preclassic cities never recovered their earlier scale. The forest slowly closed over the plazas and temples, and it has held them ever since.

Before you move on, pause for a moment. Notice the light coming down in narrow beams through the leaves. Listen for birds, insects, and perhaps the distant monkeys again. Under your feet, under that soft layer of leaves, is a plaster floor laid by people almost three thousand years ago. As you walk away, look at each low hill with new eyes. Some of them are simply hills... and some of them are the first steps of the Maya world.
