The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Here on the edge of the Tagus it rarely stops. It comes off the wide estuary, and it pushes at your jacket and lifts the flags along the promenade. Under your feet the paving is smooth and pale. In front of you a tall, slender shape rises against the sky... thin, white and sharply angled, like something pulled tight by the same wind you feel on your face.

This is the Vasco da Gama Tower. It is the tallest structure in Parque das Nações, the riverside district in the east of Lisbon. The tower rises about 145 metres, and for years it was one of the tallest structures in Portugal. Look at its outline. It does not look like an office block or a church spire. It leans and tapers. Many visitors see a sail in it, and that reading is no accident.

The tower was built for Expo 98, the world exposition that Lisbon hosted in 1998. The theme of that exposition was the oceans. The date mattered too. In 1498, five hundred years earlier, the navigator Vasco da Gama reached India by sea. He sailed from Lisbon, down the coast of Africa and around its southern tip. His route opened a sea path between Europe and Asia, and it changed trade for centuries.

So the exposition looked back to the sea, and this tower looks to it as well. Its shape is widely compared to the sail of a caravel. A caravel was the small, light sailing ship that Portuguese explorers used in the fifteenth century. Stand at the base and look up. The structure narrows as it climbs, and the top seems to cut into the wind. On a clear day the white surface shines against a deep blue sky.

Here is something that surprises many people. Before the late 1990s, almost none of what you see around you existed. This stretch of riverbank was industrial land. It held port facilities, warehouses, fuel storage and heavy industry. It was a place most residents of Lisbon never visited. The exposition was a chance to clear that land and build a new piece of the city from the ground up.

When Expo 98 closed, the site did not become empty fairground. That was the plan from the start. The pavilions, gardens and promenades were turned into a neighbourhood. People now live here, work here and bring their children to the riverside on weekends. The name Parque das Nações means Park of the Nations, a reminder of the countries that came to exhibit. The tower stayed as the district's landmark, visible from far along the river.

Turn your eyes toward the water and follow it to the north. You will see a long, low bridge running across the estuary into the haze. That is the Vasco da Gama Bridge. It also opened in 1998, and it carries the same name as the tower. When it opened, it was the longest bridge in Europe. On a misty morning its far end seems to disappear, and the bridge looks as if it runs out to sea.

Now look back at the tower itself. Its life after the exposition has had several chapters. For a time, visitors could ride up to a viewing level near the top. Later that access closed, and the tower went quiet for some years. Today a hotel stands at its base, built alongside the old structure and joined to it. The sail and the newer glass building now stand together as a single landmark on the riverfront.

That pairing is worth a moment of attention. The hotel is broad and solid. The tower is narrow and light. One holds people, and the other mostly holds the sky. Walk a little way along the promenade and watch how the two shapes change as you move. From some angles the tower seems to grow out of the hotel. From others it stands alone, like a mast on the bank of the river.

Around you, the district carries more traces of the exposition. Not far from here is the Oceanário, the large aquarium that was one of the central pavilions in 1998. Further inland is the Oriente station. Its white steel canopy looks like a row of trees and was designed by the Spanish architect Santiago Calatrava. Above the riverside, small cable cars glide along the water's edge and give riders a slow, high view of the whole park.

Listen again for a moment. You may hear the hum of the cable cars, the click of bicycle wheels, children on scooters, and the slap of small waves against the wall. On weekends families walk here with dogs and ice cream. Runners pass in pairs. This is not an old quarter full of narrow lanes. It is wide, open and modern, and the air smells of salt and river mud at low tide.

The light changes the tower through the day. In the morning the sun rises over the estuary and the eastern face catches it first. At midday the structure looks bright and flat against the sky. Toward evening the river turns silver and then gold, and the tower becomes a dark line against the colour. Many people stay until sunset simply to watch that change, sitting on the low walls by the water.

Before you move on, take one more look up. The tower was built to honour a voyage that left this river more than five centuries ago. Ships once sailed from Lisbon toward an unknown horizon. Today the same river runs past glass, steel and gardens. Notice the wind again, still pushing at the sail above you. As you walk away along the water, glance back now and then. The white mast stays in view for a long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come near sunset for the river light behind the tower.
Walk the riverside promenade to see the tower change shape.
Bring a windproof layer; the estuary wind rarely stops.
Ride the riverside cable car for a high view of the park.
Combine your visit with the nearby Oceanário aquarium.
