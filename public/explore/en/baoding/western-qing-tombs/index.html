The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through old pine trees, a long, low hiss that never quite stops. Under your feet the paving stones are uneven, worn smooth in the centre. The air smells of resin and dry earth. Ahead, yellow-glazed roofs show between dark green branches, and red walls catch the light. This is the Western Qing Tombs, in Yi County, southwest of Baoding. It is a valley of emperors, and the trees around you were planted to guard them.

The Qing was China's last imperial dynasty. Its rulers came from Manchuria, in the northeast, and they ruled China from the mid-seventeenth century until 1912. Like the dynasties before them, they treated burial as a matter of state. A tomb had to sit in the right landscape. Mountains behind, water in front, open ground between. Geomancers, experts in the art of placing buildings in harmony with the land, studied valleys like this one for years before a single stone was laid.

Here is the first surprise. This cemetery should not exist. The early Qing emperors chose a site east of Beijing, now known as the Eastern Qing Tombs. Everyone expected the Yongzheng Emperor to rest there beside his father, the great Kangxi Emperor. Instead, Yongzheng rejected it. He said the eastern site was not good enough for him, and he sent his advisers west. They chose this valley, below Yongning Mountain. Why he broke with family tradition is still debated. Some historians point to geomancy, others to his complicated relationship with his father's legacy.

Yongzheng's tomb is called Tailing, and it is the oldest and largest here. As you approach, look for the three great stone archways standing in front of it. Beyond them runs the spirit way, a long ceremonial road lined with carved stone figures. Officials, horses, and other animals stand in pairs, facing each other across the path. They were meant to escort the emperor's spirit. Walking between them is still the best way to feel the scale of the place.

Along the route you will meet a stele pavilion. Inside stands a tall stone tablet resting on the back of a carved creature that looks like a turtle. In Chinese tradition it is a mythical son of the dragon, famous for carrying heavy loads. The inscriptions record the emperor's titles and deeds, often in both Chinese and Manchu script. Look closely at the two writing systems side by side. Chinese runs in square characters, and Manchu runs in flowing vertical lines.

A story follows Yongzheng everywhere. Legend has it that he was assassinated, and that he was buried with a head made of gold. It is a popular tale, told with relish by local guides. But it is folklore, not history. Tailing's underground chamber has never been opened, and no evidence supports the story. Yongzheng's death remains a quiet mystery, which is perhaps why the legend has lasted so long.

After Yongzheng, the family faced a problem. Should later emperors go east or west? His son, the Qianlong Emperor, set a rule of alternation. A father buried in one cemetery would have a son buried in the other. In practice the rule was not followed perfectly, but it explains why Qing emperors are split between two valleys. Qianlong himself went east. His son, the Jiaqing Emperor, came here, to a tomb called Changling, close to Tailing.

The next tomb is the strangest, and many visitors miss why. It belongs to the Daoguang Emperor, who ruled in the first half of the nineteenth century. He first built his tomb at the eastern site. Then water seeped into the underground chamber. For an emperor, a flooded grave was a serious failure. He abandoned it, punished the officials responsible, and started again here. The new tomb is called Muling.

Muling looks different from its neighbours. Daoguang was famous for thrift, and the tomb is smaller, without the grand stele tower that marks the others. But the main hall hides a treasure. It is built from nanmu, a rare and fragrant hardwood, left mostly unpainted. The ceiling is covered with carved dragons. Locals say Daoguang believed dragons had caused the flood at his first tomb, so he filled this ceiling with them to keep them watched and dry. Treat that as a tale. The carving, though, is real, and remarkable.

The last tomb tells the dynasty's ending. Chongling belongs to the Guangxu Emperor, who died in 1908. He spent his final years under the control of the Empress Dowager Cixi, held as a near-prisoner. Construction of his tomb was still under way when the Qing fell in 1912. The new Republic allowed work to continue, and Chongling was finished a few years later. It is the last imperial tomb built in Chinese history.

Chongling also carries a darker chapter. In the twentieth century, robbers broke into its underground palace and stripped it of valuables. Today that damage has one strange result. Its burial chamber is the one visitors can usually enter. You walk down into cool stone vaults, through heavy marble doors, toward the place where the emperor and his empress were laid. The air changes as you descend. It becomes still and damp, and sound carries strangely off the walls.

One more surprise lies nearby. The last emperor, Puyi, who lost his throne as a child, ended his life as an ordinary citizen of the People's Republic. Decades after his death, in 1995, his ashes were moved to a private cemetery close to this valley. So the dynasty's final ruler rests near Guangxu, the uncle he succeeded. In 2000, these tombs became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing for the imperial tombs of the Ming and Qing dynasties.

Before you leave, stop for a moment under the pines. Look at the yellow roof tiles, a colour once reserved for the emperor alone. Notice the moss in the cracks of the paving, and the silence between the halls. Few crowds come this far from Beijing, and the valley often feels almost empty. As you walk away, listen again for that wind in the trees. It has kept moving here for nearly three centuries, long after the emperors stopped coming.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow most of a day; the tombs are spread out.
Wear comfortable shoes for long stone paths and steps.
Bring a light layer for the cool underground chamber.
Visit on a weekday for a quieter valley.
Arrange return transport in advance from this rural area.
