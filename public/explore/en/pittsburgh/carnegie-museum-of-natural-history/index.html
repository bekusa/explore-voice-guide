The guide

The story

Listen for the sound of this building first. Footsteps echo across stone floors. School groups murmur somewhere down a long hallway. Then you turn a corner and the ceiling opens up, and a row of enormous skeletons stands in the light. They are long-necked, heavy-boned, and very still. For a moment, the noise of the room seems to drop away. That pause is the reason many people come back to this museum again and again.

You are standing in the Oakland neighbourhood of Pittsburgh, inside one wing of a large cultural complex. The same building also holds the Carnegie Museum of Art and the main branch of the Carnegie Library. All three grew from one idea. A wealthy industrialist believed that a working city deserved books, paintings, and science under one roof, open to ordinary people. The museum you are walking through was founded in the 1890s as part of that plan.

That industrialist was Andrew Carnegie. He was born in Scotland, came to America as a poor boy, and built a fortune in steel here in Pittsburgh. Later in life, he decided to give most of that fortune away. Libraries, concert halls, and universities carry his name across the world. But this museum held a personal place in his imagination. And one animal, more than any other, captured his attention... a dinosaur.

At the end of the nineteenth century, newspapers carried stories about giant bones found in the American West. Carnegie wanted one for Pittsburgh. He paid for fossil-hunting expeditions, and in 1899 his team found a skeleton in Wyoming. It belonged to a long-necked, long-tailed plant-eater. Scientists named it Diplodocus carnegii, in honour of the man who paid for the dig. Pittsburgh soon gave it a simpler, friendlier name — Dippy.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Dippy did not stay only in Pittsburgh. Carnegie had plaster casts made of the skeleton and sent them as gifts to museums in Europe and beyond. London, Paris, Berlin, and other cities received copies. For many people around the world, their first image of a dinosaur came from this one animal. In a real sense, a fossil from Wyoming, paid for by a Pittsburgh steelman, helped introduce dinosaurs to the public across several continents.

The main dinosaur galleries here are called Dinosaurs in Their Time. The idea is simple and very effective. Instead of lining up skeletons in neat rows, the museum groups animals by the period in which they actually lived. Painted backdrops and plant models suggest their landscapes. You walk forward through deep time, one ancient world after another. Notice how the posture of the skeletons feels active. Tails are lifted. Necks reach out. These animals look like they are moving, not resting.

Now look for the Tyrannosaurus rex. This is the second surprise. The skeleton here includes the type specimen of the species. A type specimen is the original fossil that scientists used to describe and name a species for the first time. In other words, every Tyrannosaurus rex discussed anywhere is measured, in a sense, against these bones. The fossil was first found by the collector Barnum Brown, and it came to Pittsburgh from New York during the Second World War.

Another name in these halls carries a quieter story. One of the great long-necked dinosaurs here was named Apatosaurus louisae, after Louise Carnegie, Andrew Carnegie's wife. It is a small reminder that science and family pride mixed easily in that age. Many specimens in this museum came from expeditions of the early twentieth century, and the names on the labels often hold the history of the people who funded them.

When you leave the dinosaurs, the museum shifts in mood. The Hillman Hall of Minerals and Gems is darker and more intimate. Crystals glow under focused lights. Some pieces look like frozen fireworks. Others look like broken glass, or deep blue water, or rust. Spend a little time here, especially if the dinosaur halls feel crowded. The room is often calmer, and the colours change as you move from one case to the next.

Other halls take you far from Pennsylvania. There is a gallery of ancient Egypt, with objects connected to burial and daily life along the Nile. There are rooms about the peoples of the Arctic and about Native American cultures. And there are older animal dioramas, with mounted creatures set before painted landscapes. These displays come from an earlier style of museum-making. Look closely and you can see the skill in the brushwork and the sculpted ground.

One diorama here has a complicated history. It shows a man on a camel, attacked by lions. It was made in nineteenth-century France, and the museum has carried it for well over a century. In recent years, researchers examined it and found that the human figure contained real human remains. The museum chose to talk openly about this, and about the colonial attitudes of the era that produced it. It is a good example of a museum asking hard questions about its own collections.

Behind the public galleries is a part of this place most visitors never see. The museum is an active research institution. Its collections hold millions of specimens — insects, birds, plants, fossils, and much more. Scientists use them to study how species change, and how climate and habitats shift over time. The museum also runs a field station in the countryside outside the city, where researchers study birds and wildlife. The halls you walk through are only the visible surface of that work.

Before you leave, step outside onto Forbes Avenue. Near the entrance stands a life-size model of Dippy, raised in the open air. On some days you will see it dressed up for a holiday or a local sports team. That is very much a Pittsburgh habit. The dinosaur belongs to the city as much as to the museum. Students from the nearby universities pass by it every day, and few of them even look up anymore.

So take one last look around you. Whether you are in a quiet gem room, beside a giant skeleton, or out on the pavement, you are inside one man's old idea — that anyone should be able to walk in and stand face to face with the deep past. Listen again to the footsteps and the voices of children. That sound is perhaps the clearest sign that the idea still works.
