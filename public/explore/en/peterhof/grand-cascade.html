The guide

The story

Listen. Before you see it, you hear the water. It falls in sheets, white and loud, down a wide stone staircase. Gold figures shine in the sun. Spray drifts on the wind and touches your face. At the center, a giant tears open the jaws of a lion, and a tall jet of water shoots high into the air. This is the Grand Cascade. It sits below the Grand Palace at Peterhof, facing the sea.

Walk to the edge and look down. The cascade is built as two broad staircases of water. They flow on either side of a central grotto. Dozens of fountains run at once. Gilded statues stand along the steps, bright against the white foam. The whole stair seems to be made of moving water and light. Below it, a long canal carries the water away, straight toward the Gulf of Finland.

Your eye returns to the center. The great gold figure is Samson. He holds open the mouth of a lion, and from that mouth the tallest jet rises. This fountain remembers a battle. In 1709, Russian forces defeated the army of Sweden at Poltava. The battle fell on the feast day of Saint Sampson. The lion stood on the coat of arms of Sweden. So Samson over the lion means Russia standing over Sweden.

Now think about how the water moves. There are no pumps here. The fountains run by gravity alone. Water gathers in springs on high ground far to the south. It travels many kilometers along open canals and through quiet ponds. By the time it reaches the cascade, it has enough force to climb high into the sky. Engineers built this system in the early eighteenth century, in the time of Peter the Great.

Peter the Great founded Peterhof as a seaside residence. He wanted a palace and a park to rival the great courts of Europe. He took part in the planning himself, sketching ideas and choosing sites. The Grand Cascade opened in 1723. Over the following years, more statues and fountains were added. The figure of Samson came a little later, set there to mark the victory at Poltava.

Look closely at the statues. They glow because they are covered in thin gold leaf. The first figures were made of lead. Later they were replaced with bronze, then covered in gold. Among them you will find gods and heroes from old myths. Some hold shells. Some pour water from jars. Each one is part of a single story, told in metal and stone across the whole staircase.

At the foot of the cascade, behind the falling water, there is a grotto. It is a cool, shaded space built into the terrace. Inside, the sound of the water changes to a low roar. You can stand near the openings and watch the streams pour down from behind. In summer, the air here is damp and cool, a welcome relief from the heat outside in the park.

This place has known great loss. During the Second World War, German forces occupied Peterhof. The palace was burned. The park was torn apart. The original Samson figure disappeared and was never found. After the war, sculptors studied old photographs and drawings for years. They created a new Samson, and it was set back in place in 1947. Slowly, the fountains were made to run again.

Stand at the top and follow the long canal with your eyes. It runs straight from the cascade to the sea. Peter wanted ships and boats to arrive from the water and see the gold staircase ahead of them. The view links the palace, the fountains, and the Gulf of Finland in one long, clear line. It was a way to show power and order to anyone who came by ship.

Spend a moment just listening. The water never stops. Birds call from the trees along the paths. Visitors speak in many languages around you. On a bright day, the gold throws light back at you, and small rainbows form in the rising spray. The cascade works without any electricity, in the same patient way it worked three hundred years ago.

The fountains do not run all year. In the cold months they are turned off and covered against the frost. In spring, there is a ceremony when the water is switched on again. Crowds gather to watch the first jets rise into the air. For the whole warm season, the cascade flows from morning until evening, day after day.

Before you move on, take one last look. Think of the springs far away, the long canals, the patient flow of water toward this single point. Think of the people who built it, lost it, and built it again. The water falls, the gold shines, and the giant holds the lion open to the sky. Then it all flows quietly down to the sea.
