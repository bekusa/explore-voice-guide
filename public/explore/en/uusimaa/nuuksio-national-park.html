The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under your boots, the path is soft with old pine needles and damp moss. Somewhere ahead, a stream moves between stones. The air smells of resin, wet bark, and cold water. Above you, spruce branches close in and let only small patches of light reach the ground. It is hard to believe that the edge of Finland's capital region lies only a short drive behind you. Welcome to Nuuksio National Park, a landscape of rock, lake, and forest on the western side of Uusimaa.

Nuuksio sits across the borders of three municipalities: Espoo, Kirkkonummi, and Vihti. The park was established in 1994, and it covers roughly fifty square kilometres. That is not huge by Finnish standards. Lapland has parks many times larger. But size is not the point here. Nuuksio protects a piece of southern Finnish wilderness right next to more than a million people. For many residents of Helsinki and Espoo, this is their first real forest, and the place they return to most often.

Look at the shape of the land around you. The ground rises in rounded humps of grey and pink granite, then drops into hollows filled with water or bog. This pattern has a clear cause. During the last Ice Age, a thick sheet of ice covered all of Finland. As the ice moved and then melted, it scraped the bedrock smooth and left long scratches and loose boulders behind. Many of the narrow lakes here follow old cracks in the rock. The glacier carved the outline. The forest filled it in later.

This is the first surprise for many visitors. Nuuksio has more than eighty lakes and ponds, though few of them are large. They lie in chains between the ridges, dark and still, often stained brown by peat. The brown colour does not mean the water is dirty. It comes from natural humus washed in from the surrounding bogs and forest floor. On a calm morning, these lakes hold perfect reflections of the pines. On a windy day, they turn black and restless in minutes.

Now look up at the trees. Spruce dominates the damp valleys, dense and dark. Pine takes the dry, rocky ridges, where its roots grip thin soil and bare stone. Between them grow birch and aspen, with pale trunks that brighten the gloom. In some corners of the park, the forest has been left alone for a long time. There you will see fallen trunks covered in moss and fungi. This dead wood is not untidy. It is food and shelter for insects, birds, and countless small organisms.

And here is the second surprise. The symbol of Nuuksio is a small animal that most visitors will never see: the Siberian flying squirrel. It does not truly fly. It glides from tree to tree on a fold of skin stretched between its front and back legs. It is active mostly at night, and it prefers old spruce forest mixed with aspen. If you notice small yellowish droppings, like grains of rice, at the base of an aspen tree, a flying squirrel may live above you.

The lakes carry another quiet resident. In spring and summer, the black-throated diver nests on some of the larger waters here. Finns call it kuikka. It is a sleek bird with a grey head and a striped neck. Its call is long and haunting, and on a still evening it carries across the whole lake. Many Finns say this sound means summer and wilderness more than any other. If you hear it, stand still and listen. Please keep your distance from the shore where the birds nest.

Most visitors start their walk at one of the main gateways, such as Haukkalampi. From there, marked trails loop through the forest. Each trail has its own colour on the signposts and painted markers. Some routes are short and gentle. Others climb steep rocks and use wooden steps and duckboards across wet ground. The paths are rooty and uneven, even when they look easy on a map. Give yourself more time than you expect, and watch your feet on the slopes.

At several points along the trails, you will find campfire sites with stacked firewood, benches, and simple shelters. These places are a very Finnish idea. You walk for an hour, light a small fire, and grill a sausage or warm your coffee. The rule is simple: make fire only at these marked sites, and only when there is no forest fire warning. Smoke drifting between the pines, and the crackle of dry wood, are part of the sound of this park.

Finland also has a tradition called everyman's right. It allows people to walk freely in nature and to pick wild berries and mushrooms. In late summer, the forest floor here turns blue with bilberries and red with lingonberries. In autumn, people come with baskets to look for mushrooms. Inside a national park, a few extra rules apply. Stay on the marked paths in sensitive areas, camp only at official sites, and leave the plants and rocks as you found them.

Near the edge of the park stands Haltia, the Finnish Nature Centre. It is a modern building made largely of wood, set above a lake. Inside, exhibitions introduce the nature of Finland as a whole, from the southern archipelago to the northern fells. It is a good place to start on a rainy day, or with children. You can learn which animals live in the forest before you go and walk among them.

The seasons change this park completely. In summer, the light lasts almost all night, and the forest hums with insects. Autumn brings yellow birch leaves, red bilberry bushes, and early darkness. In winter, snow softens every sound. The lakes freeze, and the trails become quiet lines of footprints. Spring arrives with melting ice, rushing streams, and the return of migrating birds. No single visit shows you all of Nuuksio. Each season is a different place.

Before you leave, find a flat rock above one of the lakes and sit down for a minute. Feel how cool the stone is, even on a warm day. Notice the patches of grey and green lichen on its surface. They grow very slowly, so try not to step on them. Look across the water to the far shore, where the trees stand close together. This land was shaped by ice, then by forest, and it still changes slowly around you.

As you walk back toward the trailhead, the sounds may change. First you hear the wind in the spruce, then perhaps a woodpecker, then, faintly, a road. The city is close again. But keep the smell of the pines with you for a little longer. Look at the moss on the stones by the path, and at the roots crossing your way. This forest is always open, just beyond the edge of town, waiting for your next walk.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for rooty, rocky, uneven trails.
Start your walk from the Haukkalampi trailhead area.
Light fires only at marked campfire sites.
Check for forest fire warnings before making a fire.
Visit Haltia nature centre on rainy days.
