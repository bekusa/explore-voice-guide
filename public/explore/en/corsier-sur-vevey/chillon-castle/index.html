The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against stone just below your feet, a small, steady slap of lake against rock. The air smells cool and mineral, with a trace of damp moss from the old walls. Across the water, the mountains of the far shore turn blue and grey in the haze. And in front of you, a cluster of pale towers and red-brown roofs rises straight out of Lake Geneva, as if the castle grew from the lake itself.

This is Chillon Castle. It stands on the shore between Montreux and Villeneuve, just along the lakeside from the hills above Vevey. What makes it unusual is the ground beneath it. The castle sits on a small rocky island, separated from the shore by a narrow channel. You cross a short wooden bridge to reach it. That small gap of water once mattered a great deal. It turned a building into a fortress.

Why build here? Look at the geography. Behind the castle, the mountains drop steeply toward the water. In front, the lake. Between them runs only a thin strip of land. For centuries, travellers moving between northern Europe and the Alpine passes into Italy had to squeeze through this corridor. Whoever held this rock could watch the road, stop traffic, and collect tolls. Chillon was, in a very real sense, a gatehouse to the mountains.

People used this rock long before the castle you see today. The earliest written records of a fortification here go back to the early Middle Ages, around the eleventh century. But the castle took its present shape under the Counts of Savoy, a powerful dynasty that ruled lands on both sides of the Alps. In the thirteenth century, Count Peter the Second of Savoy expanded and strengthened it. The Savoy family used Chillon as a fortress, a toll station, a residence... and a prison.

Notice how the castle has two faces. The side facing the land is tough and defensive, with thick walls, towers, and few openings. This is the side an enemy would approach. Now walk around and look at the side facing the lake. It is softer and more open, with larger windows that look out over the water. That was the comfortable side, where the court lived. The lake itself was the defence there.

Inside, the castle is a maze of buildings arranged around courtyards. You move from one to the next through doorways, stairways, and narrow passages. In the halls, look up at the wooden ceilings, dark with age. In some rooms, traces of medieval wall paintings survive — faded reds, greens, and ochres, with patterns and figures that once covered whole walls. It helps to imagine these rooms full of colour, tapestries, and firelight, not bare stone.

Now go down. The most famous spaces at Chillon are underground. The lower vaults sit at the level of the lake, and their rough floor is the natural rock of the island. Rows of stone pillars hold up Gothic ribbed vaults — pointed arches that meet overhead like the ribs of a ship. Light enters through small openings on the lake side. The water is just beyond the wall. You can often hear it.

These vaults held prisoners. The most famous was François Bonivard, a prior from Geneva who supported the city's independence against the Duke of Savoy. He was held at Chillon for several years in the early sixteenth century. Tradition says he was chained to one of these pillars. In 1536, the army of Bern conquered the region, took the castle, and released him. With that, the Savoy era at Chillon came to an end.

For the next two and a half centuries, Chillon belonged to the Bernese. They used it as a fortress, an arsenal, and a prison. Then, at the end of the eighteenth century, the region of Vaud broke away from Bern. Vaud later became a Swiss canton in its own right, and the castle passed into its hands. Here is a surprise for many visitors. Chillon is not a ruin that was rebuilt from scratch. It has been in near-continuous use for centuries.

Now comes the twist that made Chillon famous across Europe. In 1816, the English poet Lord Byron visited the castle with his fellow poet Percy Bysshe Shelley. Byron heard the story of Bonivard and wrote a long poem called The Prisoner of Chillon. The poem turned a local political prisoner into a romantic hero of suffering and freedom. Soon writers, painters, and tourists began to arrive. The French painter Eugène Delacroix later painted the prisoner in his chains.

In the dungeon, look closely at the pillars. On one of them, the name Byron is carved into the stone. Visitors have pointed at it for generations. Whether Byron cut it himself is still debated — some think it was added by someone else. Either way, the carving has become part of the castle's story. Around it you may notice older graffiti too, names and dates scratched by travellers who came long before you.

In the late nineteenth century, the canton began a careful study and restoration of the castle. Archaeologists and architects examined the walls, dug beneath the floors, and recorded what they found. Their aim was to preserve what survived rather than invent a fantasy castle. That is one reason Chillon feels layered. Different centuries sit side by side. A Savoy hall, a Bernese room, a Romantic-era legend, all within a few steps of each other.

Before you leave, walk back along the shore and turn around. Look at the castle low on the water, its reflection moving with each small wave. Above it, the modern motorway crosses the mountainside on tall concrete legs. Here, two kinds of travel sit together — the medieval road the castle once guarded, and the fast road of today. Listen once more to the water against the rock. It is the same sound the guards, prisoners, and poets heard here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early in the day to explore the vaults quietly.
Wear shoes with grip for uneven stone floors and stairs.
Walk the lakeside path for the classic view of the castle.
Follow the marked route to see the rooms in order.
Come back near sunset to see the walls in warm light.
