The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, a priest may be chanting in Ge'ez, the ancient language of Ethiopian liturgy. The sound is low and steady. Under your feet the ground is dry and pale, and the light here in northern Ethiopia is sharp and clear. Around you, people move quietly in white cotton shawls. You are standing in one of the most sacred places in Ethiopian Christianity... the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion, in the old city of Axum.

To understand why this ground matters, start with the city itself. Axum was once the capital of a powerful kingdom. It traded across the Red Sea, minted its own coins, and raised tall carved stone pillars, called stelae, that still stand a short walk from here. In the fourth century, a king of Axum named Ezana adopted Christianity. That made this kingdom one of the earliest Christian states in the world — long before most of Europe.

Ethiopian tradition holds that the first church on this site was founded in that early Christian period. Whether the exact date is history or tradition is hard to prove today. What is clear is that a place of worship has stood on or near this ground for a very long time. It has been destroyed and rebuilt more than once. Each new church rose on the memory of the one before.

Now for the reason many visitors come. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church believes that this compound holds the Ark of the Covenant — the chest described in the Bible that held the tablets of the Ten Commandments. According to the Kebra Nagast, a medieval Ethiopian text, the Ark was brought here by Menelik, said to be the son of King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. This is a story of faith and legend. No outsider has ever been allowed to verify it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Only one person may see the relic. He is a monk chosen as its guardian. Tradition says he stays within the grounds of the chapel for the rest of his life, praying and burning incense. When he nears death, he names his successor. You will not see inside the small chapel where the relic is kept. You may, however, see the fence around it, and perhaps a robed figure moving quietly within.

That chapel is modest — a small building with a green dome, raised in the twentieth century. Its plainness surprises people. They expect gold and grandeur. Instead, the most guarded room in Ethiopia looks almost ordinary from outside. The power of this place does not come from its size. It comes from what people believe is inside.

Next, look for the older church. It is a rectangular stone building with a castle-like outline and battlements along the roofline. It was built in the seventeenth century under Emperor Fasilides, the ruler who also built the stone castles of Gondar. An earlier church here was destroyed in the sixteenth century, during wars that swept across the Ethiopian highlands. Fasilides restored the sacred site. Traditionally, women are not permitted to enter this old church, and that rule is still followed.

Inside the old church, if you are allowed in, the walls carry painted scenes from the Bible and from the lives of saints. Ethiopian church painting uses strong colours and large, wide-open eyes. Holy figures are shown facing you directly. Evil figures are often shown in profile. It is a simple visual code, and once you know it, you can read the walls more easily.

Across the compound stands the newer cathedral. It was built in the 1960s under Emperor Haile Selassie, the last emperor of Ethiopia. It has a large dome and a tall bell tower, and it is open to everyone, men and women alike. The contrast is striking. On one side, a fortress-like church from the age of Gondar. On the other, a modern cathedral built for a growing crowd of worshippers.

Axum also held a special role for Ethiopia's rulers. For centuries, many emperors came here to be crowned or to confirm their right to rule. Around the compound you may notice old stone blocks and broken stone seats. Tradition links some of these with royal ceremonies. Their exact history is not always certain, but they remind you that this was a place of kings as well as priests.

If you come in late November, the quiet changes completely. The feast of Hidar Zion draws very large crowds of pilgrims from across Ethiopia. They walk for days, wrapped in white. Priests carry ornate crosses and colourful umbrellas. The air fills with drums, chanting and the sharp ululation of women. A copy of the sacred tablet, wrapped in cloth, is carried in procession. In fact, every Ethiopian Orthodox church holds such a copy, called a tabot. Without it, a church is not consecrated.

The town around the church has also lived through hard times. In recent years, the conflict in the Tigray region reached Axum, and the community here still carries that grief. Many visitors find that the calm of the compound feels deeper because of it. People come here to pray for the living and the dead. It is good to move gently and to ask before photographing anyone at prayer.

Near the compound there is a small museum connected to the church. It holds religious objects and old manuscripts written on parchment. Some of these books are bound in wood and leather. Ethiopian scribes copied texts by hand for centuries, and some monasteries still keep that skill alive. Even a few minutes with these pages helps you see how long this faith has been written down, read aloud and carried forward.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the green dome of the chapel, the stone battlements of the old church, and the tower of the new cathedral. Three buildings, from three different ages, gathered around one belief. Listen again for the chanting, or the shuffle of bare feet on stone. As you walk out toward the stelae and the town, carry that quiet with you. It has been kept here for a very long time.
