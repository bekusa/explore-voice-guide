The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the wind. It moves across the open plateau with nothing to stop it, carrying dust and the faint smell of dry grass. Underfoot, loose stones shift and click. Around you lie low walls of pale limestone, tumbled arches, and the outlines of rooms open to the sky. This is Umm ar-Rasas, on the high desert edge of central Jordan. At first glance it looks like rubble. Give it a few minutes, and it becomes a town.

The Arabic name is often translated as Mother of Lead. Nobody is quite sure why the place carries it. Historians, however, are confident about an older name. Inscriptions found here identify the site as Kastron Mefaa. Many scholars connect this name with Mephaath, a town mentioned in the Hebrew Bible. So the same ground has been named and renamed by many peoples. Each one left something behind in the stone.

The story of the site begins with soldiers. The Romans built a fortified military camp here to guard the eastern frontier of their province. You can still trace the great rectangle of its walls. Later, under Byzantine rule, the camp became the heart of a growing town. Houses spread out beyond the old walls. A word on terms here. Byzantine refers to the eastern Roman Empire, which was centred on Constantinople and which was Christian.

And this town was deeply Christian. Archaeologists have identified more than a dozen churches at Umm ar-Rasas. That is a remarkable number for a settlement of this size. Some stand inside the old fort, others outside it. Many had mosaic floors. Small cubes of coloured stone were set by hand to form patterns, animals, people, and words. For centuries most of these floors lay hidden under fallen masonry and windblown soil.

Systematic excavation began in the 1980s. A team led by the Franciscan archaeologist Michele Piccirillo worked here for many seasons. What they uncovered changed how historians understand this region. In 2004, UNESCO added Umm ar-Rasas to its World Heritage List. The listing recognised the Roman camp, the Byzantine and early Islamic town, and above all the mosaics. The modern roof you see over one group of ruins protects the most important of them.

Walk under that roof, into the Church of Saint Stephen. Let your eyes adjust to the softer light. Spread across the floor is one of the finest mosaics in Jordan. Its colours are earthy and warm... ochre, rust red, black, cream, and soft grey. Wooden walkways carry you above it, so you can look down without stepping on the stone. Take your time here. This floor deserves a slow eye.

Now comes the first surprise. An inscription dates this mosaic to the late eighth century. By then, this land had been under Muslim rule for well over a hundred years. The Umayyad dynasty had come and gone, and the Abbasid dynasty, ruling from Baghdad, now held power. Yet here the Christian community was still building, still decorating, still commissioning skilled artisans. The floor is quiet but clear evidence. Christian life here continued long after the Islamic conquest.

Look closely at the border panels. They show a series of cities, each drawn as a small cluster of walls, towers, and gates, with its name written in Greek. Some are cities of Palestine, such as Jerusalem, Nablus, Caesarea, and Gaza. Others lie in the Nile Delta of Egypt. Still others are towns east of the Jordan River, including Madaba and Philadelphia, which is the ancient name for Amman. Together, they form a picture of the world as these people knew it.

One of those city portraits shows Kastron Mefaa itself. The artists placed their own town among the great cities of the region. It is a proud gesture, and a very human one. Find it if you can, and then look up. You are standing inside the place the mosaic describes. Few experiences in archaeology are so direct. A picture made more than a thousand years ago shows the very town around you.

Here is the second surprise. In several panels, human and animal figures have been deliberately scrambled. Their cubes were lifted and reset in jumbled patterns. Sometimes plants or blank stone replace them. This damage seems to come from a wave of iconoclasm, meaning the rejection of images of living beings. Interestingly, the work was done with care. The rest of the floor was left intact. Someone wanted the church to keep working, but without those faces.

Now leave the shelter and look north across the plain. About a kilometre and a half away, a tall square stone tower rises alone from the ground. It has no internal staircase. Most scholars believe it was a stylite tower. A stylite was a Christian holy man who lived on top of a pillar or tower, in prayer and isolation, exposed to sun and wind. It is the best-preserved structure of its kind in the region.

Near the tower lie the remains of a small church, a cistern, and other buildings. They suggest that a community gathered around the ascetic. Pilgrims may have come to ask for prayers or advice. Stand there for a moment if you make the walk. Picture one person on that platform, through cold winter nights and burning summer days. The silence of the plain makes the idea feel close and real.

Before you go, look again at the ground around you. The paths are uneven, and much of the town is still unexcavated. Under every low mound there may be another room, another floor, another inscription. Listen to the wind as it moves over the walls. Notice the shadow of the tower stretching across the plain. People prayed, traded, and raised families on this spot for centuries. Walk out slowly, and let the open land settle around you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning before the plateau heats up.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose stones and uneven paths.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is scarce.
Walk out to the stylite tower for wider views.
Stay on wooden walkways above the mosaic floor.
