The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. In the low marshy ground around you, frogs call from the reeds. Wind moves through the grass, and somewhere a tractor hums across the cotton fields. The air smells of wet earth and wild fennel. It is hard to believe that this quiet plain was once a busy harbour. Ships once tied up where cattle now graze.

This is Miletus, a short drive inland from Didim. For centuries it was one of the great cities of the Greek world. It stood on a peninsula at the mouth of the Maeander River, which the Turks call the Büyük Menderes. The sea came right up to its walls. Today the coastline lies several kilometres away. That missing sea is the first surprise of this place, and it explains almost everything else.

The river carried silt down from the hills, year after year. Slowly the bays filled with mud. Harbours that once held warships turned into swamps, and then into farmland. Over many centuries, Miletus lost its sea and its trade. A city built on ships was left stranded. When you look out across the flat green plain, you are looking at the old seabed. In wet seasons, water still collects in the lower parts of the site.

The first thing most visitors notice is the theatre. It rises from the plain like a hill of carved stone. The Greeks built an early version here, and the Romans later enlarged it into the huge structure you see now. It could seat many thousands of people. Climb the steps and turn around. From the top rows, imagine the view the audience once had... across open water, with sails moving in the harbour below.

Look carefully at the seats in the theatre. On some of the stone rows, archaeologists found carved inscriptions that reserved places for particular groups. One well-known inscription mentions Jews and people described as God-fearers. It is a small, human detail. It shows a mixed city where different communities sat together to watch the same performance. Above the theatre, on the summit, stand the walls of a later Byzantine fortress, built when the city had shrunk.

Now think about the people who walked these streets. In the sixth century before Christ, Miletus produced some of the first thinkers we call philosophers. Thales of Miletus asked a simple and radical question. What is the world made of? He suggested water. His successors, Anaximander and Anaximenes, offered other answers. What matters is the method. They looked for natural explanations, not stories about gods. Many histories of Western science begin right here.

Miletus was also a city of sailors and settlers. Its people founded a large number of colonies, many of them around the Black Sea. Greek trading towns spread along distant coasts because ships left this harbour. Wealth flowed back. So did ideas, goods, and news from the edges of the known world. For a time, this was one of the most connected places on the Mediterranean.

That success came at a price. Miletus led a revolt of the Ionian Greek cities against the Persian Empire. The revolt failed. In 494 BCE, the Persians captured and destroyed the city. Its people were killed or deported. It was a disaster that shocked the Greek world. Yet the city came back. When it was rebuilt, it was laid out on a regular grid of straight streets crossing at right angles.

That grid is the second surprise. A planner named Hippodamus came from Miletus, and his name became linked to this kind of orderly street plan. Planners still talk about a Hippodamian grid today. Walk through the lower city and you can still sense the pattern. Streets run straight. Blocks are even. Many modern cities with neat grid layouts follow an idea that grew from this ground.

As you walk, look for the Baths of Faustina. These large Roman baths are named after Faustina, the wife of the emperor Marcus Aurelius. Thick brick walls and vaulted rooms still stand. Near the baths and the old harbour, you can find the remains of market squares and sacred buildings. One sanctuary, the Delphinion, honoured Apollo. From here, a sacred road led south toward the great Temple of Apollo at Didyma, in present-day Didim.

One of the finest monuments of Miletus is not here at all. The grand Roman Market Gate was excavated by German archaeologists and taken to Berlin. It was rebuilt inside the Pergamon Museum. So part of this city now stands in a museum hall in Germany. Other visitors also passed through. According to the Book of Acts, the apostle Paul stopped at Miletus and spoke to church leaders from nearby Ephesus. And a later son of the city, Isidore of Miletus, helped design the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople.

Before you leave, walk to the Ilyas Bey Mosque. It was built in the early fifteenth century by a local Turkish ruler of the Menteşe dynasty, a small principality that controlled this region before the Ottomans. The mosque is made of stone and marble, some of it taken from the ancient ruins. Look at the carved doorway and the marble panels at the entrance. Storks often nest nearby. It is a calm, graceful building, and fewer visitors linger here than at the theatre.

Now stand still for one last moment. Feel the soft ground under your shoes, where the sea once was. Look back at the theatre, its grey stone warm in the light. Philosophers argued here. Ships sailed out to distant coasts. An empire burned the city, and its people rebuilt it in straight lines. Then the river slowly took the harbour away. As you walk back to the road, listen again for the frogs. They are the newest residents of a very old city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to the theatre's top rows for the widest view.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and muddy paths.
Bring water and a hat; the plain has little shade.
Visit early morning before the heat and tour buses.
Combine with the Temple of Apollo at Didyma nearby.
