The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moving through rough grass. A cow shifting somewhere behind a hedge. The faint click of a gate latch. Under your boots the ground is soft, green, and a little uneven, as though the land itself has been folded and pressed by many hands. At first glance, this looks like ordinary Roscommon farmland. It is not. You are standing in one of the great royal landscapes of ancient Ireland.

This is Rathcroghan, known in old Irish texts as Cruachan. For many centuries it was remembered as the royal seat of Connacht, the western province of Ireland. Here there is no castle and no ruined palace. There are no great stone walls. Instead, the history lies in the shape of the ground: mounds, ring-shaped enclosures, ditches, earthworks, and burial places. They are spread across the fields between the villages of Tulsk and Castlerea's wider hinterland. You have to read the land the way you would read a page.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. Rathcroghan is not a single monument. It is a whole complex. Archaeologists have recorded well over two hundred individual sites in this area, all within a few square kilometres. Some are clearly visible. Others are only faint ridges in a field, or circles that appear in the grass during a dry summer. Many sit on private farmland. So the place you see is only a small part of what is really here.

The heart of the complex is Rathcroghan Mound. It is a broad, low, flat-topped hill. It feels less like a grave and more like a stage. In recent decades, archaeologists have studied it with geophysical survey. This method maps what lies below the surface without digging. The surveys showed that the mound is not empty earth. Beneath the grass there are traces of large circular enclosures and timber structures, built and rebuilt over long periods. This hilltop was a place of gathering and ceremony for generations.

Now, the stories. Cruachan is the home of Medb, often written in English as Maeve. In early Irish literature, she is the queen of Connacht. She is proud, clever, and very powerful. The most famous tale connected to her is the Táin Bó Cúailnge, the Cattle Raid of Cooley. It is one of the oldest epic stories in Western European literature. And according to the tale, the whole war begins right here.

The story opens with a conversation in bed. Medb and her husband Ailill compare their wealth. They count their jewels, their herds, their fine clothes. They are equal in everything except one thing. Ailill owns a magnificent white-horned bull. Medb has nothing to match it. So she sets out to take the great Brown Bull of Cooley from the province of Ulster. Armies march, heroes fight, and the land suffers. Remember, this is legend, not history. But the story has carried the name of Cruachan for more than a thousand years.

A short distance from the mound is the second surprise, and perhaps the strangest place in the whole landscape. It is called Oweynagat, which means the Cave of the Cats. From the outside, it is easy to walk past. There is a small, low opening at the edge of a field, under a hawthorn tree. Inside, a narrow passage built of stone leads down into a natural limestone cave. The stone-lined entrance is a souterrain, a type of underground passage found across early medieval Ireland.

Look up as you enter the passage, if you go with a guide. One of the lintel stones above the entrance carries an ogham inscription. Ogham is the earliest form of writing in Ireland. It is a system of short lines and notches cut along the edge of a stone. Scholars have read the inscription as a personal name. It is a quiet, very human detail: someone, long ago, wanted their name remembered at the mouth of this cave.

In folklore, Oweynagat was far more than a cave. It was said to be a doorway to the Otherworld, the land of spirits and gods in Irish tradition. Locals say that at Samhain, the ancient festival at the end of October, creatures came out of the cave and spread across the countryside. In one tale, fierce wildcats came out of the darkness to attack heroes. In another, the cave is linked to the Morrígan, a goddess of war and fate. Some people today connect these stories to the roots of Halloween.

Around the mound and the cave, other sites fill the fields. There is Relig na Rí, whose name means the cemetery of the kings. It is a large circular enclosure. Tradition says it was a burial place for rulers of Connacht, though this is not proven. There are standing stones, ring barrows, and linear earthworks that run across the land. A little to the south is Carnfree, a cairn long linked to the inauguration of the O'Conor kings of Connacht in medieval times.

It is worth thinking about what kind of place this was. It was not a town in the modern sense. People did not crowd into streets here. Instead, Cruachan seems to have been a ritual and royal centre. People gathered here for assemblies, ceremonies, fairs, and the making of kings. The land was shaped to impress, to mark power, and to honour the dead. Every mound you see took enormous effort to build, without metal machines, basket by basket of earth.

Today, the landscape is quiet and working. Cattle and sheep graze over ancient ditches. Farmers cut silage beside burial mounds. Stone walls and gates divide fields that once formed one great ceremonial space. That mix is part of the experience. Please close every gate behind you, keep to the paths, and give animals their space. The visitor centre in nearby Tulsk is the best place to start. It explains the sites and organises guided walks, including visits to the cave.

Before you leave, stand still on the mound for a moment. Turn slowly in a full circle. Notice how wide the sky is, and how far you can see across the flat Roscommon plain. Notice the small bumps and rings in the nearby fields that you did not see when you arrived. The kings and queens are gone, but the shape of their world is still under your feet. As you walk back to the road, listen again to the wind in the grass.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the visitor centre in Tulsk for maps and context.
Book a guided tour to enter the Cave of the Cats.
Wear waterproof boots; the fields are often wet and muddy.
Close every farm gate and keep clear of grazing livestock.
Bring a torch and expect to crouch inside the cave.
