The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Fountains run somewhere close by, traffic hums along the wide avenues, and children call to each other near the water. In front of you, a pale stone building rises in a broad, gentle curve. Its walls hold the soft colour of volcanic stone, and in late afternoon that colour turns warm, almost rose. This is the Yerevan Opera Theatre, and for many people here it is the true centre of the city.

Its full name is long. It is the Armenian National Academic Theatre of Opera and Ballet, named after the composer Alexander Spendiaryan. Most locals simply call it the Opera. When someone in Yerevan says, meet me at the Opera, they rarely mean a performance. They mean this square, these steps, these trees. The building works as a meeting point, a landmark, and a compass all at once.

The architect was Alexander Tamanyan, the man who drew the plan for modern Yerevan in the early twentieth century. Before his work, this was a modest provincial town with low houses and narrow lanes. Tamanyan imagined a capital built from local stone, with wide avenues, green rings, and grand public buildings. The theatre was one of the anchors of that vision. If you walk around the city, you will see his ideas almost everywhere.

Here is the first surprise. This is not one theatre, but two. Tamanyan designed the building with two separate halls placed back to back. One side holds the opera and ballet hall, named after Spendiaryan. The other side holds a concert hall, named after Aram Khachaturian, the composer best known abroad for his fiery Sabre Dance. Two audiences can sit inside at the same time, facing in opposite directions, listening to completely different music.

Look at the shape of the building. Instead of a flat front with a single grand entrance, the walls curve in a wide, rounded form. Tall columns and deep arches run along the facade, and the details are restrained rather than showy. Tamanyan blended classical ideas with forms taken from old Armenian architecture. Armenian churches and fortresses were built in solid stone with clean, strong lines, and you can feel that tradition here.

The first performance took place in the early 1930s, while the building was still being finished. The opening opera was Almast, written by Spendiaryan himself. The story is drawn from Armenian history and legend. Building work continued for many years after that first night, and the theatre took its complete form only after the Second World War. So the building grew slowly, much like the city around it.

The second surprise comes from far away. Tamanyan's design was shown at the international exhibition in Paris in 1937, where it won a top award. Think about that for a moment. A theatre for a small and distant capital, still under construction, was honoured in one of the great cities of Europe. For Armenians, that recognition still carries quiet pride.

Now turn and take in the open space around you. This is Freedom Square, and the name carries real weight. In 1988, huge crowds gathered here during the movement connected with Nagorno-Karabakh, a mountainous region at the heart of a long conflict. Day after day, people filled the square in enormous numbers. Those gatherings became part of the path toward Armenian independence a few years later. The place where people come for music also became the place where they came to speak together.

In front of the theatre you will find two seated statues. One shows Alexander Spendiaryan, the composer whose name the opera hall carries. The other shows Hovhannes Tumanyan, a beloved Armenian poet and writer of folk tales. Almost every Armenian child grows up with his stories. Placing a composer and a poet side by side here says something simple. In this country, music and words are seen as close companions.

Just nearby lies a small pond that locals call Swan Lake, a playful nod to the famous ballet. In summer, people sit along its edges and talk in the shade. In winter, the water often freezes and turns into a skating rink. Families glide around in circles while the theatre stands behind them. It is one of the easiest places to watch everyday life in Yerevan.

The streets around the Opera carry their own rhythm. Wide avenues run outward from the square, and the pedestrian Northern Avenue leads toward Republic Square. Cafes spread their tables under the trees, and in warm evenings the air fills with coffee, conversation, and sometimes music drifting from inside. Many locals walk a loop around the building after dinner. You are welcome to join them. Nobody hurries here.

If you can, see a performance. The theatre presents opera, ballet, and concerts, and the repertoire usually mixes international classics with Armenian works. Watching a ballet set to Khachaturian music in his own country is a special experience. Dress codes are relaxed but tidy, and many local people still treat a night at the Opera as an occasion. Check the current schedule in advance, because programmes change from season to season.

Before you move on, look once more at the curve of the stone walls. Notice how the light sits on the columns and how the building seems to hold the square in a gentle embrace. People pass in every direction, students, couples, grandparents with small children. For almost a century, this building has gathered the city around it. As you walk away, listen for the fountains and the voices. That sound, too, is part of the Opera.
