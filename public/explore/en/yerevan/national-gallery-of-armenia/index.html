The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the edge of Republic Square and feel the stone under your feet. The square is paved in smooth slabs, and around you rise walls of warm, rosy tuff. Tuff is a volcanic rock, soft enough to carve and common across the Armenian highlands. In the late afternoon it glows almost apricot. Somewhere nearby, water hisses from the fountains. Ahead of you is the long, arcaded building that holds the National Gallery of Armenia.

This gallery is one of the largest art collections in the whole Caucasus region. It began in 1921, only a few years after the upheavals that followed the First World War. In those early days the art section shared space with other collections of a young state museum. Over the following decades it grew, changed names more than once, and became the National Gallery in 1991, the year Armenia regained its independence.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. This building is shared. The History Museum of Armenia occupies part of the same complex, and the two institutions sit side by side on the square. So if you feel unsure about which door to use, you are not alone. Take a moment to check the signs before you go in. The gallery itself rises through several upper floors, and the climb is part of the experience.

Look at the square around you before you enter. Yerevan was largely replanned in the twentieth century, following a master plan by the architect Alexander Tamanyan. He wanted a city of pink and honey-coloured stone, with grand public spaces and arches that echo old Armenian churches. Republic Square is the heart of that vision. The gallery building belongs fully to this style — heavy, dignified, and made almost entirely of local stone.

Now step inside. The rooms are generous and often quiet, with tall walls and wooden floors that creak gently underfoot. Light falls differently on each level. The collection moves through many centuries and many countries. You will find medieval Armenian work, Russian painting, and European canvases. But the real reason to come here is Armenian art itself — the painters who tried to put this mountain landscape, and this people, onto canvas.

One name you will meet again and again is Martiros Saryan. He became one of the most loved Armenian painters of the twentieth century. Saryan painted with pure, bold colour — hot oranges, deep blues, violet shadows. His mountains look as if they have been baked in the sun. Stand in front of one of his landscapes, and then think about the light you just left outside. Many visitors say the two feel almost identical.

Another painter you may know without realising it is Hovhannes Aivazovsky. Much of the world knows him by his Russian name, Ivan Aivazovsky. He was of Armenian descent and became famous for enormous seascapes — storms, shipwrecks, moonlight on water. Here is the surprise. Armenia today has no coastline at all. Yet one of the great painters of the sea belongs, in part, to this landlocked country. Look for the way he makes light pass through a wave.

The nineteenth century brought other Armenian landscape painters too. Gevorg Bashinjaghian is one of them. He loved the high country, with its lakes, gorges and the distant shape of Mount Ararat. Vardges Sureniants is another. He painted scenes from history and legend, and he also worked as an illustrator. Their canvases are quieter than Saryan's. They ask you to slow down, to stand back, and to let your eyes travel slowly into the distance.

Mount Ararat appears in many paintings here. Today the mountain stands across the border in Turkey, but for Armenians it remains a national symbol. It appears on the coat of arms, on postcards, and on brandy labels. On a clear day you can see its snowy peak from parts of Yerevan. Notice how often painters place it on the horizon — sometimes huge, sometimes only a pale shape behind the fields.

Further on you may find the work of Minas Avetisyan, a painter from the Soviet decades. His colours are bold and flat, with deep reds and strong yellows. His figures are simple, almost like folk images. Many of his original works were lost in a fire, which makes the surviving canvases feel even more precious. Take your time in these later rooms. You can watch Armenian art move from realism toward something more personal and modern.

The European and Russian rooms offer a different rhythm. Here you can compare how artists from Italy, the Netherlands, France or Russia handled portraits, still lifes and religious scenes. Many of these works arrived in Yerevan during the Soviet period, when collections were moved between institutions. Looking at them in this setting is a little unusual. A Dutch interior or a Russian winter scene hangs only a few rooms away from a sun-bleached Armenian valley.

The gallery is not only a place for tourists. Local families, art students and school groups come here too. You may see a student sketching quietly on a bench, or a teacher speaking to a circle of children in front of a large canvas. These moments matter. For many Armenians, this collection is a kind of national family album. It holds their landscapes, their saints, their poets and their memories.

When you step back outside, pause again on the square. Look at the pink stone, the arches, and the fountains in front of the building. In the evening, those fountains often move with music and coloured light, and the square fills with people. Before you walk on, look once more at the colour of the walls in the fading sun. You have just seen that same warm light inside, on canvas after canvas... and now it is all around you again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check signs carefully to find the gallery's own entrance.
Start on the upper floors and work your way down.
Save some energy for the climb between floors.
Return to the square in the evening for the fountains.
Pair your visit with the History Museum next door.
