The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the wind moves through steel. It is a low, steady hum, like a string that never quite stops vibrating. Above you, red and white bands climb into the sky, crossing and recrossing in a dense web of girders. The air is thinner and cooler than in the streets below. On a clear day, the light here is sharp and almost metallic. Welcome to the Yerevan TV Tower, the tallest structure in Armenia.

Let us start with the numbers, because they matter here. The tower rises just over three hundred metres. That is taller than most buildings you will ever stand beside. It was completed in 1977, during the Soviet period, when Armenia was one of the republics of the Soviet Union. For a small, mountainous country, this was a bold vertical statement. It still holds the title of Armenia's tallest structure today.

Now look at how it is built. This is not a solid concrete shaft, like the towers in Moscow or Toronto. It is a lattice — an open frame of steel members bolted and joined together. Wind passes straight through it. That design keeps the structure lighter and helps it stand up to strong gusts. From a distance, the lattice can look delicate, almost like lace. Up close, you see how heavy and serious each beam really is.

The red and white paint is not decoration. It is a safety code. Aviation rules in many countries ask tall structures to carry bright alternating bands, so pilots can see them clearly in daylight. At night, warning lights take over that job. So the colours you see are a message to the sky. They simply say: something very tall is standing here.

The tower stands on high ground on the northeastern side of the city. This placement was practical. A broadcasting antenna needs height, and a hill adds height for free. From this position, signals can travel across the Ararat Valley and into the surrounding districts. For decades, much of what Armenians watched and heard on television and radio passed through the equipment on this structure.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The tower is not mainly a tourist attraction. It is a working piece of infrastructure. Unlike some famous towers around the world, there is no regular public observation deck here. Most people experience it from the outside, from its base or from viewpoints across the city. That changes the visit. You are not here to ride an elevator. You are here to look up.

And looking up is the real experience. Stand near the base and tilt your head back. The lines of steel narrow as they rise, and your eye follows them toward a single point. Clouds moving behind the tower can make it seem to lean or drift. This is an illusion, but a strong one. Many people feel a small moment of dizziness here. It is a good reminder of just how high three hundred metres really is.

The tower also works as a compass for the whole city. Yerevan is built in a kind of bowl, with the centre lower and the districts climbing up the slopes around it. From many streets and rooftops in the centre, you can spot this red and white needle on the skyline. Locals often use it as a landmark. If you get turned around in the city, look for the tower and you will know which direction is northeast.

Think about the era when it went up. The 1970s were a period of large construction across Soviet Armenia. New housing blocks, institutes, and public buildings spread across the hills. Much of central Yerevan is built from tuff, a soft volcanic stone in shades of pink, orange, and grey. This tower is different. It belongs to the world of engineering, not stonework. Steel, cables, and antennas, set against a city of warm stone.

Here is a second surprise. Because it stands on high ground, the tower is part of a wider view that includes Mount Ararat. Ararat is the great snow-capped mountain that holds deep meaning for Armenians. It stands across the border, in Turkey, but it dominates the southern horizon on clear days. From some viewpoints in the city, you can frame the modern tower and the ancient mountain in the same glance. Two very different kinds of height, sharing one sky.

If you want to see the tower at its best, think about the light. In the morning, the sun from the east strikes its red bands and makes them glow. In the late afternoon, the lattice can turn into a dark silhouette against a bright western sky. After sunset, the warning lights blink on, one by one, up the length of the structure. Each time of day gives you a different tower.

The area around the base is quieter than the city centre. You may hear dogs barking in nearby yards, cars climbing the hill, and that constant hum of wind in the steel. There is fencing around the technical areas, so respect the boundaries and do not try to enter. The best views are often not from right underneath, but from a little distance, where you can see the full shape from ground to tip.

This structure has watched the city change around it. It went up in one country and now stands in another, an independent Armenia. The signals passing through it have changed too, from a few Soviet channels to many modern broadcasts. Yet the tower itself looks much the same. It is a piece of the 1970s that still does its daily job, quietly, above everyone's heads.

Before you leave, take one more look. Follow a single red band from one side to the other. Notice how the steel catches the light, and how the sky shows through every gap. When you walk back into the city, keep glancing over your shoulder. You will see the tower again and again, between buildings and above rooftops... a tall, patient marker, standing over Yerevan.
