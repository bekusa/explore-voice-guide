The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the edge of Republic Square and look at the stone. It is tuff, a volcanic rock that Yerevan uses everywhere. Here it glows in shades of pink, apricot and pale cream. In the morning it looks almost soft, like baked clay. When the sun drops lower, the colour deepens toward rose. Behind you, traffic circles the oval square. In front of you, a long arcaded facade holds one of the oldest stories in this part of the world.

This is the History Museum of Armenia. It shares its grand building with the National Gallery, so the doors you see lead to two institutions under one roof. The museum itself dates to 1919. That year matters. Armenia had just declared a short-lived independent republic after the collapse of the Russian Empire. In the middle of war, hunger and refugees, the new state still decided that its past deserved a home. That choice tells you a lot about this country.

The museum grew through the Soviet decades that followed. Its collection expanded as archaeologists worked across the Armenian highlands. Today it holds hundreds of thousands of objects. They range from stone tools to medieval manuscripts, coins, carpets and textiles. You will not see everything in one visit, and you should not try. Think of the rooms as layers of soil. The deeper you go, the older the world becomes. Let one or two rooms hold your attention for real.

Start with the deepest layer. Armenia sits on a high volcanic plateau between the Black Sea and the Caspian. People have lived here since the Stone Age. Obsidian, a black volcanic glass, was one of their great resources. It breaks into edges sharper than steel. In the early galleries, look for blades and arrowheads that still shine like dark mirrors. Traders carried this glass far beyond the mountains, long before anyone wrote down a single word.

Now comes the first surprise. One of the most famous objects here is a shoe. It is made from a single piece of leather, laced over the top with a leather cord. Archaeologists found it in 2008 in Areni-1, a cave in the southern province of Vayots Dzor. It is about five and a half thousand years old. That makes it one of the oldest leather shoes ever found. When the team discovered it, the shoe was stuffed with grass. Nobody knows for certain why.

The same cave held evidence of very early wine making, with presses and storage jars. So when someone in Yerevan tells you Armenians have made wine for a very long time, they are not boasting. The ground backs them up. It is worth pausing over the shoe. Someone shaped that leather around a living foot. Someone walked in it across these same hills. Small, ordinary objects like this often bring the past closer than any crown or sword.

The second surprise sits in the Bronze Age rooms. Look for the wooden wagons from Lchashen, near Lake Sevan. These are real carts, with heavy wooden wheels, more than three thousand years old. Wood almost never survives that long. These carts did survive, and the reason is an unusual one. In the Soviet era, engineers drained part of Lake Sevan for irrigation and hydropower. As the water level fell, ancient burial grounds appeared along the old shore.

In other words, an environmental mistake opened a window into prehistory. The lake lost a great deal of water, and the region paid a price for that. But the falling shoreline also gave archaeologists tombs filled with wagons, bronze weapons and jewellery. Notice the spokes and the joints of the wheels. Notice how the wood has darkened and cracked. These were funeral vehicles, buried with important people. The craftsmanship shows a society that was already organised and skilled.

Next comes the kingdom of Urartu, an Iron Age state that ruled these highlands before the Armenians appear clearly in written records. Its kings built fortresses on rocky hills and carved inscriptions in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script of ancient Mesopotamia. One of their fortresses, Erebuni, stood on a hill in what is now southeastern Yerevan. Its founding inscription is the reason the city claims an age of more than two and a half thousand years. Look for bronze shields, helmets and cuneiform stones.

After Urartu, the galleries move into the classical world and then into Christian Armenia. In the early fourth century, Armenia adopted Christianity as a state religion. It was among the very first kingdoms to do so. Soon after, a monk named Mesrop Mashtots created the Armenian alphabet. Those letters are still in use today. You will see them on stone fragments, on coins and in manuscripts. You will also see them outside, on every shop sign in the square.

The medieval rooms carry a different mood. There is carved stone, painted pottery and metalwork from the trade routes that crossed these mountains. Armenia sat between empires, Persian, Byzantine, Arab, Mongol and later Ottoman and Russian. Each left marks here. Pay attention to the details on textiles and carpets. The deep reds often came from local dyes. Patterns repeat across centuries, because weavers passed them from mother to daughter rather than copying them from books.

A practical note for your visit. The museum is large, and the floors are hard stone. Wear comfortable shoes and take short rests. Labels are generally available in English, but a few rooms are quieter on information than others. Photography rules can vary by gallery, so check at the entrance before you raise your camera. If you also want to see the National Gallery, plan for a separate block of time. It is a full visit on its own.

When you step back outside, take a moment before you cross the square. The pink tuff around you was carved from the same volcanic land that produced the obsidian blades inside. Look at the arcades, the window frames and the carved ornaments. Their shapes echo the old churches and fortresses you just saw behind glass. In the evening, the fountains in front of the museum often come alive with music and coloured light. Listen to the water, and the voices around you... and the long story you just walked through feels very close.
