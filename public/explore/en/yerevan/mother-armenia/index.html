The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the wind up here. On this hill above Yerevan, the air moves more freely than in the streets below. It carries the faint smell of pine and dry grass from the park around you. Look up. A tall figure in dark copper stands against the sky, and she holds a sword. This is Mother Armenia, and she has watched over the city from this ridge for more than half a century.

Before we talk about her, notice her posture. She does not raise the sword like a warrior charging into battle. She holds it level, across her body, almost at rest. A shield lies near her feet. Many visitors read this as a statement of peace through strength. She is ready to defend, but she is not attacking. It is a calm kind of power, and it sets the tone for this whole place.

Now the first surprise. She was not the first figure to stand on this pedestal. When the monument was built in 1950, the figure on top was a giant statue of Joseph Stalin. It was raised as a victory monument after the Second World War. In those years, the Soviet Union filled its cities with images of the leader. Yerevan was no exception, and this hilltop gave him a commanding view of the Armenian capital.

Then the political mood changed. After Stalin's death, the Soviet leadership under Nikita Khrushchev began to criticise his rule. Across the country, his statues came down. Here in Yerevan, the figure was removed in 1962. Locals still tell stories about that removal. Some say it happened quietly at night, to avoid crowds. One popular tale says a worker died during the operation. These accounts are part of city memory, but treat them as folklore rather than settled history.

For a few years, the pedestal stood without a figure. Then, in 1967, Mother Armenia took the empty place. The sculptor was Ara Harutyunyan, an Armenian artist known for monumental work. He made her from hammered copper. Copper darkens with time and weather, which is why she looks almost bronze or black from below. Up close, the metal surface shows the marks and seams of its making.

Think about what that swap meant. A statue of a foreign ruler was replaced with a female figure who represents the nation itself. The image of a mother as a protector of the homeland has deep roots in Armenian culture. Armenians often speak of their land with family words. So this change was more than a new statue. In a quiet way, the city took back its own skyline.

Now look down at the base beneath her feet. The tall pedestal belongs to the original 1950 design, by the architect Rafael Israelyan. It is built of grey basalt, a dark volcanic stone. Many observers note that its long, solemn form echoes an Armenian church, specifically a basilica — a long hall with a central aisle. Israelyan was fond of national motifs. So even under Stalin, the base carried a clear Armenian character.

Here is the second surprise. The pedestal is not solid. Inside it, there is a military museum. It is run by Armenia's Ministry of Defence. The exhibits tell the story of Armenian soldiers in the Second World War and in later conflicts. You can see uniforms, documents, weapons, and personal photographs. Many families in Armenia lost someone in these wars, and the museum treats those stories with great seriousness.

As you walk around the monument, you will see old military machines on display. There are tanks, artillery pieces, and aircraft parked on the grass. Children often climb near them, and families take photographs. The contrast is striking. Heavy steel built for war now stands among picnic paths and trees. It gives the hilltop an unusual mood — part memorial, part public park.

This whole area is known as Victory Park. It was laid out to honour the end of the Second World War. Near the monument, you will find an eternal flame dedicated to the soldiers who did not come home. On national days of remembrance, people bring flowers here. On ordinary afternoons, the park is quieter. You may hear chess games, distant music from the amusement rides, and birds in the trees.

Now turn around and face the city. From this edge, Yerevan spreads out below you. Many buildings are made of tuff, a soft volcanic stone in shades of pink, orange, and grey. That is why Yerevan is sometimes called the Pink City. On a clear day, look to the horizon. You may see Mount Ararat, with its snowy peak rising above the plain. The mountain stands across the border in Turkey, yet it remains a powerful symbol for Armenians.

Notice how the statue and the mountain relate to each other. Mother Armenia looks out over the city in the direction of the land below. For many Armenians, the view from this hill carries both pride and longing. The capital is right here, close and alive. The mountain is visible, but beyond reach. Standing between the two, you can feel why this spot matters so much to local people.

Before you leave, take one more look up at her face. It is calm and serious, without anger. The copper is dark against the bright sky, and the sword stays level, as it has for decades. Listen to the wind again, and to the city sounds rising from below. As you walk back through the trees, turn around once. You will see her above the branches, still standing guard over Yerevan.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come on a clear morning for the best chance to see Ararat.
Walk uphill from the top of the Cascade to reach the park.
Allow extra time to visit the museum inside the pedestal.
Bring a light jacket; the hilltop is often windy.
Stand at the park's edge for a wide view over the city.
