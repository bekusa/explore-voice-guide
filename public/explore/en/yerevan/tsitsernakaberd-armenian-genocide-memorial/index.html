The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here on the hill, the noise of Yerevan drops away. You may hear wind moving through the trees, the scrape of shoes on stone, and perhaps quiet music drifting from the memorial ahead. The path under your feet is wide and pale. It leads you slowly toward a ring of dark grey stone. Nobody hurries here. Even children seem to lower their voices as they walk.

This is Tsitsernakaberd, the memorial to the victims of the Armenian Genocide. The name means something gentle — "swallow's fortress." It comes from the hill itself, which rises above the gorge of the Hrazdan River on the western side of the city. Long before the memorial existed, this was simply high ground with a wide view. Today it is the place where Armenians, and visitors from around the world, come to remember.

To understand why this hill matters, we need to go back to 1915. During the First World War, the Ottoman Empire began the mass deportation and killing of its Armenian population. Historians estimate that up to one and a half million Armenians died. Whole communities that had lived in Anatolia for centuries disappeared. Families were scattered across the Middle East, Europe, and the Americas. Many Armenians alive today descend from the survivors of those years.

The date that Armenians remember is the 24th of April. On that day in 1915, Ottoman authorities arrested hundreds of Armenian intellectuals and community leaders in Constantinople, the city we now call Istanbul. Writers, priests, doctors, and teachers were taken away. Most of them never returned. Armenians see that night as the beginning of the catastrophe. Every year on this date, this hill fills with people from early morning until late at night.

Here is a detail that surprises many visitors. This memorial was built during the Soviet era, when public talk about the genocide was discouraged. In 1965, on the fiftieth anniversary, large crowds gathered in Yerevan and demanded a monument. It was one of the largest public gatherings in Soviet Armenia. The authorities agreed. Construction followed, and the memorial was completed in 1967. So this place began not as an official gift, but as a public demand.

Now walk toward the ring of stone. Twelve large slabs of grey basalt lean inward in a circle. Basalt is a volcanic stone, and Armenia has a great deal of it. Many people say the twelve slabs stand for the lost provinces where Armenians once lived. The slabs do not touch at the top. They lean toward each other like people bowing their heads, leaving the centre open to the sky.

Step down into the circle. The floor sits lower than the ground around it, so you descend as you enter. This small movement changes how the space feels. The city disappears behind the stone. In the middle, an eternal flame burns. Around it, you will almost always see flowers — red carnations, white roses, sometimes a single tulip. People place them in a ring around the flame, and the ring grows thick on the 24th of April.

The heat of the flame is real. On a cold day you can feel it on your face from the edge of the circle. The stone around you is darker near the bottom, where hands, rain, and time have touched it. Take a moment here before you move on. Many Armenians stand in silence, cross themselves, or simply look at the fire for a minute or two.

When you leave the circle, look up at the tall spire beside it. It is a pointed column of stone, split down the middle by a narrow gap. One popular interpretation says the two parts stand for the Armenian nation divided — those who live in Armenia today and the large diaspora spread across the world. The two halves rise side by side and never quite meet. From far across the city, this spire is the part of the memorial you can see.

Along the path, there is a long wall carved with names. These are not the names of people. They are the names of towns and villages where Armenians lived and died. Some will be familiar from history books. Others are small places that no longer have Armenian residents. Reading them one by one gives a sense of geography — of a whole world of homes, churches, and markets that once stretched far to the west.

Nearby is the Armenian Genocide Museum-Institute. You might miss it at first, because it is built into the slope of the hill. The architects kept it low on purpose, so it does not compete with the memorial above. Inside, there are photographs, documents, and personal objects from the period. The exhibits are serious and can be hard to see. Give yourself time, and do not feel you must view everything.

Outside, walk along the avenue of trees. Foreign leaders and visiting delegations have planted many of them over the years, often with a small plaque at the base. This is a quieter part of the hill, and many visitors miss it. Then turn toward the edge of the hill. On a clear day, you can see Mount Ararat on the horizon, with its snowy peak. For Armenians, the mountain is a national symbol, though today it stands across the border in Turkey.

Before you go, stand still once more. Notice the grey stone, the green of the trees, the white peak far away, if the sky allows it. Notice the people around you — some here for the first time, some returning as they do every year. As you walk back down the hill, the sounds of Yerevan will slowly return. Traffic, voices, ordinary life. That return to everyday life is part of the visit too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a flower to place around the eternal flame.
Dress modestly and keep voices low inside the circle.
Come on a clear morning for views toward Mount Ararat.
Allow at least an hour for the museum exhibits.
Expect very large crowds on April 24 each year.
