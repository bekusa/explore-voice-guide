The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in the entrance passage and listen. Behind you, the old city of Lahore carries on: motorbike horns, a shopkeeper calling out, the clatter of a handcart over brick. In front of you, the sound drops away. The air feels cooler here. Look up at the walls, and you will see colour everywhere — glazed tiles in cobalt, turquoise, saffron yellow and green, set into the brick like a carpet that someone has pinned to the stone.

This is the Wazir Khan Mosque, and it sits deep inside the walled city, not far from Delhi Gate. It was built in the 1630s and early 1640s, during the reign of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan. Shah Jahan is the same ruler who built the Taj Mahal in Agra. His reign is often described as the high point of Mughal architecture, and this mosque is one of the finest examples of that period anywhere in Pakistan.

The mosque takes its name from its patron. He was a physician from the Punjab named Ilm-ud-din Ansari, who rose through the Mughal court and became governor of Lahore. The court gave him the title Wazir Khan, a title meaning roughly a high minister. He did not only build a place of prayer. He also built a public bathhouse nearby, known today as the Shahi Hammam. Together, these buildings show a governor who wanted to leave his mark on the daily life of the city.

Now notice where you entered. The mosque does not stand alone in an open square. Instead, a row of small shops is built into the front of the complex, along the street and around the entrance chowk, or square. This was a deliberate plan. The rent from these shops was meant to pay for the upkeep of the mosque. It is a very practical idea — a religious building that helped to fund itself through the commerce at its gate.

The street outside also matters. The mosque stands along an old route that people call the Shahi Guzargah, or Royal Road. This route ran from Delhi Gate toward the Lahore Fort. Emperors and their processions once passed this way. So the builders placed the mosque where it would be seen by everyone travelling to the seat of power. Its tiled facade was not hidden away. It faced the busiest road in the city.

Let us look more closely at the tiles, because they are the reason many people come here. The technique is often called kashi kari, a local name for glazed tile work linked to Persian traditions. Small pieces of coloured, glazed tile were cut and fitted together to form patterns. Some panels show flowers in vases. Others show cypress trees, vines, and geometric stars. From a distance, the walls look painted. Up close, you can see the joins between each small piece.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Not all of the decoration is tile. Inside the prayer hall and in the entrance areas, much of the colour is painted directly onto plaster. This is fresco work, where pigment is applied so that it becomes part of the wall surface. The painted ceilings and arches carry fine floral designs and calligraphy. Look up as you walk under the arches, and you will find some of the most delicate work in the whole building.

Calligraphy appears almost everywhere. Panels of Arabic and Persian script run along the walls, around the arches, and across the facade. Many contain verses from the Quran. Others carry Persian inscriptions that name the patron and mark the building as his gift. For people who cannot read the script, it still works as design — long, flowing strokes in white or yellow against a deep blue ground. The writing is part of the architecture, not an addition to it.

Step into the main courtyard. It is wide, paved, and open to the sky. In the centre there is a pool for ablution, the ritual washing before prayer. At the four corners of the courtyard rise tall minarets, decorated with tile and brick. The prayer hall stands on the far side, facing west toward Mecca. Its roof carries a row of domes. When the light is low, the facade of the hall glows with colour.

Here is a second surprise. The mosque was not built on empty ground. Inside the courtyard there is the tomb of a Sufi saint, Syed Muhammad Ishaq, who is also known locally as Miran Badshah. His shrine is older than the mosque itself. The builders kept it in place and built the mosque around it. You may see people pausing there to pray or to show respect. It is a reminder that this site was holy before the Mughals arrived.

Over the centuries, the mosque suffered. Damp, salt in the walls, layers of later paint, and the pressure of the crowded city all took their toll. Shops and buildings pressed in around it. In more recent years, conservation teams have worked here, including the Aga Khan Trust for Culture together with the Walled City of Lahore Authority. They cleaned surfaces, repaired tile work, and cleared parts of the square in front. Some walls still show patches and missing tiles, which tell their own story.

Remember that this is a working mosque. Prayers take place here every day, and the call to prayer still rises from the minarets over the rooftops. Visitors are generally welcome outside prayer times. You will need to remove your shoes, and modest clothing is expected. Women usually cover their heads. Move quietly near people who are praying. If you are unsure where to walk, a caretaker at the entrance is often happy to point the way.

Before you leave, stand in the courtyard and turn slowly in a full circle. Look at the minarets against the sky, the domes on the prayer hall, the flowers in the tile panels, the saint's tomb in its quiet corner. Then walk back out through the gate. As the noise of the old city returns, notice the shops at the entrance. They are still trading, much as the builders planned, nearly four centuries later.

Before you go

Practical tips

Enter from the Delhi Gate side to walk the Royal Road.
Avoid prayer times so you can move around freely.
Wear modest clothing and carry a scarf for covering hair.
Bring socks, since you walk barefoot across the courtyard.
Combine your visit with the nearby Shahi Hammam bathhouse.
