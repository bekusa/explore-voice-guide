The guide

The story

Take off your shoes, and feel the stone. Under bare feet, the courtyard is warm in the afternoon and cool in the early morning. The colour is a deep, dusty red. Around you, the space opens so wide that people on the far side look small. Pigeons lift and settle. Somewhere, a child laughs, and the sound carries a long way across the open ground. This is the Badshahi Mosque, in the heart of old Lahore.

The name itself tells you who built it. Badshahi means royal, or belonging to the emperor. The emperor was Aurangzeb, the sixth great ruler of the Mughal dynasty, a Muslim empire that governed much of South Asia for more than two centuries. He ordered the mosque in the later seventeenth century, and it was completed in the early 1670s. Aurangzeb was known for his personal piety, and this building was meant to show both faith and power.

Look back toward the direction you came from. Across a garden square stands the Lahore Fort, the old seat of Mughal power in the city. The mosque and the fort face each other on purpose. On one side was the emperor's palace. On the other was the house of prayer. The garden between them is called the Hazuri Bagh, and it ties the two together into one royal space. Standing here, you can still read that plan clearly.

Now turn to the building itself. The main material is red sandstone, a stone quarried in the region and used again and again by Mughal builders. Against that red, white marble is set in lines, panels and borders. The effect is sharp and clean. Up close, you will see floral shapes, geometric patterns and delicate frames around the arches. From a distance, the walls look plain and strong. Only when you walk near do the fine details appear.

Above the prayer hall rise three domes of white marble. They look almost soft against the red walls below, like clouds resting on stone. At the four corners of the courtyard stand tall minarets, the slender towers from which the call to prayer was traditionally given. Each one rises in stages, with small balconies, and ends in a light open pavilion at the top. Together, the domes and minarets give the mosque its outline against the Lahore sky.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. For a long period, this was one of the largest mosques in the world. The courtyard alone can hold tens of thousands of worshippers at once. On the great festival days of Eid, the whole space fills with rows of people standing shoulder to shoulder. Try to picture it now... the silence before the prayer, then thousands of people bowing together in one movement.

Walk toward the prayer hall at the western end. Muslims pray facing Mecca, which lies to the west from here, so the hall stands on this side. Step under the great central arch and the light changes at once. It becomes softer and cooler. Inside, look up at the ceilings and the upper walls. You will find carved stucco, painted designs and patterns in fine plaster. Many of these surfaces were carefully restored in the twentieth century.

That restoration matters, because the mosque has not always been treated as a holy place. In the early nineteenth century, Lahore came under the rule of the Sikh Empire, led by Maharaja Ranjit Singh. During this time, the mosque was used for military purposes, and parts of it are reported to have housed soldiers and animals. Later, under British rule, it was also used as a garrison. Only afterwards was it returned to worship and slowly repaired.

The repairs took many decades. Work began under British rule and continued for years after, well into the middle of the twentieth century. Craftsmen replaced damaged stone, renewed the marble inlay and rebuilt fallen details. So when you look at these walls, you are seeing two layers of skill at once. One layer is Mughal, from the seventeenth century. The other is modern, from the workers who brought the building back to life.

Now a second surprise, waiting near the entrance steps. Just outside the main gate stands a modest tomb. It belongs to Allama Muhammad Iqbal, the poet and philosopher whose ideas helped shape the dream of Pakistan. He is honoured across the country as a national poet. Many Pakistani visitors pause here first, before they enter the mosque. The tomb is small and quiet, a strong contrast to the huge scale of the building beside it.

Look around the edges of the old city, too. Close by stand reminders of the Sikh era, including the memorial of Ranjit Singh and a Sikh shrine, a gurdwara, with its golden details. In the Hazuri Bagh, a white marble pavilion was added during Sikh rule. So within a few hundred metres, you can see Mughal, Sikh and British history side by side. Lahore has been ruled by many powers, and each one left its mark here.

This is still a working mosque, not only a monument. Worshippers come for daily prayers, and the call to prayer still rings out from the old towers. Visitors should dress modestly, with arms and legs covered. Women are usually asked to cover their hair, and scarves are often available at the entrance. Speak softly near the prayer hall. If prayers are in progress, wait at the edges of the courtyard and simply watch. It is a moment many travellers remember.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot in the courtyard and stand still for a minute. Notice how the red stone holds the light. Listen to the pigeons, the footsteps, the low voices of families. Look up at the white domes, then turn once more toward the fort across the garden. As you walk back through the gate, the noise of the city will slowly return... but the calm of this open courtyard often stays a little longer.
