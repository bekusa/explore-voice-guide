The guide

The story

Listen first. Pine needles shift underfoot, a woodpecker taps somewhere above, and the Baltic wind moves through the birches. Look down at the ground. Among the moss and loose gravel, small stones catch the light. Some are pale pink, some glassy white, some flecked with tiny dark grains. This quiet slope on the island of Resarö looks like any other corner of the Stockholm archipelago. Yet more chemical elements carry the name of this one small village than of any other place on Earth.

The name Ytterby means something like the outer village. That fits. Resarö lies just north of the old fortress town of Vaxholm, where the inner archipelago opens toward the sea. For centuries, people here lived from fishing, farming and the water traffic heading to and from Stockholm. Nothing about the landscape suggested it would one day appear in chemistry textbooks around the world. The story starts with what lies under the surface: an old granite rock called pegmatite, which formed from slowly cooling molten rock deep underground.

Pegmatite is a coarse rock with large crystals. The two most valuable parts for people here were quartz and feldspar. Feldspar is a pale mineral, often pink or cream, and it was useful for making porcelain and glazes. So a mine was opened in this hillside, and workers broke out the rock by hand and with explosives. The quarry worked for a long time, and it finally closed in the twentieth century. But the miners were not the people who made Ytterby famous.

In 1787, a Swedish army officer and keen amateur mineral collector named Carl Axel Arrhenius visited the quarry. Among the light-coloured rock he noticed a heavy, black, shiny stone. It looked different from everything around it. He took samples away, and the stone began to travel through the scientific circles of Europe. This is the first surprise of Ytterby. One curious man picked up one odd stone, and the history of chemistry changed direction because of it.

The black mineral reached Johan Gadolin, a chemist working in Turku, in present-day Finland. In the 1790s he analysed it carefully. He found that it contained a new earth, which was the old word for an oxide, a substance unknown to science at that time. It became known as yttria, after the village. The mineral itself was later named gadolinite in his honour. Gadolin did not know it yet, but his new earth was not one substance. It was a mixture.

This is where the story becomes a long detective case. Through the nineteenth century, chemists returned again and again to the Ytterby minerals. The elements inside them were extremely similar to each other. They behaved almost the same way in every test. Separating them took patience, repeated work, and sometimes years of effort. Each time a chemist split one substance into two, a new element appeared, and the scientists often reached back to the village for a name.

The result is remarkable. Four elements take their names directly from Ytterby. The first is yttrium. Then came terbium and erbium, separated by the Swedish chemist Carl Gustaf Mosander in the 1840s. Later in the century came ytterbium. Just say them aloud — yttrium, terbium, erbium, ytterbium. You can hear the village name being cut and rearranged each time. Chemists were running out of new variations, so they used the same root again and again.

The second surprise is that the story does not stop at four. Other elements found in the same family of minerals were named after related places and people. Holmium takes its name from the Latin name for Stockholm. Thulium comes from Thule, an old name for the far north. Gadolinium honours Johan Gadolin himself. Scandium, named for Scandinavia, also first came from minerals found in this region. So this one hillside sits at the centre of a whole family of names.

Most of these elements belong to a group today called the rare earths. The name is a little misleading, because several are not truly rare in the Earth's crust. They are simply difficult to separate. And today they are everywhere in modern life. Rare earths are used in strong magnets, lasers, phone screens, fibre-optic cables, medical imaging and electric motors. Somewhere near you, perhaps in your pocket, there is probably a small trace of the chemistry first opened up by stones from this slope.

Now look around at the site itself. The old mine is not a grand monument. It is a rough opening in the rock, with tailings, the leftover broken stone, spread across the ground. Trees have grown back over much of the area. Near the site there is a plaque that marks Ytterby as an important place in the history of materials science. Chemists and science lovers come here on quiet pilgrimages, sometimes from very far away, simply to stand where the story began.

The neighbourhood carries the story too. Walk the residential lanes nearby and you may notice street signs with names taken from the elements discovered here. It is a small, charming detail. Families live their everyday lives on roads named after substances most people only meet on a periodic table poster. Red and ochre wooden houses sit among the pines, with boats, gardens and summer furniture. The science history and the ordinary island life sit side by side.

Please treat the place with care. The old quarry is not a museum with staff and ropes. The rock can be loose, and the edges of old workings can be steep. Some of the land around the area is private, and people live close by. Stay on paths, keep to the public areas, and leave the stones where they lie. The tailings here have been picked over by collectors for generations, and the site belongs to everyone who comes after you.

Before you leave, pick a spot and stand still for a moment. Notice the pink feldspar and the grey quartz in the rock beside you. Hear the wind from the water and the gulls over the bay. More than two centuries ago, a man stood somewhere near here and saw a black stone that did not belong. As you walk back toward the road, keep your eyes on the ground... you are walking across one of the most important patches of gravel in the history of science.
