The guide

The story

Put your hand on the nearest boulder. The granite is rough and warm, almost grainy, like coarse sand pressed into stone. Around you, huge rounded rocks lie stacked on the hillside as if a giant dropped them and walked away. The wind moves through the dry grass. Below, the roofs of Prilep spread out across the plain. This is Markovi Kuli, which means Marko's Towers, the medieval fortress that once guarded this part of the Pelagonia valley.

Before we talk about kings and battles, take a moment with the rock itself. These strange shapes are not carved by people. Wind, rain, frost and heat have worked on this granite for a very long time. The stone breaks along its natural lines and wears down into rounded domes, cracked towers and balanced blocks. Many visitors come for the history and leave talking about the landscape. Look closely and you will see where the builders used the boulders as part of the walls.

That is the first surprise of this place. The fortress does not sit on top of the hill like a crown. It grows out of it. Medieval masons fitted their stone walls into the gaps between the natural rocks. In some places a wall runs straight into a boulder and simply stops, because the boulder was already a wall. The hill was a defence before anyone laid a single stone here, and the builders understood that very well.

People lived on this hill long before the medieval towers. Archaeologists have found traces of settlement here from ancient times, and the high ground made sense for anyone who wanted to watch the valley. From up here you can see the roads and fields of the Pelagonia plain, one of the largest farming plains in this region. Whoever held this hill could see trouble coming from a long way off.

The name, however, belongs to one man. In the fourteenth century this fortress was a seat of power for King Vukashin and then for his son, Marko. In historical terms, Marko was a regional ruler during a hard time. The large Serbian empire was breaking apart, and the Ottoman Turks were advancing through the Balkans. Marko ruled from Prilep, but he ruled in a world that was changing fast around him.

History gives Marko a complicated ending. He became a vassal of the Ottoman sultan, which means he kept his lands but owed loyalty and military service to the Ottomans. He died in 1395 at the Battle of Rovine, fighting on the Ottoman side. After his death, the fortress and the town passed into Ottoman control. It is not the ending of a typical hero story, and that makes what happened next even more interesting.

Here is the second surprise. The historical Marko was a minor ruler who served a foreign sultan. Yet in folk memory he became the greatest hero of South Slavic epic poetry. For centuries, singers across the Balkans told stories about Prince Marko. In these songs he is huge, brave and very strong. He drinks wine, fights monsters and defends ordinary people against cruel rulers. The real man and the legendary hero are almost two different people.

Legend gives Marko a loyal companion as well — his piebald horse, Sharats. According to the old tales, the horse could talk, drink wine with its master and leap across mountains. Locals say that some of the marks and hollows in the rocks around here come from Marko or his horse. Remember that these are folk stories, not history. But standing among these odd boulders, you can understand why people imagined a giant once walked here.

Now look along the ridge for the remains of the walls. Time and weather have not been kind, and much of what you see is ruin. Parts of the fortification line still follow the shape of the hill, and you can trace them from rock to rock. Notice how the stone in the walls is smaller and more irregular than the granite boulders. That difference helps you see where nature ends and human work begins.

At the foot of the hill lies Varosh, the oldest part of Prilep. In the Middle Ages the town grew here, close to the protection of the fortress. Several old churches and a monastery stand in this area, and some of them hold medieval wall paintings. If you have time after your climb, walk down through Varosh. The quiet lanes give you a sense of the town that once lived under Marko's towers.

The modern city below has its own story. Prilep is well known in North Macedonia for tobacco, and the fields of the surrounding plain have grown it for generations. In late summer you may notice long strings of drying leaves hanging outside houses in the villages. The fortress looks down on a landscape that is still shaped by farming, as it was when Marko's soldiers stood watch.

As you walk, take care on the paths. The granite can be smooth and slippery in places, and the trails between the boulders are uneven. Good shoes help a lot. There is little shade on the hill, so the climb feels very different in the summer heat than on a cool spring morning. Late afternoon is a fine time to be here, when the low sun turns the rocks gold and the shadows grow long.

Before you go, find a flat rock and sit for a moment. Look out over the rooftops, the fields and the far mountains at the edge of the plain. A king once watched this same view while an empire collapsed around him. Then singers turned him into a giant. Feel the rough granite under your hand once more, listen to the wind in the grass, and carry a little of Marko's legend back down the hill with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven granite paths.
Bring water because the hillside has very little shade.
Climb in late afternoon for softer, golden light.
Combine your visit with a walk through old Varosh below.
Avoid the smooth rocks after rain when they get slippery.
