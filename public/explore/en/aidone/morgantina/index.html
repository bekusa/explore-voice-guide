The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days the loudest thing here is the wind. It moves through dry grass and across pale stone, and somewhere a bird calls from the ridge. Under your feet the ground is uneven, part earth and part worn limestone. Around you the hills of central Sicily roll away in browns and greens. This quiet ridge was once a crowded Greek city. Its name was Morgantina.

You are a short drive from the hill town of Aidone, in the interior of Sicily. This is not the coast of postcards and beaches. It is farming country, high and open, with wheat fields and olive trees. That landscape matters to the story. Morgantina grew rich from grain, and much of what you see around you was built to store it, trade it, or give thanks for it.

The story begins with the Sikels. They were one of the native peoples of Sicily, living on the island before Greek colonists arrived. Their early settlement sat on a nearby hill that archaeologists call the Cittadella. Over time, Greek culture spread inland from the coast. Pottery, gods, building styles, and language mixed with local traditions. Later, the city moved to this longer ridge, known today as Serra Orlando, where most of the ruins stand.

Now look toward the open heart of the site. This was the agora, the public square of a Greek city. Here people bought and sold, argued, voted, and gossiped. The space is unusual because it sits on two levels. An upper terrace and a lower terrace are joined by a wide flight of stone steps. Many visitors walk past those steps without a second thought... but they are one of the most interesting things here.

The steps form a three-sided stairway, shaped a little like a fan. Archaeologists believe it served as a place for public assembly. In Greek, this kind of meeting place is called an ekklesiasterion — a space where citizens gathered to hear speakers and make decisions. So when you stand at the bottom, you are standing where a speaker might have stood. Imagine the steps filled with people, all listening, all ready to shout their opinion.

Not far away is the theatre. Its curved rows of seats are cut into the slope, and they face out over the valley. It is small compared with the great theatres of Syracuse or Taormina. That makes it feel more personal. Sit on one of the stone benches for a moment. Notice how the shape of the hill carries sound. A voice from the stage would reach the top rows without much effort.

Along the edge of the agora you will find long, narrow foundations. These were public buildings, including storehouses for grain. They are a reminder of what powered this city. The fields you can see from here fed Morgantina, and surplus grain could be sold. Grain was wealth in the ancient Mediterranean. It bought silver, art, and fine houses — and the houses on the hillsides show that some families here lived very well.

Religion was close to the land too. In and around the city there were sanctuaries for Demeter and her daughter Kore, also called Persephone. Demeter was the goddess of grain and harvest. Kore was linked to the underworld and to the return of spring. In grain country, these two goddesses were at the centre of daily faith. Excavations here found many small clay figures and offerings left by worshippers asking for a good harvest.

The city's fortunes changed during the wars between Rome and Carthage. Morgantina had long been within the sphere of Syracuse, the most powerful Greek city on the island. During the Second Punic War, Syracuse and many of its allies turned against Rome. Rome won. According to the Roman historian Livy, in 211 BC the Romans handed Morgantina to Spanish mercenaries who had fought on their side. Life continued for a time, but the city slowly faded and was eventually abandoned.

Here is the first surprise. For centuries, almost nobody knew exactly where Morgantina had been. Ancient writers mentioned it, but the ruins lay under fields and scrub. Systematic excavations began in the 1950s, led by archaeologists from Princeton University in the United States. Their work identified the site and uncovered the agora, the theatre, the houses, and the sanctuaries. Excavation and study have continued here for decades, often with international teams.

The second surprise is a story of loss and return. Some of Morgantina's finest treasures were dug up illegally and sold abroad. A large statue of a goddess, a set of fine silver vessels, and other objects ended up in major museums in the United States. After long investigations by Italian authorities, these pieces came back to Sicily in the years after 2000. Today they are displayed in the archaeological museum in Aidone, just up the road.

That goddess statue is worth the trip. It is built in a technique called acrolithic, where the head, hands, and feet are carved in marble, and the body is made of a different stone. Scholars still debate who she represents. She was once called a Venus, but many now think she may be Demeter or Persephone. Seeing her after walking these ruins helps everything connect. The objects were made for streets and temples you have just walked through.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Look along the line of the agora steps, then up to the theatre, then out across the fields toward Aidone on its hill. The grain still grows. The wind still crosses the ridge. As you walk back, notice the stones under your shoes. Some of them were cut and set by people who lived, traded, and prayed here more than two thousand years ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Aidone archaeological museum after the ruins.
Bring sun protection; the ridge has very little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and dirt paths.
Come early morning for cooler air and softer light.
Sit in the theatre to test its acoustics.
