The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the wind moves across open ground with almost nothing to stop it. Underfoot, the ground crunches softly. That pale, yellow-white carpet spreading across the slopes is lichen, dry and brittle on a sunny day. It looks almost like frost, even in summer. Around you, rounded grey mountains rise in every direction. They are bare, quiet, and very old. Welcome to Rondane, seen here from the Folldal side.

Rondane holds a special place in Norway's story. It became the country's first national park in 1962. That decision marked the start of a national park system that now stretches from the far south to the Arctic north. The park was enlarged in 2003, and today it covers close to one thousand square kilometres of mountain plateau, valleys and high peaks. Folldal is one of several municipalities that share it. This northern edge is quieter than the busier southern approaches, and many visitors feel it keeps more of the park's original stillness.

The mountains themselves are the first thing to understand. Rondane has ten peaks above 2,000 metres. The highest is Rondeslottet, whose name means something like the castle of Rondane. It reaches about 2,178 metres. Yet these summits do not look jagged like the Alps. They are broad and heavy, with steep walls cut into their sides. Those deep bowls, called cirques, were carved by small glaciers. During the last ice age, ice filled these valleys and ground the land into the shapes you see now.

Look down at the valley floors and you may notice odd humps, ridges and hollows. Some are long gravel ridges left by rivers that once ran underneath the ice. Others are small pits that formed when blocks of buried ice slowly melted away. Geologists value this region for these traces of the ice age. To a walker, they simply make the ground feel uneven and alive. A gentle path can rise over a ridge and drop into a hidden pond without warning.

Now, here is something many visitors miss. Rondane is one of the drier mountain areas in Norway. The high ranges to the west catch much of the rain coming in from the Atlantic. By the time the weather reaches here, it has often lost much of its moisture. That is why the lichen grows so thickly. In wetter mountains, mosses and grasses would crowd it out. Here, the dry climate lets the lichen spread across whole hillsides.

And that lichen matters, because it feeds the true residents of this park. Rondane is home to wild reindeer. Not the herded, semi-domestic reindeer of northern Scandinavia, but wild mountain reindeer. Norway holds the last significant populations of this wild animal in Europe. Lichen is their winter food. They dig for it through the snow with their hooves. The national park exists, in large part, to protect the quiet open space these animals need.

If you see reindeer, consider yourself fortunate. They are shy, and they move in groups across huge distances. Disturbance is a real problem for them. A herd that is frightened again and again will abandon good grazing land. So the best thing a visitor can do is stay on marked trails, keep dogs close, and watch from a distance. Binoculars are your friend here. A grey-brown herd against a grey-brown slope can be surprisingly hard to spot.

People have followed these reindeer for thousands of years. Across the Rondane region, archaeologists have recorded old trapping systems. Hunters dug pits into the ground along the animals' migration routes, sometimes with stone walls or fences to guide the herd. Some of these pits are still visible as shallow hollows in the terrain. When you walk here, you may be crossing the same paths that hunters watched long before any road or cabin existed. The landscape still carries those traces of human patience.

Folldal itself has a different story beneath the surface. For centuries, this municipality was known for copper mining, which began in the eighteenth century. Mining shaped the villages, the roads and the lives of the families who settled here. It is a reminder that this quiet mountain country was never empty. People farmed the valleys, grazed animals on summer pastures, mined the rock and hunted on the plateau. The national park protects the high ground, but the human history wraps all around it.

Rondane has also shaped how Norwegians imagine their own mountains. In the early twentieth century, the painter Harald Sohlberg created a work called Winter Night in Rondane. It shows the peaks under a deep blue sky, cold and still, with a single star above. The painting is often described as one of the best-loved images in Norwegian art. Standing here at dusk, you can understand why. The rounded summits really do take on that blue, almost glowing calm.

Light changes everything in this park. On a clear summer evening, the sun moves slowly and the shadows stretch across the plateau for hours. The lichen turns gold, then pale silver. In autumn, the dwarf birch and low shrubs turn red and orange, and the whole ground looks as if it has been painted. In winter, snow smooths every edge, and the mountains become simple white shapes against the sky. Each season gives you a different park.

The weather can also change quickly. A bright morning can turn to cold wind, low cloud or sleet within an hour, even in midsummer. Experienced hikers here carry warm layers, a windproof jacket and a map, whatever the forecast says. The trails are generally well marked, often with stone cairns and painted red letters. But visibility on a cloudy plateau can drop fast, and every cairn starts to look the same.

Before you move on, take one more slow look around. Notice how little sound there is, apart from the wind and perhaps running water somewhere below. Notice the pale lichen at your feet, growing slowly, a few millimetres each year. Notice the long grey line of the mountains. Rondane does not try to impress you. It simply stays as it is, and it asks you to do the same, quietly, for a little while.

What to look for

Pale yellow-white reindeer lichen carpeting the slopes.
Red-painted letters and stone cairns marking the trails.
Steep glacier-cut bowls on the sides of peaks.
Shallow hollows that may be ancient reindeer trapping pits.
Long gravel ridges left by rivers beneath ice.
Before you go

Practical tips

Pack a windproof layer even on sunny summer days.
Bring binoculars to watch reindeer from a distance.
Stay on marked trails to avoid disturbing wildlife.
Carry a paper map in case clouds roll in.
Visit at evening for long, low summer light.
