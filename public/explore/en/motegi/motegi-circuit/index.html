The guide

The story

Before you see anything, you hear it. A high, rising whine comes through the trees, then fades, then rises again. Out here in the hills of Tochigi, the forest is quiet between laps. Then an engine opens up somewhere beyond the grandstands, and the sound rolls across the valley like weather. This is Motegi, a racing circuit cut into wooded countryside, a long way from any big city skyline.

The venue you are standing in is now called Mobility Resort Motegi. For most of its life, it carried a different name, Twin Ring Motegi. It took that new name in 2022. Many racing fans still use the old one, and you will hear both around the paddock. The change says something about the place. It was never meant to be only a racetrack. It was planned as a whole landscape built around machines, movement, and the people who love them.

The circuit belongs to Honda, through its leisure and motorsport company. That ownership shapes everything here. Honda began as a small engine and motorcycle maker in post-war Japan, and grew into one of the world's largest makers of motorcycles. Motegi opened in 1997 as a kind of home ground for that story. It is a place to test, to race, and to show the public what the company has built over the decades.

Now, the old name. Why a twin ring? Because Motegi was built with two separate tracks. One is a winding road course of nearly five kilometres, with hairpins, fast curves, and heavy braking zones. The other was a high-speed oval, the kind of track more common in the United States than in Asia. The two tracks share the same ground, and the road course passes through and around the oval's space. Very few circuits in the world were designed this way.

That oval brought American open-wheel racing to Japan. From the late 1990s, the CART series raced here, and later the IndyCar Series took over. Drivers ran flat out on the banked surface, only metres from the walls. For Japanese fans, it was a rare chance to see this American style of racing at home. For American teams, it was a long trip across the Pacific to a forest in Tochigi.

Here is a moment many visitors do not know about. In April 2008, at the Indy Japan 300 on this oval, Danica Patrick took the win. It was the first time a woman had won a race in the IndyCar Series. The victory came here, far from the American tracks where she was best known. Racing history often happens in unexpected places, and this quiet corner of Japan holds one of those firsts.

Then, in March 2011, the great earthquake struck eastern Japan. Motegi was damaged, and the oval suffered serious harm. That year, the IndyCar race still went ahead, but it moved to the road course instead. After that season, IndyCar did not return. The oval has not hosted major racing since. So when you look across the site, part of what you see is a track that the earthquake brought to a stop.

The road course, however, is very much alive. It has hosted the Japanese round of the MotoGP world championship since 2004, and it held other world championship motorcycle races before that. Motorcycle fans know Motegi as a stop-and-go circuit. Riders brake hard, turn, and accelerate again, over and over. Several world champions have clinched their titles on this tarmac, and the grandstands fill with flags and team colours on race weekends.

Listen for the difference between the machines. A MotoGP bike has a sharp, screaming note that rises fast. The touring cars of the Super GT series sound deeper and heavier. The single-seat cars of Super Formula, Japan's top open-wheel category, sit somewhere in between. If you visit on a test day or a race weekend, you can often tell what is on track before it comes into view.

But Motegi is not only for people with earplugs. One of the quieter treasures here is the Honda Collection Hall. Inside, you can walk past rows of Honda motorcycles and cars from across the company's history. There are small early machines, famous road bikes, and racing cars from different eras, including Formula One cars. Many visitors come for a race and leave talking about this museum instead.

The collection is a good way to understand the circuit itself. Out on the track, everything moves too fast to study. Inside the hall, the same kinds of machines stand still. You can look at a wheel, a fairing, an engine, and see how the shapes changed over time. Engines grew more complex. Bodies grew lower and smoother. The museum gives you time that the racetrack never does.

The forest around the circuit is part of the plan, too. Motegi has an outdoor area called Hello Woods, built for families and children. It has walking paths, nature activities, and space to play among the trees. It feels a long way from the pit lane, even though it shares the same hillside. On a calm day, you can stand among the trees and still catch the distant sound of engines.

Take a moment where you are. Notice the green hills on every side, and the long lines of grandstands and fences. Somewhere nearby, the old oval still curves across the land, silent now. On the road course, the next machine may already be on its way around. As you walk on, listen for that rising whine through the trees... and think of everyone who has raced here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the event calendar before visiting for race weekends.
Bring ear protection if cars or bikes are on track.
Set aside extra time for the Honda Collection Hall.
Plan a car or bus trip; the circuit is rural.
Bring layers; hillside weather can change quickly.
