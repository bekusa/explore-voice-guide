The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the long grass, and somewhere below a sheep calls across the valley. Under your boots the ground is uneven, all turf and grey stone, and the air up here tastes of rain even on a dry day. You are standing on a ridge in Northumberland, close to the old frontier of an empire. Ahead of you, low walls run across the hillside like the outline of a sketch. This is Housesteads, one of the best preserved forts on Hadrian's Wall.

The Romans knew this place as Vercovicium. Most scholars translate the name roughly as the place of good fighters, though the exact meaning is still debated. Construction of Hadrian's Wall began in the 120s, on the orders of the Emperor Hadrian, to mark the northern edge of Roman Britain. Housesteads was added a few years after the Wall itself was started. Its builders used the line of the Wall as the fort's northern side, so the frontier and the fort share one wall.

That timing leaves a small trace that many visitors walk past. Excavators found the foundations of an earlier Wall turret inside the fort. A turret was a small watchtower, and this one stood here before the fort existed. When the plans changed, the soldiers simply knocked it down and built over it. It is a quiet reminder that the Wall was not a single perfect design. It was a working project, adjusted again and again as the army learned the ground.

And what ground it is. Look north. The fort sits on the Whin Sill, a hard band of volcanic rock that runs across this part of England. The rock breaks into sharp crags and cliffs, and the Wall rides along the top of them. From here the soldiers could watch a wide sweep of open country. On a clear day you can see for many kilometres. On a grey day, the clouds sit almost on the ramparts, and the frontier feels very far from Rome.

Now look at the shape of the fort itself. It is a rectangle with rounded corners, like a playing card, which was the standard Roman plan. There was a gate on each side, and the main streets crossed in the middle. The fort was built for a garrison of around eight hundred soldiers. For much of its later life, the unit here was the First Cohort of Tungrians. These were auxiliary troops originally raised from a region that lies in modern Belgium.

That detail changes the picture. The men who stood guard on these walls were not all Italians. Many came from across the empire, and auxiliary soldiers could earn Roman citizenship after long service. Inscriptions from the area also mention other units with Germanic origins. So imagine this hilltop full of different accents and different gods, all under one army and one set of rules. The frontier mixed people together as much as it kept people out.

Walk toward the centre and you will reach the headquarters building, the principia. This was the administrative heart of the fort. Here the unit's standards were kept, along with the pay chest and the records. Beside it stood the commanding officer's house. It was built around a courtyard in a Mediterranean style, which must have felt strange in a northern English winter. The commander likely lived here with his family and household, in some comfort compared with his men.

Nearby you will find the granaries, and they are worth a close look. Their floors were raised on rows of short stone pillars. Air could flow underneath, which kept the grain dry and made life harder for rats. You can still see many of these pillars standing in neat lines. Grain was the base of the soldiers' diet, so these buildings were vital. A fort could survive a long hard season only if these stores stayed full.

Now for a part of the fort that most people remember longest. In the south east corner, at the lowest point of the site, are the latrines. They are among the best preserved Roman latrines in Britain. Soldiers sat side by side on wooden seats over a deep channel, with water flowing beneath. A smaller channel ran in front of their feet. Many historians think they used a sponge on a stick to clean themselves, though that idea is still discussed. Privacy, clearly, was not a priority.

As you move around, look down at the gateways. At some of them, the stone thresholds still carry grooves worn by the wheels of carts. These marks were made slowly, by years of traffic going in and out. Supplies, timber, fuel and people all passed through these gaps. It is one of the simplest details on the site, and one of the most human. Someone drove a cart through here, again and again, long before your visit.

Outside the south gate, the hillside held a civilian settlement, which archaeologists call a vicus. Traders, craftspeople and families lived here, serving the garrison. One building has a darker story. Excavators found two skeletons buried beneath its floor, and one of them had the tip of a knife lodged between the ribs. Burying the dead inside a settlement went against Roman law. It is often called the Murder House, and the case has never been solved.

The story does not end with Rome. After the Roman army left Britain, the fort slowly fell into ruin. In later centuries, this border country was a lawless place, known for raids and cattle theft. Parts of the old fort were reused, and a fortified farmhouse was built into the area of the south gate. Stone from the Roman walls also found its way into local buildings. The site you see today is the result of long study and careful excavation.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the northern wall. Feel the wind come up over the crags, the way it did for the guards on night watch. Look west, where the Wall climbs and dips along the ridge toward the far hills. As you walk back down the slope, notice the stones at your feet, the curve of the ramparts, and how small the fort becomes behind you. People served here, lived here, and made this cold hilltop their home.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep uphill walk from parking.
Bring a waterproof layer, as weather changes fast on the ridge.
Visit the site museum first to understand the fort's layout.
Walk a short stretch of the Wall west for ridge views.
Come early in the day for a quieter, emptier site.
