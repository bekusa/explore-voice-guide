The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Water laps against a pebble shore. A woodpecker hammers somewhere deep in the forest. The air smells of damp moss, wet bark and cold mountain water. Around you, the lakes shift between deep blue and a clear, glassy green. This is Los Alerces National Park, in the Andes of Argentine Patagonia. It is a place built by ice, rain and time.

The park sits in Chubut Province, close to the Chilean border. The nearest towns are Esquel and Trevelin, both in the Futaleufú Department. Argentina protected this land in the 1930s, during the same period it created several other large Andean parks. The goal was clear. These forests held something rare, and logging was already a threat. Today the park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 2017 for its forests, lakes and wildlife.

The park takes its name from one tree, the alerce. Scientists call it Fitzroya cupressoides. The name honours Robert FitzRoy, the captain of HMS Beagle, the ship that carried the young Charles Darwin around South America. In English the tree is sometimes called the Patagonian cypress. It has soft, reddish, fibrous bark that peels away in long strips. Its small, stiff leaves grow in tight groups along the twigs.

Here is the first surprise. The alerce is among the longest-living trees on Earth. It grows very slowly — sometimes only a millimetre or two of trunk width in a year. Some trees in this park have lived for thousands of years. The most famous one grows on the far side of Lake Menéndez, and its age is estimated at well over two thousand years. That tree was already old when the Roman Empire fell. To reach that ancient grove, most visitors take a boat trip across the lakes, followed by a short forest walk.

That slow growth made the wood very valuable. Alerce timber is light, straight-grained and very resistant to rot. For generations, people in this region split it into roof shingles and building planks. Across the border in Chile, heavy logging removed huge areas of alerce forest. Because the trees grow so slowly, a felled forest cannot recover within a human lifetime. Part of what you see here exists because someone drew a line on a map and stopped the saws.

Now look at the water. The park holds a chain of glacial lakes, linked by short, clear rivers. Their names include Futalaufquen, Menéndez, Rivadavia, Verde and Krüger. Glaciers dug these deep basins during the last ice age. When the ice melted, water filled them. The colours come from depth, from light, and from very fine rock dust carried down from the mountains. On a sunny day, the shallows near the shore can look almost tropical.

The forest here is wet in a way that surprises many visitors. Patagonia is often imagined as dry, windy steppe. That steppe does exist, just a short drive east. But on this western side, moist air arrives from the Pacific Ocean. The air rises over the Andes and drops heavy rain and snow. That moisture feeds a dense, temperate rainforest, with ferns, mosses and climbing vines. Driving east from the park, you can watch the forest thin out and turn into open grassland within a short distance.

Along the rivers and lakeshores, watch for a tree with smooth, cinnamon-orange bark. This is the arrayán, a relative of the myrtle. Its bark feels cool to the touch, and it often shows pale, patchy spots where old layers have peeled away. Nearby you will see thick stands of caña colihue, a native bamboo. Its tall, straight canes crowd the forest edges and the trail sides. Here is the second surprise. This bamboo flowers only after many years, then the whole stand dies back at once.

The park is also home to shy animals that many visitors never see. The huemul is an endangered Andean deer, and it appears on Chile's national coat of arms. The pudú is one of the smallest deer in the world, roughly the size of a small dog. Both hide in thick forest. You are more likely to hear the Magellanic woodpecker. The male has a bright red head. Its knocking can carry far through the quiet trees. Look up, too — Andean condors sometimes ride the air above the ridges.

People lived in this landscape long before it became a park. Indigenous peoples, including Mapuche and Tehuelche communities, travelled, hunted and lived in these valleys. Near Lake Futalaufquen, ancient painted rock art survives on stone surfaces. The designs are mostly geometric shapes in red tones. Their exact meaning is not fully known. Later, European settlers arrived in the region, including Welsh families who founded farms around Trevelin. Their tea houses still welcome travellers in the town today.

In the south of the park, the landscape changed in the twentieth century. Engineers built the Futaleufú hydroelectric dam, which created a large reservoir called Amutui Quimey. The dam produces electricity for an aluminium industry on the Atlantic coast. Its waters covered older valleys and lakes. For some visitors, this is an uncomfortable part of the story. A protected park also contains one of the region's largest industrial structures. It shows how protection and development often sit side by side.

The small settlement of Villa Futalaufquen serves as the main base inside the park. Here you can find the visitor centre, rangers and information about trails and boat trips. Paths range from short lakeside walks to long hikes up steep, forested slopes. Weather changes fast in these mountains. A bright morning can turn into cold rain by afternoon. Even in summer, the lake water stays very cold, because much of it comes from snowmelt.

Before you move on, take one more slow look around you. Notice the line where the dark forest meets the bright water. Notice the snow on the higher peaks, and the quiet between the bird calls. Some trees around you were seedlings long before any map named this place. They will likely stand long after today's visitors are gone. As you walk away, listen for the woodpecker, feel the cold air off the lake... and let the forest stay quiet behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the lake boat trip to reach the ancient alerce grove.
Pack a rain layer even on sunny summer mornings.
Stop at Villa Futalaufquen visitor centre for trail advice.
Base yourself in Esquel or Trevelin for easy access.
Bring binoculars for condors circling above the ridges.
