The guide

The story

Red walls rise high above you, curving away in both directions. The stone is warm to the touch where the sun has reached it. Far below the wall, the river moves slowly. You pass through a heavy gate, and the noise of the road fades behind thick sandstone. Inside, the air feels older and cooler, and your steps begin to slow.

This is Agra Fort, and it is not only a fort. Behind these walls sit palaces, halls, gardens, and mosques, built across several reigns. For many years it served as the main home of the Mughal emperors, before the court moved away to other cities. The red sandstone gave the whole place its strength and its deep, warm color.

The emperor Akbar rebuilt this fort in red sandstone. The work began in 1565 and took about eight years to complete. Before that, an older brick fort stood on the same ground, worn down and broken. Akbar wanted something far stronger. The walls he raised run for more than two kilometers around, and stand over twenty meters tall above the ground.

Walk along the high wall and look out. From here the guards could see anyone coming for a great distance. A wide ditch once circled the base of the wall. Old stories say it held water, and even animals, to stop attackers. Only one narrow, twisting path led to the inner gate, so no enemy could ever rush straight inside.

Inside, the styles begin to change as you move. Akbar built in solid red stone, plain and heavy and strong. His grandson, Shah Jahan, loved white marble instead. So you walk from dark red courtyards into bright marble rooms, and you can feel two different tastes, two different emperors, set side by side within a single wall.

Find the wide hall called the Diwan-i-Am. This was the place of public audience. The emperor would sit raised above the crowd, and ordinary people could bring their troubles and their requests to him. Rows of arches hold up the roof. Stand in the open court below and imagine it full, with hundreds of people waiting for a single word.

Deeper in lies the Diwan-i-Khas, the hall of private audience. Here the emperor met chosen guests and envoys from far away. The marble is carved with fine flowers and set with pieces of colored stone. This is where great matters of the empire were settled quietly, away from the eyes and the ears of the wider court outside.

Look for the small tower of marble called the Musamman Burj. It stands on the river side and catches the breeze. Its walls are carved like screens, so air and light can pass through them. From this tower there is a long, clear view across the bend of the river, toward a famous white tomb in the far distance.

That tower holds a sad story. In his old age, Shah Jahan was removed from power by his own son. He was held here, inside the very fort he had helped to build. From the marble tower he could see the Taj Mahal, the tomb of his lost wife. He spent his final years looking at it from afar, a prisoner in his home.

Notice the mix of patterns as you go. Some carvings show flowers and clean, open shapes. Among the red stone you may spot signs of older designs, kept from buildings that stood here before. The fort was changed again and again, each ruler adding new rooms, gates, and gardens to suit his own needs and his own eye.

Like the great tomb across the river, the fort is now a World Heritage site. Much of it is open to visitors, though some military parts are still closed off. Guides wait near the main gate, ready to tell its long story. The red stone is cleaned and repaired so that it can stand for many more years to come.

Before you leave, rest a hand on the warm red wall. These stones held emperors at their highest, and one emperor at his lowest, kept prisoner within his own home. Power rose here and then slipped away, as it always does in the end. The river still moves below, slow and patient, long after the kings are gone.
