The story

Before you even reach the tomb, you pass through a gate so large that it could be a palace on its own. Red sandstone glows warm all around you. Four white marble towers rise from its corners. Deer move slowly across the wide lawns, and monkeys sit watching on the warm stone. The grave of an emperor lies somewhere ahead, deep within the green.

This is the tomb of Akbar, the third and perhaps the greatest of the Mughal emperors. It stands at Sikandra, a short way outside the city of Agra. Akbar ruled for nearly fifty years, a very long reign. He widened the empire by war and by marriage, and, more unusually, he tried to bring its many faiths into peace with one another. It was a bold and rare aim for any ruler of his age.

Akbar began the tomb himself, during his own lifetime. This was a common Mughal habit. A ruler would start his own resting place and then watch it slowly rise from the ground. He died in 1605, before the work was finished. His son, Jahangir, completed it for him, and the whole building was largely done by about 1613.

The shape is unlike the great domed tombs nearby. There is no single high dome here at all. Instead the building rises in tiers, like a low stepped pyramid, with each level a little smaller than the one below it. Red sandstone forms the heavy lower floors. The open top level is built of white marble, bright and clear against the wide sky.

Look hard at the great gateway as you pass beneath it. Four slim white towers rise from its corners toward the sky. These are among the earliest minarets of their kind in all Mughal work, and the same idea would later grow into the famous four towers of the Taj Mahal. The gate is richly patterned with inlaid stone in bold, clear shapes.

The decoration carries a quiet message. Among the patterns you can find signs drawn from many different faiths, set together side by side on purpose. This matched Akbar's own deep belief. He held that the different religions of his land each carried some part of the truth, and he tried, with mixed success, to draw them all toward common ground.

The garden around the tomb is vast, laid out in the fourfold charbagh plan. Long paved paths cross it in straight, clean lines toward the center. Water channels once ran beside them, catching the light. The space is wide and open, so the eye travels far before it finally reaches the green edges and the old red walls that close it in.

Watch the animals closely as you walk. Spotted deer graze on the open lawns without fear of people. Black-faced langur monkeys leap along the high walls and the rooftops. Peacocks call out from the trees, sharp and sudden in the quiet. The garden is partly a calm park now, alive with creatures that have grown used to the slow stream of visitors.

Walk into the cool heart of the building. A long sloping passage leads down to the real grave, set in a plain and high stone chamber. After all the bright color and pattern outside, this inner room is bare and simple. The plainness feels chosen, not poor. Voices drop low here, and a single sound can ring against the smooth stone walls.

Think about who lies here. Akbar himself could not read well, yet he gathered scholars, artists, poets, and holy men of every kind to his court. He sat and listened to long debates between them late into the night. The man who built no single lasting faith still shaped how a huge and mixed land would learn to live together.

Time has not been gentle to every part of it. Long ago the tomb was robbed and badly damaged during unsettled, warlike years. Some of the old surface and treasure was lost for good. What remains is now guarded and repaired with real care. The red stone is cleaned, the loosened inlay reset, and the wide gardens kept green and open for all.

Before you go, turn and look back at the rising tiers of red and white. An emperor who spent his whole life trying to hold many peoples together chose, in the end, a tomb without a single crowning dome. It rises in calm, even steps, and then it simply stops. Even the greatest of reigns, it seems to say, comes quietly to rest.

Key facts

Holds Akbar, the third Mughal emperor
Begun by Akbar, completed by Jahangir around 1613
Akbar died in 1605 after nearly fifty years ruling
Built in tiers of red sandstone and marble
Located at Sikandra, outside the city of Agra

What to look for

Four white marble towers on the great gateway
The stepped tiers rising without a central dome
Inlaid patterns drawn from several different faiths
The sloping passage down to the bare grave

Practical tips

Watch for deer and monkeys across the lawns
Go early for soft light and cool air
Carry water for the long walk through gardens
Keep food hidden, as monkeys may grab it
