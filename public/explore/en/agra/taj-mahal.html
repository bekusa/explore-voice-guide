The guide

The story

The white marble seems to change color while you watch it. Soft pink at dawn. Plain bright white under the noon sun. Pale gold as the evening comes on. You stand at the tall red gateway, and the tomb waits far ahead, at the end of a long pool of water. It is calm and still, and the crowd around you grows quiet without being told.

This building is the Taj Mahal, and it is a tomb. The Mughal emperor Shah Jahan built it for his wife, whose name was Mumtaz Mahal. She died in 1631, while giving birth to their fourteenth child. The emperor was crushed by her loss. He chose to build her a resting place that no one would ever forget, a gift to a woman who was already gone.

Work began around 1632. The central tomb took roughly ten years to raise. The wider complex, with its gateway, its gardens, and its mosque, took about twenty years to finish. Many thousands of workers came to the city of Agra to build it. White marble was carried from Makrana, in Rajasthan, dragged across the land on carts pulled by oxen and elephants.

Walk a little closer along the path. The garden ahead is cut into four equal parts by channels of water. This pattern has a name. It is called a charbagh, a fourfold garden. In the old idea, it stands for paradise, with four rivers flowing from a center. The long pool holds the reflection of the tomb, so you see the white shape twice, once in stone and once in water.

Look now at the four tall towers that stand around the raised platform. They are called minarets. Each one leans a tiny bit outward, away from the central tomb. The builders did this on purpose. If an earthquake were ever to topple them, they would fall outward, away from the grave, and not crush it. The makers were thinking of dangers many lifetimes ahead.

Bring your eyes close to the walls. The flowers you see there are not painted on. Each one is made from colored stone, cut into small pieces and set into the marble surface. This craft is called parchin kari. A single bloom may hold dozens of tiny fragments, in yellow, red, green, and deep blue. The pieces fit so tightly that the joins nearly vanish.

Around the great arched doorway runs a wide band of black writing. These are sacred verses, set into the stone by a calligrapher named Amanat Khan. He shaped the letters with a clever trick. The higher letters are made larger than the lower ones. So from where you stand on the ground, every line looks the same size. Your eye is gently and kindly fooled.

Step inside, and the light turns soft and grey. At the center stand two carved tombs, wrapped in a screen of marble cut as fine as lace. But these are not the real graves. The true resting places lie in a closed chamber one floor below. Mumtaz Mahal lies at the exact middle of the building. Her husband was later laid down at her side.

And here is a small secret to carry with you. The whole building is balanced with great care, the left side matching the right. But the tomb of Shah Jahan himself breaks that balance. It was added later, set off to one side of his wife. It is the single part of the entire Taj Mahal that does not follow the rule of symmetry.

The emperor did not always see this view from the garden. In his last years, his own son took the throne and held him inside the nearby fort. From a small marble tower there, across the bend of the river, the old man could still see the tomb of his wife. He watched it from far away, day after day, until his own death.

Today the Taj Mahal is guarded as a World Heritage site. Cars and buses are kept well back, so the air near the marble stays cleaner. You come the last stretch on foot, or on a quiet electric cart. Teams wash and mend the stone year after year, holding back the slow marks of weather, dust, and time.

Stay one more moment before you turn to go. Watch the color move across the marble again. A man raised all of this because he could not keep the person he loved most. Grief was turned into stone, and the stone was made calm. That quiet is the thing you take with you, long after the gates close behind you.

Key facts

Built by emperor Shah Jahan for wife Mumtaz Mahal
Mumtaz Mahal died in 1631 during childbirth
Construction began around 1632, complete after about twenty years
White marble was carried from Makrana in Rajasthan
Named a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1983
Look closer

What to look for

Inlaid stone flowers set into the white marble walls
The four minarets leaning slightly away from the tomb
Black calligraphy framing the tall arched doorway
Shah Jahan's off-center tomb breaking the symmetry inside
Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive at dawn to see soft pink marble
Leave large bags behind; many items are banned inside
Walk to the river side for a quieter view
Remove shoes or wear cloth covers inside the tomb
