The guide

The story

The wind moves through empty courtyards here. Red sandstone stretches in every direction, warm and glowing under the sun. Your footsteps echo across open squares where a royal court once gathered in its thousands. This is Fatehpur Sikri, a whole city built and then left behind. It stands about forty kilometers west of Agra, raised along a low rocky ridge above the plain.

The emperor Akbar founded this city. He made it his capital in 1571. For about fourteen years, the empire was ruled from these halls and courtyards. Then, around 1585, the court moved away again. People say the water supply ran short and could not serve so many. Whatever the true cause, the city was left almost exactly as it stood.

That is why it feels so whole today. Most old cities are built over again and again, each age erasing the last. This one simply stopped. The buildings you walk through are close to what the Mughals themselves knew. The name means the City of Victory. Akbar chose it after his military campaigns in the west of the country.

There is a story behind the founding. Akbar had power and wealth but no surviving son to follow him. He visited a holy man, a Sufi named Salim Chishti, who lived on this ridge. The saint foretold the birth of an heir. When a son was indeed born, Akbar built his new capital here in gratitude. Faith, the story says, laid the first stone.

Begin at the great gateway, the Buland Darwaza. It rises in a steep cliff of red stone faced with white marble. It is counted among the tallest gateways anywhere in the world. Akbar raised it to mark his victory in Gujarat in 1573. Climb the long flight of steps toward it and feel how small the high, dark doorway makes you as you pass beneath.

Pass through into the wide courtyard of the mosque. Near one side stands a small, low tomb of white marble, bright against the surrounding red. This holds the saint, Salim Chishti. Look closely at the screens around it. The marble is cut into fine lattice, thin almost as cloth, in patterns of stars and waves. Light passes through and falls in soft shapes on the floor.

Visitors still tie threads to these lattice screens. Each knotted thread carries a wish or a prayer, most often for a child. People have done this for centuries, and they do it still today. The white marble seems to glow against the red stone of the rest of the city. Of all the buildings here, this one alone remains fully alive with daily use.

Walk now into the palace area beyond the mosque. Find the hall called the Diwan-i-Khas, the hall of private audience. Inside stands a single great carved pillar at the very center. Its top spreads into a wide round platform, joined to the four corners by narrow stone bridges. The emperor is said to have sat up there, above the men who came to debate and to plead.

Nearby rises the Panch Mahal, a palace of five open floors. Each storey is smaller than the one below, held up by rows of carved columns, and open on every side to the air. There are no walls here, only pillars and shade. The wind passes straight through. It was built as a place to catch the breeze and to look out over the gardens and rooftops.

Move between the courtyards and look closely at the carving. The red sandstone has been cut to imitate wood, cloth, brackets, and beams. Craftsmen drawn from many regions worked here side by side. You can see Indian, Persian, and Central Asian forms standing together in one wall, much as the many peoples of the empire stood together in Akbar's court.

Find a quiet corner and sit for a while. Listen. There is wind, and birdsong, and the distant voices of other visitors. Now picture these same courtyards full of life: horses and grooms, traders and scholars, cooks, guards, and servants. For fourteen years this was the beating heart of an empire. Then the people walked away, and the wind took their place.

It is worth remembering how short that time was. A great city, planned in fine detail and built in lasting stone, served its purpose for barely more than a decade. Empires move their centers for reasons of water, war, and trade. The stone stays where it was set. Fatehpur Sikri is what remains when the power that made it has gone elsewhere.

As you leave, look back across the red rooftops and gateways. A city built upon a promise, raised in stone, and then quietly set down again. It was not burned or thrown down by enemies. It was simply left. That makes Fatehpur Sikri rare among old places. Walk out slowly, and let its silence stay with you on the road back toward Agra.
