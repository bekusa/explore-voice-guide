The guide

The story

Start with the stone. Pale limestone, almost white in strong sun, turning honey-coloured as the light drops. Run your eye along the walls and you will see the fine lines between the blocks, cut and fitted by hand. Up here on the plateau the air is thinner and cooler than in the valleys below. On a quiet day you hear mostly wind, a few birds, and your own footsteps on the paving.

You are standing before Ghazanchetsots Cathedral, also known as the Cathedral of the Holy Saviour. It was built as a church of the Armenian Apostolic faith, one of the oldest national churches in Christianity. Construction ran through the second half of the nineteenth century, roughly from the late 1860s into the late 1880s. For generations it was the main church of the Armenian community of this town, which Armenians call Shushi and Azerbaijanis call Shusha.

The name itself carries a small story that many visitors miss. Ghazanchetsots means, roughly, belonging to the people of Ghazanchi. Ghazanchi was a village far to the southwest, in the region of Nakhichevan. Families from there settled in this town, and the church took their name. So the building does not honour a saint in its everyday name. It honours a community of migrants who made a new home on this rocky height.

Look at the overall shape. The plan follows a cross, with a large central dome raised on a tall drum. This is a classic arrangement in Armenian church architecture, where the dome sits at the meeting point of the four arms. Here the proportions are large and upright. The walls are thick and the windows narrow, which keeps the interior cool in summer. From a distance, the dome makes the cathedral visible above the rooftops around it.

Near the main building stands a separate bell tower. Freestanding bell towers like this appear at a number of Armenian churches of this period. Take a moment to compare the two structures. Notice how the tower repeats the same pale stone, and how it gives the whole site a second vertical line against the sky. Standing between them, you can feel how the builders arranged the space for processions and gatherings.

Now for a harder part of the story, because this building has lived through a great deal. In 1920, during violent conflict in the region, the Armenian quarter of the town was largely destroyed and much of its population was killed or fled. The cathedral survived, but it lost its congregation. A church without people to fill it changes in character, and for decades this one stood mostly silent.

During the Soviet period, religious life across the region was heavily restricted. Like many churches in the Soviet Union, this one was not used for worship. Accounts say it served practical purposes, including storage. Here is something that surprises many visitors: for much of the twentieth century, this large sacred building was treated simply as useful covered space, with its role as a church set aside.

In the early 1990s, the town became a battleground in the first war over Nagorno-Karabakh. Armenian forces took control of it in 1992. In the years that followed, the cathedral was repaired and returned to use as a working church. Pilgrims and visitors came again, and services were held under the dome. For many Armenians it became one of the most important symbols of the town.

In October 2020, during the second war over the region, the cathedral was struck and damaged. A second strike on the same day injured journalists who had come to report on the first. Images of the damaged interior travelled around the world. Shortly after, under the ceasefire agreement of November 2020, the town came under the control of Azerbaijan, which considers Shusha one of its most important cultural centres.

Since then, the building has been the subject of works that Azerbaijani authorities describe as restoration. These works have drawn international attention and debate, particularly about changes to the dome and the building's Armenian character. Depending on when you visit, you may find scaffolding, closed areas, or a changed appearance. It is worth simply observing what is in front of you today, and remembering that different communities read this place in very different ways.

If you can step closer, look at the surfaces carefully. Limestone weathers in a soft, uneven way. You may see darker patches where rain has run for years, and lighter patches where stone has been cleaned or replaced. Look too for marks of repair, where new blocks sit beside older ones. Every layer of work on a building like this leaves a trace, and here there are many layers.

Then turn around and look at the town itself. Shusha sits on a high plateau, with steep cliffs dropping away on several sides. That position made it a natural fortress for centuries, and it explains why so many powers have wanted to hold it. From the edges of the town you can see deep green gorges and distant ridges. On clear days the views reach a long way across the mountains of the South Caucasus.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the stone under your feet and the cool air moving across the plateau. Look up once more at the dome, or at the place where it rises against the sky. This building has been a church, a storehouse, a symbol, and a target. Whatever you see in it today, you are standing in a place that many people, on all sides, carry deeply in their memory.
