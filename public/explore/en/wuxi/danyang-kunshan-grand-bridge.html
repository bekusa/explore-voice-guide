The guide

The story

Listen for it before you see it. A low, rising hush, like wind moving through a field of rice. Then a white train slides across the sky above the paddies, smooth and nearly silent, and is gone. Beneath it, the grey concrete piers stand in a long, patient line. They march across the flat green land of southern Jiangsu until they fade into the haze. This is the Danyang–Kunshan Grand Bridge, and you are standing near one small part of it.

Here is the first surprise. Most visitors expect a bridge to cross something, a river or a bay. This one mostly crosses land. It is a railway viaduct, a raised track on pillars, and it carries the Beijing–Shanghai High-Speed Railway through the Yangtze River Delta. It runs for roughly 164 kilometres. For comparison, that is longer than the drive from many cities to the next big city. Since its completion, it has held the Guinness World Record as the longest bridge on Earth.

Why build a bridge across dry ground? The answer is under your feet. This region is a delta, a wide plain built from river mud over thousands of years. The soil is soft and wet. Canals, ponds, and fish farms cut through it in every direction. A track laid directly on this ground would settle and shift. A train at very high speed needs a track that stays perfectly level, mile after mile. So the engineers lifted the railway into the air.

The bridge also saves space, and here space is precious. The delta is one of the most densely populated and farmed parts of China. A raised viaduct takes only the narrow footprint of its piers. Farmers can keep working the fields beneath it. Roads, canals, and villages can pass underneath. Look around you now, and you may see exactly that: rice, vegetables, or water, carrying on in the shadow of the line.

The structure was built in the late 2000s and opened to passenger trains in 2011, along with the rest of the Beijing–Shanghai line. Construction moved at a pace that surprised many observers abroad. Much of the deck was made from large, precast concrete sections. Crews cast these box-shaped beams in factories near the route, then lifted them onto the piers one by one. The method is repetitive by design. Every span looks like the next, and that sameness made fast, precise work possible.

The name tells you the route. Danyang lies to the west, near Zhenjiang. Kunshan lies to the east, close to Shanghai. Between them, the line passes the cities of Changzhou, Wuxi, and Suzhou. These are old cities of silk, canals, and water towns, and today they are also centres of modern industry. In a sense, the bridge ties together one of the oldest landscapes of trade in China with one of its newest.

Wuxi itself sits beside Lake Tai, one of the largest freshwater lakes in China. For centuries, this city lived by water. Boats moved rice, silk, and clay along the canals, and the city grew rich on that traffic. Today, a passenger can cross the whole Wuxi stretch of the bridge in a matter of minutes. It is worth pausing on that contrast. A journey that once took a day by boat now passes in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea.

Here is the second surprise. Not all of the bridge stands on farmland. Toward its eastern end, the line crosses open water at Yangcheng Lake, near Suzhou. For several kilometres, the piers rise straight out of the lake. That lake is known across China for its hairy crabs, a seasonal delicacy in autumn. So the fastest trains in the country pass directly above one of its most traditional food harvests.

Trains on this line travel at very high speeds, often around 300 kilometres per hour or more. At that pace, the bridge must handle more than weight. It must resist vibration, heat, cold, and the slow movement of soft ground. Engineers also had to consider earthquakes and floods, both real concerns in this region. The result is a structure designed to be quiet and dull in the best possible way. When it works, nobody notices it at all.

It is important to be honest about one thing. This is not a bridge you can walk across. There is no footpath on top, and the line is a working railway. Visitors experience it in one of two ways. You can see it from below, as you are now, from roads and fields along its path. Or you can ride a high-speed train between Shanghai and Nanjing, and feel the bridge beneath you without seeing it at all.

If you take the train, watch the window. Notice how smooth the ride is, and how little the carriage rocks. Notice the view. Because the track is raised, you look down on the delta as if from a low hill. Rooftops, canals, greenhouses, and fish ponds slide past in neat patterns. Many passengers never realise they spent most of that journey on a single bridge.

From the ground, the bridge feels different. Up close, the piers are thick and plain, made of smooth grey concrete. Some carry small numbers or markings for maintenance crews. The underside of the deck forms a long concrete ceiling, and it throws a straight band of shade across the land. In summer, that shade can be a cool place for a farmer to rest. On a misty morning, the line of piers simply disappears into white.

So take a last look along the line. Follow the piers with your eyes, one after another, until you cannot tell where they end. Somewhere far to the east is Shanghai, and somewhere far to the west is Danyang. In between are fields, lakes, and millions of lives. And any moment now, you may hear that soft rising hush again, as another train passes above you and is gone.

Practical tips

Book a window seat for views over the delta.
View from public roads only; stay off railway land.
Come on a misty morning for soft, layered views.
