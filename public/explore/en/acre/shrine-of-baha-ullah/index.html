The guide

The story

Listen first to the gravel. Each step on the garden path makes a soft, dry crunch, and in this quiet that small sound feels loud. Around you, rows of trees stand in precise lines. Lawns are cut so evenly they look drawn with a ruler. The air smells of warm leaves and watered earth. Somewhere a sprinkler ticks. You are only a short distance from the old walls of Acre, yet the noise of the coast seems to fall away here.

This place is called Bahjí, a word that comes from Arabic and is often translated as delight. At its heart is the Shrine of Baháʼu'lláh. For members of the Baháʼí Faith, this is the holiest spot on Earth. It is the resting place of the man they regard as the founder of their religion, and as a messenger of God for this age.

Who was he? Baháʼu'lláh was born in Tehran in 1817, into a noble Persian family. His given name was Mírzá Husayn-ʻAlí. The title he later carried, Baháʼu'lláh, means the Glory of God. In the middle of the nineteenth century, Persia saw a new religious movement led by a young merchant known as the Báb. Its followers faced violent persecution. Baháʼu'lláh became a follower, was imprisoned, and then began a long road of exile.

That road led west — first to Baghdad, then to Constantinople, the Ottoman capital, then to Adrianople, the city now called Edirne in Turkey. In each place, the authorities pushed him farther away. Finally, in 1868, the Ottoman government sent him and a group of family members and companions to Acre. At that time the city was a remote prison town. It had a reputation for foul water and disease, and it was a place where troublesome exiles were sent.

The first years were hard. The exiles were held inside the citadel of Acre, the fortress you may already have seen near the sea walls. Some of the group fell ill. Over time, though, the restrictions loosened. Local people and officials came to respect the prisoners. Eventually Baháʼu'lláh was allowed to live outside the city walls, in the countryside to the north. Here is a detail many visitors miss: he was still formally a prisoner of the Ottoman state when he came to live in these quiet fields.

His last home was the large mansion you can see near the shrine. It is a stately building of pale stone, with arched windows and a balcony that looks over the gardens. Baháʼu'lláh spent his final years there. He wrote, received visitors, and walked in the surrounding land. He died at Bahjí in 1892, and he was buried in a modest house right beside the mansion. That small building became the shrine you see today.

Now look at the shrine itself. From outside, it is surprisingly simple. There is no towering dome and no grand facade. The importance of the place does not come from size. It comes from what it holds. For Baháʼís, this shrine is also the Qiblih — the direction they face each day when they say their obligatory prayers. Wherever a Baháʼí prays in the world, whether in Brazil or Mongolia, they turn toward this spot.

Inside, the building opens around a central space filled with plants and light, open to the sky through the roof above. The room where Baháʼu'lláh is laid to rest lies to one side. Pilgrims remove their shoes before they enter. They often walk in silence, or kneel and pray quietly. Even if you only stand at the threshold, you may notice how carefully people move here. Voices drop. Phones go away. The building is small, so the stillness becomes part of the space.

The gardens around you came later. Over the twentieth century, and especially in the 1950s, the Baháʼí community shaped this land into the formal landscape you are walking through. The design uses strong geometry. Paths run outward from the shrine like spokes from a wheel. Hedges, flower beds, and ornamental gates mark the edges. Each line seems to lead your eye back to the same low building at the centre.

Here is a second surprise. In 2008, UNESCO added the Baháʼí holy places in Haifa and the Western Galilee to its World Heritage List. This shrine is one of the core sites. The listing recognized not only the buildings, but also their meaning for a world religion. It was one of the first times that sites connected to a religion this young, born in the nineteenth century, were honoured this way.

The link to Haifa matters. Across the bay to the south rises the long ridge of Mount Carmel. On a clear day you can see it from the edge of these gardens. On its slope stands the Shrine of the Báb, surrounded by terraces. Baháʼís see the two cities as two halves of one spiritual centre. Acre holds the resting place of the founder. Haifa holds the resting place of his forerunner, along with the administrative heart of the faith.

Who comes here? You will hear many languages on these paths. Baháʼí pilgrims travel from every continent, often after waiting a long time for their visit. Next to them walk local families, curious travellers, and students. The gardens are kept by staff and volunteers, many of them Baháʼís who give a period of service. The neat edges and the swept paths are the result of steady, daily work.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the sun on your shoulders and the crushed stone under your shoes. Look back at the low shrine with its trees and pale walls, and at the mansion behind it. A nineteenth-century exile, sent here as a punishment, ended his life in this garden. Today people cross the world to stand where you are standing now. As you walk back toward Acre, notice how quickly the sounds of traffic return — and how quiet this place was.
