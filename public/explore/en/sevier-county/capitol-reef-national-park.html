The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through cottonwood leaves along the river. The air smells of sage, warm stone, and sometimes ripe fruit from the orchards. All around you, the cliffs glow in bands of rust, cream, and deep red. Underfoot, the ground is dry and gritty, a fine sand that came from these same rocks. Welcome to Capitol Reef, one of the quietest and least crowded of Utah's national parks.

The name sounds strange at first. Why a reef, so far from any ocean? Early travellers used the word for any barrier that blocked their way, the way a coral reef blocks a ship. And this land is a barrier. A long wall of tilted rock cuts across the desert, and for generations it stopped wagons, cattle, and people on foot. The capitol part comes from one rounded white dome of sandstone. To some eyes, it looks like the dome of a government building.

That great wall has a name — the Waterpocket Fold. It is a wrinkle in the Earth's crust, and it runs for roughly one hundred miles through south-central Utah. Geologists call this kind of feature a monocline. That simply means a single, steep bend in otherwise flat layers of rock. Imagine a carpet pushed against a wall, so one side rises in a long ramp. Here, the push came from deep forces many millions of years ago.

Over time, wind and water wore the raised layers down. Now you can see them in cross-section, like the pages of a book seen from the side. The red-brown lower rock is the Moenkopi Formation, laid down in ancient mudflats. Above it sit the softer, colourful slopes of the Chinle. Higher still are the sheer cliffs of Wingate Sandstone, and on top, the pale Navajo Sandstone. That pale layer formed from enormous sand dunes. It forms the white domes that gave the park its name.

And the waterpockets? Look closely at the bare sandstone surfaces. Many of them hold small hollows, worn by water and wind. After rain, these basins fill and hold water for days or weeks. For desert animals, and for the people who lived here long ago, they meant survival. Tiny creatures live in some of these pools. That is one reason visitors are asked never to step in them, touch them, or let a dog drink from them.

People lived beside the Fremont River for a very long time. Archaeologists use the name Fremont culture for the farmers and foragers who lived across this region roughly one thousand years ago. They grew corn, beans, and squash, and they hunted and gathered wild foods. Along the main road east of the visitor centre, a boardwalk leads to a cliff covered with their petroglyphs. These are images pecked into the dark surface of the stone. You can see figures with broad shoulders, headdresses, and animals such as bighorn sheep.

Those petroglyph panels are fragile and cannot be replaced. Natural oils from fingers damage them, so admire them with your eyes only. The meaning of many figures is still uncertain. Some modern Native American communities in the region see these places as part of their own heritage. Stand quietly for a minute, and the panel becomes less like an exhibit. It becomes a message left on a wall by people who watched this same river.

Now for a surprise. In the middle of all this rock, you will find green orchards. In the late nineteenth century, Latter-day Saint pioneers settled along the river here. They named their small community Fruita. They dug irrigation ditches and planted apple, cherry, peach, pear, and apricot trees. The settlement was never large, only a handful of families. But the orchards survived, and today the National Park Service still cares for them. Many of the trees grow in the same places where the settlers planted orchards.

In season, visitors can often pick fruit in the orchards that are open for harvest. Signs at the gates explain which trees are ready and what the rules are. There is something unusual about this. In most national parks, you must not take anything away. Here, eating an apple from a tree is part of the experience. Watch for mule deer too. They wander between the rows in the evening, quite relaxed around people.

Several pioneer buildings still stand. The small Fruita schoolhouse, made of squared logs, once served the children of the community. Nearby, the Gifford Homestead shows how a farm family lived here in the early twentieth century. The house often sells homemade pies and other local goods in the busy season. They tend to sell out early in the day. Further east, the tiny Behunin Cabin, made of stone, shows how small and hard early homes could be.

If you drive south on the Scenic Drive, the road leads into Capitol Gorge. For many years, this narrow canyon was the main wagon route through the fold. On a short walk along the gorge, you reach the Pioneer Register. It is a cliff where travellers carved or painted their names and the year they passed. Some of the dates go back to the late nineteenth century. Today, of course, adding new names is illegal. The old ones show how hard the journey through this canyon was.

Capitol Reef first gained federal protection in the 1930s as a national monument. It became a national park in 1971. Paved roads now cross the area, but much of the park is still remote. To the north lies Cathedral Valley, with tall, separate rock towers rising from the desert floor. To the south, rough roads follow the fold for many miles. At night, the sky here is very dark. The park is recognised as an International Dark Sky Park, and on clear nights the Milky Way can stretch from horizon to horizon.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. Notice the line where the green of the river valley meets the red of the cliffs. One side holds orchards and old fences. The other holds rock that is far older than any human story. As you walk back to the car or the trail, listen again for the cottonwoods and the river. They are small sounds, but in this desert they mean water — and water is the reason anyone ever stayed here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Gifford Homestead early before the pies sell out.
Check orchard gate signs before picking any fruit.
Carry plenty of water even on short desert walks.
Stay after dark for the Dark Sky Park stargazing.
Ask rangers about road conditions before entering dirt roads.
