The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go in. Traffic hums along Avenida del Libertador, one of the widest roads in Buenos Aires. Behind you, the trees of Recoleta move in the breeze. In front of you stands a long, low building painted a warm reddish colour, almost like terracotta. It does not look like a palace of art. It looks practical, almost industrial. That is no accident. This building began its life doing something far more ordinary than holding paintings.

This is the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, the National Museum of Fine Arts of Argentina. Before the paintings arrived, the structure served the city's water system. It was a pump house, part of the network that moved water through a growing capital. In the early twentieth century, the architect Alejandro Bustillo adapted it for the museum. The collection moved here in the early 1930s. So the walls around you once held machinery and the steady noise of pumps. Today they hold some of the most important art in South America.

The museum itself is older than this building. It was founded in the 1890s, at a moment when Buenos Aires was growing fast. Ships arrived from Europe full of immigrants, and the city's wealthy families wanted institutions to match the great capitals across the ocean. The first director was Eduardo Schiaffino, a painter and critic. He believed Argentina needed a national collection of its own. For years the museum moved between temporary homes, before it finally settled here.

Step inside and the noise of the avenue fades quickly. The air feels cooler. The floors are smooth and the light is soft and even. Many visitors begin on the ground floor, where the European collection is displayed. Here you can walk through several centuries of painting in a short time. Medieval religious panels give way to Renaissance and Baroque canvases. Then come the bright, loose brushstrokes of the nineteenth century. It is a compact journey through European art history.

The Impressionist rooms surprise many people. You might not expect to find Monet, Renoir, Degas and Manet this far south, but they are here. There is also work by Vincent van Gogh, including a view of the windmills of Montmartre in Paris. These paintings arrived through purchases and through gifts from Argentine collectors. Many of those families travelled often to Paris and brought art home with them. The collection carries traces of a time when Buenos Aires looked to Paris almost as a second capital.

Look out also for sculpture. The museum holds a number of works by Auguste Rodin, the French sculptor famous for his rough, expressive surfaces. Stand close and notice how the bronze or marble seems unfinished in places. Rodin left marks of the tool and the hand on purpose. He wanted you to feel the act of making. Walk slowly around each piece. The shapes change as you move, and the light catches different edges from every side.

Now for one of the quieter surprises. Upstairs and in the Argentine rooms, the story turns local. And this is where many visitors spend the least time — which is a pity. These galleries show how artists in Argentina looked at their own country. They painted the open grasslands of the pampa, the gauchos who worked the land, and the city as it changed. Here you meet painters whose names are less known abroad but whose work feels very close to this place.

Find the paintings of Cándido López if you can. They are long, narrow canvases full of tiny figures, soldiers and tents spread across wide landscapes. López fought in the war between Paraguay and an alliance that included Argentina, in the 1860s. He lost his right arm in battle. Afterwards he taught himself to paint again with his left hand. His scenes are calm, careful and strangely quiet... for paintings of war. Look closely and you see the patience in every small figure.

Another painting stops many people in front of it. It is called Sin pan y sin trabajo, which means Without Bread and Without Work. Ernesto de la Cárcova painted it in the 1890s. A worker sits at a bare table, his fist clenched. Beside him, his wife holds a baby. Through the window, you can see a factory and signs of a strike. The scene is simple and direct. It shows the hard side of the same years when this museum was founded.

Eduardo Sívori's work is worth finding too. He painted a scene of a servant woman waking in the morning, shown with a frankness that caused controversy in its day. Critics at the time were shocked by its realism. Today it reads as an early sign of Argentine artists choosing ordinary life as their subject. As you move through these rooms, notice the shift. The museum tells a story about how a young nation learned to see itself.

The twentieth century rooms bring colour and experiment. Artists such as Emilio Pettoruti and Xul Solar brought new ideas back from Europe and made them their own. Pettoruti broke forms into sharp, bright planes. Xul Solar invented his own symbols, even his own languages, and filled small paintings with strange cities and floating figures. Antonio Berni later turned to the lives of poor neighbourhoods and workers. Together, these rooms show Buenos Aires as a place of argument, invention and change.

When you leave, take a moment outside. The museum sits in a green part of the city. Across the avenue is the large Faculty of Law building, with its heavy columns. Nearby, a giant metal flower sculpture opens and closes its petals in a plaza. A short walk away is the Recoleta Cemetery, with its rows of marble tombs. This whole area was shaped by the same ambition that built the museum — a wish to give the city a cultural heart.

Stand for a minute and look back at the reddish facade. It is easy to pass this building on a busy day and never think about it. Yet behind those plain walls hang paintings that crossed an ocean, and paintings made just a few streets away. A pump house once moved water through the city. Now it moves people through centuries. As you walk on, listen again to the traffic, and to the trees... and carry a few of those faces with you.
