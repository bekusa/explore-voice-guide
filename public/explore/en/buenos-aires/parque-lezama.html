Parque Lezama

The ground here is not flat.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

The ground here is not flat. It rises and falls in slow waves of grass, shaded by tall old trees. Somewhere nearby a chess player sets out his pieces on a stone table. The smell of mate and fresh-cut grass drifts past on the breeze. This is Parque Lezama, at the southern edge of San Telmo, where the city begins to dip toward the old port.

The park sits where Defensa Street meets Brasil Street. On one side, the land falls away toward the wide river plain. This kind of slope is rare in Buenos Aires, a city built mostly flat. The high ground here has always given the place a feeling of being a little apart, a little older and quieter than the busy streets that surround it.

Tradition says this hill may be the place where Pedro de Mendoza first tried to found Buenos Aires in 1536. That first settlement failed and was soon abandoned. Whether it ever stood on this exact ground, no one can truly prove. The story is a legend, not a settled fact. Still, a monument to Mendoza stands at the edge of the park, facing out toward the water.

The park takes its name from José Gregorio Lezama. He bought the land in 1857 and turned it into a private garden. He brought in plants and trees from far away and laid out terraces and winding paths. His own home stood on the grounds. For many years this was counted among the finest private gardens anywhere in the city.

After Lezama died, his widow sold the property to the city in 1894. The garden was opened to the public as a park. The family home was kept standing and later turned into a museum. In this way the private retreat of a single wealthy family slowly became a shared space, open to everyone who cares to walk through its gates.

That old house now holds the National Historical Museum. Inside are paintings, flags, weapons, and objects from the country's long fight for independence and its early years as a nation. If you have the time, the museum is well worth a visit. From outside, its pale walls and tall, quiet windows look out calmly over the trees.

Across the street, at the northern corner of the park, rises something you might not expect. Blue domes, shaped like onions and topped with crosses, catch the light above the rooftops. This is a Russian Orthodox church, consecrated in 1901. Its bright domes and patterned front feel very far from Buenos Aires, and yet here they are, woven into the everyday view.

The park truly belongs to the neighborhood. Old men play chess and cards at the stone tables for hours. Families spread blankets out on the grass. Dogs run loose between the trees. Drummers gather on weekends, and their steady beat carries across the slopes. Vendors sell cold drinks and snacks along the paths. The place is rarely empty, and rarely loud.

The trees are the park's quiet wealth. Many of them are very old, with wide trunks and heavy branches that lean low over the paths. Tall palms stand here and there among them. In summer the shade beneath them is deep and cool. Birds move and call in the high leaves above. Walking here, you lose the hard edges of the city for a little while.

Paths wind up and down across the uneven ground. Stone steps and worn railings still hint at the careful design of the old garden. Statues stand among the trees, pale and softened by age. An open-air stage holds small concerts and plays. Each turn of the path opens onto a new view, a fountain, a bench in the shade, a long line of trunks.

At the park's borders, the ordinary city returns. Cafés sit waiting across the street. Antique shops line the way back up Defensa toward the center of San Telmo. This sharp contrast is part of the pleasure of the place. You step from the shaded, sloping calm into the busy street, or turn and come back the other way, out of the noise.

Find a bench on the high ground before you go. Look out over the trees toward the place where the river once met the land. People have gathered on this rise for a very long time, drawn by the height, the shade, and the wide open sky above. Sit a moment, and let the slow life of the park carry on quietly around you.
