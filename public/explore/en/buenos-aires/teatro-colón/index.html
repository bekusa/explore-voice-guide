The story

Stand on Cerrito street and look up. Stone walls rise seven floors above the traffic. Behind them, a single voice can fill a hall of three thousand people without a microphone. This is Teatro Colón, the great opera house of Buenos Aires. Many musicians call it one of the finest rooms for sound on earth.

The building took about twenty years to complete. Work began in 1889 under Francesco Tamburini, an Italian architect. He died two years into the project. His colleague Vittorio Meano took over the plans. Meano was killed in 1904, four years before the doors opened. A Belgian architect, Jules Dormal, finished the interior. Three architects shaped one building, and two of them never saw it complete.

The theater opened on May 25, 1908, with Aida, the opera by Giuseppe Verdi. It was not the first Teatro Colón. An earlier theater with the same name stood on Plaza de Mayo from 1857 until 1888. The city sold that building and promised a grander replacement. The promise took two decades to keep.

From outside, the building mixes styles. The base is heavy and Italian. The details above turn French. This blend was common in Buenos Aires at the turn of the twentieth century, when the city grew rich on grain and beef and wanted to look like Paris. The theater faces Plaza Lavalle, a quiet square with old trees, statues, and the courts of justice.

Enter through the main doors and you reach a hall of marble. A wide staircase climbs toward the boxes. Columns of yellow, pink, and gray stone surround you. Stained glass panels glow overhead. The builders imported materials from Italy, France, and Belgium, because the young republic wanted proof that it belonged in Europe's company.

The main hall is shaped like a horseshoe. Seven levels of seats and boxes curve around the stage. There are close to 2,500 seats, and standing room lifts the capacity toward 3,000. Red velvet covers the chairs. Gold leaf catches the light on every railing.

The sound is the true treasure. The horseshoe shape sends music around the room. Wood, plaster, and soft fabric balance each other, so notes stay clear instead of blurring. Singers say a whisper on stage reaches the last row of the highest gallery. Many conductors and sound engineers rank this hall among the best on the planet.

Look up at the dome above the great chandelier, which holds around 700 lights. The Argentine painter Raúl Soldi covered the dome in 1966 with floating dancers and musicians. The original ceiling painting had been damaged decades earlier, so the theater asked Soldi to create a new one. His figures hover in soft color above every performance.

The list of past performers reads like a history of music. Enrico Caruso sang here. So did Maria Callas and Luciano Pavarotti. Igor Stravinsky conducted his own work on this stage, and Arturo Toscanini led orchestras here as well. Astor Piazzolla, the master of the new tango, also brought his music into this hall.

The theater is also a factory. In workshops below the building, staff build the sets, sew the costumes, and craft wigs and shoes by hand. Hundreds of people work behind each production. The Colón keeps its own orchestra, choir, and ballet company, so almost everything you see on stage is made in house.

Time wore the building down, and in 2006 it closed for a full restoration. Craftspeople cleaned the stonework, repaired the gold leaf, and renewed the acoustic details with care. The theater reopened on May 24, 2010, one day before the two hundredth anniversary of the May Revolution. The timing was a national statement.

You can enter without an opera ticket. Guided tours run most days and visit the main hall, the marble staircase, and the Golden Hall, a long room of mirrors and gilded columns inspired by French palaces. If you can, return at night for a performance. Even the cheapest standing place shares the same sound.

Before you leave, pause in the hall and listen. An empty theater is never fully silent. Somewhere a rehearsal note hangs in the air, part of a conversation between voices and walls that has continued since 1908. Then step back onto the loud avenue, carrying a little of that quiet with you.

Key facts

Opened on May 25, 1908, with Verdi's opera Aida
Built over twenty years by architects Tamburini, Meano, and Dormal
Holds about 2,500 seats, near 3,000 with standing room
Raúl Soldi painted the dome ceiling in 1966
Closed in 2006 for restoration; reopened May 24, 2010

What to look for

The chandelier with around 700 lights above the hall
Golden Hall mirrors and gilded columns on the upper floor
Colored marble columns along the grand entrance staircase
Seven curved levels of boxes rising around the horseshoe hall

Practical tips

Book a guided tour online a few days ahead in high season
Try standing-room tickets for performances; the sound is equally fine
Combine your visit with Plaza Lavalle and the nearby courts district
Check the theater website for concert and ballet season dates
