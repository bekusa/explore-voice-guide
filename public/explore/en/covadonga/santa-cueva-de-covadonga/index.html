The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below your feet, water is falling. It drops out of the rock face in a thin white column and crashes into a small pool. The air here is cool and damp, even on a warm day. It smells of wet limestone, moss and candle wax. Above you, a small chapel sits inside a hollow in the cliff, as if the mountain had opened a pocket to hold it.

This is the Santa Cueva de Covadonga, the Holy Cave of Covadonga. It lies in the mountains of eastern Asturias, in northern Spain, on the edge of the Picos de Europa. For many Spaniards, this small hollow in the rock is one of the most meaningful places in the country. It is part church, part legend, and part national origin story. And it is much smaller than most visitors expect.

To understand why people come here, we need to go back to the early eighth century. By then, Muslim armies from North Africa had taken control of most of the Iberian Peninsula within a few years. The old Visigothic kingdom, the Christian kingdom that ruled Spain before them, had collapsed. But in these steep northern valleys, a small group of Christian nobles and mountain people refused to submit. Their leader was a man remembered as Pelayo.

According to tradition, Pelayo and his followers sheltered in this very cave. When a larger force came up the narrow valley to crush them, they fought back. The battle of Covadonga is traditionally dated to 722. Historians still debate the exact year and the true size of the fight. Some see it as a modest skirmish. But the result mattered. Pelayo became the first king of Asturias, and this small mountain kingdom grew, over centuries, into the Christian kingdoms of medieval Spain.

This is where history and legend meet. The old chronicles, written long after the event, say the Virgin Mary protected Pelayo's men. One popular tale says that arrows fired at the cave turned back in the air and struck the attackers. Locals say that a hermit had already kept an image of the Virgin here before the battle. These stories are faith and folklore, not documented fact. Still, they explain why a battlefield became a shrine.

The image of the Virgin that stands in the chapel is known to Asturians simply as La Santina, a warm and affectionate name that means something like the little saint. She is the patroness of Asturias. Look at how small she is, and how richly she is dressed. The figure you see today is not the original. The first images and the old wooden chapel were lost to a fire in the eighteenth century.

After that fire, the cave stood without its old sanctuary for a long time. The chapel you see now is a twentieth-century building. It is made of stone, in a simple style that echoes the early churches of Asturias. Notice how it fits into the hollow, using the rock itself as a back wall. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The chapel does not stand on the ground. It is built out over the drop, held in the cliff.

Inside the cave, along one side, you will find a stone tomb. Tradition holds that this is the resting place of Pelayo himself. Nearby lie tombs associated with members of his family and with a later king of Asturias. Whether the bones are truly his cannot be proven. But for centuries, people have stopped here in silence. Watch the faces of the visitors around you. For many, this is less a museum and more a family grave.

Now look down at the water again. Below the cave, the stream gathers in a pool, and nearby there is a fountain with several spouts. Locals call it the Fountain of the Seven Spouts. A well-known piece of folklore says that a young woman who drinks from it will marry within a year. Couples, families and curious travellers all try it. Nobody keeps records of the results, which is probably for the best.

Turn around and look across the valley. On a green rise stands a large basilica of pink stone. It was built in the late nineteenth century, in a neo-Romanesque style. Romanesque is the round-arched, heavy style of medieval churches, and neo-Romanesque is a later revival of it. The pink colour comes from local limestone. In soft evening light, the stone turns almost rose against the dark green mountains. It is the second half of this sanctuary.

Between the cave and the basilica, you may notice a bronze statue of Pelayo. He holds up a cross, the symbol linked to his victory. That cross became important far beyond these mountains. A jewelled cross associated with Pelayo's legend became a symbol of Asturias, and it appears on the regional flag today. So when you see that yellow cross on blue across this region, you are looking at a thread that runs back to this cave.

The road you came up continues higher into the mountains. It climbs to the Lakes of Covadonga, two glacial lakes high in the Picos de Europa. If the weather is clear, the drive or bus ride is worth the effort. But clouds often fill these valleys quickly. One minute the basilica glows in the sun, and the next it disappears into mist. That changing weather is part of the character of this place, so be ready for it.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the edge of the cave. Hear the water again, the same sound that has filled this hollow for centuries. Feel the cold air coming off the rock. Watch the candles flicker near the Virgin. Kings, pilgrims, soldiers and tourists have all stood where you are standing. As you walk away, look back once. The chapel will seem very small against the cliff... and that is exactly the point.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a light jacket; the cave air stays cool and damp.
Come early morning to avoid tour bus crowds.
Speak quietly; services and prayers often take place inside.
Check mountain weather before driving up to the lakes.
Wear shoes with grip for wet stone paths.
