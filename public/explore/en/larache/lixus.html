The guide

The story

Listen first. A breeze comes off the Atlantic and moves through dry grass on the hillside. Below you, the Loukkos River bends in wide, slow loops across the flat green marshland toward the sea. The ground underfoot is uneven, with loose earth, broken stone, and the occasional edge of an old wall breaking the surface. This is Lixus. It sits on a low hill just outside Larache, and for many centuries it was one of the oldest and most important towns on the Atlantic coast of Africa.

The position explains everything. Look at how the hill rises from the river plain. In the ancient world, the Loukkos estuary gave ships a sheltered route inland from the open ocean. A town on this height could watch the water, control trade, and defend itself. The sea has shifted since then, and silt has filled much of the old estuary. But stand still for a moment and you can picture the river as a working harbour, with boats moving in and out below.

Here is the first surprise. Some ancient writers believed Lixus was older than almost any Phoenician town in the western Mediterranean world. The Roman author Pliny the Elder repeated the claim that it was founded very early, even earlier than Gades, the Phoenician town that became Cádiz in Spain. Historians treat that claim with caution. Archaeologists have found Phoenician material here from early in the first millennium before Christ, so the town is certainly very old. The exact founding date remains an open question.

The Phoenicians were sailors and traders from the coast of what is now Lebanon. They built a network of trading posts across the Mediterranean and beyond the Strait of Gibraltar. Lixus was one of their footholds on the Atlantic side. Over time, local North African communities and Phoenician newcomers mixed here. The town later belonged to the kingdom of Mauretania, a Berber kingdom that ruled much of this region before Rome took direct control.

Now for the myths, and these are stories, not history. Greek and Roman writers placed the Garden of the Hesperides near Lixus. In that legend, nymphs guarded a tree of golden apples, and the hero Hercules came to steal them as one of his famous labours. Some ancient authors also linked this area with the giant Antaeus, whom Hercules wrestled and defeated. Locals and visitors still repeat these tales. Some people even point to the curving river below as the coils of the dragon that guarded the golden fruit.

Walk toward the lower part of the site, near the old road. Here you find something very practical — rows of rectangular basins built into the ground. These were salting tanks. Lixus produced salted fish and fish sauce on a very large scale. The Romans called this sauce garum, and they used it the way many cuisines today use soy sauce or fish sauce. The complex here is considered one of the largest fish-salting installations known from the ancient western Mediterranean.

Imagine the smell. Fresh fish from the Atlantic was gutted, layered with salt, and left to cure in these tanks for weeks. It was hard, messy, and probably very loud work. The finished products travelled in clay jars called amphorae to markets across the Roman world. So the grand temples on the hilltop depended on something very ordinary. This was a town that grew rich, at least in part, from fish and salt.

Rome took full control of the region in the first century after Christ, and Lixus became a Roman town. As you climb, you pass the remains of Roman buildings. One of the most unusual is the theatre. It has a curved seating area like a theatre, but its central space is round, more like an amphitheatre. Many scholars think it was adapted so it could host gladiator fights and animal shows as well as plays. That mix of forms is rare in North Africa.

Nearby are the remains of Roman baths. Look for low walls and the small stacked supports that once held up heated floors. Hot air from a furnace moved under these floors and warmed the rooms above. Some decorated mosaic floors were found at Lixus. Several were later moved to museums for protection, so what you see on site today is mostly the structure, not the decoration. Still, the layout shows clearly how a Roman town organised daily comfort.

At the top of the hill is the acropolis, the highest and most sacred part of the town. Here stood large temples and public buildings. The ground is a puzzle of foundations from many periods, built one on top of another. Archaeologists still debate which walls belong to which era. Take your time here. This is the best place to understand the town as a whole, with the river, the marshes, the modern city, and the ocean all in view.

The second surprise is that the story does not end with Rome. After the Roman period, the town shrank, but people did not leave completely. On the acropolis you can find the remains of a small mosque from the Islamic era. It is a modest building, but it shows that this hill still mattered to local people long after the temples had fallen. Lixus was not one city frozen in time. It was a place that many cultures used and reused.

Much of what we know comes from excavations in the twentieth century. During that time, Larache was part of the Spanish protectorate in northern Morocco, and Spanish archaeologists carried out major work here in the middle of the century. Moroccan archaeologists and international teams have continued the research since then. Large parts of the site are still unexcavated. The hill you are standing on almost certainly holds more streets, houses, and stories under the soil.

Before you leave, stop once more and look down at the Loukkos. Watch how the water catches the light as it bends through the plain. Listen for birds over the marshes and traffic on the road below. Fishermen once worked the same river mouth that boats from Larache still use today. As you walk back down, notice the stones at your feet. Each one was cut, carried, and placed by someone who also looked out at this river.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for loose stones and uneven paths.
Bring water and sun protection on the exposed hillside.
Climb to the acropolis first for the full river view.
Visit late afternoon for softer light over the marshes.
Pair your visit with Larache's old town and seafront.
