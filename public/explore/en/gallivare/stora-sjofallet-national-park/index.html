The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across bare rock, and somewhere below you, water runs over stone. The air is thin and clean, with a cold edge even in summer. Low birch trees bend close to the ground. Across the water, a mountain rises in a cluster of white peaks. This is Stora Sjöfallet, far above the Arctic Circle in Swedish Lapland. Here the road starts to run out and the mountains take over.

The name means, quite simply, the great lake fall. For centuries, a long chain of lakes emptied through this valley in a series of powerful falls and rapids. Travellers who came here in the nineteenth century described a roar you could hear long before you saw the water. Spray rose like smoke over the rocks. For many early visitors, that sound was the reason to make the long journey north.

In 1909, Sweden became the first country in Europe to create national parks. That year, a small group of protected areas was set aside, and Stora Sjöfallet was one of them. The idea was new and bold. Some landscapes, the founders argued, should be kept as they were, for their own sake. Lapland's high valleys, with their glaciers, lakes, and wide open sky, were among the first places chosen.

Now for a twist that surprises many visitors. The waterfall that gave this park its name has largely gone quiet. Only a few years after protection, Sweden's need for electricity grew fast. Engineers looked at the great volume of water falling through this valley and saw power. In the early twentieth century, the state redrew the park's borders. The areas around the falls and the lakes were taken out of the protected zone, so that dams and power stations could be built.

The result is in front of you today. The large lake you can see, Akkajaure, is a regulated reservoir held back by the Suorva dam. Its water level rises and falls with the needs of the power system. Look closely at the shoreline. When the water is low, you can see a pale band of bare stone and gravel, like a bathtub ring. That band marks the distance between full and empty. It is a quiet record of how much water is drawn away for electricity.

On some days, when the gates are opened and extra water is released, the old falls return for a while. Most of the time, though, the riverbed shows bare, smooth rock where white water once ran. For people who care about nature in Sweden, this valley became a lesson. The loss of the great fall helped push the country toward stronger protection for other rivers. It is a national park with a scar, and the scar is part of its story.

Now raise your eyes to the mountain across the water. This is Akka, known in the Sámi language as Áhkká. People often call it the Queen of Lapland. Its massif carries several summits and small glaciers, and its highest point rises to around two thousand metres. In Sámi tradition, Áhkká is connected with the female spirits of the old Sámi belief system. Many people say the name refers to an old woman or grandmother figure.

That brings us to the people of this land. The Sámi are the Indigenous people of northern Scandinavia and Finland, and parts of Russia. For generations, Sámi families have moved with their reindeer through these valleys. In summer, the herds graze in the high mountains, where cool winds keep the insects away. In winter, they move east toward the forests. This movement still happens today. If you see reindeer near the road, you are watching a working herd, not wild animals.

In 1996, Stora Sjöfallet became part of Laponia, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Laponia joins several national parks and nature reserves into one large protected landscape, including the neighbouring parks of Sarek and Padjelanta. UNESCO recognised it for two reasons together. The first is its natural landscape of mountains, glaciers, and wetlands. The second is the living Sámi culture of reindeer herding. Laponia is one of the largest areas in Europe where a traditional way of life continues in its original landscape.

Near the road, you will find the Naturum Laponia visitor centre. A naturum is a type of Swedish nature centre, built to explain the landscape around it. This one presents both the mountains and the Sámi view of them. It is a good place to orient yourself, ask about trail and weather conditions, and see the region on a map. From its windows, the mountains fill the view, and the building gives you shelter on days when the wind is strong.

The road you travelled on continues west to Ritsem, at the edge of the reservoir. There, the public road ends. Beyond it, travel is on foot, by boat, or by helicopter. Hikers set out from here toward Sarek and Padjelanta, two of the least developed mountain areas in Sweden. The well-known long-distance trail called Kungsleden, or the King's Trail, also passes through this region. For many walkers, this valley is where civilisation ends and the long walk begins.

The light here follows its own rules. In midsummer, the sun does not set. It circles low around the sky, and the mountains glow orange at midnight. In early autumn, the low birch and berry plants turn deep red and gold, often for only a short time. In midwinter, the sun barely rises at all, and on clear nights the northern lights can move across the sky above the reservoir. Each season changes the same view completely.

Before you go, take one more look. Follow the line of the water to the foot of Áhkká. Notice the pale band of stone along the shore, and the dark rock where the falls once ran. Listen to the wind, and to the water that still finds its way through the valley. This is a place where people protected nature, then changed it, and are still learning from both choices. As you walk away, the mountain will stay in view for a long time behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Naturum Laponia first for trail and weather updates.
Pack warm, windproof layers even on summer days.
Drive slowly and give way to reindeer on the road.
Come in midsummer to see the midnight sun.
Continue to Ritsem if you want trailheads into wilder country.
