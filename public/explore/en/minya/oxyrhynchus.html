The guide

The story

Listen to the ground under your feet. It crunches. The sound comes from dry earth, broken pottery and pale sand, all mixed together over many centuries. Around you, low mounds roll away toward the edge of the desert. To the east, a line of green marks the fields and the canal. To the west, there is only sand. This quiet stretch of Middle Egypt was once one of the busiest provincial cities in the Roman world. Today it is one of the most important places on Earth for anyone who loves old books.

The ancient name, Oxyrhynchus, is Greek. It means "sharp-nosed", and it refers to a fish. The elephant-snout fish of the Nile has a long, curved mouth, and the people here held it sacred. According to one Egyptian myth, this fish swallowed part of the body of the god Osiris after his murder. Many Egyptians avoided eating it for that reason. Here, the town took the fish as its emblem. Today the village nearby is called el-Bahnasa, a name that comes from the town's older Egyptian roots.

In the Greek and Roman periods, Oxyrhynchus served as the capital of a district, or nome. Egypt was divided into these regional units, a little like provinces. The city stood beside the Bahr Yusuf, a long canal that runs parallel to the Nile on its western side. That water fed the fields and carried trade. Here lived officials, soldiers, priests, merchants, teachers and weavers. Many of them were Greek-speaking, and they kept written records of nearly everything they did.

This is where the story turns surprising. The city's greatest treasure was never a temple or a palace. It was its rubbish. For centuries, the people of Oxyrhynchus threw old paperwork onto dumps at the edge of town. That paperwork was papyrus, a writing material made from Nile reeds. Because these dumps lay above the reach of groundwater, the desert air kept them dry. Ordinary trash became a sealed archive, buried under sand.

In the late 1890s, two young Oxford scholars arrived here. Their names were Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt. They came looking for early Christian texts, and they dug into the mounds. What they found went far beyond their hopes. Papyri came out of the ground in huge numbers, sometimes in thick layers. Over several seasons, they shipped crates of fragments back to England. The flow was so great that scholars are still reading them more than a century later.

The total is usually counted in the hundreds of thousands of fragments. Most are now kept in Oxford, and a long series of published volumes, called The Oxyrhynchus Papyri, continues to grow. Each volume adds new texts. Some are only a few lines. Others are long documents. Together they form one of the largest windows we have into daily life in the ancient Mediterranean. And it all began with what people here simply threw away.

So what was in the rubbish? Literature that the world had lost. Fragments of poems by Sappho, the Greek poet of Lesbos, surfaced here. So did pieces of comedies by Menander, a playwright who was famous in antiquity but survived mostly in quotations. There is a fragment of Euclid's geometry, with a diagram. Some of these texts exist nowhere else. When you read a modern translation of certain Greek poems, the words may have come from this field.

The early Christian finds were just as striking. Among the first papyri published were sayings of Jesus that did not match the familiar gospels. Decades later, scholars matched them with the Gospel of Thomas, a text known in full from a separate discovery elsewhere in Egypt. Oxyrhynchus also gave us some of the oldest surviving copies of New Testament passages. For historians of early Christianity, these scraps are among the most valuable documents they have.

But the real heart of the archive is ordinary life. There are tax receipts and census returns. There are leases, wills and complaints to officials. There are letters home, invitations to dinner, school exercises and horoscopes. One famous letter comes from a boy who scolds his father for leaving him behind on a trip. Reading these texts, you meet people who worried about money, family and bad harvests. They feel very close.

Later, the city became strongly Christian. Late Roman writers described Oxyrhynchus as full of churches and monks. One travel account claims the monks and nuns numbered in the thousands. Historians treat that figure as a pious exaggeration, but it shows how the city looked to visitors. After the Arab conquest of Egypt in the seventh century, the town became el-Bahnasa. Local tradition holds that many who died in the fighting were buried here.

That tradition is still alive. Not far from the ancient mounds stand domed tombs and shrines linked to those early Muslim figures. Families from the region visit them to pray and to ask for blessings. Locals sometimes describe the area as a place of many saints. So this ground holds two kinds of memory at once. One belongs to scholars and their papyri. The other belongs to the people who still live here and honour their dead.

Archaeology here did not stop with the Oxford team. Since the 1990s, a mission from the University of Barcelona has worked at the site with Egyptian colleagues. They have studied the city's tombs, its later Christian buildings and its temple areas. Their work has turned Oxyrhynchus back into a place, not only a collection of texts. They have uncovered burials, painted walls and stone remains that show how the city looked and how its people honoured the dead.

As you walk, look for the few features that still stand above the ground. A single tall stone column rises from the ruins, a lonely sign of the city's grand streets. Near it, you will see pits and trenches from old and new excavations. The mounds themselves are full of small potsherds, the broken pieces of clay jars. Please leave everything where it lies. Each fragment belongs to the site, and its exact position can matter to the archaeologists.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind moving across the mounds, and the distant sound of the village. Somewhere beneath you, there may still be scraps of writing that no one has read for nearly two thousand years. A letter. A poem. A receipt. As you walk back toward the green fields, notice the dust on your shoes. It comes from a city that was saved, almost by accident, through the things it threw away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to avoid the fierce midday desert heat.
Bring plenty of water and a hat for the open mounds.
Wear sturdy closed shoes for uneven ground and loose sherds.
Combine your visit with the domed shrines of el-Bahnasa village.
Dress modestly if you plan to visit the nearby shrines.
