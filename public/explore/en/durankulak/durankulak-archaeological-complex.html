The guide

The story

Listen first. Reeds move against each other in the wind off the Black Sea, with a dry, steady hiss. Somewhere out on the water a heron lifts, slow and heavy. The path under your feet is soft earth and pale limestone. Ahead of you, a low island rises from Lake Durankulak. It does not look like much at first: grass, a few stones, open sky. But this small piece of land holds one of the longest records of human life on the European coast.

You are in the far northeast corner of Bulgaria, only a few kilometres from the Romanian border. The lake is a coastal lagoon, separated from the sea by a strip of sand. Fresh water, fish, birds and fertile ground all meet here. People noticed this very early. For thousands of years, families came back to this island again and again. Each group built on top of the traces left by the people before them.

Archaeologists call this place the Big Island. Systematic excavations began here in the 1970s, led by Bulgarian researchers. What they found surprised them. The lowest layers belong to the late Stone Age, the Neolithic. That is the period when people in this region first settled down to farm, keep animals and make pottery. The oldest traces here are many thousands of years older than the Greek colonies along this coast.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. Some of the prehistoric houses on this island were built with stone foundations. In the Copper Age, around the fifth millennium before Christ, people here laid limestone walls. Elsewhere in the region, most homes of that time used wood, clay and reeds. Stone architecture of this age is rare in Europe. So the low lines of rock you see in the grass are not just ruins. They are an early experiment in building to last.

Just across the water, on the shore, lies the second great discovery: a prehistoric cemetery. Archaeologists have excavated more than a thousand graves there. That makes it one of the largest known prehistoric burial grounds in southeastern Europe. Most graves belong to the Hamangia culture. That is the name scholars give to the early farming communities of the western Black Sea coast. Later burials belong to the people of the Varna culture.

The Varna culture may sound familiar. It is linked to the famous Varna cemetery further south, where some of the oldest worked gold in the world was found. Durankulak belongs to the same world. Its graves also held gold ornaments, copper tools, shell jewellery and carefully shaped pottery. Some of the shells came from the Mediterranean, far to the south. This was not an isolated village. It was part of a long chain of trade along the coast.

The burials also carry clues about how these people saw life and death. Men and women were often laid in different positions. Some graves held no body at all, only objects. Archaeologists call these symbolic graves. Perhaps they honoured someone lost at sea, or someone who died far away. Nobody knows for certain. Standing here, it is worth remembering that every grave was dug by a family that grieved, just as families do today.

Now move forward thousands of years. In the centuries before Christ, the Thracians and the Getae lived in this region. The Getae were a Thracian people of the lower Danube and the coast. On the island, they used a cave sanctuary cut into the rock. It was later linked with the worship of a mother goddess, often identified with Cybele. Cybele was a great earth goddess honoured across the Greek and Anatolian world.

This is the second surprise. The cave is small, but it connects the island to a far wider religious world. Greek ideas, Thracian traditions and local beliefs mixed together on this coast. A farmer from a nearby village and a sailor from a Greek town might both have climbed to this rock to leave an offering. The island was no longer a village. It had become a holy place.

The story does not stop there. In the early Middle Ages, people settled on the island once more. Archaeologists found the remains of an early medieval settlement, connected with the period of the First Bulgarian State. There were houses, storage pits and everyday objects. So, on one island, you can trace farmers of the Stone Age, Copper Age builders, Thracian worshippers and medieval Bulgarians. Few places hold so many chapters in such a small space.

As you walk, look at the land around you as well as the ruins. Lake Durankulak is today a protected wetland and an important stop for migrating birds. In winter, large flocks of geese gather on the lake and the nearby fields. In spring and autumn, waders and herons move through the reeds. The same rich environment that drew the first farmers now draws birdwatchers with binoculars and long lenses.

The village of Durankulak itself is quiet and agricultural. Wide fields of grain and sunflowers stretch inland. The wind here is almost constant, and the light is wide and flat over the water. In the evening, the lake turns silver and then grey. It is easy to imagine smoke rising from a cluster of stone houses on the island, and canoes moving between the reeds. The landscape has changed less than you might think.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look back at the Big Island. Notice how low it sits, how close the water comes to its edges. Notice the pale stones in the grass, laid by hands that never knew writing. You are standing where people have lived, prayed and buried their dead for thousands of years. As you walk away, listen again to the reeds. That sound was here long before the first house... and it will still be here after you go.

What to look for

Thin strip of sand dividing the lagoon from the sea.
Herons or geese rising from the shallow water.
Before you go

Practical tips

Bring binoculars for the birds on Lake Durankulak.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven grass and limestone paths.
Carry a windproof layer; coastal winds rarely stop here.
Visit in late afternoon for softer light over the lake.
Combine with a walk along the nearby lagoon shore.
