The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the wind. Here on the southern edge of Ashgabat, the air often comes down from the Kopetdag mountains, dry and clean. Under your feet, pale stone gives back the sunlight. Above you, three tall white legs join and rise toward a single point. At the very top, a golden figure catches the light so brightly that it can be hard to look at directly.

This is the Neutrality Monument. Locals often give it a plainer name: the Tripod. That nickname comes from its shape. Three legs support a slender central tower, the way a camera stand holds a lens. It is one of the clearest shapes on the Ashgabat skyline. Once you know it, you will find it again from car windows, hotel balconies, and the long avenues of the city.

To understand why this tower exists, we need one date. On 12 December 1995, the United Nations General Assembly formally recognised Turkmenistan as a permanently neutral state. For a young country, independent from the Soviet Union only since 1991, this was a defining moment. Neutrality became a core part of the national story. Today, 12 December is still a public holiday here, called Neutrality Day.

The first version of this monument did not stand where you are standing now. It was built in the late 1990s in the centre of the city, close to the main government buildings. That original arch was the work of a state that wanted big symbols. It was meant to be seen from everywhere. For more than a decade, it stood at the heart of the capital as a kind of national landmark.

And here is the first surprise. The golden statue on top was not a symbol of an idea. It was a portrait of a real man — Saparmurat Niyazov, the country's first president. He used the title Turkmenbashi, which means "leader of the Turkmen". On the original arch, the statue was mounted on a turning base. It rotated slowly through the day, so that the gilded figure always faced the sun.

That detail tells you a lot about the era. Under Niyazov, the leader's image filled public life. Portraits hung in offices and schools. His name was given to towns, streets, and even months of the year. A golden statue that followed the sun across the sky was the most dramatic expression of that style. Visitors from abroad often described it as one of the strangest sights in Central Asia.

Niyazov died in 2006. His successor began, step by step, to reshape parts of the capital. Around 2010, the arch was taken down from its central site. It was then rebuilt here, in the southern part of Ashgabat, closer to the foothills. The new structure was completed in the early 2010s. It is taller than the original, and it now stands in a wide, open setting rather than among government buildings.

This move was more than a change of address. Moving the tower from the political centre to the edge of the city changed its meaning. It is still a national monument. It is still crowned by the golden figure. But it no longer looks down on the presidential palace. Many observers saw the relocation as a quiet way to step back from the personality cult of the first president, without destroying the monument itself.

Now look at the design with fresh eyes. The three legs are not only decoration. They carry the weight of the central column and the platform near the top. Many visitors miss a practical detail — the tower is not just to be admired from below. Inside, an elevator can carry people up to a viewing level. From there, the white city spreads out below you, with the mountains on the other side.

That white city is worth a word. Ashgabat is famous for its white marble. Ministries, apartment blocks, and monuments are faced with pale stone, and the effect from above is almost like a model. The streets are wide and very clean. In the evening, many buildings are lit with coloured lights. From the Neutrality Monument, you can see how carefully the whole capital has been planned around views and symbols.

The area around the base is designed for slow walking. Look for paved paths, fountains, flower beds, and open lawns. On a hot afternoon, the stone can feel warm through your shoes, and the light can be very strong. In the cooler hours, local families sometimes come to walk here. The atmosphere is calm and quiet. You will hear wind, water, and traffic in the distance more than crowds.

A practical note about cameras. Turkmenistan can be sensitive about photography, especially near government buildings, police, and soldiers. Pictures of the monument itself are a normal part of a visit. But it is wise to be polite and careful. If a guard or official asks you to put your camera away, simply do so. Avoid photographing people in uniform, and ask before you take a portrait of anyone.

As you stand here, think about the journey this tower has made. It began as a celebration of a new country's place in the world. It became the stage for a leader's golden image. Then it was lifted, moved, and rebuilt at the edge of the city. Few monuments anywhere have changed places like this and survived. The tower is in some ways a record of how Turkmenistan has told its own story.

Before you walk away, look up one more time. Follow the three white legs as they meet, then the column as it rises, and finally the gold at the top. Notice which way the figure is facing right now, and where the sun is. Then turn around and look toward the mountains. That quiet line of brown peaks was here long before the tower, and it will frame this place long after.

Key facts

UN recognised Turkmenistan's permanent neutrality on 12 December 1995.
Neutrality Day, 12 December, remains a national public holiday.
Original arch stood in central Ashgabat from the late 1990s.
Golden statue depicts first president Saparmurat Niyazov.
Monument was dismantled around 2010 and rebuilt further south.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon to avoid the harshest midday sun.
Ride the elevator to the viewing level for city panoramas.
Avoid photographing guards, police, or people in uniform.
Bring water and a hat for walks across open paving.
Return after dark to see the city's coloured night lighting.
