The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across open ground here, and it carries a faint dryness from the desert beyond the city. Under your feet the paving is pale and smooth, and in strong sun it throws light straight back up at you. Ahead, a white building rises with a golden dome at its centre. Around it, four slim minarets point at the sky. The scale arrives slowly. You keep walking, and the mosque keeps getting larger.

You are standing at the Türkmenbaşy Ruhy Mosque, in the village of Gypjak, a short drive west of central Ashgabat. The name means, roughly, the Spirit of Türkmenbaşy. Türkmenbaşy, or Head of the Turkmen, was the title taken by Saparmurat Niyazov. He was the first president of independent Turkmenistan. This village is his birthplace. That single fact explains much of what you see around you.

The mosque was completed in the early 2000s. It is often described as one of the largest mosques in Central Asia. Its main prayer hall is commonly said to hold around ten thousand worshippers. Construction was carried out by the French company Bouygues, which built many of the grand projects of the Niyazov era. So this is a modern building. It is not an ancient shrine. It is a statement made in stone and gold by a young country.

Look up at the minarets. There are four, one at each corner, and they are tall and very thin. Their height is widely reported as ninety-one metres. Many guides link that number to 1991, the year Turkmenistan became independent from the Soviet Union. Here is something many visitors miss. Numbers in Ashgabat architecture often carry this kind of meaning. Once you know that, you start to notice it everywhere in the capital.

Now look at the walls. The surfaces are clad in white marble, a material that appears across Ashgabat on a remarkable scale. In the morning the stone looks cool and almost blue. By late afternoon it warms to cream and soft gold. The dome sits above it all. Its gilded surface changes with the angle of the sun, from bright yellow at noon to a deep amber near dusk.

Here is the detail that surprises most visitors. Inside and on parts of the exterior, the inscriptions include verses from the Quran. But they also include passages from the Ruhnama. The Ruhnama, or Book of the Soul, was a work written under Niyazov's name. It mixed history, moral guidance and national myth. During his rule it was required reading in schools and offices across the country.

Placing lines from a president's book alongside Quranic text was controversial. Many religious scholars, both inside and outside Turkmenistan, criticised it. For them, setting any human writing beside scripture on a mosque crossed an important line. This tension is part of the building's story. It is a working mosque, and also a monument to one man's vision of national identity. Both things are true at the same time.

Beside the mosque stands a smaller domed structure. This is the mausoleum of Saparmurat Niyazov. He died in December 2006, and he was buried here, in his home village. Members of his family also rest here. His mother and two brothers died in the great earthquake that destroyed much of Ashgabat in 1948. Niyazov was a child at the time. That loss shaped his life, and it shaped how he later presented himself to the nation.

The mausoleum is a quiet place. Guards are usually present, and visitors tend to lower their voices. Photography rules can be strict here and around the complex, and they may change. It is always wise to ask before raising a camera, especially inside. Watch how local visitors behave. Some come simply to look. Others pause in silent respect. Following their lead is the easiest way to feel at ease.

If you are allowed into the prayer hall, take a moment at the threshold. The space feels very wide, and the sound behaves differently. Footsteps soften on the carpet. Voices seem to drift up into the dome. Look down, because the floor covering deserves attention. Turkmenistan is known for its carpets, and the patterns here draw on that tradition. Look for the eight-pointed star, a shape that appears again and again in Turkmen design.

Step back outside and look at the grounds. Wide plazas, fountains and neat gardens surround the complex. Much of this layout was planned for ceremony and arrival, and it works best when seen from a distance. Walk out toward the edge of the paving and turn around. From there you can see the whole composition at once. The dome sits in the middle, and the four minarets frame it like the corners of a page.

It helps to place this building within the wider story of Ashgabat. After independence, the capital was rebuilt in white marble and gold on an enormous scale. Monuments, ministries and apartment blocks all followed a shared style. This mosque belongs to that same project. It shows how a new state wanted to be seen. It wanted to look confident, rooted in faith, and tied closely to its leader.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Notice the wind again, and the glare from the pale stone. Notice how small the people near the entrance look against the walls. Listen for traffic on the road toward the city, or the call to prayer if it is time. Whatever you think about the politics behind it, this place was built to make you feel exactly this. Take that feeling with you as you walk back.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ask before taking photos, especially inside the buildings.
Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered.
Women should bring a scarf to cover their hair.
Visit in late afternoon to escape the strongest midday sun.
