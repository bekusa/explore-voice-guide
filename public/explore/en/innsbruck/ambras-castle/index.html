The guide

The story

Gravel crunches underfoot as you climb the slope above Innsbruck. The air smells of cut grass and damp pine. Behind you, the city spreads across the valley floor, and the mountains rise on every side like grey walls. Ahead, white walls catch the light. This is Ambras Castle, and for a few decades in the sixteenth century it held one of the most remarkable private collections in Europe.

The man behind it was Archduke Ferdinand the Second of Tyrol, a son of the Habsburg Emperor Ferdinand the First. He took over an older medieval fortress here in the 1560s and turned it into a Renaissance residence. A Renaissance residence was built for comfort and display, not for defence. Gone were the cramped towers of earlier centuries. In their place came halls, gardens and rooms made for guests, music and conversation.

At the heart of the story is a love affair the family did not approve of. Ferdinand married Philippine Welser, the daughter of a wealthy merchant family from the city of Augsburg. She was not of royal blood. For a Habsburg prince, that was a serious problem. The marriage was kept secret for years, and their children were excluded from the line of succession. Ambras became, in a sense, the home of this unequal couple.

Philippine left her own mark on the castle. She was known for her interest in medicine and herbal remedies, and she kept handwritten books of recipes and cures. Her bathing room survives here — a sunken tub in a small chamber, warmed with heated water. It is a rare glimpse of private Renaissance comfort. Stand there for a moment and imagine the steam rising off the water in a cold Tyrolean winter.

Now for the first surprise. Ferdinand did not simply fill his home with treasures. He built separate buildings just to house his collections and to show them to visitors. For this reason, Ambras is often described as one of the oldest museums in the world. The objects were arranged on purpose, in cases and cabinets, so a guest could walk through and learn. That idea feels ordinary today. In the sixteenth century, it was new.

The Lower Castle is where those collections still live. One wing holds armour, rows of it, gleaming under soft light. Ferdinand gathered suits of armour that had belonged to famous rulers and commanders of his age. He called this his heroes' armoury. He even had an illustrated catalogue printed, with a picture and a short biography for each warrior. It was part museum, part hall of fame.

Look closely at the armour as you pass. Some pieces are plain and battered, made for real fighting. Others are etched, gilded and engraved with fine patterns, made for tournaments and ceremony. The steel shows dents, rivets and hinges. You can see how a body was meant to move inside it. Some suits are surprisingly small, a reminder that the people who wore them were often shorter than visitors today.

Then comes the Chamber of Art and Curiosities. In German this is called a Kunstkammer, a room that tried to hold the whole world in miniature. Here, natural wonders sit beside human craft. Coral branches, carved ivory, scientific instruments, strange glass and clockwork objects share the same cases. The goal was not to sort things neatly by subject. The goal was to astonish, and to show that everything in creation was connected.

The second surprise waits among the portraits. Ambras holds a famous painted portrait of Vlad the Third, the Wallachian prince better known as Vlad the Impaler. Later writers linked his name to the legend of Dracula. He stares out with large eyes and a thin moustache. Nearby hang portraits of Petrus Gonsalvus and his children, a family whose faces were covered in thick hair. Ferdinand's world collected people's stories as eagerly as objects.

The castle also carries tales of lively hospitality. Ferdinand is said to have kept a grotto for drinking games. According to the accounts that survive, a guest had to empty a large glass in one go, and those who joined in signed their names in special drinking books. Some of those books still exist. They are a small human detail, scrawled signatures from evenings of laughter, centuries ago.

Walk up to the Upper Castle and the mood changes. The Spanish Hall is a long, high room, one of the finest Renaissance halls north of the Alps. Its wooden coffered ceiling is set with carved and painted panels. Along the walls stand full-length portraits of the rulers of Tyrol. Today the hall still hosts concerts, and when music fills it, the room feels close to what Ferdinand intended. Elsewhere in the Upper Castle, a large gallery of Habsburg portraits traces the dynasty face by face.

After Ferdinand died, much of the collection eventually moved to Vienna. Today the castle belongs to the same institution as the Kunsthistorisches Museum, the great art museum of the Austrian capital. Yet a remarkable amount remains here, in the setting it was made for. Outside, the gardens and the wooded park slope away from the walls. Paths wind under old trees, and the sound of the city fades into birdsong and wind.

Before you leave, turn and look back at the white facades. Notice the painted decoration on the walls and the way the castle sits on its rise above the valley. Think of Philippine walking these paths, and Ferdinand planning his cabinets of wonders. Then let your eyes drift down to Innsbruck below, and to the mountains beyond. The view has hardly moved... even if the world around it has changed completely.
