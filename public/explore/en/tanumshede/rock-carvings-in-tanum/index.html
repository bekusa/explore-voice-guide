The guide

The story

Put your hand flat on the rock. It is cool, smooth, and almost polished, like the back of a huge grey animal asleep in the grass. Pale lichen spreads across it in small rings. Then your eye catches a line that is too straight to be natural... and then another. A boat appears out of the stone, then a figure with raised arms. You are standing on one of the great picture galleries of prehistoric Europe.

These are the rock carvings of Tanum, in the province of Bohuslän on the west coast of Sweden. Most of them date from the Nordic Bronze Age, roughly between 1700 and 500 BCE. That was a time when bronze tools and weapons reached Scandinavia through long trade networks. The carvings are spread across many separate rock faces in the landscape around Tanumshede. In 1994, UNESCO listed the area as a World Heritage Site.

Start with the rock itself, because the story begins long before any carver arrived. This is granite bedrock, among the oldest stone in this part of the world. During the last ice age, thick glaciers moved slowly across it. The ice ground the surface down and left these long, rounded slabs. Thousands of years later, people found a ready-made canvas here — smooth, open, and facing the sky.

Now look at how the images were made. They were not cut with a fine chisel. Most were pecked into the surface, probably with a harder hammerstone, one small blow after another. Each shallow dent is tiny. Together, they form outlines and grooves that have lasted for about three thousand years. Run your eyes along a single ship and try to imagine the hours of patient tapping inside that one shape.

Ships are everywhere on these panels, and here is the first surprise. Today the sea feels some distance away. In the Bronze Age, it was much closer. After the ice melted, the land slowly rose, free of the glacier's weight, and it is still rising very slightly. Many of these slabs once sat near the shoreline. The people who carved them could likely see water from where you stand now.

The boats are drawn in a particular style. Many have high, curving ends at the bow and stern, and short vertical lines along the hull. Researchers often read those lines as the crew. Some ships seem to carry figures performing an action, or objects like discs that may represent the sun. Nobody knows exactly what these boats meant. They may be real vessels, or ships in a religious story, or both at once.

People appear here too, and they are rarely standing still. Some figures hold axes or spears high above their heads. Some blow long, curved horns. Others ride in wagons or appear in hunting scenes with animals. Many male figures are drawn with exaggerated features, which most researchers connect to ideas about power, fertility, and ritual. These are not portraits. They are signs in a visual language we can only partly read.

The best-known panel in the area is at Vitlycke. It is a large sloping face packed with images. Among them is a scene that visitors often call the Bridal Couple — two figures facing each other closely, as if in an embrace. It is one of the most tender images in Scandinavian prehistory. Whether it shows a wedding, a myth, or a sacred ritual is still debated. The modern name is a guess, not a translation.

Here is the second surprise. The red paint you may see inside many carvings is not ancient. It was added in modern times, so that visitors could read the faint lines more easily. This practice is controversial. Some specialists worry that paint can harm the rock or change how people interpret the images. You will notice that some panels are painted while others are left bare. Compare them, and judge for yourself.

The most common motif here is also the easiest to miss. Look for small round hollows in the rock, often called cup marks. Some sit alone. Others gather in clusters. They are simple, but there are a great many of them, and they appear on rocks across Scandinavia. Their purpose is unknown. Some people link them to offerings, others to the sun or to fertility. They show that even the smallest mark here had meaning.

Tanum is not just Vitlycke. Other carved sites lie scattered through the surrounding fields and forest edges, with names such as Aspeberget, Fossum, and Litsleby. At Litsleby there is a large figure holding a spear, much bigger than most others in the area. Each site has its own character. Some are open and sunny, while others sit quietly among trees, where moss creeps toward the edges of the stone.

These carvings are also fragile. Lichen, frost, and rain slowly wear the surface. Air pollution in the twentieth century added acid to that rain and sped up the damage. Conservators study and protect the panels, and some are covered for part of the year. So please stay off the carved surfaces. A single boot on a wet slab can scrape away detail that has survived since the Bronze Age.

If you can, return when the light is low, in the early morning or the late evening. When the sun is close to the horizon, shadows fall into the pecked grooves. Unpainted figures that were invisible at midday suddenly appear. Rain has a similar effect, because a wet rock darkens and the lines stand out. The stone does not change. Only the light changes, and that is enough.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind in the grass and the birds at the edge of the trees. Look again at the boat closest to your feet, with its tiny crew and its curling prow. Someone knelt where you are standing and tapped it into the granite, one blow at a time. As you walk back toward the road, keep looking down at the bare rock beside the path... there may be more carvings than you think.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early or late when low sun sharpens the lines.
Come after rain for clearer views of faint figures.
Stay off the rock surfaces to protect the carvings.
Explore several sites beyond Vitlycke for contrast.
Wear sturdy shoes for paths across uneven rock.
