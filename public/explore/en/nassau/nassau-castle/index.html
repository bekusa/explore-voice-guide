The guide

The story

Start with the climb. The path rises through beech and oak, and the air turns cool and green. Below you, the river Lahn bends through its valley, slow and brown-green. Then the trees open, and grey stone walls appear on the ridge above the little town of Nassau. Wind moves through the branches. Somewhere far below, a train runs along the riverbank. This is Nassau Castle, and from this modest hilltop a family name spread across Europe and then across the oceans.

It helps to know where you are standing. This is the Lahn valley in western Germany, in the state of Rhineland-Palatinate, a short distance east of the Rhine. The river cuts a deep, winding course here, with wooded slopes on both sides. Medieval lords valued high ground like this ridge. It gave them a view of the river traffic and the valley roads, and it gave them a place that was hard to attack. The castle sits exactly where a careful builder would put it.

The story begins in the early twelfth century. A noble family known as the Counts of Laurenburg built a fortress on this hill. Over time, they took the name of their new castle as their own. They began to call themselves the Counts of Nassau. That choice of name matters more than anything else about this place. A family that started on a forested ridge above a small river would one day give its name to royal houses, cities and colonies.

The early years were not peaceful. The land under the castle was claimed by a powerful church landlord, and the counts did not have a clean title to the hill. Medieval Germany was full of disputes like this one, where bishops, archbishops and noble families competed for rights over land, tolls and people. The matter was eventually settled by agreement, and the counts kept their fortress. Even so, the quarrel shows how fragile a young family's position could be.

Now look at the stone around you. The walls are built from rough, local rock, laid in uneven courses and bound with mortar. Notice how the colours shift from grey to rust to a soft brown where the weather has worked on them. Some sections are clearly old, worn and irregular. Others are straighter and cleaner, the result of much later repair. Reading the difference between the two is one of the quiet pleasures of a visit here.

The tall tower is the clearest example. For a long period the castle stood as a ruin, abandoned as the family moved its power elsewhere. In the twentieth century, the keep was rebuilt so that visitors could once again see the castle's profile against the sky. So when you look up at the tower, you are seeing a modern reconstruction on a medieval foundation. It is not a fake. It is a deliberate effort to give the old ruin back its shape.

Here is the first surprise. The family that began on this ridge later divided into several branches, and those branches spread far beyond the Lahn. One line inherited the Principality of Orange in southern France and became known as the House of Orange-Nassau. That is the royal family of the Netherlands. Another branch became the ruling family of the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. Two modern European monarchies trace their name back to this hilltop.

You can even hear this castle in a national anthem. The Dutch anthem, the Wilhelmus, is one of the oldest national anthems in the world. Its opening line names William of Nassau, the prince who led the Dutch revolt against Spanish rule in the sixteenth century. When Dutch crowds sing that song at football matches today, they are singing a name that was first attached to the stones around you.

And here is the second surprise. The name travelled even further. In the late seventeenth century, an English settlement in the Bahamas was renamed Nassau in honour of William the Third. He was a Prince of Orange-Nassau who also became King of England. Today Nassau is the capital of the Bahamas, a Caribbean city of beaches and cruise ships. Few of its visitors know that its name points back to a quiet castle above a German river.

The town below has its own famous family. Look down the slope and you can see the ruins of a second castle, Burg Stein, on the same ridge. It belonged to the vom Stein family. Their most famous member, Baron Karl vom Stein, was born in Nassau in 1757. He became a leading Prussian reformer in the early nineteenth century. His family residence still stands in the town centre, so this small place produced two names that shaped European history.

Take a moment at the walls to look out over the valley. The Lahn runs below, and the railway follows its curves. On a clear day the wooded hills fold one behind another, darker green in the distance. In autumn the beeches turn copper and gold. In spring the valley floor is bright and fresh. The view also explains the castle's purpose. From here, anyone moving along the river could be seen long before they arrived.

The castle today is a calm, human place. There is usually a place to sit, and you may find refreshments within the walls. Families walk up from the town, and hikers pass through on the trails that follow the valley. It is worth slowing down. Run your hand along the stone, listen to the wind in the trees, and notice how small the town looks from here. The hill feels remote, yet the town is only a short walk below.

Before you leave, stand once more and look at the tower against the sky. A family took its name from this rock nearly nine centuries ago. That name now belongs to a royal house, a grand duchy, an anthem and a Caribbean capital. Yet the ridge itself has not changed much. The river still bends below, the trees still crowd the slope, and the stones are still cool under your hand. As you walk back down, listen for the river... it was here long before the name, and it will be here long after.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep wooded path from town.
Visit in autumn for copper beech colour across the valley.
Combine the castle with a stroll to the vom Stein residence.
Bring a light jacket since the ridge is windy and cool.
Allow time to walk the riverside trail along the Lahn.
