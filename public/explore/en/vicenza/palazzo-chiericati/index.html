The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the open square and look up. A long, pale facade stretches across your view, made of two rows of columns stacked one above the other. In the right light the stucco glows almost cream. Pigeons settle on the cornice. Traffic slides past at the edge of the piazza, and behind you the old town of Vicenza goes about its day. Before any history, notice the rhythm here — column, gap, column, gap — running steadily from one end to the other.

This is Palazzo Chiericati, one of the city palaces designed by Andrea Palladio. Palladio was the sixteenth-century architect whose ideas shaped buildings across Europe and, much later, across America. He looked carefully at ancient Roman ruins and tried to bring their order and proportion back into everyday buildings. Vicenza was his home ground. Many of his works stand within a short walk of this square, and the city and the surrounding villas are protected together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The palace was built for Girolamo Chiericati, a member of a local noble family. Work began around the middle of the sixteenth century. Chiericati wanted a residence that would announce his family's standing, and Palladio gave him something unusual for a town house. Instead of a closed wall with a grand doorway, he designed a facade that opens to the square, almost like the front of an ancient temple or a public forum.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The ground-floor colonnade does not sit fully on private land. Chiericati asked the city for permission to build the portico out over public ground. In return, the covered walkway stayed open to the people of Vicenza. So the shaded arcade in front of you was, from the start, a gift and a bargain at the same time — a private palace that shares its front porch with the town.

Now look at the columns themselves. The lower row follows the Doric order, the simplest of the classical styles, with plain, sturdy capitals at the top. The upper row follows the Ionic order, which you can recognise by the curled scrolls, called volutes, at the top of each column. This stacking of a heavier order beneath a lighter one comes straight from Roman practice. Palladio used it to make the building feel grounded below and lighter as it rises.

The upper floor holds a second surprise. At the two ends, the loggia stays open, so you can see through the columns to the shadows behind. But the central section is closed in, with windows set between the columns. This creates a solid block in the middle, framed by two airy wings. The effect is subtle from a distance. Walk slowly along the square, and you will see how the open and closed parts shift against each other.

Notice also that the whole palace stands on a raised base, reached by steps. This square was once known as the Piazza dell'Isola, the Island Square, because rivers ran close by and the area flooded. Lifting the building lifted it above the damp ground. It also gave the palace a stage-like presence. Standing below, you look up at the colonnade much as an ancient Roman might have looked up at a temple front.

Along the top edge of the facade, a line of statues and decorative urns stands against the sky. They break the straight roofline and lead your eye from one end of the building to the other. From the square they read as small figures, but they are life-sized or larger. On a bright afternoon their shadows fall across the cornice, and the outline of the palace changes as the sun moves.

Like many grand projects of its era, this palace was not finished quickly. Construction slowed and stopped after the sixteenth century, and part of the building remained incomplete for a long time. The facade you see today was completed much later, in the seventeenth century, following Palladio's design. So what looks like a single, unified vision is actually the work of several generations who stayed faithful to one plan.

In the nineteenth century the palace passed from the family to the city. Vicenza turned it into a public museum, and today it houses the civic art gallery. That change completed the idea that began with the public portico. A building once made to display one family's status now displays the city's shared collection of paintings, drawings and sculpture, open to anyone who walks up those steps.

Inside, the rooms still carry their sixteenth-century character. Several ceilings are richly painted, with figures, clouds and mythological scenes set inside carved and gilded frames. It is worth tilting your head back in each room, because some of the best work is above you rather than on the walls. The collection itself focuses strongly on painting from Vicenza and the wider Veneto region, including Bartolomeo Montagna, a leading painter of Vicenza in the decades before Palladio.

Outside again, turn and look across the square. Close by stands the Teatro Olimpico, the indoor theatre that was Palladio's final project. Together, the palace and the theatre make this corner of the old town a kind of open-air lesson in his career. From here, the main street of Vicenza runs west toward the Basilica Palladiana, so the whole story of the architect sits along a single walk.

Before you leave, step back under the portico for a moment. Feel the cool stone underfoot and the shade between the columns. Look out through the openings at the square, framed the way Palladio intended. Then walk away slowly and glance back once more. As the distance grows, the columns line up again into that steady rhythm — the same measured beat you noticed when you first arrived.
