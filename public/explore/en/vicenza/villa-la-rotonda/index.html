The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The noise of Vicenza fades behind you. There is gravel underfoot, a breeze moving through the trees, and maybe a bird somewhere down in the valley. Ahead, on a low green hill, stands a pale building that looks almost too calm to be real. It is a white cube under a shallow dome, with an identical temple front on every side. This is Villa La Rotonda, and for more than four centuries architects have travelled here to stand exactly where you are standing now.

The building's formal name is Villa Almerico Capra. Locals simply call it La Rotonda, the round one, after the circular hall at its heart. It was designed by Andrea Palladio, the most influential architect of the Italian Renaissance. Palladio began life as a stonecutter in Padua. He settled in Vicenza and spent most of his career here, filling the city and the countryside of the Veneto with palaces, churches and villas. Of all his country houses, this is the one people remember.

The client was Paolo Almerico, a churchman who had served at the papal court in Rome. In the 1560s he came home to Vicenza and wanted a retreat close to the city. He did not want a working farm. Most Palladian villas were practical estates, with barns, granaries and stables stretching out from the main house. La Rotonda is different. It was built for living well, for receiving guests, and above all for looking at the view.

And the view is the whole idea. Palladio wrote about this site in his famous treatise, the Four Books of Architecture, published in 1570. He described the hill as easy to climb, surrounded by other gentle hills, with a river running below. He said the setting felt like a great theatre. So he gave the house four faces, one towards each direction. Wherever the owner stood, he looked out across a different stage of fields, slopes and sky.

Walk slowly around the building and you will see what makes it so unusual. Each of the four sides carries the same grand portico. A portico is a porch held up by columns, topped with a triangular pediment, just like the front of an ancient Roman temple. Here there are six Ionic columns on each side. Ionic columns are the ones with curled, scroll-shaped tops. Broad flights of steps rise to each portico. No side is the back. No side is the servants' entrance.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The house is not lined up exactly with north, south, east and west. It is turned at an angle, roughly halfway between the compass points. The result is practical as well as beautiful. Sunlight reaches every face of the building at some point in the day, so no room is left permanently in shadow. It is a small decision on paper, but it shapes how the whole house feels inside.

Now look up at the dome. It is lower and flatter than you might expect. Palladio died in 1580, before the villa was finished. The work passed to Vincenzo Scamozzi, a younger architect who completed it. Palladio's own drawings show a taller, more rounded dome. Scamozzi built a lower one, with an opening at the top, an idea borrowed from the Pantheon in Rome. So the silhouette you see today is really the work of two minds, not one.

Along the pediments and the edges of the steps stand stone statues, figures from classical mythology and allegory. They break up the hard geometry of the building against the sky. From a distance they look tiny. Up close they are life-sized or larger. They were added in the generations after Palladio, and they help give the villa its theatrical quality. The building is a stage, and these figures are its silent audience.

Inside, if the house is open on the day of your visit, everything leads to the centre. Four corridors run in from the four porticoes and meet in the round hall under the dome. The walls and ceilings are covered in frescoes. Frescoes are paintings made directly onto wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall itself. Here they show gods, virtues and painted architecture that seems to open the rooms even further. Standing in the middle, you are at the exact point where the whole plan begins.

After Almerico's death, the villa was sold in 1591 to two brothers of the Capra family. That is why the Capra name joined his in the official title. In the twentieth century the property passed to the Valmarana family, who still own it and look after it. This is worth remembering as you walk around. La Rotonda is not a state museum. It is a private home that opens its doors to visitors, and it has been kept in remarkably good condition.

The influence of this one building is hard to overstate. In eighteenth century England, wealthy landowners copied it almost line for line. Chiswick House in London and Mereworth Castle in Kent both look back to La Rotonda. Across the Atlantic, Thomas Jefferson admired Palladio deeply, and Palladian ideas shaped his home at Monticello. Domes, temple fronts and perfect symmetry on government buildings and country houses around the world can often be traced to this hill outside Vicenza.

That legacy was formally recognised by UNESCO. In 1994 the City of Vicenza was listed as a World Heritage Site for its Palladian buildings. Two years later the listing was extended to include the Palladian villas of the wider Veneto region, and La Rotonda is among them. The villa has also appeared on film. Its rooms and porticoes were used as a setting for Joseph Losey's 1979 film of Mozart's opera Don Giovanni. Its symmetry makes it look like a stage set, even when it is entirely real.

Before you go, step back from the steps and find a spot where you can see two porticoes at once. Notice how the building changes as you move. From one angle it is a temple. From another it is a solid cube. As you walk away, along the lane or up the path towards Monte Berico, turn around once or twice. Watch the dome sink into the trees... and the white columns catch the light. Palladio built this house to be looked at from every side. Now you have done exactly that.
