The guide

The story

Stand at the water's edge and listen. There is a low, steady hum that never quite stops: engines idling, motorcycles weaving, the hiss of tyres on warm asphalt. The air carries salt from the strait, diesel from the queues, and sometimes the smell of rain moving in from the south. In front of you, a long, flat line of stone and concrete runs straight across the water. That line is the Johor–Singapore Causeway, and on the far side lies another country.

At first glance, it is not dramatic. It has no soaring towers and no graceful arches. It is a solid embankment, built low across the Straits of Johor, the narrow channel that separates the Malay Peninsula from the island of Singapore. Yet this plain strip of land may shape more daily lives than almost any monument in the region. Every morning, a river of people flows across it, and every evening, that river flows back.

The Causeway opened in 1924, during the period of British colonial rule over both Singapore and the Malay states. Before it existed, people and goods crossed this water by ferry. A railway already ran down the peninsula to Johor Bahru, but trains had to stop at the shore. The Causeway changed that. For the first time, a train could run from the peninsula straight onto the island, and road traffic could follow without waiting for a boat.

The structure is roughly one kilometre long. Builders made it as a rock-filled embankment rather than a bridge on pillars, which is why it looks so solid from the shore. That choice had a cost that people still talk about today. Because the Causeway blocks the strait almost completely, water cannot flow freely from one side to the other. Over the decades, this has been linked to changes in currents, sediment and water quality in the channel.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Near the Johor end, the original design included a small channel with a lock and a lifting bridge. This allowed small boats to pass from one half of the strait to the other. It was a practical answer to a simple problem: a solid wall across the sea leaves fishermen and traders with no way through. Look closely along the structure, and you may notice where the old crossing points sit.

The Causeway carries more than cars and trains. Running along it are large pipes that carry water. Singapore has long drawn raw water from rivers in Johor, under agreements first signed in the early years after independence. Some of that water is treated in Singapore and part of it returns north. So when you look across at this embankment, you are also looking at a lifeline. It is one reason the Causeway carries such political weight between the two countries.

The darkest chapter of the Causeway came during the Second World War. In early 1942, Japanese forces advanced rapidly down the Malay Peninsula. British and Commonwealth troops retreated across the Causeway onto Singapore island. Then they blew a gap in it, hoping to slow the advance. The gap did not hold for long. Japanese troops crossed the strait, and within weeks Singapore had fallen. The breach was later repaired, and traffic returned.

After the war came new borders. Malaya gained independence in 1957. Singapore joined Malaysia in 1963, then separated two years later in 1965 to become an independent nation. Overnight, a link between two parts of one colonial system became a crossing between two sovereign states. Immigration counters, customs checks and passport stamps became part of the journey. The Causeway itself did not move, but its meaning changed completely.

Today, the crossing is one of the busiest land border points in the world. Many people live in Johor Bahru and work in Singapore, crossing in both directions every working day. Some leave home before dawn to beat the queues. On weekends and public holidays, the flow reverses as Singaporeans drive north for food, shopping and family visits. The traffic reports on local radio often begin with one simple question: how bad is the Causeway this morning?

On the Johor side, travellers pass through a large customs and immigration complex close to the city centre. On the Singapore side, they meet the Woodlands Checkpoint. Between these two buildings, the queue becomes a small world of its own. You see motorcycle riders in raincoats, buses packed with factory workers, and families in cars with snacks on the dashboard. A crossing that takes minutes on a quiet night can take hours at peak times.

The Causeway has never been the only plan. In the 1990s, a second crossing, known as the Second Link, opened far to the west. Later, Malaysia proposed replacing the Johor half of the Causeway with a curved bridge, sometimes called the crooked bridge, that would let ships pass underneath. The idea was debated for years and then shelved. More recently, both countries have worked on a new rail link to ease the pressure on this old road.

For the best view, walk along the Johor Bahru waterfront near the old town. From here, the embankment stretches out in a clean, low line, with Singapore's apartment blocks rising on the far shore. Up on the hill behind you, the white walls of the Sultan Abu Bakar Mosque look out over the same water. At dusk, headlights form two long chains of light, one moving slowly, one moving a little faster.

Take a moment before you leave. Watch the water on either side of the embankment and notice how still it can look, held in place by stone. Listen to the engines, the horns, the distant rumble of a train. Every vehicle out there carries someone with a reason to cross: a shift to start, a parent to visit, a meal to share. As you turn back toward the city, the hum follows you for a while... and then fades into the streets of Johor Bahru.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the old town waterfront at dusk for the best view.
Avoid crossing during weekday rush hours and holiday weekends.
Carry your passport even for a quick trip across.
Check live traffic apps before heading to the checkpoint.
Bring water and patience if queuing by car or bus.
