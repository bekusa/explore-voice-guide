The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your hand. It is basalt, dark and heavy, almost black in shadow and grey where the sun has dried it. The surface is rough, full of tiny holes from ancient volcanic gas. Around you, the hill falls away toward the Afrin River valley. Olive trees cover the slopes in rows that run toward the horizon. On a still day you can hear the wind move through their silver leaves.

You are standing on a tell, the Arabic word for an artificial mound. A tell grows slowly over centuries as people build, collapse, and build again on the same spot. This one holds the remains of the Ain Dara temple, an Iron Age sanctuary from the world of the Syro-Hittite kingdoms. These were small states that rose across northern Syria and southern Turkey after the great Hittite empire of Anatolia fell apart.

The discovery came by chance. In the mid-twentieth century, a large basalt lion appeared at this site, and that sculpture drew the attention of archaeologists. Serious excavation followed in the 1980s, led by Syrian archaeologists, including Ali Abu Assaf. As the soil came away, they found the platform and walls of a temple, with carved lions and sphinxes lining its base like guards.

Scholars generally describe the temple as built and rebuilt over several phases, across a long stretch of the late second and early first millennium before Christ. We do not know for certain which god or goddess lived here. Some researchers link the temple to Ishtar, a goddess of love and war in the ancient Near East. Others suggest a storm god. The honest answer is that the stones do not tell us the name.

Now look down at the threshold. This is the detail that most visitors remember. Carved into the stone are enormous bare footprints — each one roughly a metre long. The first pair sits side by side at the entrance, as if someone stood there and paused. Further in, a single footprint appears, and then another, each one spaced as a long stride toward the inner room.

Think about what that means. The builders did not carve the steps of a priest or a king. The size suggests something far larger than a human being. Many scholars read the footprints as the path of the deity, walking into the temple to take a seat in the sanctuary. The worshipper who came here could follow, in stone, the movement of the divine. It is a rare image... a god shown not as a face, but as a passage.

Here is the second surprise. The layout of this temple has a close parallel in the Hebrew Bible. The Book of Kings describes the Temple of Solomon in Jerusalem with three parts — a porch, a main hall, and an innermost room. Ain Dara follows the same three-part plan. Biblical archaeologists have pointed to this temple as one of the closest physical comparisons we have, since nothing of Solomon's Temple survives above ground.

That comparison does not mean the two buildings were connected. It means they belonged to a shared architectural language across the Levant, the lands along the eastern Mediterranean. Builders in different kingdoms used similar ideas about sacred space. A god needed a house. A house had a door, a hall, and a private room. Standing here, you can see that idea in real stone instead of in a text.

Look at the carvings along the base of the platform. The lions are the most recognisable. Their heads face outward, their mouths often open, their bodies carved in strong blocks of basalt. Among them are sphinxes, creatures with animal bodies and human heads. Some reliefs also show mountain gods and other figures. Wind and rain have softened many faces, but the strong lines of the paws and manes still show the hand of the sculptors.

The recent history of this site is painful, and it is important to say it plainly. In early 2018, during military fighting in the Afrin region, the temple suffered serious damage from airstrikes. Reports at the time described large parts of the platform and many sculptures as destroyed or broken. What you see now may look very different from older photographs. Some carvings that appear in books may no longer stand here.

So as you walk, notice both layers. There is the ancient structure, built and rebuilt by people who lived here three thousand years ago. And there is the modern damage, with broken blocks and scattered fragments. Both are part of the story of this hill. A site like this does not stay frozen. It carries the marks of every century that passes over it, including our own.

The landscape around the tell has its own long memory. The Afrin valley is famous across Syria for its olives, and many families here have tended groves for generations. The river runs below, and fields stretch out along its banks. It is easy to imagine why ancient builders chose this rise. From the top, a temple would be visible across the valley, and the river would bring water and trade.

Before you leave, return to the threshold one last time. Stand beside the great footprints and look toward the inner room. The temple builders wanted every visitor to make this short walk, from the bright outside world into a darker, holier space. That intention survives, even where the walls do not. The steps still point inward.

As you walk back down the slope, listen again to the olive leaves and feel the basalt dust on your shoes. The lions, the footprints, and the broken stones will stay on this hill behind you. They have watched this valley for a very long time. Take a moment, and look back once more — at the dark stone against the sky.
