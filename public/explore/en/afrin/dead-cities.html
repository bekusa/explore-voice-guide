The guide

The story

Pale limestone lies everywhere here. It is in the fields, in the low walls, in the doorframes that still stand with no house behind them. Pick your way across the ground and the rock feels warm and rough under your hand, worn smooth in places by centuries of wind. Olive trees grow in red soil between the ruins. On a quiet day the only sounds are the wind, a dog in a distant village, and perhaps the bell on a goat somewhere up the slope.

You are standing in a landscape that people call the Dead Cities. The name is a little misleading. These were never really cities. They were villages, hundreds of them, spread across the limestone hills of northwestern Syria, between Aleppo and the Turkish border. In 2011 UNESCO listed them together as the Ancient Villages of Northern Syria. Scholars count roughly seven hundred abandoned settlements across several hill groups. The hills around Afrin hold one of the richest clusters.

Most of what you see dates from late antiquity, between about the first and the seventh centuries after Christ. In those centuries this region belonged first to the Roman Empire and then to its eastern successor, the Byzantine Empire, which ruled from Constantinople. The villagers were farmers. They grew olives and grapes on terraced slopes, pressed oil and wine, and sold it into the trade networks of the eastern Mediterranean. That trade made them prosperous enough to build in solid stone.

And that is the first surprise. These were ordinary rural people, not emperors or bishops. Yet they built houses, churches, tombs and oil presses from large, carefully cut blocks of limestone. Many walls were laid with little or no mortar. The blocks simply sit on one another, held by their own weight and precise cutting. Because the stone was so good and the building so careful, many structures still stand to their full height, with doorways, windows and even carved lintels in place.

Look closely at those lintels — the horizontal stones above the doors. Many carry carved crosses, circles, rosettes, or short inscriptions in Greek, the language of education and church life in this part of the empire. Some inscriptions name a builder or offer a short blessing for the household. These small carvings are some of the most personal traces left here. They show what ordinary families wanted others to see as they walked through their front door.

The villages followed a pattern. Houses were often two storeys, with a portico of stone columns along the front, facing a small courtyard. Animals and storage were on the ground floor. The family lived above. Nearby you may find a rock-cut cistern for rainwater, a press for olives, and a small church or two. Tombs stand at the edges of settlements, some cut into the rock, some built as small stone pyramids or columns.

A few kilometres from Afrin, on the ridge known as Mount Simeon, stands the most famous monument of this whole region. It is the great church built around the pillar of Saint Simeon Stylites. Simeon was a Christian ascetic of the fifth century. He chose to live on top of a stone column, exposed to sun, cold and rain, for many years. Pilgrims came from across the Roman world to see him and hear him preach.

After his death in the middle of the fifth century, a vast church complex rose on the site. Four basilicas, long halls with aisles, were arranged in a cross around an open octagon. At the centre stood his pillar. Today only a worn stump remains. Here is the second surprise — the column did not fall all at once. Over centuries, pilgrims chipped away small pieces to take home as sacred keepsakes, and slowly reduced it to what is left.

So why are these places empty? The honest answer is that historians still debate it. Most villages were gradually abandoned between the seventh and tenth centuries. Possible reasons include wars between the Byzantine and Persian empires, the Arab conquests that redrew political borders, and the decline of the Mediterranean olive trade. There seems to have been no single disaster. Instead, families drifted away over generations, and the stone houses were left standing.

Much of what we know comes from careful fieldwork in the twentieth century. The French architect and archaeologist Georges Tchalenko spent years surveying these hills and published a major study of the villages in the 1950s. His work helped show that this was not a ruined frontier but a well-organised farming society. Later researchers built on his maps and plans, and many of the sites still carry the names he recorded.

The recent history here is harder. The Syrian conflict that began in 2011 reached these hills, and in 2013 UNESCO added the ancient villages to its list of World Heritage in Danger. Reports in recent years describe damage at several sites, including the Church of Saint Simeon and the nearby Iron Age temple of Ain Dara. Some stones have been moved, some walls hit. If you see fresh breaks or scattered blocks, they may belong to this most recent chapter.

Life has not fully left the landscape, though. Modern villages sit close to the ruins. Farmers still tend olive groves on the same slopes that ancient families terraced. Shepherds move flocks through old streets. In some places, people have reused ancient blocks in newer walls and houses. The line between the old village and the living one is often blurred, and that continuity is part of what makes this place feel inhabited rather than frozen.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Look at the nearest doorway and imagine a family stepping through it fifteen centuries ago, carrying oil jars or a basket of grapes. Run your eye along the cut edges of the blocks and the carved cross above. Then look out at the olive trees on the hillside. They grow in the same soil, under the same light, that fed the people who built these walls.
