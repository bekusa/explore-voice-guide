The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through pale, twisted trees. Somewhere a peacock calls, harsh and sudden. Dust rises from the track in a soft golden cloud, and the air smells of warm stone and dry leaves. Here, on the plateau around Shivpuri, the forest does not feel dense or green like a jungle. It feels open, patient and watchful. Welcome to Madhav National Park, one of the oldest protected landscapes in central India.

This forest sits in the northern part of Madhya Pradesh, on the high ground between the plains of Gwalior and the hills further south. The land here is a mix of low ridges, rocky slopes, grassy clearings and quiet lakes. Most of the trees are dry deciduous species. That means they drop their leaves in the hot months to save water. So the forest changes its face with the seasons — green and soft after the monsoon, thin and silver-brown by early summer.

Before it was a national park, this was a royal hunting ground. The Scindia family, the Maratha rulers of the princely state of Gwalior, used these forests as their private reserve. Shivpuri itself became a summer retreat for the court, because the plateau stays a little cooler than the plains below. The park takes its name from Madho Rao Scindia, one of the Gwalior maharajas. So the name you see on the gate carries the memory of a royal household, not a wilderness explorer.

After India became independent, the old hunting ground changed its purpose. In the late 1950s the area was given protection as a national park. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The same forest where princes and their guests once hunted tigers lost its tigers completely in the decades that followed. For many years, people came here for deer, crocodiles and birds — but not for the great striped cat that once made the forest famous.

That story has a new chapter. In 2023, tigers were brought back to Madhav through a reintroduction programme, with animals moved here from other reserves in Madhya Pradesh. More recently, the area was declared a tiger reserve. So when you drive these tracks today, you travel through a forest in the middle of a comeback. Sightings are never promised, and the tigers stay shy. But simply knowing they are back changes how the silence feels.

If you look carefully at the forest edges, you will see much more than tigers. Chinkara, the small and graceful Indian gazelle, often stand in the open grassland. Nilgai, the large blue-grey antelope, move slowly between the trees. Spotted deer, called chital, gather in groups near water. Sambar, the big dark deer of Indian forests, prefer the thicker cover. Leopards also live here, but they are even harder to find than the tigers. Most visitors only see their tracks in the dust.

Water shapes everything in this landscape. Two large lakes lie within the park, Sakhya Sagar and Madhav Sagar. Both are artificial lakes, made by damming a local river during the royal period. Sakhya Sagar sits close to the main road and the town. Its edges hold one of the park's quiet residents — the mugger crocodile, a broad-snouted freshwater crocodile found across South Asia. On a warm morning, they lie on the banks like grey logs, completely still.

The lakeside also carries traces of the royal era. On the shore of Sakhya Sagar stands an old boat club, a wooden structure that stretches out over the water. Here the court and its guests once gathered to enjoy the lake. Today the wood is weathered and quiet. Nearby you may also hear of the Shooting Box, an old viewing building from which the royal hunting parties watched for game. These buildings are small, but they hold the whole history of the park in a single view.

The best-known building in the park is George Castle. It stands on high ground in the forest, with wide views across the trees and water. The castle was built by the Scindia rulers to host King George the Fifth of Britain during a royal visit to India. Locals like to tell a curious tale about it. According to the popular story, the king hunted in the area but never actually spent a night in the castle built for him. Treat this as local lore rather than settled history.

Now look at the trees around you. One of the most common here is the kardhai, a slender tree with drooping branches and small leaves. On dry slopes it forms grey, open woodland that looks almost ghostly in the afternoon light. You may also see khair trees, whose wood was traditionally used to make an ingredient for betel leaf preparations. These are not glamorous trees. But they hold the soil on the rocky ridges and feed the deer through the hardest months.

Birds give the park much of its life and sound. Peafowl walk boldly along the tracks. Kingfishers flash blue over the lakes. In the cooler months, migratory waterbirds arrive on Madhav Sagar and Sakhya Sagar, and the lakes fill with ducks and wading birds. Even if you see no large mammals, a slow drive with your eyes on the water and the branches can feel full. Patience matters more than luck here.

The park also sits very close to town, and that is the second surprise. Shivpuri is not a remote outpost. Its streets, markets and the marble memorials of the Scindia family lie only a short drive from the forest gate. Few protected forests in India sit so close to a living town. You can spend a morning watching crocodiles on the lake shore and an afternoon walking among royal cenotaphs. The wild and the ceremonial share the same plateau.

So take a moment before you leave. Feel the dry ground under your feet and the heat lifting off the stones. Listen again for the peacock, the rustle in the grass, the slap of water against the lake edge. This forest has been a royal playground, a protected refuge and now a home for returning tigers. As you walk or drive away, watch the tree line one last time. Something in there is almost certainly watching you back.

Before you go

Practical tips

Go on an early morning safari for active wildlife.
Bring binoculars for chinkara, nilgai and lakeside birds.
Visit in winter months to see migratory waterbirds.
Carry water and sun protection on the dry tracks.
Combine your visit with the Scindia memorials in Shivpuri.
