The guide

The story

Feel the grass underfoot as the slope rises. The wind comes up from the valley, cool and steady, and carries the faint hum of traffic from below. Ahead of you, one tall gatehouse stands almost alone on the crest of the hill. Two rounded towers frame a narrow arch. Around it, the ground ripples in strange humps and hollows. Nothing here is flat or quiet, and every bump in this grass has a reason.

This is Donnington Castle, on a ridge just north of Newbury in Berkshire. The castle looks out over the valley of the River Lambourn. For a fortress, that view was everything. From up here, a garrison could watch the roads that crossed this part of southern England. Travellers moving between London, Oxford and the West Country passed close by. Remember that view. It explains almost the whole story of this place, from its first stones to its final siege.

The story begins in the late fourteenth century. In 1386, King Richard the Second gave Sir Richard Abberbury a licence to crenellate. That was royal permission to fortify a home with battlements. A licence like this was partly about defence. It was also about status. A crenellated house told the neighbours that its owner had the king's favour. Abberbury was a knight with close ties to the royal court, and this castle was his statement.

What Abberbury built was a compact courtyard castle. Picture a ring of stone walls enclosing an open yard. Round towers stood at the corners, and square towers sat in the middle of the walls. Most of that circuit has gone now. You can still trace its outline in the low foundations around the hilltop. Walk along them, and the size of the original building becomes clear. It was never huge. It was a fortified home, built for comfort as well as for safety.

The gatehouse in front of you is the part that survived. It rises several storeys, with twin drum towers on either side of the entrance passage. Look at the walls closely. The building is made largely of flint, set with dressed stone at the corners and around the openings. Flint is common in this chalk country. It is hard, durable and dark, and it gives the walls their speckled grey and brown surface. Up close, the stones catch the light like broken glass.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the early fifteenth century, the castle passed to Thomas Chaucer. He was the son of Geoffrey Chaucer, the poet who wrote The Canterbury Tales, one of the founding works of English literature. Thomas was a wealthy and powerful man in his own right. He served as Speaker of the House of Commons several times. So this quiet hilltop has a direct family link to the most famous English writer of the Middle Ages.

For about two centuries after that, Donnington passed between noble and royal hands. It was a residence more than a war machine. Then, in the 1640s, England fell into civil war. King Charles the First and his supporters, called Royalists, fought against the forces of Parliament. Suddenly, the old view over the valley mattered again. The roads below were vital routes for moving troops and supplies. The Royalists placed a garrison in the castle.

The garrison was led by Colonel John Boys, a Royalist officer from Kent. Under his command, the defenders held out against Parliamentarian forces for about twenty months, from 1644 into 1646. That is a remarkable length of time for such a small fortress. The castle came under heavy bombardment. Cannon fire battered the walls and towers. Yet the garrison refused to give up, even as the war turned against the king in other parts of the country.

Now look again at those humps and hollows in the grass. They are the remains of earthwork defences dug during the Civil War. The Royalists built banks and ditches around the castle in a pointed, star-like pattern. This design came from continental military engineering. The angles let defenders fire along the faces of the earthworks, so attackers had no safe blind spot. Earth also absorbed cannonballs far better than stone. These grassy ridges were once the real front line.

In the autumn of 1644, the Second Battle of Newbury was fought close to the castle. Royalist and Parliamentarian armies clashed across the fields and lanes around here. The outcome was not decisive for either side. During and after the fighting, the castle stayed in Royalist hands. It served as a strongpoint where the king's artillery and stores could be kept safe. The surrounding countryside, so calm today, saw thousands of soldiers marching, camping and fighting through those weeks.

The siege ended in 1646. By then, King Charles had lost the war. With his permission, the garrison finally surrendered, and the defenders were allowed to march out with honour. Parliament then ordered the castle to be destroyed so it could never be used again. This deliberate demolition is called slighting. Workers pulled down the walls and towers. Much of the stone was carried away. Only the gatehouse was left standing, as it remains in front of you today.

Stand back from the arch and look up at the towers. Notice the scars and patches in the stonework. Some damage dates from the Civil War bombardment. Some comes from centuries of weather on this exposed hilltop. The castle is now cared for by English Heritage, and the site is open as a ruin set in grassland. Families picnic on the slopes. Dog walkers cross the old ditches. It is a working landscape of memory, used gently every day.

Before you leave, walk once around the hilltop. Keep the gatehouse on one side and the valley on the other. Watch how the view opens to the south, towards Newbury, and how the land falls away beneath you. That is the same view the builders wanted, and the same one the garrison guarded. As you head back down the slope, listen to the wind in the grass... and notice how quietly these old defences now rest under your feet.

Practical tips
