The guide

The story

Listen first. On a still morning here, there is almost nothing to hear. Maybe the dry rustle of grass, a lark calling somewhere low, the tick of the vehicle engine cooling. The land runs flat in every direction, pale gold and grey-green, with no hill to stop your eye. The sky feels larger than usual. And then, far off, a line of dark shapes lifts its heads all at once... and watches you.

Those shapes are blackbuck, the antelope that gives this park its name. You are standing in Velavadar, in the Bhal region of Gujarat, in Bhavnagar district. The Gulf of Khambhat lies not far to the south. This is one of the few protected places in India built entirely around open grassland, a habitat that is easy to overlook. There are no great trees here, no waterfalls. The open space is the whole point.

The park was declared in 1976. It covers a modest area, roughly thirty-four square kilometres, small enough that you can sense its edges. Before it was a park, this land was a grassland used by the royal family of Bhavnagar, the princely state that once ruled this part of the Saurashtra peninsula. Grasslands like this were valued by the rulers for hunting and for fodder. That old royal use is one reason the grass survived here while farms spread across much of the surrounding plain.

Now look closely at the herd. The animals with very dark backs, almost black on top and bright white underneath, are mature males. Their horns rise in long spirals, twisting like a corkscrew. The paler, sandy-brown animals are females and young males. Here is a detail many visitors miss — a male is not born dark. His coat darkens slowly as he grows older, so the darkest bucks are usually the most mature in the herd.

If something startles them, watch carefully. Blackbuck can run very fast across open ground, and as they flee, some of them leap high into the air with stiff legs, almost bouncing. Naturalists describe this kind of jump as stotting or pronking. One common explanation is that the leap signals strength to a predator — a way of saying, I am healthy, do not waste your energy on me. Seen across this flat land, the leaps carry a long way.

That brings us to the predators. Velavadar is one of the better places in India to look for the Indian wolf, a lean, sandy animal that hunts in these open plains. Striped hyenas live here too, though they move mostly at night. Jungle cats and golden jackals cross the tracks. You may not see any of them. But knowing they are out there changes how you read the herd — every raised head is a small act of watchfulness.

Now for the second surprise, and it belongs to winter. Each year, birds of prey called harriers arrive here from Central Asia and Europe. They are slim hawks that fly low over grass, hunting by sight and sound. In the evening, they come down to roost on the ground among the grasses. Velavadar is known as one of the largest harrier roosts recorded anywhere. At dusk, the sky can fill with them, gliding in from every side.

Several kinds of harrier gather here, including Montagu's harrier, the pallid harrier, and the marsh harrier. Telling them apart takes patience and good binoculars. But you do not need to name them to feel the moment. They tilt their wings in a shallow V, drift just above the grass, and then drop out of sight. A bird you were following a second ago is suddenly gone, folded into the ground.

The grassland also shelters a rarer bird, the lesser florican, a small bustard. Bustards are ground-living birds related loosely to cranes. During the breeding season, the male florican jumps straight up out of the grass, again and again, to show himself to females. It is a strange, almost comic display, and very hard to witness. Its presence here matters, because floricans need exactly this kind of healthy grassland to survive.

Notice what is growing at the edges of the park. You may see thorny, spreading shrubs pushing into the open grass. Many of these are Prosopis juliflora, a plant brought to India from the Americas. It spreads quickly and shades out the grasses that blackbuck depend on. Keeping this grassland open is ongoing work. The flatness that looks so natural is, in part, something people must protect year after year.

The seasons change the park completely. During the monsoon, rain soaks the low ground, and parts of the plain turn to mud and shallow water. The park usually closes to visitors for these wet months. After the rains, the grass grows tall and green, then slowly dries to gold through winter. By the hot months, the land looks bare and bleached, and the animals gather where shade and water are easiest to find.

Think, too, about the villages around you. This park is not a wilderness far from people. Farms and settlements sit close to its boundaries, and blackbuck sometimes wander into nearby fields. That closeness brings real tension, but it also explains why Velavadar feels so different from a deep forest reserve. Here, wild animals and human life share one wide, flat landscape, separated by little more than a fence line and a habit of tolerance.

Before you leave, take one more slow look across the plain. Notice how the light sits on the grass, how far you can see, how small the vehicles look against the horizon. Watch one dark buck with his spiral horns, standing still and alert. As you drive away, keep glancing back. The herd will likely be watching you too, until you become just another shape at the edge of their open world.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive at dawn when blackbuck are most active.
Stay for dusk in winter to see harriers roost.
Bring binoculars for distant herds and raptors.
Check seasonal closure before planning a monsoon visit.
Wear muted colours to avoid startling the herds.
