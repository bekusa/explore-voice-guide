The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the nearest wall. The stone is red sandstone, soft and warm in colour, the shade of old brick or dried clay. Where the rain has worked on it for centuries, the surface has worn into shallow hollows and ridges. Underfoot, the grass is springy and uneven, because it is growing over the foundations of rooms that once held kings. Listen for a moment. You may hear jackdaws in the high windows, and wind moving through openings that no longer have glass.

You are at Kenilworth Castle, a few miles north of Leamington Spa, on the edge of the small town that shares its name. Today it is a ruin, cared for by English Heritage. But for around five hundred years, this was one of the great fortresses and palaces of England. Almost every age left something behind here. Norman lords, medieval princes and an Elizabethan favourite all built on this same patch of Warwickshire ground.

The story begins in the early twelfth century. Geoffrey de Clinton, a powerful official in the court of King Henry the First, founded a castle here. The oldest major structure you can see is the Great Tower, a massive square keep. Look at the thickness of its walls where they are broken open. These were built to resist attack, not to welcome guests. Over time, the castle passed into royal hands, and the crown began to invest in it seriously.

Now here is something most visitors miss. Look out across the low, green land around the castle. For much of its life, this ground was underwater. Medieval builders dammed local streams to create a great artificial lake, often called the Mere. The water wrapped around the castle on several sides. It turned the fortress into something close to an island. The lake is gone now, drained long ago, but once you know it was there, the shape of the landscape starts to make sense.

That water mattered in 1266. After the barons' rebellion against King Henry the Third, and the death of their leader Simon de Montfort the year before, rebel defenders held Kenilworth against the royal army. The siege lasted around six months. It is often described as the longest siege in medieval English history. Attackers struggled to get close across the water. In the end, hunger and disease, not walls breaking, brought the defenders out. The terms of their surrender became known as the Dictum of Kenilworth.

The castle has a darker royal chapter too. In the 1320s, King Edward the Second was held here as a prisoner after his queen and her allies turned against him. It was at Kenilworth that he gave up his crown in 1327. Imagine news of that moment leaving these walls, carried by riders along muddy roads, changing the course of the kingdom from inside a building you can still walk through.

In the late fourteenth century, the castle changed character. John of Gaunt, one of the richest and most powerful men in England and a son of King Edward the Third, rebuilt much of the inner court as a palace. His great hall was one of the finest of its time. Look for the tall window openings high in the walls. They show how much light and space he wanted. Fortress was becoming residence, and status was now measured in glass and height, not just in thickness of stone.

Then comes the most famous chapter. Queen Elizabeth the First granted Kenilworth to Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, her close friend and, many believed, the man she might have married. Dudley rebuilt parts of the castle to impress her. He added large new apartments, now called Leicester's Building, with huge windows. At the time, wide windows full of glass were an open display of wealth. Even in ruin, you can see how they would have glowed at night.

In the summer of 1575, Elizabeth came to stay. Dudley staged one of the most lavish entertainments of her whole reign. It lasted around nineteen days. There were feasts, music, hunting, fireworks and performances on the water of the Mere. Some historians see the whole visit as a final, very public attempt by Dudley to win the queen's hand. If that was the plan, it did not work. Elizabeth never married.

Here is a second surprise. The formal garden in front of you, if you visit it, is a careful modern recreation. English Heritage rebuilt Dudley's Elizabethan garden, using a detailed eyewitness description written by a visitor in 1575. That letter described an aviary, a marble fountain, fruit trees and scented flowers. So when you walk its paths, you are following the words of someone who stood here and wrote home about it more than four centuries ago.

Why is the castle a ruin? After the English Civil War of the seventeenth century, Parliament ordered that Kenilworth should never again serve as a stronghold. Around 1650, soldiers deliberately damaged it. They broke down one side of the Great Tower and drained the Mere. This deliberate damage is called slighting. Look closely at the keep, and you will see that one whole wall is missing. That gap is not decay. It was done on purpose.

Leicester's Gatehouse survived because it was turned into a home. People went on living in it for centuries after the rest of the castle fell silent. Inside, an exhibition tells the story of Elizabeth and Dudley. Look for the carved fireplace, decorated with initials and emblems linked to Dudley. In the early nineteenth century, Sir Walter Scott's novel, simply called Kenilworth, brought romantic visitors here in large numbers, and the ruins have drawn them ever since.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot and look back at the whole site. Picture water where the grass is now. Picture glass in those empty windows, smoke from the kitchens, and music drifting across the lake on a summer night in 1575. Then let the picture go, and see what is actually here. Red stone, open sky, birds in the walls. As you walk away, notice how the castle sits low in the land, and how long it stays in view behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the viewing platforms for views over the old Mere.
Start in Leicester's Gatehouse to understand the Elizabethan story.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven grass and worn stone steps.
Visit the garden in summer when the flowers are out.
Allow time to walk the outer walls, not just the inner court.
