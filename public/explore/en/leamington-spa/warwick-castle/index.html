The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look up at the towers, notice the sound of the River Avon sliding past the foot of the walls. The water is slow and brown here, and it carries the cries of the castle peacocks across the grounds. Underfoot, the gravel crunches. Above you, sandstone rises in shades of honey, rust and grey, stained by centuries of English rain. You are standing a short journey from the Regency streets of Leamington Spa, in the old county town of Warwick. And this rock above the river has been guarded for more than a thousand years.

The story begins before any castle at all. In the early tenth century, Æthelflæd, daughter of Alfred the Great, ruled the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Mercia. Mercia covered much of central England. She fortified Warwick as one of a chain of defended towns, called burhs, against Viking raiders. Nothing of her defences survives in the stone you can see today. But her choice of site explains everything that followed. A rise of ground, a river crossing, and a clear view across the Midlands... it was simply too useful to abandon.

Then came the Normans. In 1068, two years after his victory at Hastings, William the Conqueror ordered a castle built here. It was a motte and bailey, a classic Norman design. That means a tall earth mound topped with a timber tower, and a fenced yard below it. Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. That original mound still exists. It sits at the western end of the grounds, now grassy and planted with trees. Climb it, and you are standing on earth that Norman labourers piled up by hand.

Over the following centuries, timber gave way to stone. The great walls you see now took shape in the later Middle Ages, under the Beauchamp family, who held the title Earl of Warwick. In the fourteenth century, they added the two towers that still dominate the skyline. Caesar's Tower overlooks the river. Guy's Tower faces the town. Despite the name, Caesar's Tower has no link to Rome at all. Look closely at its top, and you will see a double layer of battlements, built so defenders could fight from two levels at once.

Guy's Tower takes its name from a legend. According to medieval romance, Guy of Warwick was a giant-slaying knight who fought monsters across England and beyond. Locals say he killed a terrifying beast called the Dun Cow, a huge wild cow that roamed the countryside. There is no evidence that Guy ever lived. But the earls of Warwick loved the story and claimed him as an ancestor. A legend became family propaganda, and the propaganda became architecture. That tower is, in a sense, a stone advertisement for a hero who probably never existed.

In the fifteenth century, the castle belonged to one of the most powerful men in English history. Richard Neville, Earl of Warwick, became known as the Kingmaker. During the Wars of the Roses, the long civil war between the rival royal houses of York and Lancaster, he helped put kings on the throne and take them off again. He held enormous wealth and armies of followers. He died in battle in 1471. Walk the ramparts and imagine messengers riding in with news that could change who wore the crown.

Now a surprise. Warwick Castle never saw the kind of grand siege that its walls seem built for. It did come under attack during the English Civil War in the seventeenth century, when Parliament's forces held it against royalist troops. But for most of its life, its power was political rather than military. The towers and gatehouses were about control and status as much as defence. A great castle told everyone, friend and rival alike, exactly who ruled this part of England.

In 1604, King James the First granted the castle to Sir Fulke Greville, a poet and courtier. Greville spent heavily turning the old fortress into a comfortable home. His end was grim. In 1628, he was stabbed by his own servant in London and died of his wounds. Legend has it that his ghost now walks the Watergate Tower, near the river. Whether you believe that or not, the tower is a good place to pause. The stone there is cool, and the light comes in narrow and dim.

As warfare changed, the castle softened. Later owners filled the riverside range with state rooms, panelled walls, paintings and armour. The Great Hall is the largest room, and it suffered a serious fire in 1871. It was restored afterwards, so much of what you see inside reflects Victorian taste as well as older history. That mix is worth noticing. This is not a frozen medieval ruin. It is a building that families kept changing for nearly a thousand years, each generation adding its own idea of grandeur.

Step outside and look at the landscape. In the eighteenth century, the earls hired Lancelot Brown, known to history as Capability Brown, England's most famous garden designer. He earned that nickname by telling clients their land had great capability for improvement. Brown swept away formal gardens in favour of open lawns, trees and gentle curves of river. The broad green slopes running down to the Avon carry his ideas. Later came the Peacock Garden and a Victorian rose garden, adding colour and a touch of theatre.

In the late nineteenth century, Daisy Greville, Countess of Warwick, hosted lavish parties here for royalty and high society. Today a waxwork exhibition inside recreates one of those gatherings. That points to the castle's modern life. In 1978, the Earl of Warwick's family sold it to the Tussauds Group, the company behind the famous waxworks. Today it is run as a major visitor attraction. Some people find the shows and costumed actors surprising in such an old place. But in a way, the castle has always been a stage.

One of the most popular sights stands near the river. It is a working trebuchet, a giant medieval siege engine that throws heavy objects using a swinging arm and a counterweight. This is a modern reconstruction, built to medieval designs. When it fires, the arm swings up with a deep creak of timber, and the crowd goes quiet for a heartbeat. It is a rare chance to see how armies once broke down walls like the ones standing behind you. The thud of the landing carries across the water.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot by the riverbank and look back. From here, Caesar's Tower rises straight out of the rock, and its reflection shivers in the Avon. Listen again for the peacocks, the water, the voices drifting down from the ramparts. Æthelflæd's lookout, William's mound, the Kingmaker's walls and Brown's lawns are all part of one view. As you walk back towards town, notice the sandstone in the older houses along the way. It is the same warm stone, quarried from the same ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow most of a day to see towers, rooms and grounds.
Wear sturdy shoes for steep, narrow tower staircases.
Check the daily schedule for trebuchet firings before arriving.
Walk the riverbank for the clearest view of Caesar's Tower.
Arrive early to climb the ramparts before crowds build up.
