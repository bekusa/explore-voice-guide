The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, the city hums with buses and bicycles. In front of you, a tall tower of rough, honey-grey stone rises straight up, plain and almost windowless. Run your eyes over its surface. The blocks are uneven and patched, softened by nearly a thousand years of English rain. Nearby, water slides past in a narrow channel called the Castle Mill Stream. This is Oxford Castle, and it is far older than the colleges most visitors come to see.

The story begins just after 1066, when William the Conqueror took the English crown. The Normans needed to control the towns they had won. In Oxford, that task went to a Norman baron called Robert D'Oyly. Around 1071, he began building a castle on the western edge of the town. It was not a fairy-tale palace. It was a tool of control — a fortress meant to watch over the river crossings and remind local people who was now in charge.

The first castle was mostly earth and timber. Workers piled up a huge mound of soil, called a motte, and crowned it with a wooden tower. Around it lay an enclosed yard, known as a bailey, protected by banks and ditches. You can still see the motte today. It is the grassy, steep-sided hill nearby, now covered in trees. Many visitors walk past it and assume it is just a park feature... but it is the core of the Norman castle.

Now look again at the stone tower. This is Saint George's Tower, and it carries a small mystery. Historians still debate exactly when it was built. Some believe it is Saxon, built before the Normans arrived, as part of the older town defences. Others think it is early Norman work. Either way, it is one of the oldest standing structures in Oxford. That surprises many people, who expect the university to hold that title.

The tower was linked to a chapel dedicated to Saint George. A small college of priests served it, founded within a few years of the castle itself. Beneath the site, a crypt survives, with short, heavy columns and rounded arches typical of early Norman building. One of the priests connected with this college was Geoffrey of Monmouth. He wrote a famous history of the kings of Britain. That book helped spread the legends of King Arthur across medieval Europe.

That detail is worth pausing on. Long before Oxford became a centre of learning, writers here were already shaping stories that would last for centuries. Geoffrey's history mixed real events with myth. Merlin, Arthur, and the ancient kings of Britain all appear in it. Scholars today do not treat it as reliable history. Yet its influence was enormous. In a quiet way, this castle site stands at the start of a long literary tradition in the city.

The most dramatic chapter came in the twelfth century, during a long civil war. Two cousins, Stephen and the Empress Matilda, both claimed the English throne. In 1142, Matilda was trapped inside Oxford Castle while Stephen's forces surrounded it. The siege lasted for weeks through a hard winter. Food ran short. Surrender seemed close. Then, according to the medieval chroniclers, she simply vanished from the castle.

The accounts say she escaped at night, wearing white to blend into the snow. With a few companions, she slipped past the enemy and crossed the frozen river on foot. Some versions say she was lowered from a tower on a rope. The details may have grown in the retelling, and historians treat the most colourful parts with caution. But the chronicles agree that she got away. It is one of the great escape stories of English history.

Over the following centuries, the castle lost its military importance. During the English Civil War of the seventeenth century, Oxford served as the headquarters of King Charles the First. The castle was used again, but afterwards much of it was pulled down. What remained took on a darker role. For hundreds of years, this site was a prison. The walls that once kept enemies out now kept people in.

That prison history continued well into modern times. Oxford's jail operated here until 1996, when it finally closed. Many buildings you see around the courtyard date from later rebuilding, especially in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Look for the long blocks of stone with small, barred windows set in neat rows. Those rows mark the former cells. Some of the prison was later turned into a hotel, where guests now sleep in rooms built from cells.

Today the site is open as a heritage attraction. Guided tours are often led by guides in costume, playing characters from the castle's past. On a tour, you can usually climb Saint George's Tower, step down into the crypt, and walk through parts of the old prison. You may also be able to climb the motte. Inside the mound lies a medieval well chamber, a cool stone space that few visitors expect to find there.

The climb up the tower is narrow and steep, with a winding stone staircase. At the top, the view opens across the rooftops. You can see spires, domes, and the green line of hills beyond the city. From this height, it becomes clear why the Normans chose this spot. The river channels, roads, and approaches all lie below. Whoever held this tower could see trouble coming long before it arrived.

Around the castle, the modern city has grown right up to its edges. A paved square now holds cafes, restaurants, and benches. On a warm day, people sit outside with coffee where prisoners once exercised. The contrast is sharp. Ancient stone stands beside glass and steel. Children run across the courtyard. Office workers pass on their lunch break, barely glancing up at a tower that has watched this ground for nearly a thousand years.

Before you leave, take one more look. Notice the grassy mound, the plain grey tower, the rows of small windows, and the dark water of the stream. Each part belongs to a different chapter — conquest, faith, siege, punishment, and now quiet reuse. As you walk back towards the centre, you will pass colleges and shops that feel very old. Remember that this rough stone tower was here first, watching the town grow up around it.
