The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind the old stone walls, the traffic on the High Street fades into something softer. Gravel crunches underfoot. Somewhere nearby, water moves slowly past the reeds of the River Cherwell, and the air smells of damp earth, clipped box hedges, and whatever happens to be flowering this week. You are standing in the oldest botanic garden in Britain, a walled garden that has been growing plants for science for four hundred years.

The garden was founded in 1621. At that time, it had a very practical name: the Physic Garden. Physic was the old word for medicine. The idea was simple and serious. Students of medicine at the University of Oxford needed living plants to study, because most medicines of the period came from herbs, roots, and seeds. A doctor who could not tell one leaf from another could easily poison a patient instead of curing one.

The money came from Henry Danvers, the Earl of Danby, a soldier and courtier who gave the university a large sum to create the garden. His name is still here, carved in stone. The great gateway you may have walked through, or can see at the end of the main path, is called the Danby Gateway. It was built in the early 1630s. Its design is usually credited to the mason and sculptor Nicholas Stone. Look closely and you will find statues of King Charles the First and King Charles the Second, along with a bust of Danby himself.

Here is a detail that most visitors miss. This piece of land had a very different life before it became a garden. In the Middle Ages, it lay just outside the city wall, and it was used as a burial ground for Oxford's Jewish community. That community was expelled from England in 1290, along with all Jews in the kingdom. Today a small memorial stone near the entrance remembers them. It is a quiet reminder that this peaceful ground carries a much older and sadder story.

The site also had a practical problem. It sat low beside the Cherwell, and the river flooded. So before planting could begin, the ground had to be raised. According to accounts of the garden's early years, thousands of cartloads of soil, rubble, and dung were brought in to lift the level of the land. The high stone walls went up at the same time. They protected the plants from wind, from grazing animals, and from people who might be tempted to help themselves to valuable herbs.

The first keeper of the garden was a man named Jacob Bobart. He was German, he had served as a soldier, and he ran the garden for decades. Stories about him are colourful. Locals liked to say he walked the streets with a pet goat, and that his beard was trimmed with silver on special days. Treat those tales with a smile rather than as hard history. What is certain is that his son, also called Jacob Bobart, followed him as keeper and became a respected botanist in his own right.

Now for one of the strangest stories connected to this place. A small yellow daisy-like plant, now known as Oxford ragwort, was grown here after it arrived from the volcanic slopes of Sicily. It did not stay inside the walls. Its seeds escaped and spread across the old stone walls of Oxford. Later, when the railways came, the plant found its perfect home. The loose stones and cinders along the tracks reminded it of volcanic rock, and the wind of passing trains carried its seeds across much of Britain.

As you walk, notice how the garden is laid out. The oldest part is the Walled Garden, with its long straight paths and rectangular beds. Here the plants are arranged with a purpose. They are grouped to show how different plant families are related to each other, so a walk along the beds becomes a walk through the history of plant evolution. Small labels give each plant its scientific name. For a student four centuries ago, and for a student today, this is a living library.

Beyond the walls lies the Lower Garden, which feels looser and more open. There are herbaceous borders, a water garden, and wide lawns that run down towards the river. On a warm day you will often see punts gliding past on the Cherwell, with someone pushing a long pole into the riverbed. Across the water, meadows and willows add to the feeling that the city is far away, even though the busy road is only a short walk behind you.

The garden has a strong place in literature too. Lewis Carroll, the author of Alice in Wonderland, lived and taught in Oxford, and he walked here with the real Alice Liddell and her sisters. Many people believe parts of the garden found their way into his stories, though that connection is more tradition than proof. Much more recently, the novelist Philip Pullman placed a wooden bench in this garden at the end of his trilogy His Dark Materials. Fans still come looking for it, and many leave small notes or simply sit for a while.

Another writer loved this place as well. J. R. R. Tolkien, the author of The Lord of the Rings, spent most of his working life in Oxford. He was very fond of a large old black pine that grew here, and he was photographed beside it late in his life. Sadly the tree became unsafe as it aged, and it was taken down in 2014. Many visitors still ask about it. Its story shows how a single tree can matter deeply to one person, and then to thousands of readers.

On the river side of the garden, you will find the glasshouses. Step inside and the air changes at once. Some houses are warm and heavy with moisture, full of tropical leaves, orchids, and plants that eat insects. Others are dry and bright, home to cacti and succulents from deserts. The glass lets the garden grow plants that could never survive an English winter outdoors. For scientists, these collections are just as important as the outdoor beds.

Before you go, take a moment to look up and out. Just beyond the walls rises the tall tower of Magdalen College, a great stone landmark of Oxford. Each year on the morning of the first of May, a choir sings from the top of that tower at dawn, and crowds gather on the bridge below to listen. From here you can enjoy the same tower at any season, framed by the branches and beds of the garden.

So pause where you are standing now. Notice the age of the stone in the walls, darkened and softened by four hundred years of rain. Notice the labels, the bees, the sound of the river. This is still a working garden, still teaching, still growing. As you walk back through the gateway and into the street, you carry a little of its quiet with you... and perhaps, on some railway line far from Oxford, you will spot a small yellow flower that once lived here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the Walled Garden, then walk down to the river.
Bring a light layer for the warm, humid glasshouses.
Visit in late spring or summer for the fullest borders.
Combine your visit with a walk around nearby Magdalen College.
Come early on a weekday for a quieter, slower visit.
