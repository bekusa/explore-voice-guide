The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves through the birch trees. Somewhere, a gravel path crunches under another visitor's feet. In front of you rises a large timber house, its boards weathered and painted. It looks like a working farm, plain and practical. Inside, though, the walls are covered in flowers, vines, figures and colour. That contrast is the whole story of the Decorated Farmhouses of Hälsingland.

Hälsingland is a province in central Sweden, stretching inland from the Gulf of Bothnia across forests, lakes and river valleys. It is not a single town or museum. The UNESCO World Heritage listing, granted in 2012, covers seven farms spread across the region. Each one is a separate visit. Together, they show how farming families here built and decorated their homes during a period of unusual prosperity, mostly from the late eighteenth century into the nineteenth century.

So where did the money come from? Much of it came from flax. Hälsingland farmers grew flax and turned it into linen, and linen sold well. Forests mattered too, as timber became more valuable. These were independent, land-owning farmers, not tenants working for a lord. With their new wealth, they did something interesting. They did not leave the countryside or try to copy city life. Instead, they built bigger farmhouses, and they made them beautiful.

Here is the first surprise. Many of the finest rooms were hardly lived in at all. Families often kept their everyday life in a modest part of the house. The grand, painted rooms were saved for special occasions — weddings, funerals, holiday feasts, and visits from important guests. Some farms even had a whole separate building for celebrations. Imagine spending money on a room you might open only a few times a year. It tells you how much these gatherings mattered.

Now think about who painted these rooms. The work was usually done by travelling painters. Some were local craftsmen. Others came from neighbouring provinces, especially Dalarna to the southwest, which had its own strong tradition of folk painting. These artists moved from farm to farm, sometimes staying for weeks. They brought pattern books, ideas and techniques with them. Because of this, you can sometimes notice similar motifs in houses many kilometres apart.

The styles mix in a fascinating way. The painters borrowed from the fashions of the upper classes, such as Baroque and Rococo. Baroque is a heavy, dramatic style full of curves, and Rococo is its lighter, more playful successor. But the farm painters translated these ideas into their own language. Swirling flowers grow larger than life. Columns and draperies are painted flat on the wall. A city fashion becomes something warmer and more personal.

Look especially for the large, curling leaf and flower shapes. In Dalarna-style painting, this motif is often called kurbits, a word linked to the gourd plant in a Bible story. It grows across walls, cupboards and doorframes in bold reds, greens, blues and yellows. It is decorative, but it also fills space with a sense of abundance. For a farming family, abundance was the point. A full harvest meant a good year.

Here is the second surprise. In many rooms, biblical scenes appear with people dressed in the clothing of the painter's own time. A prophet or a king might wear a coat and hat that a Swedish farmer would recognise. Distant cities from scripture can look like local towns. This was common in Scandinavian folk painting. It made old stories feel close and familiar, as if they happened just down the road.

Not all the decoration was painted directly on the boards. Some rooms used painted textiles or paper hung on the walls, and some used stencilled patterns. Over time, owners also bought printed wallpaper as it became more available. In some houses you can see several layers of taste from different decades side by side. The rooms show how a family kept updating its home, one generation after another, without throwing everything away.

Now step back outside and look at the farm as a whole. These farmsteads were often arranged with several buildings grouped around a yard. There might be the main house, barns, storehouses and stables. The timber construction is typical of the region, with logs and boards made from local forests. The size of the main house is worth noticing. For a rural family, a two-storey home this large made a clear statement about success.

The seven listed farms each have their own character. Among them are Kinsta, Gästgivars, Pallars, Jon-Lars, Bommars, Fågelsjö Gammelgård, and Erik-Anders. Some are still privately owned. Some are run as museums or cultural sites. Because of this, access differs from farm to farm. Many open mainly in the summer season, and some interiors can only be seen on a guided tour. Checking ahead is wise, especially if you plan to visit several in one trip.

The seven are not the only decorated houses in the province. Hundreds of farms in Hälsingland have painted interiors of some kind. The UNESCO selection represents the best preserved and most complete examples. So as you drive between sites, notice the large wooden farmhouses along the roads. Some of them may hold painted rooms that few visitors will ever see. The landscape itself carries this history.

Why did this tradition survive so well? Partly because the festive rooms were used so rarely. Less daily use meant less wear, less smoke and less need to repaint. Many families also kept their farms over long periods, and they valued what their ancestors had made. Today, conservation work helps protect the fragile paint. Inside, you may be asked not to touch the walls. Even the oils on a fingertip can damage old pigment.

Before you leave, take one last look at the house in front of you. From the outside, it belongs to the forest and the fields. Inside, it holds the hopes of a family that wanted to celebrate well. As you walk back along the path, listen to the wind in the trees again. Somewhere behind those plain timber walls, painted flowers are still blooming, waiting for the next feast.
