The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves across the sand. A bell sounds somewhere behind thick, pale walls. Then there is a quiet that feels heavier than silence. The light here is sharp and white, and the ground underfoot is fine and dusty. You are standing at one of the oldest living monasteries in the Christian world. Monks have prayed in this stretch of desert for more than sixteen hundred years, and the rhythm of their day still sets the pace of this place.

This valley is Wadi El Natrun, in Egypt's Beheira Governorate. It sits west of the Nile Delta, roughly between Cairo and Alexandria. The name comes from natron, a natural salt that forms around the shallow lakes here. Ancient Egyptians collected it and used it to dry and preserve bodies for mummification. So long before any monk arrived, this low, salty basin already had a link to death and the afterlife. In early Christian writings, the area had another name — Scetis.

The monastery carries the name of Saint Macarius the Great, an Egyptian ascetic of the fourth century. An ascetic is someone who gives up comfort to focus on prayer. According to a popular tradition, Macarius once worked as a camel driver, carrying natron across this very desert. Whether or not that story is true, it is clear that he settled in Scetis in the fourth century. He became one of the central figures among the people we now call the Desert Fathers.

Who were they? They were men, and some women, who left the towns of Roman Egypt to live alone or in small groups in the wilderness. Their short sayings and stories were collected and copied, and they spread across the Christian world. These texts still shape monastic life today, from Ethiopia to Ireland. Many of those stories feature Macarius himself. He often appears gentle and humble — a teacher who preferred patience to harsh words.

Life here in those early years did not look like the walled complex in front of you. The first monks lived in simple cells scattered across the desert. They spent the week alone, praying and working with their hands. A common task was weaving baskets and mats from palm fibre. At the weekend they gathered for shared worship and a common meal. Slowly, those gatherings grew into a settled community around a church. That community became the monastery you see now.

The desert gave the monks solitude, but it did not give them safety. Over the centuries, raiders attacked the monasteries of Wadi El Natrun several times. Buildings were burned and monks were killed or scattered. Each time, the community returned and rebuilt. In the ninth century, high protective walls were raised around the monastery. Look at their thickness and their plain, solid surfaces. They were built for defence, not decoration, and that purpose is still easy to read in the stone.

Inside the walls stands one of the most striking structures here — the keep. This is a tall, fortified tower, a last refuge for the monks during an attack. Traditionally, it could only be entered by a drawbridge from a nearby building. Once inside, the monks could survive for a time with stored food, water, and a chapel for prayer. Many monasteries in this valley have a keep like this. It is a reminder that prayer and danger often lived side by side.

The heart of the monastery is the Church of Saint Macarius. Parts of it are very old, and it has been repaired and extended many times. One sanctuary inside is linked to Pope Benjamin the First, a leader of the Coptic Church in the seventh century. The Coptic Church is Egypt's ancient Christian church, with its own language of worship and its own traditions. Here you may see traces of old wall paintings, wooden screens, and the soft glow of lamps against dark plaster.

The monastery also keeps relics, which are physical remains of holy figures. Among them are those of three saints who shared the name Macarius, including the founder himself. Monastic tradition also holds that relics found here during renovation work in the twentieth century belong to John the Baptist and the prophet Elisha. This is a belief of the community rather than a settled historical fact. For the monks and pilgrims, though, it adds deep meaning to the church.

Here is something many visitors miss. This monastery has long had a special role in choosing the leaders of the Coptic Church. Over the centuries, many Coptic popes came from the monks of Saint Macarius. For a period, newly chosen popes would even travel here as part of their path into office. So this quiet desert community has shaped decisions far beyond its walls, in Alexandria and Cairo, and across the Coptic world.

And here is a second surprise. By the middle of the twentieth century, this ancient monastery was close to empty. Only a handful of elderly monks remained. Then, in 1969, a monk known as Father Matta El Meskeen — his name means Matthew the Poor — arrived with a group of followers. Their arrival began a remarkable revival. The community grew again, new buildings rose, and the monastery became a respected centre of study, writing, and prayer.

As you look beyond the old core, you may notice fields and orchards. The monks have spent decades farming land that was once bare sand. They work it with modern irrigation, and the green rows stand out sharply against the beige desert. This continues an old idea from the Desert Fathers — that work with the hands is part of spiritual life. Bread, crops, and daily labour still matter here as much as books and chant.

Remember that this is a working monastery, not a museum. The monks pray at set hours, and some areas are closed to visitors. Dress modestly and speak softly. If a monk offers to guide you, accept — they often share stories you will not find on any sign. Visits can also be limited during major fasting periods in the Coptic calendar, so it is wise to check before you travel.

Before you leave, pause for a moment. Feel the warmth coming off the walls and the dry air on your skin. Listen again for the wind, the footsteps of a monk crossing a courtyard, perhaps a bell. Macarius walked this same desert sixteen centuries ago, looking for quiet. As you head back toward the road, notice how slowly that quiet seems to fade behind you.
