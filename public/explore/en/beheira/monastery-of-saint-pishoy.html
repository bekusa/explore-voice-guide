The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here in the desert, the loudest sound may be the wind moving over sand and the soft scrape of sandals on stone. The air is dry and carries a faint mineral smell. Mud-brick walls the colour of the desert itself rise around you, and above them small white domes catch the light. You are in Wadi El Natrun, between Cairo and Alexandria, at one of the oldest living monasteries in the Christian world.

This is the Monastery of Saint Pishoy, whose name is also written Bishoy. It belongs to the Coptic Orthodox Church, the ancient Christian church of Egypt. The word Coptic comes from an old word for Egyptian. For more than fifteen centuries, monks have prayed, worked and slept inside these walls. It is not a museum. It is a home, and the people walking past you in black robes are part of a tradition that started in this valley.

The valley itself has a story to tell. Wadi El Natrun means the Valley of Natron. Natron is a natural salt that collects in the shallow lakes here. Ancient Egyptians used it to dry and preserve bodies during mummification. So long before any monk arrived, this low, salty depression in the Western Desert was already tied to ideas of death, preservation and the life beyond. In early Christian writings, the area was known as Scetis.

In the fourth century, men began coming here to live alone in the desert. They wanted silence, hardship and a life given entirely to prayer. Their leader and teacher was Saint Macarius the Great. These Desert Fathers, as they are now called, lived in caves and simple cells scattered across the valley. Their sayings were written down and copied across the Christian world. Many ideas of Christian monasticism, in both East and West, can be traced back to this sand.

Saint Pishoy was one of the disciples of Macarius. He lived in the late fourth and early fifth centuries. Coptic tradition remembers him for his long fasts and his strict discipline. One popular tale says that, to stay awake through the night prayers, he tied his hair to a rope fixed to the ceiling of his cell. Whether that is history or legend, it shows how the Copts remember him: a man who refused to let even sleep come between him and God.

Another story is loved even more. According to tradition, Pishoy once met an old, tired traveller on the road and carried him on his back. The traveller became very heavy... and then revealed himself as Christ. Because of this tale, the saint is often called the beloved of our good Saviour. Look at the icons inside the churches. You may see him shown with a figure on his shoulders or washing feet. Locals tell these stories with real warmth.

Life in the valley was not always peaceful. Over the centuries, desert raiders attacked the monasteries again and again. Pishoy himself is said to have left Scetis after one of these raids and died in Upper Egypt. Later, his body was brought back here. Today the monks keep his relics in the main church, and believers say his body has remained whole. Pilgrims come from across Egypt to pray beside it, often touching the reliquary with great care.

Those raids also shaped the buildings around you. Find the tall, square tower inside the walls. This is the keep, called a qasr in Arabic. In times of danger, the monks climbed into it and pulled up the bridge behind them. Inside were stores of food, a well, and even a chapel, so they could survive a siege and continue to pray. It is a fortress built not for war, but for waiting.

Now look at the walls and domes more closely. Many of the older parts are built of mud brick covered in lime plaster. The domes are low and rounded, which helps keep the rooms below cooler in the desert heat. Doorways are often small, so you must bow your head to pass through. Inside, the light is soft and dim, and the air smells of incense and old wood. The thick walls hold back the noise of the outside world.

The monastery has a more recent chapter too, one that many visitors miss. In 1981, the Coptic Pope, Shenouda the Third, was removed from office by the Egyptian government and sent to live here. He stayed at the monastery for several years before he was allowed to return. He loved this place. When he died in 2012, he was buried here, and his tomb now draws large crowds of pilgrims, especially on feast days.

Because of his attention, and because of the revival of Coptic monastic life in the late twentieth century, the monastery has grown a great deal. Around the ancient core you will see newer buildings, gardens, guest houses and farmland. Palm trees and green fields stand beside the desert. This mix of very old and very new is part of the story. The monks are not only keeping history; they are still building a working community, with its own crops and its own daily rhythm.

As you walk, notice the people. Families arrive by bus from Cairo and the Nile Delta. Children run ahead toward the relics. Older women light candles and whisper prayers. A monk might stop to bless a visitor or answer a question. Dress modestly, speak quietly in the churches, and ask before you photograph anyone. Remember that a few minutes away stands the Monastery of the Syrians, another ancient community. The two are close enough to visit in one trip.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in an open courtyard. Feel the heat in the stone under your feet. Look up at the tower against the pale blue sky, and listen again for the wind. Monks have heard that same wind here for more than a thousand years. When you walk back through the gate toward the road, the desert will still be quiet... and somewhere behind you, the prayers will continue.
