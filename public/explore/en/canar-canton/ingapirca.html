The guide

The story

The wind reaches you first. It comes across the high grass in long, cold breaths and carries the smell of damp earth and eucalyptus. Underfoot, the ground is uneven and soft. Ahead of you, grey stone walls rise out of the green hillside. Some are rough and patchy. One is so smooth that the joints between its blocks look like pencil lines. You are standing at Ingapirca, high in the Andes of southern Ecuador, at the largest known Inca ruins in the country.

The name comes from Kichwa, the Andean language that is still spoken in the villages around you. It is usually translated as "wall of the Inca." That is a fair name, because the walls are what most visitors remember. But the Inca were not the first people to treat this hill as sacred. Long before they arrived, it belonged to the Cañari, the people who held this region of mountains and valleys. To understand Ingapirca, you need to keep both of these peoples in mind at once.

The Cañari were a proud and independent nation. Their territory covered much of what is now the provinces of Cañar and Azuay. Their traditions connected them closely with the moon, and they honoured mountains, lakes, and serpents in their stories of origin. When you walk across the site, remember this. Beneath the famous Inca stonework there is an older Cañari place. In many spots, the newer walls stand directly on top of the older ones.

In the second half of the fifteenth century, the Inca Empire pushed north from its capital in Cusco, in present-day Peru. The Cañari resisted, and the conquest was not quick or easy. The Inca rulers eventually took control of the region. They did not simply destroy what they found here. Instead, they built beside and over the Cañari sacred ground. Many historians see this as a deliberate choice. It joined the faith of the conquered people with the faith of the new rulers in a single place.

Now look for the largest structure on the site. It is often called the Temple of the Sun, and locals also call it the Castillo, which means "the castle" in Spanish. Its most unusual feature is its shape. The platform is not a simple square. It is a long oval, with rounded ends. Elliptical buildings like this are very rare in Inca architecture. Some researchers suggest the shape combines the round forms the Cañari favoured with the precise building style of the Inca.

Walk close to the walls of the platform and look carefully at the stonework. This is classic Inca masonry. Each block was shaped to sit tightly against its neighbours, without mortar between them. The faces of the stones are slightly cushioned, so they swell outward a little in the middle. Run your eye along a joint. It is tight and even. This style of building was reserved for the most important places in the empire, so its presence here tells you how much this hill mattered.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The doorways and niches in Inca walls are not rectangles. They are trapezoids — wider at the bottom and narrower at the top. You will see this shape again and again at Ingapirca. It was a practical design. It helped walls stand firm in a region that often shakes with earthquakes. The same shape appears across the old Inca world, from Ecuador to Chile, like a signature in stone.

Now turn your attention to the area known as Pilaloma. This is one of the clearest Cañari parts of the site. Archaeologists excavated burials here in the twentieth century. They found the remains of a number of people arranged around a central tomb, with offerings placed beside them. The burial is generally interpreted as Cañari and ceremonial. Standing here, you are close to the oldest human layer of the hill, a reminder that this ground was sacred before any Inca soldier arrived.

After the Spanish conquest in the sixteenth century, Ingapirca slowly lost its purpose. The religious life that filled it disappeared. Over time, people from the surrounding area carried away dressed stones for new houses, churches, and farm walls. If you visit the nearby villages, you may notice old cut blocks in later buildings. This is one reason the site looks incomplete today. What survives is only part of what once stood here. Even so, the main platform kept much of its shape.

Ingapirca was also one stop on a much larger network. The great Inca road system, called the Qhapaq Ñan, crossed the Andes for thousands of kilometres. It linked Cusco with the northern parts of the empire. Messengers, armies, and llama caravans moved along it. A route of this road passed through this region. That connection helps explain why the Inca invested so much here. Ingapirca was a religious centre, but it was probably also a place for rest, storage, and administration.

Before you leave the main complex, walk down toward the small ravine below the site. Along the path you may find a large rock that locals call the Face of the Inca. From the right angle, its natural outline looks like a human profile. Locals say it watches over the valley. There is no evidence that anyone carved it. Still, the rock has become part of the experience of the place, and it is a pleasant short walk through quiet, open country.

Also watch for a stone with small carved hollows and channels cut into its surface. Its exact use is not known. Some people suggest it held water for rituals. Others connect it with tracking the sky or the seasons. The honest answer is that its purpose is still debated. Near the entrance, a small museum displays pottery, tools, and other objects found in the area. It is worth visiting, because it helps you put faces to the Cañari people.

The site sits at more than three thousand metres above sea level. You may notice it in your breathing as you climb the small slopes. Walk slowly and drink water. The weather also changes quickly here. Bright sun can turn into cold mist in minutes. Often you will see llamas grazing between the ruins. They are a reminder of the animals that once carried goods along the Inca roads that crossed these highlands.

Before you go, stand still for a moment and face the oval platform again. Notice the light on the stone and the way the wind moves the grass around it. Two peoples, the moon worshippers and the sun worshippers, shaped this hill. Today, families from Cañar still live in the valleys you can see from here. As you walk back toward the gate, listen for the wind and the bells of grazing animals. These sounds have filled this hillside for centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk slowly because the site is above three thousand metres.
Bring a warm layer for sudden wind and mist.
Visit the small on-site museum to understand the Cañari.
Walk down the ravine path to see the Face of the Inca.
Stay on marked paths to protect fragile walls.
