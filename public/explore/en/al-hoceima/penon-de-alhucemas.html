The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the water and listen first. Small waves fold over grey pebbles and pull back with a dry, rattling sound. The air smells of salt and, faintly, of wild herbs from the hills behind you. Now look out across the bay. A short distance from the shore, a small rock rises straight out of the sea. It is covered almost entirely by pale walls, rooftops and a fortress. That crowded little island is the Peñón de Alhucemas.

At first it can look like a ship at anchor, or a village that drifted away from the coast. In a sense, it did drift away. It is not part of Morocco's daily life at all. The Peñón is held by Spain, and has been for centuries. You are looking at one of Europe's quietest frontiers, sitting in a Moroccan bay, close enough to see its windows on a clear day.

The Peñón is one of three small islands in this part of the bay. Together they are known as the Alhucemas Islands. The other two are lower and bare, with no buildings. Only the Peñón carries a settlement. The Spanish name, Alhucemas, comes from an Arabic word for lavender. The plant still grows on the dry slopes of the Rif mountains around you. The Moroccan city behind you, Al Hoceima, takes its name from the same root.

So how did a European flag end up on this rock? Spain took control of the Peñón in the late seventeenth century, usually dated to 1673. This was an age when Mediterranean powers fought over coasts, ports and sea lanes. Spain held a chain of small fortified points along North Africa. Spaniards came to call them the plazas de soberanía, which means places of sovereignty. Most were tiny. Their value was strategic, not economic.

Life on the rock was never easy. There is no river, no farmland, and very little space. Food and fresh water had to arrive by boat. For long periods the island was a garrison and a place of exile, with soldiers, officials and sometimes prisoners living side by side. Imagine a whole community squeezed onto a rock you could walk around in minutes. Every visitor, every supply and every letter came across the water you are looking at now.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look closely at the shape of the buildings. They do not spread out. They stack upward and press against the edges, because there is nowhere else to go. The walls drop almost straight into the sea. The rock and the architecture have become one shape. From the beach, it is hard to tell where the natural stone ends and the masonry begins.

The bay itself is older than any of these walls. In the early medieval period, a Muslim state called the Emirate of Nekor grew up near here, along the river that still reaches the sea in this bay. Its capital stood inland, among the farmland of the valley. Little of it is visible today. But it is a reminder that this coast was a centre of trade and power long before European fortresses arrived.

In the twentieth century, this bay became a battlefield. During the 1920s, the Rif War set Rifian fighters, led by Abd el-Krim, against Spanish forces. Abd el-Krim was a local leader who built a short-lived Rif republic in these mountains. In 1925, Spanish and French forces carried out a large landing from the sea, close to this bay. It was one of the first major amphibious operations of its kind. Historians still study it. The Peñón looked on from the water.

After that war, the Spanish built a new town on the cliffs behind you. Under the Spanish protectorate it had a different name. After Moroccan independence in 1956, the city became Al Hoceima. Yet the Peñón stayed Spanish. Morocco has long claimed it, along with Spain's other enclaves on this coast. Spain has kept it. The rock remains a small but real point of diplomatic tension between two neighbours.

Today, no civilians live on the Peñón. A small Spanish military detachment keeps watch, and supplies still come by sea or by helicopter. Ordinary visitors cannot land there. There is no ferry, and no tour boat will take you across. That is the second surprise for many travellers. A place so close and so visible is completely out of reach. You can only experience it from where you stand.

Because of that, the view from the shore is the whole experience. The beaches east of the city, such as Sfiha, give one of the closest and clearest views. Here the island sits low on the horizon with the open Mediterranean behind it. In the morning, the light falls across the walls from the east. In the late afternoon, the buildings turn warmer in colour, and the sea around them darkens to deep blue.

Take a moment to notice the people around you, too. On summer days, Moroccan families come down to these beaches with picnics and umbrellas. Children run into the shallow water. Fishermen work the bay in small painted boats. For them, the Spanish rock is simply part of the view they grew up with. Its strangeness belongs mostly to the visitor. That contrast between ordinary beach life and a foreign fortress is part of what makes this place memorable.

Before you leave, look once more across the water. Count the windows if you can. Find the highest point of the fortress, and the place where the rock meets the waves. Behind you, the Rif mountains rise in folds of brown and green. In front of you, a few hundred years of history sit on a single stone in the sea. As you walk back up the beach, listen again to the pebbles... and to how quiet that small island stays.
