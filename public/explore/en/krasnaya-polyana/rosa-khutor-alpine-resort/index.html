The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath the voices and the clatter of ski boots on paving stones, there is a steady roar. That is the Mzymta River, running fast and cold through the middle of the village. Its water is the colour of pale jade in some seasons and milky grey in others. Look up from the river and the slopes rise almost straight from the rooftops. The air here is cooler than on the coast below, and it smells of pine, wet stone, and woodsmoke.

You are standing in Rosa Khutor, a mountain resort in the Krasnaya Polyana area, high above the Black Sea city of Sochi. The coast is not far away as the crow flies. Yet the climate, the light, and the mood feel completely different. Palm trees grow on the seafront. Up here, the peaks of the Western Caucasus hold snow for much of the year. That short distance between subtropical beach and alpine ridge is one of the most unusual things about this whole region.

The name itself is worth a moment. In Russian, a khutor is a small rural settlement, something like a farmstead or hamlet. So the name points back to a modest place in the valley, long before any cable cars arrived. Today the word sits a little playfully on a resort of hotels, restaurants, and lifts. But it is a reminder that people farmed and lived along this river well before it became a destination for skiers.

The wider valley carries a heavier history too. Krasnaya Polyana means Red Meadow. For centuries this was the land of Circassian and related mountain peoples. In 1864, the long Caucasian War between the Russian Empire and these peoples ended in this area. Many Circassians were forced to leave their homeland afterwards, most of them for the Ottoman Empire. Their descendants still remember this loss. It is an important layer of the place, even though you will see few traces of it in the resort around you.

After that war, new settlers came to the valley in the late nineteenth century. Among them were Greek families and Estonian villagers. Locals say the Estonian settlement nearby still keeps a few traces of its origins in family names and old houses. For many decades, Krasnaya Polyana stayed a quiet mountain village. Hunters, beekeepers, and a few adventurous hikers made the journey up the winding road from the coast.

Then everything changed. When Sochi won the right to host the 2014 Winter Olympics, this valley became the stage for the mountain events. Rosa Khutor hosted the Olympic alpine skiing races. Its extreme park hosted snowboarding and freestyle skiing. A new road and a new railway were built through the gorge to connect the coast with the mountains. Almost all of what you see around you was built in a few intense years before the Games.

Here is something many visitors miss. The village was designed to look old, even though it is very new. Walk along the embankment and look at the facades. The colours, the steep roofs, and the painted shutters borrow ideas from Alpine towns in Switzerland, Austria, and France. The clock tower near the river works as a central meeting point. It is a deliberate stage set, and an enjoyable one, but it is not a historic town.

Now look up again, towards the ridge. This is the Aibga Ridge, and the ski area climbs to well above two thousand metres. From the top, on a clear day, you can see rows of Caucasus peaks in one direction. In the other direction, if the haze lifts, the Black Sea is sometimes visible as a pale band on the horizon. Standing on snow and seeing the sea at the same moment is a small surprise that many visitors remember for years.

The lifts are the real arteries of this place. Gondolas carry people up through several climate zones in a single ride. At the bottom, you pass broadleaf trees like beech and chestnut. Higher up, fir and pine take over. Higher still, the forest thins out into open alpine meadow and rock. Watch the view through the cabin window as you rise. The change in vegetation is a quick lesson in mountain ecology.

In winter, the heavy snowfalls here come from the moist air of the Black Sea. When that air hits the mountains, it cools and drops its moisture as deep snow. That is why the snow on these upper slopes can be thick and soft. Lower down, though, warm spells can arrive suddenly. Skiers often say the conditions here can change during a single day, so it pays to watch the sky and the forecasts.

Summer brings a different crowd. The lifts keep running, and the upper slopes turn green and full of wildflowers. Hikers follow marked trails along the ridges. Families ride up simply for the cool air and the views. Down in the village, people sit beside the river in the evening, and the sound of the water fills the gaps in conversation. Many visitors are surprised that this is a year-round destination, not only a winter one.

If you have a spare hour, the river path is the quiet heart of the resort. Walk upstream and the hotels fall behind you. The noise of the Mzymta grows louder, and the forest comes closer to the path. You may notice how clear and fast the water is, even in midsummer. Snowmelt from the peaks keeps it cold. This is a good place to slow down after the rush of lifts and crowds.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. On one side, there is a polished resort, built quickly for an international event. On the other side, there are the old mountains, the river, and the forests, which have watched far longer stories unfold. Notice the sound of the water again, and the cool air on your face. As you walk away, look back once at the ridge. The peaks you see now are the same ones that settlers, hunters, and Olympic athletes all looked up at, each in their own time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the gondola early on clear mornings for distant sea views.
Bring warm layers, even in summer, for the upper stations.
Walk the river path upstream to escape the busy promenade.
Check the mountain forecast, since conditions change quickly here.
Visit in summer for wildflower meadows and ridge-top hiking trails.
