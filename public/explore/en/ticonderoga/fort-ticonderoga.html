The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the water and runs across the grass. Somewhere below the walls, waves slap against rock. Under your feet, the ground is firm and slightly uneven, packed earth and old stone. Ahead of you, grey walls rise in sharp angles, like the points of a star. You are standing on a narrow neck of land above Lake Champlain. For more than a century, armies fought hard to own this one small piece of ground.

To understand why, look at the water. Lake Champlain runs north toward Canada. Just south of here, a short, fast river called La Chute drops down from Lake George. Together, these waters formed a route through the wilderness, from the Saint Lawrence River toward the Hudson River and New York. In the eighteenth century, there were few roads. Boats carried soldiers, cannon, and supplies. Whoever held this point could control that traffic. That is the whole story of the fort, in one sentence.

The name itself comes from an Indigenous language of the region. It is often translated as something like "the place between two waters." Scholars debate the exact meaning, but the description fits. Long before any fort stood here, people travelled through this passage by canoe. In 1609, the French explorer Samuel de Champlain came down the lake that now carries his name. Near this area, he and his Indigenous allies fought a group of Mohawk warriors. That clash began a long and bitter history.

The fort you see began in 1755. At that time, France and Britain were fighting for control of North America. The French built this post and called it Fort Carillon. Many people link the name to the sound of the nearby falls, which some compared to ringing bells. The design followed European military ideas. Pointed bastions, the angled corners of the walls, let defenders fire along every face of the fort. No attacker could hide close to the walls.

Now the first surprise. The most famous battle here was not really fought at these walls. In July 1758, a large British army marched against the fort. The French commander, the Marquis de Montcalm, had far fewer men. So his soldiers worked fast. They built a long earthwork on high ground to the west. In front of it, they cut down trees and laid them with the sharpened branches facing outward. This barrier is called an abatis.

The British general, James Abercromby, ordered attack after attack straight into that tangle. Soldiers caught in the branches were easy targets. By evening, the British had suffered terrible losses, and they withdrew. Among the regiments that suffered most was the Black Watch, a Scottish Highland unit. For France, it was one of its great victories of the war. Today, the site of those French lines is quiet, open ground. It is hard to imagine the noise and smoke of that long afternoon.

One year later, the story turned around. In 1759, a new British army under Jeffery Amherst came south. This time, the French garrison was too small to hold. As they left, the French blew up part of the fort. The British took control, and the name Ticonderoga replaced Carillon. Then the war moved north, and the fort slowly lost importance. For several years, it held only a small garrison, and its walls began to decay.

The second surprise comes in May 1775. The American Revolution had just begun. Before dawn, a small band of colonists crossed the lake in boats. They were led by Ethan Allen and his Green Mountain Boys, with Benedict Arnold beside him. The British garrison was tiny and not expecting trouble. The Americans walked in almost without a fight. Allen later wrote that he demanded surrender in the name of the Great Jehovah and the Continental Congress. Whether he said exactly those words is still debated.

The real prize was the cannon. That winter, a young bookseller turned officer named Henry Knox arrived. His task was to move dozens of heavy guns from this region to the American army near Boston. His men used sledges, oxen, and frozen rivers, and they crossed snowy mountains. It took weeks of brutal work. In March 1776, the guns appeared on the heights above Boston. Soon after, the British army left the city. Part of that victory began right here.

Now turn and look across the water to the south-west. You will see a steep, wooded hill. Today it is called Mount Defiance. In 1777, the British general John Burgoyne came down the lake with a large army. His engineers dragged cannon to the top of that hill. From there, they could fire down into the fort. The American commander, Arthur Saint Clair, saw that his position could not hold. He ordered a quiet retreat in the night. The fort that seemed so strong had a weakness, and it stood in plain view.

After the Revolution, soldiers left for good. Local people carried away stone for their own houses and walls. The fort slowly became a ruin. In the early nineteenth century, a merchant named William Ferris Pell bought the land. His family protected the site for generations. In the early twentieth century, his descendants Stephen and Sarah Pell began a serious restoration. The fort opened to visitors in 1909, three hundred years after Champlain's arrival on the lake.

So be aware of what you are seeing. Much of these walls is restoration, rebuilt on the old outline. Some original stone and foundations remain, mixed with later work. This is not a weakness. It is part of the story. The fort shows how one family and later a museum chose to remember the past. Inside, the museum holds a large collection of weapons, uniforms, and objects from the colonial wars. Beyond the walls, the King's Garden has flower beds and vegetable plots beside the lake.

On many days, staff in eighteenth-century clothing walk the grounds. You may hear the dull boom of a musket demonstration, or the steady beat of a drum. You may smell wood smoke from a fire. These sounds help the place feel less like a monument and more like a working post. Notice how small everything is. The rooms are tight, and the ceilings are low. For soldiers here in winter, life was cold, crowded, and often very boring.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the walls and look out over the lake. The water is calm now. Boats pass for pleasure, not for war. The hills of Vermont rise on the far shore, and Mount Defiance still watches from the south. Thousands of people fought and died for this view. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again to the wind and the water. They are the same sounds the soldiers heard.

Key facts

The French began building the fort in 1755.
Its original French name was Fort Carillon.
Montcalm defeated a much larger British army here in 1758.
Ethan Allen and Benedict Arnold captured it in May 1775.
Henry Knox hauled its cannon to Boston in winter 1775.

What to look for

Pointed bastions shaping the walls into a star outline.
Mount Defiance rising steeply across the water to the south-west.
Rows of heavy cannon lining the restored ramparts.
The point where La Chute River meets Lake Champlain.
Differences between older stonework and restored sections.
Before you go

Practical tips

Walk to the old French lines west of the fort.
Drive up Mount Defiance for a view over the fort.
Visit the King's Garden beside the lake in summer.
Check the daily program for musket and drum demonstrations.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and stone steps.
