The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves through the pine woods around Coca, and the air smells faintly of resin and warm earth. Then you look up, and the castle fills your view. It is not grey stone. It is brick, thousands upon thousands of bricks, glowing in shades of rose, salmon and rust. In the low sun of morning or evening, the walls seem almost to hold the heat of the day. This is the Castillo de Coca, and it looks like no other castle in Castile.

Coca is a small town in the province of Segovia, in the high central plains of Spain. The land here is flat and open. Wheat fields stretch toward the horizon, broken by dark patches of pine. Two rivers, the Eresma and the Voltoya, meet close to the town. For a builder in the fifteenth century, this landscape posed a simple problem. There was little good building stone nearby. But there was clay, and plenty of it. So the castle rose from fired brick.

The man behind it was Alonso de Fonseca, a powerful churchman who served as Archbishop of Seville. The Fonseca family was one of the great noble houses of Castile, and they wanted a seat that matched their ambition. Around the middle of the fifteenth century, with royal permission, work began here. Over the following decades, the family turned this spot into a statement of wealth and taste. It was meant to impress visitors as much as to defend anyone.

The style has a name worth knowing — Mudéjar. The word describes the work of Muslim craftsmen, and craftsmen trained in their traditions, who stayed and worked in Christian Spain after the Christian kingdoms expanded south. They brought centuries of skill with brick, plaster and geometric pattern. At Coca, that tradition meets the Gothic architecture of northern Europe. Historians often call the result Gothic-Mudéjar. You can see both worlds in a single wall.

Look closely at the towers. Many of them are not simple squares. They are many-sided, with corners turned at angles, and their tops break into rows of small turrets. Along the walls, bricks are set in patterns — zigzags, diamonds, rows of tiny arches. From a distance, the surface looks almost woven. Up close, you can see how each band of decoration was built brick by brick. This is ornament made from the same material as the structure itself.

Here is the first surprise. For all its towers, gun openings and battlements, this castle saw very little real fighting. By the time it was finished, the age of the great medieval siege was ending. Cannon had changed warfare, and a brick palace on open ground was not the strongest defence. The military features are real, but they also served as display. The Fonsecas were saying something to their neighbours: we are rich, we are powerful, and we have excellent taste.

Now walk to the edge of the moat. It is deep, wide and lined with steeply sloping walls. Many visitors expect to see water. But this moat was designed to stay dry. An attacker who climbed down would be trapped at the bottom, exposed to fire from the walls above. Today the dry ditch gives you a different gift. It lets you see the full height of the castle, from its foundations to its crown, in one long sweep of brick.

The castle has two main lines of walls, one inside the other. You cross into the outer enclosure first, then pass inside toward the inner core. Rising above everything is the main keep, the tallest and strongest tower. In a medieval castle, the keep was the last place of refuge, and often the home of the lord himself. Its windows and openings are small near the bottom and become a little more generous higher up, where danger was further away.

Inside, if you join a guided visit, keep your eyes on the walls and ceilings. In some rooms, traces of painted plaster survive. The designs are geometric — interlocking lines, stars and repeating shapes in faded reds, whites and ochres. These patterns come straight from the Mudéjar tradition. They give a small glimpse of how colourful the interior must once have been, when tapestries, carpets and painted surfaces filled these now quiet rooms.

Over the centuries, the castle passed through marriage and inheritance into the hands of the House of Alba, one of the most famous noble families in Spain. Like many great houses, it suffered periods of neglect. Some of its fittings and decorations were removed or lost. In the twentieth century, the castle passed to the Spanish state. Restoration work followed, and the building was given a new and rather unexpected life.

That is the second surprise. This castle is a working school. Since the middle of the twentieth century, it has housed a training school for forestry. Students learn about the care and management of woodland — fitting, given the pine forests that surround the town. So while you study the towers, there may be people inside studying trees. The castle is not a frozen monument. It still has a daily purpose.

The town itself has a much longer story. In Roman times, Coca was known as Cauca. According to ancient tradition, it was the birthplace of Theodosius the Great, one of the last emperors to rule over a united Roman Empire. Walk into town and you will find other layers too — old walls, a church, and quiet streets where the pace of life is slow. The castle sits slightly apart, on the edge of town, close to where the land drops toward the river.

Before you leave, step back and take one last look. Notice how the colour of the brick changes as clouds pass over the sun. Notice the shadows inside the zigzag patterns, and the small turrets lined up against the sky. Listen again for the wind in the pines. Five centuries ago, a family built this place to be remembered. Standing here now, on this quiet edge of the Castilian plain, you are doing exactly what they hoped.
