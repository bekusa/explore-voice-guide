The guide

The story

Run your hand along the pale stone of the pier, and it feels cool even on a summer afternoon. The surface is smooth in some places and pitted in others, worn by two thousand years of rain, wind, and passing shoulders. Above you, a wide stone opening frames a strip of sky. Scooters hum past on the road. Somewhere nearby, a café clinks its cups. You are standing at the Arch of Augustus, one of the oldest surviving Roman arches anywhere.

This arch was raised in 27 BCE. That date matters. It is the same year the Roman Senate gave Octavian a new title — Augustus. He had ended decades of civil war, and he was now the first emperor of Rome in all but name. Rimini, which the Romans called Ariminum, honoured him with this monument. It was one of the first public works of his new age.

Why here, in a seaside town on the Adriatic coast? The answer runs right under your feet. This arch marks the end of the Via Flaminia, the great Roman road that ran north and east from Rome, across the Apennine mountains, to the sea. Augustus had the road restored, and the arch thanked him for it. For a traveller coming from Rome, this gate was the welcome into the city.

Ariminum itself was much older than the arch. The Romans founded a colony here in the third century before Christ. It was a frontier town at first, a gateway to the north of Italy. Roads met here. The Via Flaminia ended at this arch, and other roads continued onward. So this spot was a crossroads, a place where armies, merchants, and messengers all passed through.

Now look closely at the opening itself. It is very wide — wider than you might expect for a city gate. Many historians point out that no ordinary wooden doors could easily close a span like this. One popular reading is that the arch was a message, not a defence. Rome was at peace, so the gate could stand open. Whether or not the builders meant it that way, it is a lovely idea to hold in mind as you walk through.

The arch is built of stone, with a single central opening. On each side, slender columns with leafy Corinthian capitals — the most decorative of the classical styles, carved with curling acanthus leaves — frame the passage. Above them sits a triangular pediment, like the roof line of a temple. The design is calm and balanced. It feels less like a fortress and more like a doorway into a sacred space.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. High on the arch, there are four round medallions, each holding the carved head of a god. You can find Jupiter, the king of the gods, and Apollo, the god of light and music. You can also find Neptune, the god of the sea — fitting for a town on the coast. The fourth is Roma, the goddess who stood for the city of Rome itself. Take a moment to find each face.

Now look at the very top. Those square notches along the upper edge — the crenellations, like the teeth of a castle wall — are not Roman at all. They were added in the Middle Ages, when the arch became part of the town's defences. So the monument you see today is a layered thing. A Roman gate of peace, wearing a medieval crown. It is a small surprise, and once you notice it, you cannot stop seeing it.

What stood above the arch in Roman times is lost. Many scholars believe a statue of Augustus once crowned it, perhaps in bronze. Nothing of it survives today. Bronze was valuable, and over the centuries metal like this was often melted down and reused. So imagine the empty space above the stone. Picture the emperor looking down the road toward Rome, watching travellers arrive.

For much of its life, the arch was surrounded by buildings and stretches of city wall. In the twentieth century, during the 1930s, the area around it was cleared. The aim was to make the Roman monument stand alone, so that it could be seen from every side. That is why it stands so openly today, ringed by traffic and green space. The view you have now is, in a sense, quite modern.

Turn around and look through the arch into the town. The street ahead is the Corso d'Augusto, and it still follows the line of the old Roman main road. If you walk its full length, you pass through the heart of the historic centre. At the far end you reach another Roman survivor — the Bridge of Tiberius, completed under the emperor who followed Augustus. The arch and the bridge are like two bookends of the Roman town.

This is one of the quiet pleasures of Rimini. Many people know the town for its long beaches and its summer crowds. Fewer realise how much of the Roman city still shapes the streets. The film director Federico Fellini grew up here, and his memories of Rimini fed many of his films. But long before cinema, long before beach umbrellas, this arch was already old.

Before you move on, stand back a few steps and look once more. Notice how the pale stone catches the light, warmer in the late afternoon, flatter at midday. Listen to the traffic flowing around a monument that has watched carts, horses, soldiers, and pilgrims pass by. Then, when you are ready, walk through the opening — just as travellers from Rome did two thousand years ago — and let the old road carry you into the town.
