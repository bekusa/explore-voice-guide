The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps quietly against old brick, and somewhere a duck pushes through the reeds. The castle lake holds a second, upside-down castle on its surface: red walls, pale stone trim, green copper spires. On a still morning the reflection is so sharp that it is hard to tell where the building ends and the water begins. Take a moment here before you walk in. You are looking at one of the largest Renaissance buildings in Scandinavia.

This is Frederiksborg Castle, in the town of Hillerød, north of Copenhagen. The castle does not stand on one piece of land. It stands on three small islands in the lake, linked by bridges. As you cross from one island to the next, the buildings grow grander, and the courtyards become more formal. That layout was deliberate. Every visitor approached the king step by step, and each bridge brought them closer to royal power.

The name comes from King Frederick the Second, who acquired an older manor on this site in the sixteenth century. But the castle you see today belongs to his son, Christian the Fourth. He was born here, and he rebuilt the place almost completely in the early seventeenth century. Christian the Fourth was a restless builder. In Copenhagen he left behind Rosenborg Castle and the Round Tower. Frederiksborg was his most ambitious project, and he spent a fortune on it.

Look closely at the style. It is often called Dutch Renaissance. That means red brick walls combined with carved sandstone details, tall decorative gables, and slender towers. Christian the Fourth hired craftsmen and architects from the Netherlands, which was then one of the richest and most artistic places in Europe. The red and the pale gold work together like a uniform. From a distance the walls look warm. Up close, you can see figures, masks, and scrolls cut into the stone.

In the outer courtyard you will find a large bronze fountain with Neptune, the Roman god of the sea, at the top. Here is something many visitors miss. This fountain is a copy. The original was made by the Dutch sculptor Adriaen de Vries. In 1659, during a war with Sweden, Swedish soldiers took the bronze figures as war booty. They are still in Sweden today. The copy you see was made in the late nineteenth century, so the fountain could return to its place.

Now walk toward the main wing and the chapel. For almost two centuries, this chapel was where Danish kings were anointed. From 1671 until 1840, the absolute monarchs of Denmark came here for that ceremony. Absolute monarchy meant the king held all power, with no parliament to share it. The anointing was a religious act. It told the people that the king's authority came from God, not from the nobles or the voters.

Inside the chapel, the light falls on gold, carved wood, and painted ceilings. Above the altar and along the walls, you will see rows of painted coats of arms. These belong to members of two Danish royal orders: the Order of the Elephant and the Order of the Dannebrog. Each knight receives a shield here. Some are centuries old. Others are modern, because new members are still added today. So this wall is not finished. It keeps growing.

And there is a sound you may be lucky enough to hear. The chapel holds a wooden organ built by the German organ maker Esaias Compenius in the early seventeenth century. It is one of the oldest organs in Europe that still plays with much of its original material. It has no electric motor. Air is pushed by hand-operated bellows, just as it was four hundred years ago. If you can catch a demonstration, the tone is soft and reedy, almost like breath.

Here is the second surprise. Much of what you see inside the castle is not original. In 1859, a fire swept through the main buildings and destroyed large parts of the interiors. The chapel survived better than most rooms, but many halls were lost. For a time, it was not clear whether the castle would be rebuilt at all. The money for that came from an unexpected place: a brewery.

J. C. Jacobsen, the founder of the Carlsberg brewery, offered to fund the restoration. In return, the castle would become a museum of Danish history. That is what it remains today: the Museum of National History. Room after room is filled with portraits, furniture, and objects that tell the story of Denmark across five centuries. The Carlsberg Foundation still supports the museum. So, in a sense, Danish beer helped save a royal palace.

Inside, the Great Hall is a highlight. It is long and tall, with a richly carved ceiling and large tapestries. Remember the fire as you stand there. The hall you see is a careful reconstruction from the late nineteenth century, based on old descriptions and drawings. It is a reconstruction, not a fake. It shows how people at the time imagined the glory of Christian the Fourth. The museum also includes a modern portrait collection, so the story continues into the present day.

When you leave the main buildings, walk out to the Baroque Garden behind the castle. Baroque gardens use strict geometry: straight paths, clipped hedges, and terraces that step down toward the water. This garden was first laid out in the early eighteenth century and later restored using old plans. From the top terrace, turn around and look back. The castle rises above the lake, framed by green shapes. It is one of the best views of the whole complex.

The town of Hillerød grew up around this castle. Craftsmen, servants, and traders settled close to the royal court. Today the town centre is only a short walk away, and the castle still shapes its skyline. You can walk the path around the lake for a quieter perspective. There are benches by the water, and on warm days, people sit with coffee and simply watch the reflection.

Before you go, find the water again. Look at the copper spires, the green that comes from centuries of rain on metal. Notice how the brick changes colour as clouds move: brighter in sun, almost purple in shadow. A king built this place to impress. Fire nearly ended it. A brewer brought it back. As you walk away across the bridges, look back once more... and see the castle settle again into its reflection on the lake.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for still water and clear reflections.
Ask whether an organ demonstration is scheduled during your visit.
Walk the lakeside path for quieter views of the castle.
Climb the Baroque Garden terraces for the classic castle view.
Allow several hours to see the museum and gardens.
