The guide

The story

Listen first. There is no traffic here, no hum of power lines, no distant engine. There is wind moving through black spruce and jack pine. There is water lapping against pink and grey granite. Somewhere across the lake, a loon calls, and the sound carries for a long time before it fades. Under your boots, the ground is a thin skin of moss and lichen over some of the oldest rock on Earth. You are in the boreal forest, in a place its people call Pimachiowin Aki.

The name comes from Anishinaabemowin, the language of the Anishinaabe people. It is usually translated as "the land that gives life." That translation is worth holding onto as we walk. This is not a park in the usual sense, with a gate and a visitor centre at its heart. It is a homeland. It is a living landscape of lakes, rivers, wetlands, and forest that families have travelled, fished, hunted, and harvested for thousands of years. They still do.

Pimachiowin Aki stretches across the border between Ontario and Manitoba. It covers roughly 29,000 square kilometres of forest and water. That is an area larger than many countries. On the Ontario side, it includes Woodland Caribou Provincial Park, a vast canoe country of linked lakes and portages. On the Manitoba side, it includes Atikaki Provincial Park and the ancestral lands of four Anishinaabe First Nations. Together, these pieces form one continuous territory, and the provincial border means very little to the land itself.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not realise. In 2018, UNESCO inscribed Pimachiowin Aki as a World Heritage Site. It was recognised for both its cultural value and its natural value — a so-called mixed site. That double recognition is rare anywhere in the world. It was the first mixed World Heritage Site in Canada. In other words, the forest and the people were not listed separately. They were listed as one thing, because here they cannot be separated.

The road to that recognition was not quick. An earlier nomination did not succeed at first. One reason was that international heritage systems were not yet used to a place where culture is not mainly in buildings or ruins. Here, culture lives in practice. It lives in the knowledge of where the fish spawn, which slope holds the blueberries, and how to read ice in early spring. The communities worked for years to explain this way of seeing the land.

At the centre of that explanation is a principle often written as Ji-ganawendamang Gidakiiminaan. It means, roughly, "keeping the land." It is a set of responsibilities, passed down through families and elders. It guides how people use resources, how they share them, and how they show respect to the other beings who live here. Moose, caribou, beaver, sturgeon, wild rice — in this way of thinking, they are relatives, not simply resources. Taking from the land comes with a duty to care for it.

Now look around at the landscape itself. This is the Canadian Shield, a huge plate of ancient bedrock that covers much of central Canada. Glaciers scraped across it during the last ice age. They left behind rounded rock outcrops, scattered boulders, and countless basins that filled with water. That is why the map here looks like broken glass — lake after lake, joined by rivers, rapids, and short overland trails. For people travelling by canoe, this water was the road network.

The forest grows in a patchwork. Fire is a natural part of the boreal cycle, and you may see it in the trees around you. Some stands are tall and mature. Others are young and dense, with charred trunks still standing among them. Jack pine depends on fire. Its cones can stay sealed for years and open in great heat, releasing seed onto fresh ground. So a burned hillside here is not a scar. It is the start of the next forest.

This country is also home to woodland caribou. They are shy, and they need large areas of undisturbed forest to survive. Many visitors travel for days and never see one. That rarity is part of the point. Across much of southern Canada, caribou have disappeared as roads and development cut up their range. Here, the forest is still large enough and quiet enough to hold them. Wolves, moose, black bears, lynx, and bald eagles share the same lands and waters.

The human story is written on the land too, though quietly. Along some lake shores, on smooth rock faces near the water, you may find red ochre paintings. These pictographs show figures, animals, and shapes made long ago. Their exact meanings are not something an outside guide should claim to know. Many are sacred, and communities ask visitors to look with respect and never touch them. Skin oils and rubbing damage the pigment that has lasted so long.

There are other traces, easier to miss. A cleared campsite on a point of land. An old portage trail worn into the moss. A trapline cabin. Places where families have returned to the same shore, season after season, for generations. Elders hold the names of these places, and each name often carries a story or a teaching. The land, in this sense, works like a library. Lose the people who read it, and the knowledge goes too.

Getting here takes effort, and that is the second surprise for many travellers. There are very few roads. Most visitors arrive by floatplane or by canoe. On the Ontario side, the town of Red Lake often serves as a gateway for trips into Woodland Caribou Provincial Park. Travel inside the territory means paddling, portaging, and camping, often for many days. You need real wilderness skills, or a qualified outfitter. Weather changes fast, and help can be far away.

If you do travel here, the communities ask for a simple kind of conduct. Pack out everything you bring in. Keep fires small and use existing sites. Leave rock paintings, cultural sites, and old camps untouched. If you are travelling on First Nation lands, seek permission and respect local guidance. Many visitors find that the best experiences come from listening — to a local guide, to an elder, or simply to the forest at dawn.

So take a moment where you are now. Look at the water and the colour of the rock beneath it. Notice the lichen, grey-green and slow-growing, on the stone by your feet. Listen again for the wind and the loon. People have stood on shores like this one for thousands of years, reading the same signs in the sky and on the water. When you leave, the land will go on giving life, as its name promises. Your task, like theirs, is simply to leave it as you found it.

What to look for

Jack pine cones still sealed tight on older branches.

Charred trunks standing among young regrowth on burned slopes.
Worn portage trails leading between neighbouring lakes.
Before you go

Practical tips

Plan fly-in or canoe access; almost no roads enter the area.
Hire a qualified outfitter if you lack wilderness canoe experience.
Ask permission before travelling on First Nation community lands.
Pack out all waste and use existing campsites only.
Bring insect protection for the boreal summer months.
