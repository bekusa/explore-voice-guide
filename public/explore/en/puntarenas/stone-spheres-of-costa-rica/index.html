The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The air here is heavy and warm, and somewhere nearby insects hum in the grass. In front of you sits a ball of grey stone, smooth enough to rest a hand on. Its surface feels cool, even in the heat. It looks almost too round to be real. Yet human hands shaped it, many centuries ago, on this flat green delta in southern Costa Rica.

These are the stone spheres of the Diquís region. Diquís is an old Indigenous name for the area around the Térraba River, close to the Osa Peninsula. The river carries water down from the mountains to the Pacific Ocean. Over thousands of years it laid down the rich soil under your feet. That soil drew farming communities, and those communities made the spheres you see today.

Archaeologists link most of the spheres to people who lived here between roughly the first centuries of the common era and the arrival of the Spanish in the sixteenth century. These were chiefdoms. A chiefdom is a society led by a ranking family or leader, larger than a village but smaller than a state. They grew maize, worked gold, and traded along the coast. They left no written records. So the spheres must speak for them in another way.

Here is the first surprise. The modern world only noticed these stones in the twentieth century, and it was almost by accident. In the 1930s, the United Fruit Company began clearing this jungle for banana plantations. Workers with machetes and bulldozers kept striking large, round stones buried in the forest floor. Some spheres sat in lines. Others formed triangles or curves. Nobody in the company expected to find a lost landscape under the bananas.

The first serious study came from the archaeologist Doris Stone, who wrote about the spheres in the 1940s. The American researcher Samuel Lothrop followed with a larger survey. They mapped where the stones lay and how they related to one another. That work mattered, because the spheres were already disappearing. Many were moved, sold, or broken. Some now sit in front of government buildings, schools, and private gardens across the country.

The second surprise is darker. A rumour spread that the spheres held gold inside. Treasure hunters drilled into them, lit fires beside them, and even used dynamite to split them open. They found nothing but solid rock. Today you can still find spheres with cracks or missing pieces from that period. Each broken stone is a small record of greed and misunderstanding, and a reminder of how easily a heritage can be lost.

So how were the spheres made? Most are carved from gabbro, a hard, dark volcanic rock that forms deep underground. A few are made of limestone or sandstone. Researchers believe the makers shaped a rough boulder by pecking it with harder hammer stones, then grinding and polishing the surface. Some suggest that heating and cooling helped crack away the outer layers. It was slow work. A single large sphere may have taken a very long time to finish.

The sizes vary a great deal. Some spheres are small enough to hold in two hands. The largest are taller than a person and weigh many tonnes. Moving them across soft river soil without wheels or draft animals was a serious task. And yet the people of Diquís carried many of them far from the quarries in the hills. That effort tells us the stones mattered deeply to them.

What did they mean? Honestly, nobody knows for certain. Many archaeologists think the spheres marked status and power, standing in front of the homes of important families. Others suggest they marked boundaries or meeting places. You may also hear wilder ideas, linking them to lost continents or star maps. These ideas have no support in the evidence. The real mystery is quieter, and perhaps more human.

In 2014, UNESCO added four sites from this region to its World Heritage list. The listing is called the Precolumbian Chiefdom Settlements with Stone Spheres of the Diquís. Precolumbian simply means before the voyages of Columbus. The four sites are Finca 6, Batambal, El Silencio, and Grijalba. At these places, some spheres still rest where their makers placed them, alongside mounds, paved areas, and the outlines of old houses.

This detail matters more than it first seems. A sphere in a museum is beautiful, but it is cut off from its story. A sphere in its original position, next to a house mound, shows how people lived around it. You can see the relationship between the stone, the home, and the open land. That context is what archaeologists value most, and it is rare.

As you walk, notice the landscape itself. The flat delta stretches out under a wide sky. Palms and grasses fill the edges. In the rainy season, the ground turns soft and the green deepens. On a dry morning, the light falls low across the stones and every bump of the old pecking shows on the surface. Look closely and you can see the stone is not perfectly round. It is very close, but human.

Take one last look at the sphere in front of you. Someone chose that boulder in the hills. Someone struck it, day after day, until it turned round. Someone moved it here and set it down with care. The people who did this are gone, and their names are lost. But the stone is still here, sitting in the warm grass, while the insects keep humming around you.

Practical tips

Visit Finca 6 to see spheres in their original positions.
Come early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Bring insect repellent for the grassy, humid delta grounds.
Carry water and a hat for the open, shadeless sites.
Pair your visit with a stop in nearby Palmar Sur.
