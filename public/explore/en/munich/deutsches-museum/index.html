The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beneath the traffic on the bridges, you can hear the Isar. The river splits here, cold and green, and runs fast on both sides of a long, narrow island. On that island stands a building of pale stone with a tall tower. People come here to see how almost everything in the modern world was made.

This is the Deutsches Museum, often called the German Museum. It ranks among the largest museums of science and technology anywhere on Earth. Its subject is very broad: tools, machines, ships, aircraft, instruments, energy, chemistry, and the long human effort to understand how things work. You will not see all of it in one visit. Very few people do. The museum is planned that way, so do not worry about seeing everything.

The idea came from one man, Oskar von Miller. He was a Bavarian engineer and a pioneer of electrical power. In 1891, he helped to organise a famous experiment. Electricity travelled more than one hundred and seventy kilometres, from the town of Lauffen to an exhibition in Frankfurt. It showed that power could travel long distances to the people who needed it. Von Miller understood something simple: people learn more from real machines than from words on a page.

In 1903, he founded the museum. His goal was ambitious. He did not want a quiet hall of objects behind glass. He wanted original machines, working models, and experiments that visitors could start themselves. Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Pressing a button to make a machine move feels normal in museums today. The Deutsches Museum was one of the places that made this kind of hands-on display popular.

The building around you took years to finish. The First World War and the hard years after it slowed the work. The main building finally opened in 1925, on Oskar von Miller's seventieth birthday. Think about that for a moment. A man saw his idea grow from a proposal into this island of stone. He lived to walk through it. Less than twenty years later, bombs in the Second World War damaged much of the building. Staff and supporters then spent years rebuilding it.

Now look at the island itself. This land was once a raft landing and a place for timber and gravel at the edge of the city. Today, the Isar runs on both sides. Bridges connect the island to the old town on one bank and to the Au and Haidhausen neighbourhoods on the other. On warm days, people sit on the gravel banks below. In spring, the water often runs high and grey with snowmelt from the Alps.

Inside, the scale changes quickly. In one hall, full-size ships fill the space. There are wooden boats, a large sailing vessel, and engines as tall as a house. Nearby is one of the most surprising objects in the collection: an early German submarine. It is cut open along its side. You can look into a cramped steel tube where a crew once lived, slept, and worked under the sea. Most visitors stay quiet when they see how little space the crew had.

Then go down below. Under your feet, the museum has a recreated mine. It has narrow tunnels, timber supports, dim light, and old mining machines. The air feels cooler, and the walls press close. It is easy to forget that you are still inside a museum in the middle of Munich. Many visitors say this is the part they remember longest, years later. Children often call it the best part of the whole visit.

Here is the second surprise. The modern planetarium has a strong link to this building. In the 1920s, the museum asked the optical company Zeiss for a new way to show the night sky indoors. The result was the projection planetarium. Lenses project stars onto a dome above the audience. The first demonstrations took place in connection with this museum. Every planetarium you have visited, in any country, uses the idea that was developed here.

The collections also include aviation, with real aircraft hanging above the halls. They cover musical instruments, and how sound is made and shaped. There are sections on chemistry, physics, and the instruments scientists used to measure the world. In some areas, you can watch live demonstrations. One well-known demonstration shows the power of high voltage, with loud, bright discharges. It reminds visitors that electricity was dangerous and exciting long before it became ordinary.

The museum has also spent many years on a major renewal. Galleries closed in stages, were redesigned, and then reopened. Because of this, the museum you see today may look different from the one a friend saw ten years ago. Some exhibitions may still be closed or have moved. It is a good idea to check the current map at the entrance. Then choose three or four areas that you really want to see.

The museum is larger than this island. The Deutsches Museum also runs separate sites. One, at Schleissheim north of the city, focuses on aircraft. Another, near the Theresienwiese, shows transport, including trains and cars. It also has a branch in Bonn and a newer one in Nuremberg. The island is the main site, but it is not the only place where the collection is shown.

So, before you leave, step back from the entrance and look up at the tower. Listen again for the river. All around you are the results of centuries of human curiosity: engines, lenses, hulls, wires. As you cross the bridge back towards the old town, notice the streetlights and the trams. Even the bridge under your feet came from the same kind of practical thinking that this museum was built to show.
