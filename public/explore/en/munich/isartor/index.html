The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Trams grind around the curve, bicycles tick past, and traffic hums on every side. In the middle of all this motion rises a pale stone gate with a steep roof and a painted wall. Cars flow around it the way water flows around a rock. This is the Isartor, the Isar Gate, and for close to seven centuries it has marked the eastern edge of old Munich.

The name gives you the first clue to its purpose. The Isar is the river that runs through Munich, a short walk east of here. In the Middle Ages, travellers coming from that direction crossed the river and entered the city through this gate. Merchants, farmers, soldiers and pilgrims all passed under these arches. Today you can cross the same line in a few steps, but for them it meant entering a walled and guarded town.

Munich grew up around trade, and salt was the most valuable trade of all. In the twelfth century, the road carrying salt from the Alps to the west crossed the Isar here. Duke Henry the Lion moved that river crossing to Munich, and a small market settlement grew quickly. As the town expanded, its first wall became too tight. A larger ring of walls was built in the fourteenth century, and the Isartor was one of its main gates.

The gate is usually dated to 1337, during the reign of Louis the Bavarian. Louis was a Duke of Bavaria who also became Holy Roman Emperor, the title held by the most powerful ruler in medieval Central Europe. Under him, Munich became far more important. Walls like these did more than keep out enemies. They also controlled who entered, what goods came in, and which taxes were paid at the gate.

Look at the shape of the structure in front of you. It is not one tower but a small group of buildings. A tall central tower rises behind two shorter, sturdier towers that face outward. Between them is a gateway with a passage beneath. Once, this formed a layered defence, so attackers who passed one barrier would face another. Of Munich's three surviving medieval gates, this one keeps its tall main tower, which makes it a rare complete picture of the old defences.

The other two survivors are the Karlstor to the west and the Sendlinger Tor to the southwest. Most of the rest of the city wall disappeared in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Like many European cities, Munich tore down its walls to make room for wider streets, new districts and fresh air. The gates were lucky. Some people saw them as obstacles to traffic, but others valued them as monuments, and that argument saved them.

In the 1830s, King Ludwig the First of Bavaria wanted Munich to look like a great cultural capital. His architect Friedrich von Gärtner restored the Isartor in a romantic medieval style. This was the century when Europeans fell in love with the Middle Ages. So what you see today is partly real fourteenth-century stone and partly a nineteenth-century vision of how a medieval gate should look. That mix is part of its story.

Now turn to the painting on the side facing away from the old town. It is a long fresco, a painting made on fresh plaster, showing a grand procession. It shows Louis the Bavarian returning in triumph after the Battle of Mühldorf in 1322, a victory that secured his position. The artist Bernhard von Neher created it in the 1830s. Look for riders, banners, and crowds greeting the victorious ruler as he moves toward the city.

Here is something many visitors miss. The painting celebrates the same ruler who expanded the city and built this wall. So a nineteenth-century king used a medieval gate to honour a medieval emperor, and in doing so he honoured his own family line. Gates like this were never only practical. They were also messages, telling every person who arrived exactly who ruled the city and how proud that ruler wanted them to feel.

The twentieth century was harsh to Munich. Bombing raids during the Second World War caused heavy damage across the city centre, and the Isartor did not escape. After the war, the gate was repaired along with much of the old town. Notice how clean and sharp some surfaces look compared with others. Those differences carry traces of reconstruction. Like much of central Munich, this monument is both old and carefully rebuilt.

And now the second surprise. Inside this solemn defensive tower is one of Munich's most playful museums. It is dedicated to Karl Valentin, a Munich comedian of the early twentieth century, and to his long-time stage partner Liesl Karlstadt. Valentin was famous for absurd, word-twisting humour, and some people call him a kind of Bavarian Charlie Chaplin. The museum even spells its own name with an odd letter, a little joke in the spirit of the man himself.

Inside, you will find strange objects, old photographs, and gags that reward slow attention. One well-known exhibit is a so-called winter toothpick, a tiny toothpick wrapped in fur. It makes no sense, and that is exactly the point. Higher up in the tower there is a small café, so you can sit inside medieval walls with a coffee. It is hard to imagine a gentler fate for a building made for war.

From here, the street called Tal leads west toward Marienplatz, the main square of Munich. Its name means valley, and it was one of the old routes into the heart of the city. To the east, the road continues toward the river. If you walk that way, you follow the direction of the old salt road and the travellers who came before you. The gate sits exactly between the city and the water.

Before you move on, look up once more at the tower and the painted procession. Listen to the trams, the voices, and the bicycle bells around you. Seven centuries ago, this was a checkpoint, a tax post and a symbol of power. Today, people walk beneath it without slowing down, often without looking up. Now you know what stands above them... and perhaps you will look up a little longer as you walk away.
