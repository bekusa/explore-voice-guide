The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel first. It crunches under every step here, a soft, steady sound that follows you along the straight paths. Above you, rows of trees throw a patterned shade across the ground. Somewhere ahead, you may hear a violin or an accordion, and the music seems to carry further than it should. Welcome to the Hofgarten, the court garden of Munich. For centuries it belonged to the rulers of Bavaria. Today it belongs to anyone who walks through its gates.

The garden sits right at the edge of the old city centre. To the south stands the long facade of the Residenz, the former palace of the Wittelsbach family, who ruled Bavaria for more than seven hundred years. To the west, just beyond the arcades, rises the ochre-yellow tower of the Theatine Church. The busy square of Odeonsplatz is only a few steps away. And yet, once you are inside these walls, the traffic noise drops away surprisingly fast.

The garden was laid out in the early seventeenth century, under Duke Maximilian the First. He was one of the most powerful princes in the German lands at the time, and later became an Elector of the Holy Roman Empire. He wanted a garden in the Italian Renaissance style. That meant order and symmetry. Paths run in straight lines. Flowerbeds form clean geometric shapes. Everything points toward the centre, as if the whole garden were drawn with a ruler and a compass.

And at that centre stands the pavilion that most visitors photograph first. Locals call it the Dianatempel, the Temple of Diana. It is a round, open structure with twelve sides, arched openings, and a dome on top. It was built around the same time as the garden itself. Walk inside it if you can. The stone floor feels cool even on a warm day, and the dome above you gathers every sound.

Here is the first surprise. The bronze figure on top of the dome is usually called Diana, the Roman goddess of the hunt. But many historians identify her as Tellus Bavarica, a symbolic figure who represents the land of Bavaria itself. She holds objects that stand for the wealth of the country. The figure you see today is a copy. The original bronze is kept indoors, inside the Residenz, where it is safe from weather and pollution.

Now, back to that music. The temple has remarkable acoustics. A single guitar or cello sounds full and rich under the dome. Because of this, street musicians love to play here, and on many afternoons you will find someone performing for whoever stops to listen. Some evenings, people even dance in the open space nearby. It is one of the small rituals that makes this garden feel lived in rather than preserved.

Turn toward the long covered walkways on the western and northern sides. These are the Hofgarten arcades. In the nineteenth century, under King Ludwig the First, artists painted frescoes along these walls. The scenes showed episodes from the history of the Wittelsbach dynasty, along with Italian landscapes. Ludwig loved Italy, and he wanted Munich to feel a little like Rome or Florence. Many of the paintings have faded or suffered damage over time, but you can still trace some of them on the walls.

At the western side, look for the main gate, the Hofgartentor. It was designed by Leo von Klenze, the architect who shaped much of royal Munich in the early nineteenth century. Klenze gave the city its classical look, with columns, clean lines, and calm proportions. The gate is a modest piece compared with his grand buildings, but it frames the view into the garden perfectly. Stand just inside it and look back toward the temple.

Now turn east. The large building at the far end of the garden is the Bavarian State Chancellery, the office of the Bavarian Minister-President. Here is the second surprise. Its central dome is much older than the wings around it. That dome once belonged to the Bavarian Army Museum, which was badly damaged in the Second World War. For years the ruin stood empty. In the 1990s, the old domed core was restored, and modern glass wings were built on either side.

The Second World War left other marks here too. In front of the Chancellery, look for the war memorial. A sunken crypt holds the stone figure of a lying soldier, a quiet and sombre image of loss. Nearby, a memorial also honours those who resisted the Nazi regime. Munich was the home of the White Rose, a student resistance group in the 1940s. This part of the garden asks for a slower pace and a lower voice.

Away from the memorials, the garden returns to everyday life. On the gravel areas, you may see people playing boules, the French game where players toss heavy metal balls toward a small target ball. Office workers eat lunch on the benches. Students read in the shade. Older Munich residents walk the same circuit they have walked for years. Watch for a while and you will notice how many people treat this place as their own backyard.

If you have time, follow the paths to the northern edge. Here the formal garden opens toward the trees of the Englischer Garten, Munich's huge public park. The contrast is clear. The Hofgarten is all straight lines and planned views. The English Garden beyond feels wild and loose by comparison. In just a few hundred metres, you move from the style of a Renaissance prince to the style of a romantic landscape park.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the temple. Look along one of the straight paths toward the arcades. Notice how the lines of trees guide your eye, exactly as the garden's planners intended four centuries ago. Listen again to the gravel under passing feet, and perhaps to a last few notes from under the dome. Then, as you walk out through the gate, glance back once. The garden looks just as calm behind you as it did when you arrived.

Before you go

Practical tips

Step inside the Dianatempel to hear its unusual acoustics.
Visit in the late afternoon when street musicians often play.
Combine your visit with a walk into the Englischer Garten.
Bring lunch and sit on a bench like local office workers.
Lower your voice near the war memorial and its crypt.
