The guide

The story

Gravel crunches under your feet. Ahead, a long canal runs straight as a ruler toward a pale palace, and swans drift across the water without hurry. On a still morning you can hear the wingbeats of geese before you see them. The façade spreads left and right, pavilion after pavilion, so wide that it is hard to take in with one look. This is Nymphenburg Palace, the summer home of Bavaria's rulers for more than two centuries.

The story begins with a birth. In 1662, after years of waiting, Elector Ferdinand Maria of Bavaria and his wife, Henriette Adelaide of Savoy, finally had a son and heir. His name was Max Emanuel. To celebrate, the Elector gave his wife land west of Munich. She was an Italian princess from Turin, and she wanted a country villa in the style she knew from home. Work began in 1664 under the Italian architect Agostino Barelli.

What Barelli built was much smaller than what you see now. It was a single cube of a building, the tall central block that still stands in the middle. Everything to either side came later. The name came from the princess's taste for classical myth. Nymphenburg means, quite simply, the castle of the nymphs. Nymphs were nature spirits from Greek legend, guardians of springs, woods and meadows. It was a fitting name for a summer retreat built among fields and water.

The baby born in 1662 grew up to transform the place. Max Emanuel became a restless, ambitious ruler who spent years at war and in exile in France. He came back with French ideas about power and display. Under his rule, and with architects such as Enrico Zuccalli and later Joseph Effner, the villa grew wings, galleries and side pavilions. Bit by bit, an Italian country house became a sprawling baroque residence. Its frontage now stretches well over half a kilometre.

Walk inside, and the first great room is the Stone Hall. It rises through several floors, white and gold, filled with light from tall windows on both sides. Look up. The ceiling fresco was painted in the mid-eighteenth century by Johann Baptist Zimmermann, one of the great Bavarian painters of the rococo — the playful, curling late phase of the baroque. The scene shows the gods of Olympus and the goddess Flora, and nymphs, of course, in honour of the name.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Stand in the hall and turn to face the garden side, then the city side. The hall was designed as a hinge between two worlds. One side looks back toward Munich along the canal. The other opens to the formal garden and the woods beyond. The rulers who held feasts here sat literally between their capital and their private paradise. It is a piece of political theatre built into the walls.

From the south side of the palace, a suite of rooms leads to one of the most talked-about collections in Munich. It is the Gallery of Beauties. King Ludwig I of Bavaria commissioned it in the nineteenth century. The court painter Joseph Stieler painted most of the portraits. Ludwig wanted to show the women he found most beautiful, and he chose them from every class of society — princesses and noblewomen, but also the daughter of a shoemaker.

That shoemaker's daughter was Helene Sedlmayr, and her portrait is one of the most loved in the room. Another face here carries a scandal. Lola Montez was a dancer who called herself Spanish but was born in Ireland. Ludwig became so attached to her that public anger grew, and it fed into the unrest that ended his reign in 1848. So as you look at her calm painted face, remember: this portrait hangs near the story of a king losing his throne.

The palace holds one more royal link that surprises people. In 1845, a boy was born here who would become King Ludwig the Second, the dreamer later known for building Neuschwanstein. Before the fairy-tale castles in the mountains, there was this — a childhood among canals, swans and painted ceilings. Swans would become one of his favourite symbols. It is hard not to see a connection when a pair glides past on the canal outside.

Now step out into the park, because the park is half the experience. The formal garden directly behind the palace has the clean geometry of the baroque. Further out, the paths begin to curve. In the early nineteenth century, the garden designer Friedrich Ludwig von Sckell reshaped much of the grounds in the English landscape style. That style hides its design. Lakes look natural, meadows open suddenly, and trees frame distant views as if by chance.

Scattered through these woods are small pavilions, each a world of its own. The Amalienburg is a hunting lodge designed by François Cuvilliés in the 1730s. Its round Hall of Mirrors shines in silver and pale blue, with carved birds, vines and hunting scenes. The Badenburg holds one of the first heated indoor bathing pools of its era. The Pagodenburg is decorated with Chinese and Dutch-style tiles. And the Magdalenenklause was built to look like a crumbling hermit's ruin — a fake ruin, new when it was made.

The palace complex holds more than royal rooms. In the former stables, the Marstallmuseum displays royal coaches and sleighs, including the gilded, almost unbelievable carriages of Ludwig the Second. In the north wing, a natural history museum fills rooms once used by the court. And in the curved buildings near the front, the Nymphenburg porcelain manufactory still makes fine porcelain by hand. A royal workshop founded in the eighteenth century, it moved here and never left.

Locals treat this place less like a monument and more like a garden for daily life. You will see joggers on the long paths, families feeding the ducks, and older couples on benches facing the water. In cold winters, when the canal freezes, people have long come here to skate and to play ice stock sport, a Bavarian game a little like curling. The palace was built for a single family. Today, the whole city shares it.

Before you leave, stand for a moment by the canal and look back. Notice how the white pavilions repeat, how the central block rises above the others, how the water doubles everything. That first small villa, built to celebrate a baby's birth, is still there at the heart of it all. As you walk away, listen for the gravel, the geese, and the fountain behind you... the same sounds the court once heard on a summer evening.
