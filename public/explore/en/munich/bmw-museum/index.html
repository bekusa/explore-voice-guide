The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind the hum of the Middle Ring road, you may catch the soft rush of traffic and a faint metallic echo. In front of you rises a low, silver-white bowl. It has no visible windows and no obvious front door. It looks less like a museum and more like something that landed here quietly overnight. Its smooth, rounded shell catches the Munich sky and turns it pale grey or bright white, depending on the weather.

This is the BMW Museum. Locals have long called the building the salad bowl, and once you see its shape, the nickname is hard to forget. It opened in 1973, just a year after Munich hosted the Summer Olympics. The Olympic Park is only a short walk away, and you can see its tower from here. That timing matters. In the early 1970s, Munich wanted to show the world a modern, confident face. This corner of the city became part of that statement.

Look up and to the side of the bowl. The tall tower beside it is the BMW headquarters. Its shape is made of four linked cylinders, standing side by side. Many people say it resembles the cylinders of an engine, and the company embraced that comparison. Both the tower and the museum bowl came from the same architect, the Austrian Karl Schwanzer. He designed them as a pair. One is tall and vertical, the other low and round. Together they act like a signature on the skyline.

Here is a detail most visitors on the ground never see. The flat roof of the bowl carries the BMW logo, the blue and white circle. You cannot read it from the pavement. But from the top of the Olympic Tower nearby, or from an aircraft above the city, the roundel is clear. The building was designed to be recognised from the air. That is a fitting idea for a company whose story begins in the sky.

Because BMW did not start with cars. The company traces its founding to 1916, during the First World War, and its first products were aircraft engines. The name stands for Bayerische Motoren Werke, which means Bavarian Motor Works. Engines were the heart of the business from the very beginning. Motorcycles came next, in the 1920s. Cars arrived only at the end of that decade. So when you walk through the galleries, you are following a path from propellers, to two wheels, to four.

That sky connection feeds one of the most repeated stories about the logo. Legend has it that the blue and white roundel shows a spinning propeller against a clear sky. It is a lovely image, and you will hear it often. But it is better treated as a popular tale than as the original design idea. The propeller image was spread by advertising some years after the logo first appeared. The blue and white segments are generally linked to the colours of Bavaria, the state you are standing in right now.

Step inside, and the feeling changes. The interior of the bowl winds upward along spiralling ramps. There are few hard corners. You move in a slow, continuous curve, as if you are driving gently up a ramp in a parking garage, but through history instead. This was part of the original thinking. The building encourages you to keep walking, to keep turning, to see what waits around the next bend. It feels less like a series of rooms and more like a road.

In the 2000s, the museum was closed for a major renovation and expansion. It reopened in 2008 with much more space. The original bowl is still the centrepiece, but the exhibition now spreads into a larger, low building next to it. Inside, the designers used the idea of streets and squares. Walkways and bridges connect themed areas, almost like a small city. You can look across open spaces and see cars displayed on different levels at once.

Among the vehicles, look for the small, rounded Isetta. It is a tiny bubble car with a door that opens at the front. The whole front panel swings out, and the steering wheel moves with it. In the 1950s, Germany was rebuilding after the war, and many people could not afford a full-sized car. Little cars like the Isetta offered cheap, practical transport. Their success helped carry the company through difficult years. It is one of the most cheerful objects in the building, and children often gather around it.

The galleries also give real space to motorcycles. BMW's first motorcycle appeared in 1923, and the company has built them ever since. You will notice how often the engine sits out in the open, with its cylinders sticking out to each side. That layout became a long-running signature for the brand. Elsewhere you will find racing cars, design studies, and concept vehicles that never reached the road. Some look surprisingly futuristic, even decades after they were drawn.

Keep an eye out for one of the quietest and most hypnotic displays. In one space, many small metal spheres hang from thin wires. They rise and fall slowly, moving together to form the outline of a car in the air. Then the shape melts and becomes something else. There is no engine noise, only patient, smooth motion. People often stop here longer than they planned. It is a reminder that this museum is about design and movement, not only machines.

When you step back outside, look across the road at the large building with the swooping, cloud-like roof. That is BMW Welt, which means BMW World. It opened in 2007 as a delivery centre and showroom, where some customers collect their new cars. Its glass and steel shapes form a strong contrast with the calm, closed bowl of the museum. Between the two buildings, you can see how ideas about modern architecture changed across more than thirty years.

Before you move on, pause by the bowl once more. Run your eyes along its smooth curve, and notice how it reflects the sky above you. Listen again to the traffic on the ring road, the steady sound of a city that still moves by engine. Somewhere nearby, behind the tower, cars are still being built in Munich today. The museum is not a story about a closed past. As you walk away, you are walking through the very streets that this story helped to shape.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with BMW Welt directly across the road.
Visit the Olympic Tower to see the logo on the roof.
Wear comfortable shoes for the long, gently sloping ramps.
Arrive early to enjoy the kinetic sculpture with fewer crowds.
Walk to Olympic Park afterwards for a relaxed green break.
