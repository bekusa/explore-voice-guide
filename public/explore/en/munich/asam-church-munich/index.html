The guide

The story

Step in from Sendlinger Strasse and the street noise drops away almost at once. The air is cool and still. Your eyes need a moment to adjust, because the light here is low and golden, and it seems to come from nowhere in particular. Gilded stucco, dark marble, and painted clouds crowd the space from floor to ceiling. Before you know any history at all, this church does something to your body. It pulls your gaze upward, again and again, toward a glow above the altar.

This is the Asam Church, officially dedicated to Saint John of Nepomuk. Most people in Munich simply call it the Asamkirche, after the two brothers who built it. Egid Quirin Asam was a sculptor and stucco artist. His older brother, Cosmas Damian Asam, was a painter famous for ceiling frescoes. Together they were among the most sought-after church decorators in southern Germany in the first half of the eighteenth century. Their workshops filled monasteries and abbey churches across Bavaria.

Here is the first surprise. This church was not commissioned by a bishop, a prince, or a monastery. The brothers built it themselves, with their own money, in the 1730s and 1740s. Egid Quirin bought land on this street, including the house right next door, where he lived. The church was meant as a private devotional space. It was also, quite openly, a showcase. Here the brothers could work with no patron telling them what to cut or simplify.

That freedom shows everywhere. Think of it as a calling card in stone and gold. A wealthy client could walk in and see exactly what the Asam workshop could do when nothing held it back. The style is late Baroque, edging into Rococo. Baroque means art designed to overwhelm the senses and move the emotions. Rococo is its lighter, more playful final phase, full of curves, shells, and gilded ornament. Both of those impulses meet in this one narrow room.

The second surprise is about who was allowed inside. Because the church was private, the brothers expected to control access. The people of Munich had other ideas. They wanted to worship here, and public pressure grew. In the end, the church opened to the public. Still, one private privilege remained. A window in Egid Quirin's house looked directly onto the high altar. He could, in effect, attend Mass without leaving home. Few artists in history have built themselves a better view.

Now look around you and notice the scale. The church is remarkably narrow and deep, squeezed onto a plot no wider than an ordinary townhouse. A lesser designer would have made it feel cramped. The Asams turned the limitation into drama. They stacked the space vertically, with a gallery running above, and they controlled the light with great care. The darker lower level feels heavy and earthly. The upper zone grows brighter as it rises, as if heaven sits just above your head.

That brings us to the saint himself. John of Nepomuk was a fourteenth-century priest in Bohemia, in what is today the Czech Republic. He was killed in Prague in 1393 and thrown from a bridge into the Vltava River. According to a popular tradition, he died because he refused to tell the king what the queen had said in confession. Historians debate the real reasons for his death. But the legend made him a patron of confessional secrecy and of protection against drowning.

Timing matters here. John of Nepomuk was declared a saint in 1729, only a few years before this church began. His cult was new, popular, and spreading fast through Catholic central Europe. By dedicating their church to him, the Asams tied their personal project to one of the most fashionable devotions of the day. Look at the ceiling fresco by Cosmas Damian. It tells the story of the saint's life and his glory in heaven, painted to open the roof into the sky.

Now turn your attention to the high altar, the focus of the whole building. Above it is a sculpted group known as the Throne of Grace. This is a traditional image of the Holy Trinity. God the Father holds his crucified Son, while the Holy Spirit appears as a dove. Behind and above the figures, light falls from a source you cannot easily see. That hidden lighting is the key trick of the room. It makes the gold appear to glow from within.

Step back outside for a moment, if you can, and look at the facade. It rises straight from the pavement between ordinary buildings. At its base, the stone is carved to look like rough, natural rock. It is as if the church grows out of the earth. Above that, a figure of Saint John of Nepomuk appears on the front, lifted by angels. The doors carry carved scenes too. Many people walk past without realising how much story is packed into this one tall, slim front.

Like much of Munich's old centre, the church suffered damage during the Second World War. It was restored in the decades that followed. As you look at the gilding and the marbled surfaces, remember that some of what you see is careful repair work. That is not a weakness. It is part of the building's real life. A church like this survives because people decide, generation after generation, that it is worth the effort of keeping.

Also keep in mind that this is a working church, not only a museum of Baroque art. People still come here to pray, to light candles, and to sit quietly. You may notice someone kneeling in a pew while visitors drift past with cameras. The best way to experience the space is to slow down. Sit for a few minutes. Let your eyes travel from the dark floor, up the twisting columns, to the light at the top.

There is one more thing worth noticing as you sit. Everything here works as a single design, not a collection of separate objects. Painting, sculpture, architecture, and light all serve one purpose. The Germans have a word for this idea, Gesamtkunstwerk, meaning a total work of art. The Asam brothers did not invent the idea. But in this small, private church on a shopping street, they came as close to achieving it as almost anyone in their age.

When you are ready, walk slowly back toward the door. Notice how the gold fades behind you and the grey daylight of Sendlinger Strasse returns. The traffic, the shop windows, the people with shopping bags... all of it is right there, only a few steps away. Before you go, glance once more at the house beside the church. Somewhere behind those walls, an artist once looked through his own window at the altar he had made.
