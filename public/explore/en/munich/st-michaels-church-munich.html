The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Shopping bags rustle. A street musician tunes a guitar. Footsteps echo on the stone of Neuhauser Strasse, Munich's long pedestrian street. Then look up at the pale facade in front of you. It rises like a tall, flat wall of gables and niches, almost like a stage set. Behind that wall stands one of the most important churches in Germany. You are standing in front of Saint Michael's Church.

This is a Jesuit church. It was built in the late sixteenth century, roughly between the 1580s and the late 1590s. The man who paid for it was Duke Wilhelm the Fifth of Bavaria. He was a deeply religious ruler, so devout that history remembers him as Wilhelm the Pious. His goal was clear. He wanted a monument to the Catholic faith at a moment when that faith was under pressure across Europe.

That moment has a name — the Counter-Reformation. In the sixteenth century, the Protestant Reformation split Western Christianity. The Catholic Church answered with renewal, education, and powerful new buildings. The Jesuits, a religious order founded in that same century, led much of this effort. This church was meant to show the people of Munich, in stone and gold, that Bavaria would stay Catholic. Saint Michael was the perfect patron. He is the archangel who defeats the devil.

Now look at the facade itself, between the two main doorways. There stands a bronze statue of Saint Michael. He holds his weapon high, and under his feet lies a defeated demon, half man and half beast. The sculptor was Hubert Gerhard, a Dutch-born artist who worked for the Bavarian court. Take a close look at the demon's face. It is twisted and strange, and many visitors walk past without noticing it at all.

Above the doors, the niches hold stone statues of rulers. These are members of the Wittelsbach family, the dynasty that governed Bavaria for more than seven centuries. They stand in rows, like a family tree carved into the wall. The message was simple. The ruling house and the Church stood together. Wilhelm placed himself in this line too, so his family literally became part of the building.

The church almost turned out very differently. While the building was going up, the original tower collapsed. That was in 1590, and it destroyed part of the choir. A lesser patron might have given up. Wilhelm, according to the traditional account, read the collapse as a sign that the church was too small. So he made it larger. A new and grander choir was added. The church you see today grew out of that disaster.

Step inside, and let your eyes adjust. The first thing most people notice is the ceiling. One enormous white barrel vault — a ceiling shaped like half a tunnel — spans the whole nave without any columns to hold it up. For its time, this was a bold piece of engineering. It is often described as one of the largest barrel vaults in the world from that era, second only to Saint Peter's in Rome. Stand in the centre aisle and simply look up.

The style here is Renaissance. That means the architecture borrows the calm forms of ancient Rome: round arches, columns, balanced proportions. Saint Michael's is widely called the largest Renaissance church north of the Alps. Notice how bright and orderly the space feels. It does not overwhelm you with gold like later Baroque churches in Bavaria. Instead, white plaster, grey stone, and soft light do most of the work.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. Much of what you see was rebuilt. During the Second World War, bombing raids hit Munich hard. The church was badly damaged, and parts of the great vault fell in. After the war, it was carefully restored over several years. So when you look up at that smooth white ceiling, you are seeing both a sixteenth-century idea and a twentieth-century act of repair.

As you walk along the side of the nave, look for a large white marble monument. It is the tomb of Eugène de Beauharnais, the stepson of Napoleon. He married into the Bavarian royal family and spent his later years in Munich. The monument is by Bertel Thorvaldsen, a famous Danish sculptor of the early nineteenth century. Its cool, classical figures feel very different from the older church around them. It is an unexpected link between Munich and the story of Napoleon.

Now comes perhaps the biggest surprise. Beneath your feet lies a royal crypt. Several members of the Wittelsbach family rest down there, including Duke Wilhelm himself, the founder of the church. But the coffin that draws most visitors belongs to King Ludwig the Second. Many people know him as the Fairy Tale King, the builder of Neuschwanstein Castle. He died mysteriously in 1886. Even today, admirers leave fresh flowers on his tomb.

The crypt usually has its own entrance and a separate small ticket, so check at the church before you go down. It is quiet and cool, and the space is fairly small. Take your time and read the names on the coffins. You will find centuries of Bavarian history gathered in a few rooms. Just remember that this is still a place of burial and of worship. Speak softly and move slowly.

The church is still active today. Masses are held here, and music plays a large part in its life. If you are lucky, you may hear the organ or a choir rehearsing. The tall vault carries sound beautifully, so even a few notes seem to hang in the air. If a service is happening when you arrive, it is best to wait at the back, or come back a little later.

Before you leave, pause at the doorway and turn around once more. Look down the long white nave towards the altar, with the light falling in from the side windows. Then step back out into the noise of Neuhauser Strasse. Shoppers will hurry past without looking up. But now you know what stands behind this calm facade — a duke's ambition, a fallen tower, a rebuilt vault, and a sleeping king.
