The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see much at all, you will hear the forest: a dry rattle of leaves, the sharp call of a bird you cannot find, the low hum of insects in the heat. Underfoot, the ground is hard and dusty in the dry months and soft with fallen leaves after the rains. The air smells of warm bark, salt and something green. This is the quiet corner of Bali, far from the rice terraces and beach clubs most visitors picture.

West Bali National Park covers the far northwestern end of the island. It crosses two regencies, Buleleng in the north and Jembrana in the south. Regencies are Bali's large administrative districts, a little like counties. The park includes low mountains, dry savanna, monsoon forest, mangrove shores and a wide area of sea and coral reef. Bali was settled densely for centuries. This is one of the few large places where the land was largely left to the wild.

The area was protected step by step through the twentieth century. It was later formally designated as a national park in the 1990s. Today it is the only national park on Bali. That fact surprises many visitors. The island is small and crowded, and every hillside seems to hold a temple or a field. Here, by contrast, the main residents are deer, monkeys, monitor lizards and birds. People are the guests.

The park's most famous resident is small and white. It is the Bali myna, also called the Bali starling, or Jalak Bali in Indonesian. It lives naturally only on this island. Its feathers are almost pure white, with black tips on the wings and tail. Around each eye is a patch of bare, bright blue skin. When it lifts its crest, it looks like a tiny feathered crown.

Here is the hard part of the story. The Bali myna came close to vanishing in the wild. Its beauty made it valuable in the cage-bird trade, and trapping pushed numbers down to a very small group. It is still listed as critically endangered. For decades, conservation teams have bred the birds in captivity and released them into the park. Local villages now help protect them too. If you see one, you are looking at a real survival story.

Another lost animal belongs to this landscape. The Bali tiger once lived on the island. It was the smallest kind of tiger, and it is now extinct. The last confirmed sightings date to the first half of the twentieth century. Some of its final range was in these western forests. No tiger walks here today. Still, it is worth remembering that this park protects what remains of a much wilder Bali.

Walk the trails and look closely at the trees. In the dry season, many lose their leaves, and the forest turns grey and gold. Light falls straight through the branches onto the path. After the rains, the same forest fills with green again. This seasonal change is the mark of monsoon forest. It feels very different from the lush, wet jungle of central Bali, and it gives the park a quiet, open character.

You may share the trail with long-tailed macaques. They move in noisy groups and watch visitors with great interest, especially anyone carrying food. Higher in the canopy, look for dark leaf monkeys, often called ebony langurs or lutung. They are calmer and shyer. Rusa deer graze at the edges of clearings. A small deer called the muntjac is also found here. It is sometimes called the barking deer, because its alarm call sounds like a short, sharp bark.

Off the north coast lies Menjangan Island. Its name means deer island, after the deer that swim and wander there. The island is part of the national park. Boats leave from the mainland coast for the short crossing. The real treasure is underwater. Coral walls drop steeply from the shallows into deep blue water. Divers and snorkellers come for clear water, soft corals, sea fans and schools of reef fish.

Menjangan is also a place of prayer. Small Hindu temples stand on the island, and Balinese pilgrims visit them for ceremonies. You may see people dressed in white, carrying offerings of flowers and rice in woven palm-leaf baskets. Visitors are welcome to look, but please be respectful. Cover your shoulders and legs near the temples, speak softly and never step over offerings placed on the ground.

At the western tip of the park sits the port town of Gilimanuk. From here, ferries cross the narrow Bali Strait to Java. On a clear day, you can see the mountains of Java across the water. Near Gilimanuk, archaeologists have found prehistoric burial sites. They show that people lived on this coast long before written records. This small edge of Bali has been a crossing point between islands for a very long time.

Along the shoreline, mangroves grow in the shallow water. Their roots rise from the mud like rows of thin legs. They protect the coast from waves and shelter young fish and crabs. At low tide, you may hear clicking and popping sounds from the mud. These are small creatures moving between the roots. Kingfishers often sit on low branches here, waiting very still before they dive.

Take a moment now, wherever you are standing. Notice how much quieter it is than the rest of the island. No scooter horns, no beach music, just wind, birds and perhaps the sea. Look up into the branches once more before you leave. Somewhere above you, a white bird with a blue mask may be watching. Walk slowly on the way out. The forest often shows its best moments to the patient.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange a licensed park guide before starting any trek.
Start walks early to see more birds and wildlife.
Bring snorkel gear or book a Menjangan boat trip.
Keep food hidden from macaques along the trails.
Wear a sarong and cover shoulders near island temples.
