The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the square and look up. A great grey-gold dome fills the sky above the rooftops, wide and low and calm. Underfoot, the paving stones are warm. Cars and buses pass around the edge of the square, and the limestone walls in front of you glow a soft honey colour in the Maltese sun. This is the Rotunda of Mosta, and it sits in the town of Mosta, a short drive from Attard, near the middle of Malta.

Its formal name is the Parish Church of the Assumption of Our Lady. Most Maltese simply call it the Rotunda, or refer to it by the town's name. The church is dedicated to the Virgin Mary taken up into heaven, a feast the islands celebrate every August. Behind the grand front, though, is a story of an ordinary farming village. The people here decided, in the early nineteenth century, to build something far bigger than they seemed able to afford.

The design came from Giorgio Grognet de Vassé, an architect of French descent who lived in Malta. His model was clear. Look at the front portico, with its row of tall columns and triangular pediment. Then look at the round body and the dome behind it. Anyone who has visited Rome will recognise the Pantheon there. The Pantheon is the ancient Roman temple famous for its huge round dome. Grognet borrowed its idea of a vast circle of space under a single roof.

Construction began in 1833. And here is the first detail many visitors miss. The villagers did not tear down their old parish church first. They built the new walls around it, so the community kept worshipping inside the old building while the new one rose on every side. Only when the great circle was nearly complete was the old church taken down and carried out. The work took decades, and the church was finished around 1860.

Much of the labour came from the parishioners themselves. Local men gave their evenings and their Sundays to haul and cut stone. The building is made from Maltese limestone, the same pale stone you see in farm walls and town houses across the island. It is soft when first quarried, which makes it easy to carve. Then it hardens as it weathers. That is why the columns and mouldings still look crisp after more than a century and a half.

A popular story says the dome was raised without any scaffolding. Historians treat this claim with some caution, and the exact methods are debated. What is certain is the result. The dome is one of the largest in Europe, and it rests on thick circular walls rather than on a forest of internal pillars. When you step inside, you will feel how open the space is. Nothing blocks your view from one side of the circle to the other.

Now walk through the doors. The noise of the square falls away, and the air feels cooler on your skin. Your eyes go straight up. The inside of the dome is covered in coffers, rows of sunken square panels that grow smaller as they climb toward the top. They are painted in deep blue and touched with gold. At the very centre, light comes down from above, and the whole ceiling seems to float over the floor.

The floor is wide and round, and the side altars sit in recesses around the edge. Many are framed with marble and gilded detail. Paintings of saints and scenes from the life of Mary hang above them. Take a slow walk around the circle rather than standing only in the middle. Each step shows the dome from a slightly different angle. The light also changes during the day, moving across the coffers like the hand of a clock.

Then comes the moment that made this church famous far beyond Malta. During the Second World War, Malta was one of the most heavily bombed places on Earth. On the ninth of April 1942, German aircraft attacked the area. A large bomb pierced the dome while a congregation of a few hundred people was gathered inside. It crashed onto the floor, skidded across the stone... and did not explode.

Nobody inside was killed. Two further bombs fell nearby and also failed to go off. For the people of Mosta, there was only one explanation, and they still call it a miracle. The hole in the dome was later repaired, and you will not find it easily from the floor. But the memory is kept alive. In the sacristy, a replica of the bomb is on display, and visitors often pause there longer than anywhere else.

It helps to imagine that evening. Outside, sirens and engines. Inside, candles and prayer under the painted dome. Then a sudden crash of stone from above, dust in the air, and a heavy metal shape lying still on the floor. Many families in town have a grandparent or great-grandparent who was there, or who knew someone who was. Ask a local about it, and you may hear the story told with quiet pride.

The Rotunda is still a working parish church, not a museum. Masses are held here, and the community gathers for weddings, funerals and feast days. The biggest celebration comes in mid-August for the Feast of the Assumption. During the feast, the façade is lit up, statues are carried through the streets, brass bands play, and fireworks crack over the rooftops. If you visit at another time, you will still see locals stopping in for a moment of prayer.

Outside again, look at the two bell towers that stand on either side of the portico. They frame the columns and add height to the low, wide front. Around the square, cafés and small shops face the church, and their terraces are good places to sit and look at the façade. The church sits right in the town centre, and buses from many parts of the island pass close by.

Before you leave, turn once more toward the dome. Notice how it sits above everything else in Mosta, visible from roads and fields across this part of the island. A village built it by hand, stone by stone, around its old church. A war tried to break it, and failed. As you walk away, listen for the bells, and watch the limestone change colour as the light moves across it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cover shoulders and knees; this is an active parish church.
Avoid visiting during Mass so worshippers are not disturbed.
Visit in mid-August to experience the Feast of the Assumption.
Walk the full circle inside to see the dome from every angle.
Sit at a café on the square to study the façade.
