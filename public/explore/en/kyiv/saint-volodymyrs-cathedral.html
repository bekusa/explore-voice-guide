The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of Taras Shevchenko Boulevard and look up. The walls are a warm, buttery yellow, and the light catches them differently every hour of the day. Above, seven golden domes sit against the sky. Traffic hums behind you, chestnut trees rustle along the boulevard, and somewhere a bell may be ringing. This is Saint Volodymyr's Cathedral, one of the great churches of Kyiv. It sits in the Shevchenko district, a short walk from the university and the park that carry the poet's name.

The cathedral takes its name from Prince Volodymyr the Great. He ruled the medieval state of Kyivan Rus, and in 988 he brought Christianity to his people. Tradition says he had the citizens of Kyiv baptised in the waters of the Dnipro River. Nine centuries later, in the nineteenth century, church and city leaders wanted a monument to that moment. The idea grew into this building, planned to honour the nine hundredth anniversary of the conversion.

The project was slow and difficult. Construction began in the early 1860s, and the cathedral was not consecrated until 1896. That is more than three decades of work. Early on, the walls cracked, and the design had to be revised. Several architects worked on the plans over the years. The first design is usually credited to Ivan Strom, and Alexander Beretti later reworked it. The building you see is the result of many hands and many corrections.

Notice the style. Architects call it Neo-Byzantine — a nineteenth-century revival of the church architecture of Byzantium, the eastern Roman empire centred on Constantinople. Kyiv took its first churches and its faith from Byzantium, so the choice was deliberate. The rounded arches, the clustered domes, and the strong, symmetrical outline all point back to that eastern tradition. It is a building that looks to the past to tell a story about origins.

Now step inside, and let your eyes adjust. Here is the first surprise. Many visitors come for the golden domes, but the real treasure of this cathedral is the painting. Almost every surface carries colour. Saints, angels, prophets, and patterns of vines and flowers cover the walls, the pillars, and the vaults. The air often smells of candle wax and incense, and the light falls softly through the windows onto deep blues, reds, and gold.

The decoration was organised by the art historian Adrian Prakhov. He gathered some of the most talented painters of the Russian Empire at the time. The leading figure was Viktor Vasnetsov, a painter known for his scenes from folklore and early history. He spent years working here. Alongside him were artists such as Mikhail Nesterov, Wilhelm Kotarbiński, and Pavel Svedomsky. Together they created one of the largest painted church interiors of their era.

Look toward the far end of the church, to the curved wall behind the altar. There you will find Vasnetsov's best-known work here, a towering image of the Mother of God holding the Christ Child. She seems to step forward on clouds, against a gold background. Many people find her face gentle but serious, almost thoughtful. For generations, this image has been one of the most recognised religious paintings in Ukraine.

As you walk, search for scenes from the story of the cathedral's namesake. Vasnetsov painted the baptism of Prince Volodymyr and the baptism of the people of Kyiv. These pictures connect the building directly to the events of 988. Here is the second surprise. The style mixes medieval tradition with the art movements of the late nineteenth century. The figures are elongated and flowing, and the ornament sometimes looks closer to Art Nouveau than to an ancient icon.

That modern touch has another source. The young Mikhail Vrubel also worked on decorations connected with this cathedral, mainly ornamental designs. Vrubel later became one of the most original painters of his generation. His ideas for some of the larger wall paintings were not accepted, and today art lovers still wonder what this interior might have looked like with his vision. Look closely at the patterned borders, and you will see how much care went into every curl of a vine.

The cathedral's twentieth century was turbulent. Under Soviet rule, religion was suppressed, and the building was closed for worship in the late 1920s. For years it was used for non-religious purposes. Worship returned during the Second World War, and the church stayed open afterward. Many of Kyiv's churches were not so lucky — some were demolished entirely. The survival of these paintings is, in part, a matter of fortune.

Today, this is a working church, not a museum. Services take place here regularly, with choral singing that fills the vaults. Since Ukraine's independence, the cathedral has become an important centre for Ukrainian Orthodoxy, and it now belongs to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine. You may see people lighting thin candles, kissing icons, or standing quietly in prayer. Please move slowly and speak softly while you are inside.

If you visit during a service, you will notice that most worshippers stand. There are few seats in a traditional Orthodox church. Women often cover their hair with a scarf, and men remove their hats. Visitors are generally welcome, but modest clothing is wise, with shoulders and knees covered. Photography rules can vary, so look for signs or ask before you raise your camera, especially during prayers.

As you leave, pause on the steps and turn back once more. The yellow walls, the gold domes, and the green of the boulevard trees make a simple, bright picture. Behind you is a building that took more than thirty years to finish, and that survived a century of change. In front of you, Kyiv carries on — students walking to class, trams and cars moving along the boulevard. Take a moment here before you rejoin the city.
