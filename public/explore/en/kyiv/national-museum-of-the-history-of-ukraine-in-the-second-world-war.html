The guide

The story

Start with the wind. Up here, on the high right bank of the Dnipro, the air moves freely across open stone. Under your feet, wide paved terraces run down the slope toward the river. Above you, a figure of polished steel rises into the sky, holding a sword in one hand and a shield in the other. When the sun hits it, the metal turns almost white. When clouds pass, it goes grey and cold, like the river below.

You are standing at the National Museum of the History of Ukraine in the Second World War. It is not one building, but a whole memorial landscape. There are halls, sculptures, open-air displays of military equipment, and that enormous statue. The museum itself sits inside the base of the monument. So when you walk through the exhibition halls, you are walking beneath the figure you just looked up at.

The complex opened in 1981, during the Soviet era. At that time, it carried a different name. It was the museum of what the Soviet Union called the Great Patriotic War. That phrase described the war against Nazi Germany from 1941 to 1945. It left out the earlier years, when the Soviet Union and Germany divided Eastern Europe between them. In 2015, Ukraine changed the name. The new title points to the Second World War as a whole, a wider and more honest frame.

Now look again at the statue. For decades, people called her the Motherland. She is one of the tallest statues in Europe, and from many parts of Kyiv you can see her on the skyline. Her body is made of stainless steel panels. The design came from Soviet monumental sculpture, a style built to impress from a great distance. Up close, you can see the seams where the steel sheets meet, like the plates of armour.

Here is something many visitors do not notice at first. Look at the shield. For more than forty years, it carried the Soviet coat of arms, with a hammer and sickle. In the summer of 2023, workers removed it. In its place, they mounted the trident, the national symbol of Ukraine. The statue also received a new name. Today, people call her Mother Ukraine. The change happened during a full-scale war, and many people in Kyiv watched the cranes work.

That detail tells you something about this place. A memorial is not frozen. It carries the ideas of the people who build it, and the people who come later can change it. The Soviet state used this hillside to tell one story about the war. Independent Ukraine now uses it to tell another one. Both stories are written into the stone and steel around you, and you can read them side by side.

Walk down toward the main entrance, and the scale changes. The open air gives way to dim halls. Inside, the exhibitions follow Ukraine through the Second World War. You will see uniforms, weapons, letters, photographs, and personal objects. Some of the most powerful items are very small. A worn spoon. A handwritten note. A child's shoe. These objects belonged to ordinary people, and they make the huge numbers of the war feel personal.

Ukraine suffered terribly in that war. The front line passed across its territory twice, first eastward and then back west. Cities burned. Villages disappeared. Millions of people died, among them soldiers, civilians, and Jewish communities murdered in the Holocaust. Kyiv itself was occupied by German forces for more than two years. Not far from here, at Babyn Yar, the occupiers carried out one of the largest mass shootings of the Holocaust. The museum keeps these memories close.

The second surprise is more recent. In the years since 2014, and especially since 2022, the museum has also documented the current war with Russia. Outside, you may find displays of destroyed or captured Russian military vehicles. Inside, some rooms show objects from the front lines of today. This is unusual for a war museum. Most museums look back at a finished past. Here, the past and the present stand in the same space, and the story is still being written.

As you walk the grounds, look for the large sculptural groups along the approaches. They show soldiers, partisans, and civilians in heavy, dramatic poses. These figures belong to the Soviet monumental tradition. Faces are strong and simple. Bodies lean forward as if pushing against a storm. You do not need to share the ideas behind them to feel their weight. Notice how the artists used bronze and concrete to make human figures look almost like part of the landscape.

The setting also matters. This memorial stands in Pechersk, one of the oldest parts of Kyiv. Just nearby is the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, a great monastery complex with golden domes and caves where monks once lived. The contrast is strong. On one side, centuries of church bells and quiet prayer. On this side, steel, armour, and the memory of modern war. Many visitors combine both places on one walk along the river slopes.

From the terraces, turn and face the Dnipro. On a clear day, you can see across the wide river to the left bank, with its bridges and long rows of apartment blocks. This view explains why the hillside was chosen. The river was a key line in the fighting for Kyiv. Armies crossed it under fire. Today, joggers, families, and couples walk here on weekends, and the slope feels calm. The calm is part of the memorial too.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the base of the statue. Listen. You may hear the wind against the steel, the voices of other visitors, perhaps a bird in the trees along the slope. Look up at the trident on the shield, then down at the river. As you walk away, notice how the figure stays with you. Turn back once or twice. From almost any path in this part of the city, she is still watching over Kyiv.

Practical tips

Ask on site whether the viewing platforms are open.
