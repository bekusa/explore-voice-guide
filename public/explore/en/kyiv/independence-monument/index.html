The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the square for a moment and listen. Water runs in the fountains. Traffic hums along the wide avenue. Feet move across granite, and someone, somewhere, is playing a guitar. Then tilt your head back. A tall white column rises above you, and at its top a gilded figure catches the light. You are on Maidan Nezalezhnosti, Independence Square, the open heart of Kyiv. The column in front of you is the Independence Monument.

The monument is young by the standards of this ancient city. It was unveiled in 2001, for the tenth anniversary of Ukrainian independence. Ukraine declared that independence in August 1991, as the Soviet Union was coming apart. So this column does not mark a battle or a king. It marks a beginning, the moment a country began to write its own story again after centuries of rule from elsewhere.

Look first at the shape. The column is slender and very tall, roughly sixty metres from the ground to the top of the figure. Its surface is clad in pale stone, which turns warm and creamy in the late afternoon. Decorative bands and ornament run along it at intervals. From a distance it reads as one clean white line, a vertical mark that the eye finds from almost anywhere on the square.

Now look up at the figure on top. She is a woman in flowing robes, made of gilded metal, with her arms raised. In her hands she holds a branch of viburnum. In Ukraine this plant is called kalyna, a shrub with clusters of bright red berries. Kalyna appears in folk songs, embroidery and wedding traditions. It stands for home, for love, and for the land itself.

The woman is often described as Berehynia. In Ukrainian tradition, Berehynia is a protective female spirit, a guardian of the home and the hearth. The idea is not of a warrior or a ruler, but of someone who keeps watch. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The figure at the top of the national monument is not a general or a statesman. She is a protector, holding a branch from a garden hedge.

Walk closer to the base. The lower part of the monument is shaped like a small church pavilion, with columns and a curved dome. Its style draws on Ukrainian baroque, a local version of the ornate European style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Kyiv's old churches often carry the same green domes and white walls. The base links this modern column to that older architecture of the city.

Before independence, this square looked and sounded very different. In the Soviet period it carried a name honouring the October Revolution, and a large Soviet monument stood here. That monument was taken down around the time Ukraine became independent. When you stand here, you are standing on the same ground where one story was removed and a new one was set in its place.

The column itself is only part of the meaning. The square around it has become the place where Ukrainians gather when the country reaches a turning point. In 2004, crowds filled Maidan during the Orange Revolution, a wave of peaceful protest over a disputed presidential election. Orange was the colour of the opposition movement. For weeks the square was full of tents, flags and people who refused to go home.

Nine years later, in the winter of 2013, people came back. The protests began after the government turned away from closer ties with the European Union. They grew into what Ukrainians call the Revolution of Dignity. Through a freezing winter, the square became a camp with barricades, field kitchens and stages. In February 2014, more than one hundred protesters were killed in and around this area. Ukrainians remember them as the Heavenly Hundred.

Here is the second detail many visitors miss. The Independence Monument stood through all of it. Smoke from burning tyres rose around the column. Barricades crossed the streets below it. Photographs from those weeks show the gilded figure above a square full of fire and frost. The monument was built to celebrate independence, but those winters gave it a heavier meaning. To many Ukrainians it now marks the price of that independence as well.

Since the full-scale Russian invasion in 2022, the square has taken on yet another layer. On the lawns near the monument, people have planted many small flags, most of them blue and yellow. Many flags carry a name, a photograph or a short message. Each one remembers someone killed in the war. Relatives and friends add new flags, so the memorial changes as you watch it. If you walk among them, move slowly and quietly. This is a place of grief, and people come here to mourn.

Turn now and take in the wider square. The broad avenue running past is Khreshchatyk, Kyiv's main street, lined with large post-war buildings. Below your feet is an underground shopping centre, with glass domes rising out of the paving. On one side, a gilded statue of Saint Michael the Archangel stands on top of a decorative gate. Saint Michael is the patron saint of Kyiv. Up the hill, a large hotel looks down over the whole scene.

So the square holds many things at once. It is a place for shopping, for meeting friends, for street music and evening walks. It is also a national stage and a memorial. Locals cross it on the way to work, often without looking up. Then, on certain days, the same people return in thousands. Both of those versions of Maidan are true, and both belong to the monument.

Before you move on, look up once more. Notice how the light sits on the gold figure, and how the white column changes colour as the sky changes. Look down at the flags in the grass and the people walking among them. Listen again to the fountains and the traffic. This is a working city square, and a country's memory, in the same space. As you walk away along Khreshchatyk, glance back. You will see her still there, arms raised, keeping watch.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit at late afternoon when the column turns warm.
Walk the flag memorial slowly and keep voices low.
Follow air-raid alerts and know the nearest shelter.
Cross to the far side for a full-height view.
Combine your visit with a walk along Khreshchatyk.
