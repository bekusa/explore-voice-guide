The guide

The story

Tilt your head back. The steel lattice above you narrows into the sky until it looks thin as a pencil line. On a windy day you may hear a low hum from the metal, and the long red and white bands seem to move as clouds pass behind them. Underfoot, the ground is ordinary city ground: grass, asphalt, cracked paving. Above it stands the tallest structure in Ukraine.

This is the Kyiv TV Tower. Locals often just call it the telebashta, the television tower. It rises roughly 385 metres above the city's western neighbourhoods. From almost anywhere in Kyiv, if you look towards the northwest, you can find it. It is a thin grey shape at dawn, a row of red warning lights at night. For many residents it is less a landmark than a compass needle.

The tower was built in the late Soviet period and completed in the early 1970s. In those years, television was becoming the main way millions of people received news, sport, and entertainment. A city the size of Kyiv needed a strong, high signal. A tall mast meant a wider broadcast range, so engineers reached as high as the materials allowed. The result is this column of steel, which still carries television and radio transmitters today.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The tower is a welded steel structure. Most lattice towers of this scale are held together with bolts or rivets. Here, the joints were welded. It is often described as the tallest freestanding welded lattice tower in the world. And notice something else as you walk around the base. There are no guy wires. Nothing holds the tower to the ground except its own legs.

Stand back and study the shape. The base is wide and heavy, and the frame tapers as it climbs. Higher up, you can see platforms and equipment clustered on the structure. These carry antennas and service areas. The painted bands are not decoration. Aviation rules require very tall structures to be easy for pilots to see. The red and white stripes do that job in daylight. The lights do it at night.

The tower is a working piece of infrastructure, not a tourist attraction in the usual sense. There is no public observation deck, and visitors cannot simply ride to the top. So the experience here is from the ground. That is worth accepting rather than regretting. Few tall towers can be seen this honestly, without a gift shop or a queue. It is just steel, wind, and scale.

Now look at the neighbourhood around you. The tower stands close to Babyn Yar, a ravine with one of the darkest histories in Europe. In late September 1941, after German forces occupied Kyiv, Nazi units murdered tens of thousands of Jewish men, women, and children here over two days. In the months and years that followed, many more people were killed at the site, including Roma, Ukrainian civilians, and Soviet prisoners of war.

For decades in the Soviet era, the full story of Babyn Yar was not openly told. The Jewish identity of most victims was rarely named in official memorials. Today, the area holds a group of monuments and a growing memorial complex. When you visit the tower, you are standing at the edge of this landscape of memory. Many people walk from the tower into the park, and the mood changes quickly. The traffic noise fades, and the paths grow quiet.

The tower's story continued into the present war. On 1 March 2022, in the first week of Russia's full-scale invasion, a Russian strike hit the area of the tower. Several people nearby were killed. Some broadcasting was briefly interrupted. The strike also landed close to the Babyn Yar memorial, which shocked people across Ukraine and abroad. A place linked to the Holocaust had come under fire again.

The tower itself stayed standing. Broadcasts were restored, and the structure kept its place on the skyline. For many Kyivans, that matters. In the first days of the invasion, people checked the skyline and the airwaves for signs the city still functioned. Television and radio carried official news and air raid warnings. The tower was not only a symbol. It was part of how the city stayed informed.

Because of the war, this is a sensitive site. Broadcast towers are critical infrastructure. Be thoughtful about how you photograph it, and follow any instructions from police or security staff. Do not linger close to fences or service buildings. If an air raid alert sounds, move to the nearest shelter or metro station without delay. The calm of a quiet afternoon here can change, and locals take these warnings seriously.

If you want the best view of the tower's full height, step away from it. Up close, it is too tall to take in at once. From the paths in the nearby park, or from a wide street with an open line of sight, the whole frame comes into view. Late afternoon light falls on the steel at a low angle. On clear evenings, the warning lights come on one by one against a darkening sky.

It is easy to think of a TV tower as purely technical. Yet this one carries several layers at once. It is a piece of Soviet engineering ambition. It is a working transmitter. It stands beside a site of mass murder and memory. And it is a survivor of a modern attack. None of these layers cancels the others. They sit together on the same patch of ground.

Before you leave, look up one more time. Follow the lattice from the wide legs to the thin point at the top. Listen for the wind in the steel, and the sound of the city carrying on around it. As you walk away, turn back every few minutes. You will see the tower from new angles, between trees and over rooftops... still there, still sending its signal out across Kyiv.
