The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. You are standing on one of the high hills above the Dnipro River, and the air up here moves a little faster than on the streets below. Old lime trees rustle overhead. Somewhere nearby, church bells may be ringing. And just in front of you rises a pale, columned building. Behind those doors sits one of the largest collections of Ukrainian history anywhere in the world.

This is the National Museum of the History of Ukraine. It stands on what Kyivans call Starokyivska Hill, the Old Kyiv Hill. The name is not decoration. This spot sits at the heart of the earliest city. Princes lived here. Churches rose here. When you walk these paths, you are moving through the oldest layer of Kyiv. Much of it now lies under grass, stone and pavement.

Before you go inside, turn toward the open ground beside the museum. Look for the low outlines of stone foundations marked out on the earth. They trace the Church of the Tithes. Prince Volodymyr the Great built it in the late tenth century, soon after he adopted Christianity for his people. It was the first major stone church in Kyiv. Volodymyr gave it a tenth of his income — and that gift is where the church gets its name.

The church did not survive. In 1240, Mongol armies besieged Kyiv. Accounts of the siege describe people climbing onto the church and its vaults for safety. Under their weight and the attack, the structure collapsed. For centuries, only ruins remained. Today the foundations are a quiet outline in the ground. Many visitors walk straight past them on the way to the museum steps. Take a moment here. This patch of earth marks the end of an era.

Now look at the museum building itself. It dates from the late 1930s, the Soviet period. It shows the restrained classical style of that time — clean lines, tall columns and a calm, formal front. The building does not try to compete with the golden domes around the city. Instead it feels like a library or an archive. That suits it well. Its job is to hold things carefully and to keep them safe.

The museum itself is older than its building. It traces its roots to a city museum of antiquities founded at the end of the nineteenth century. Over time, it changed names, collections and roles, as the country around it changed. Empire, revolution, Soviet rule and independence each left a mark on what was collected and how it was shown. The museum is a record of history. It is also part of that history.

Inside, the collection runs into the hundreds of thousands of objects. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Only a small part is ever on display at one time. The rest waits in storage — coins, documents, textiles, weapons, tools and archaeological finds. Exhibitions change. So two visits, years apart, can feel quite different. Ask the staff what is showing now. The answer may shape how you spend your time.

The story usually begins very early, long before Kyiv existed. Look for the pottery of the Trypillia culture. These farming communities lived across much of present-day Ukraine thousands of years ago, in the Stone and Copper Ages. Their pots carry bold spirals and swirling bands painted in red, black and white. Up close, the patterns feel almost modern. The people who made them built large settlements — some among the largest in Europe at that time.

As you move forward in time, you meet the world of Kyivan Rus. This was the medieval state that grew around this very hill. Look for fragments of everyday life: jewellery, crosses, tools and pieces of buildings. Some objects come from the ground just outside these walls. Here is a second surprise. Many objects in these cases were found only a short walk from where you are standing. The city's past lies close below its streets.

Later rooms follow the Cossack era, the centuries of empire, and the upheavals of the twentieth century. Pay attention to the banners, the maces and the hand-written documents. Each one marks a moment when people argued about who would govern this land, and how. The museum does not tell one simple story. It shows many voices, often in conflict. Read the labels slowly. The small objects often say more than the grand ones.

In recent years, the museum has also faced the present. Since Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022, Ukrainian museums have protected their collections and have started to record the war as it happens. Here, that work may include objects and stories from the current conflict. Seeing them next to medieval swords and Cossack banners is a strong experience. History in this building is not finished. It is still being written, and collected, every day.

When you come back outside, walk to the edge of the hill. From here the ground drops toward Podil, the old riverside district of merchants and craftsmen. The Dnipro shines beyond the rooftops. Nearby, the blue and white Saint Andrew's Church sits high on its own ridge. At its foot, Andriivskyi Descent winds down toward Podil. This is a good place to stop, breathe, and connect what you saw inside with the real landscape.

So before you leave, look once more at the outline of the old church in the grass. Listen to the trees above you and the city sounds rising from below. Princes, monks, soldiers and ordinary families all walked across this hill. Now you are walking here too. As you go down toward the river, notice the slope under your feet. It is the same slope that the first Kyivans climbed, more than a thousand years ago.
