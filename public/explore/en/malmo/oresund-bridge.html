The guide

The story

Stand on the shore at the western edge of Malmö and listen first. Wind comes off the water in steady gusts. Small waves slap against the stones. Far out on the strait, a long grey line runs low across the sea and then simply stops, as if someone forgot to finish it. That line is the Øresund Bridge. And the place where it seems to vanish is part of the story we are about to tell.

The bridge links two countries. Behind you is Sweden, and the southern province of Skåne. Ahead, across the strait called the Øresund, is Denmark and its capital, Copenhagen. For most of history, the only way across this water was by boat. Ferries carried people, cargo, and cars back and forth for generations. Then, on the first of July in the year 2000, the crossing opened to traffic, and the journey between the two cities shrank to the length of a short train ride.

Here is the first surprise. The structure you can see is not the whole crossing. The full link runs roughly sixteen kilometres from coast to coast, and it is made of three parts. The bridge itself carries you about half of the way. Then the road and railway drop onto an island in the middle of the strait. From there, they dive under the sea into a tunnel and come up again on the Danish side, close to Copenhagen Airport.

Why not build a bridge the whole way? There were two good reasons. The first was the airport. A tall bridge near the Danish coast would sit in the path of planes coming in to land. The second was shipping. One of the busiest channels in the strait runs near Denmark, and engineers wanted to keep it open for large vessels. So the solution was to go underneath. That is why, from Malmö, the bridge appears to end in open water.

Now look at the tallest part of the bridge. Near the middle, two pairs of concrete towers rise high above the strait. From their tops, fan-like rows of steel cables reach down and hold up the deck. This design is called cable-stayed. The cables carry the weight directly to the towers, rather than hanging from a long curved main cable, as on a suspension bridge. The section between the towers is long enough to let big ships pass beneath without trouble.

The bridge also has two levels, and this is easy to miss from the shore. Cars, lorries, and buses travel on the upper deck, a motorway open to the sky. Below them, inside the steel framework, runs a double-track railway. Trains connect Malmö, Copenhagen Airport, and central Copenhagen, and many continue further along the coast in both directions. So if you hear a low rumble rolling across the water, it may well be a train passing under the traffic.

Let us return to that island in the middle. It is called Peberholm, and it did not exist before the bridge. Engineers built it from material dug out of the seabed during construction. Its name means something like pepper islet. That is a small joke with a purpose. A natural island nearby is called Saltholm, which means salt islet. So the new island was named as its partner: salt and pepper, side by side in the sea.

And here is the second surprise. Peberholm was largely left alone after construction. No one planted gardens or built homes there. Instead, nature was allowed to arrive on its own. Seeds came on the wind, on the feet of birds, and with the waves. Over the years, plants, insects, and birds have settled on the island. Scientists visit to study how life moves into a brand-new place. Ordinary visitors cannot go ashore, but every train and car passes right across it.

The bridge changed daily life in this region. Before it opened, Malmö and Copenhagen were close on the map but separated by water. Today, thousands of people cross for work, study, and shopping. Some live in Sweden and work in Denmark, or the other way around. The two cities are often described as one shared region. You may hear Danish spoken on Malmö streets, and Swedish in Copenhagen cafés. The two languages are close enough that many speakers can follow each other, with a little patience.

There is an older layer of history here, too. This province, Skåne, was part of Denmark for centuries. It became Swedish in 1658, after a peace treaty that ended a war between the two kingdoms. For a long time, the strait marked a hard line between rival powers. So a fixed link across this water carries real meaning. It joins two peoples who once fought over these very coasts.

The bridge has also found a second life on screen. A Danish and Swedish crime drama called The Bridge became popular far beyond Scandinavia. In its opening episode, a body is found on the crossing, placed exactly on the border between the two countries. Detectives from each side must work together to solve the case. The series gave many viewers abroad their first image of this grey line across the sea, often shown in cold, pale light.

If you want to see the bridge well, the Malmö shoreline gives you good choices. The long sandy beach at Ribersborg offers a wide view across the water. At its edge stands an old wooden cold-bath house on a pier, where locals swim in the sea all year and then warm up in saunas. Further south, around the Limhamn district, you come closer to the point where the bridge meets the Swedish coast. At sunset, the towers turn dark against an orange sky.

The experience of crossing is worth trying at least once. By train, the ride to Copenhagen is short and simple, and you can watch the sea flash past between the steel beams. Sit on the right side of the train when leaving Malmö for a view of the open water to the north. By car, you will see the towers rise slowly ahead of you, then the island, and then the sudden drop into the tunnel's light.

Before you leave, look out once more at the strait. Notice how the bridge sits low and level on the water for most of its length, then climbs toward the towers and falls away again. Notice the ships, the wind turbines on the horizon, and perhaps the faint shape of the Danish coast. Somewhere out there, past the point where the line disappears, another city is going about its day. And the road and rails in front of you connect the two, quietly, every minute.

Before you go

Practical tips

Watch the bridge at sunset from Ribersborg beach.
Take the train across for a quick Copenhagen day trip.
Sit on the right side leaving Malmö for sea views.
Bring a windproof layer; the shoreline is often gusty.
Carry your passport, as border checks can occur.
