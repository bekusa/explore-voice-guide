The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the edge of the moat. The water is dark and calm, and it gives back a reflection of red brick, low and heavy against the sky. Ducks drift along the bank. Somewhere behind you, traffic hums along the city streets, but here the sound fades. The walls in front of you were not built to impress a visitor. They were built to keep an army out, and then, for a long time, to keep people in.

This is Malmö Castle, known in Swedish as Malmöhus. It is one of the oldest surviving Renaissance castles in the Nordic countries. Today it holds museums, galleries and quiet courtyards. But for most of its life, this building had a harder job. It was a fortress, a seat of royal power, and a prison. To understand it, you first need to know one surprising fact about this city.

For centuries, Malmö was not Swedish at all. It was Danish. The whole southern region of Skåne, the flat farmland that surrounds the city, belonged to the Kingdom of Denmark. The first fortress on this site went up in the 1430s, under Eric of Pomerania, a king who ruled Denmark, Norway and Sweden together. His castle guarded the coast and the busy waters of the Øresund, the narrow strait between Denmark and Sweden. Ships paying tolls to the Danish crown passed close by.

That first castle did not last. In the 1530s, Denmark went through a bitter civil war, often called the Count's Feud. Malmö took the losing side, and the townspeople pulled down parts of the old fortress. When the war ended, the victorious Danish king, Christian the Third, rebuilt the castle. The new version was stronger, and it had a clear message. It watched over the coast, and it also watched over the town that had rebelled.

Look at the round towers at the corners. These are cannon towers, built for the age of gunpowder. Their thick, curved walls were designed to turn aside cannon balls rather than take the full blow. The earth ramparts and the wide moat served the same purpose. This was a castle for artillery, not for knights in armour. It is a building from the moment when warfare changed, and the shape of its walls still shows that change today.

Now for the most famous prisoner. In 1568, a Scottish nobleman arrived here as a captive. His name was James Hepburn, the Earl of Bothwell. He was the third husband of Mary, Queen of Scots. Bothwell had fled Scotland after Mary's fall from power, and his ship was stopped by Danish authorities. He was held here at Malmöhus for several years before he was moved to another prison in Denmark, where he later died.

Think about that for a moment. A man who had married a queen spent years inside these brick walls, looking out over flat Danish fields. His story is one of the strangest links between this quiet corner of southern Scandinavia and the dramatic politics of the Scottish court. Many visitors walk past the castle without knowing that this chapter of British royal history passed through Malmö.

The great turning point came in 1658. Under the Treaty of Roskilde, Denmark gave up Skåne to Sweden. Overnight, Malmö changed countries, and the castle changed owners. For a while, Denmark tried to win the region back, and the fortress saw more fighting. But Skåne stayed Swedish. Over time, the castle lost its importance as a frontier stronghold. The border had moved, and the cannons faced a sea that was no longer a front line.

So the castle found a new use. For a long period, it served as a prison. Ordinary convicts, not royal captives, filled its cells. A fire in the nineteenth century damaged parts of the buildings, and they were later repaired. When you walk through the older rooms, notice how thick the walls are, and how small some windows feel. These spaces were shaped by the need for control, not comfort.

In the twentieth century, the castle's story turned once more. The prison closed, and the buildings were restored. They became home to Malmö Museums. Today the castle holds collections on the city's history, art, and natural history. There is even an aquarium inside, which surprises many first-time visitors. Children often find the fish more exciting than the cannons. Adults sometimes agree, quietly.

The castle also played a gentle role during the Second World War. In 1945, near the end of the war, survivors released from Nazi concentration camps were brought to Sweden. Some of them were cared for in Malmö, and parts of the castle were used to receive them. It is a moving thought. A building made for war and imprisonment became, briefly, a place of shelter and recovery.

Around you, the landscape is softer than the walls. The castle sits beside green parks, with lawns, old trees and calm ponds. To one side, an old windmill rises above the grass. Paths lead along the water, and local families come here to walk, run, or sit in the sun. On a bright afternoon, the red brick glows warm, and the reflection in the moat is almost perfect.

As you leave, look back once more. See the low profile of the walls and the solid round towers. Notice how the castle seems to sit down into the earth, rather than reach for the sky. It was built to survive, and it has, through rebellion, war, a change of nations, and years as a prison. Now it simply waits beside the water, while the city moves on around it.
