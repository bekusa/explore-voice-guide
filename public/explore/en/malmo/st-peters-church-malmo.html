The guide

The story

Stand close to the outer wall and look straight up. Dark red brick rises above you in steep, narrow lines, thousands of hand-sized blocks laid one on another. Some are deep plum, some are almost orange, and a few are scorched nearly black. Behind you, the city carries on with bicycles, buses and footsteps. Step through the doors, though, and the noise falls away. The air turns cool. You may catch the smell of old wood and candle wax.

This is Saint Peter's Church, or Sankt Petri kyrka in Swedish. It is the oldest building still standing in Malmö, and it has outlived almost everything that stood around it when it was new. Work began in the early fourteenth century. At that time Malmö was a small and busy trading town on the coast. For roughly seven hundred years, merchants, fishermen, sailors and their families have come through these doors to be baptised, married and buried.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The people who built this church were not Swedish. For most of its history, Malmö and the whole southern province of Skåne belonged to Denmark. The region passed to Sweden in 1658, after a war between the two kingdoms. So a Danish town raised this church, and a Swedish city inherited it. That shift is part of the story of the building, even if the bricks show no sign of it.

Now look again at the style. This is Brick Gothic, a way of building that spread along the southern shores of the Baltic Sea in the Middle Ages. Its home was the Hanseatic League, a powerful network of merchant towns that traded across the Baltic and North Sea. Good building stone is scarce in much of this region, but clay is everywhere. So builders fired clay into bricks and pushed it as high as it would go.

The design of Saint Peter's is often linked to Saint Mary's Church in Lübeck, the leading city of the Hanseatic world. Lübeck's great church served as a model for many towns around the Baltic. Malmö's merchants traded with those cities, and they wanted a church that matched the ambition of their partners. The result is a tall central hall with lower side aisles, a plan you would recognise in several northern German ports.

Walk slowly around the outside if you have time. Gothic builders, working in the style that followed the heavier, round-arched Romanesque, wanted height and light. To support high walls with large windows, they added buttresses, thick brick piers that brace the walls from outside. Some of them reach across the air in arches toward the upper walls. These are flying buttresses. They hold the outward push of the roof, so the walls can stay thinner and the windows can stay large.

The tower has its own story, and it is not a smooth one. Medieval towers were heavy, and the ground beneath them was not always firm. According to the church's history, the tower here collapsed during the Middle Ages, more than once. Each time, the town rebuilt it. The tower you see today is much younger than the rest of the church. It dates from the late nineteenth century. So the oldest building in Malmö carries a relatively modern crown.

Inside, the first thing most people notice is the brightness. The walls and vaults are almost entirely white. That was not the original look. In the Middle Ages, the interior was covered in colour. Then came the Reformation in the sixteenth century, when the Danish church broke with Rome and became Lutheran. Many painted walls were covered with layers of white lime. The space you stand in now is calmer, simpler and full of reflected light.

That makes the next surprise even better. In one side chapel, known as the Merchants' Chapel, some of the medieval paintings survived beneath the whitewash. They were uncovered and restored in the twentieth century. Look for painted figures, patterned borders and climbing plant designs on the vaults and walls. The colours are softer now, with faded reds, ochres and greens. Still, they give you an idea of how the whole church may once have looked.

Now turn toward the east end of the church. The altarpiece rises in tiers of carved and painted wood, with columns, small figures and scenes stacked upward toward the vault. It was made in the early seventeenth century, and it is often described as one of the largest altarpieces of its kind in Northern Europe. Stand back for a moment. Its sheer height shows how the town wanted to fill this white space with colour again after the Reformation.

The pulpit is a little older, from the late sixteenth century. It is richly carved, and it stands where the preacher could be heard across the nave. After the Reformation, the spoken sermon became the heart of Lutheran worship, so pulpits grew larger and more decorated. Then look down and along the walls. You will find memorial stones and epitaphs for local families. Many of the floor slabs are worn smooth by centuries of footsteps.

Saint Peter's is not a museum. It is still a working parish church of the Church of Sweden. Services, weddings and funerals continue here, and the church often hosts concerts. If you are lucky, the organ may be playing while you walk around. The sound fills the long brick hall and seems to hang under the vaults. Please keep your voice low, and be ready to pause if a service or ceremony is taking place.

When you leave, notice how close the church sits to everyday Malmö. The central station is only a short walk away. Stortorget, the main square, is also nearby, with the narrow streets of the old town around it. Shops, cafés and canal bridges surround a building that has stood here since the Middle Ages. The city has grown up around this church, much as it grew up around the harbour.

Before you go, step outside and look back at the brick one more time. Find a single block near your eye level. Someone shaped that clay, fired it and set it in place many centuries ago, in a Danish town that traded across the Baltic. Now it stands in a Swedish city, beside traffic lights and passing bicycles. As you walk away, listen to the city noise return. Then glance back once more at the dark red walls rising above the street.
