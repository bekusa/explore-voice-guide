The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Dry leaves shift in the scrub. Somewhere close, an iguana scrapes across warm limestone. The air smells of dust and green forest, and the heat rises off the stone in slow waves. In front of you, a great tower of grey rock lifts out of the low Yucatán jungle. Its sides do not end in sharp corners. They curve. This is the Pyramid of the Magician, the tallest building at the ancient Maya city of Uxmal.

Look first at its shape, because this is the detail most visitors miss. Almost every Maya pyramid stands on a square or rectangular base. This one does not. Its base is rounded at the ends, closer to an oval than a square. Walk a little way around it and you will see the walls bend gently out of view. In a world of crisp right angles, this soft outline makes the pyramid instantly recognisable among all the ruins of the region.

Uxmal sits in the Puuc hills, a low, rolling ridge south of the modern city of Mérida. The word Puuc also names an architectural style. Puuc builders loved smooth lower walls and richly carved upper walls, made from thousands of small cut stones fitted together like a mosaic. You will see that style all around you here. The city flourished roughly between the seventh and tenth centuries, when it was one of the most powerful centres in this part of the peninsula.

Now for the name. The Spanish called this building the House of the Soothsayer, and it is also known as the Pyramid of the Dwarf. Both names come from a story. Locals still tell it, and it is legend rather than history. According to the tale, an old woman who lived near Uxmal hatched a child from an egg. The child never grew tall, but he grew very wise, and very bold.

Legend has it that the dwarf challenged the ruler of Uxmal. The ruler set him impossible tasks, hoping to crush him. In one version of the story, the ruler demanded a building taller than any in the city, finished by morning. The dwarf built this pyramid in a single night. When the people woke, the tower stood above them, and the dwarf became their new king. It is a wonderful story... and the stone itself tells a very different one.

Archaeologists have shown that the pyramid was not built in one night, or even in one generation. It grew over a long period, in several stages. Each new phase covered the older structure, like layers wrapping around a core. Researchers usually count five main temples, one placed over another, across the building's history. So when you look at this pyramid, you are looking at several buildings at once — most of them hidden inside.

That layering gives us the second surprise. Some of the older temples were not fully buried. Parts of them remain visible at different heights on the pyramid. This means the facades you see do not all match. Different builders, working in different periods, left their own style on different faces. The pyramid is less a single design and more a conversation between generations of Maya architects.

Walk around to the western side, the side that faces the large courtyard of buildings known as the Nunnery Quadrangle. Here the stairway is very steep, rising at an angle that seems almost vertical from the ground. High up, near the top, there is a temple doorway framed by a huge carved face. The open mouth of this creature forms the door itself. A person walking through it would have seemed to enter the mouth of a sacred being.

This kind of mask doorway belongs to another regional style, called Chenes, from an area to the south. Its presence here shows that the builders of Uxmal knew and borrowed from their neighbours. Across the site you will also see masks with long, curling noses. Most scholars link these to Chaac, the Maya god of rain. Look for them on corners and facades. Once you notice one, you will start to see them everywhere.

The rain god mattered enormously here. The Puuc region has no rivers on the surface, and Uxmal had no large natural sinkholes of water nearby. The people depended on rain, which they collected and stored in underground cisterns cut into the rock. A dry year could mean real hardship. So the many faces of Chaac were not decoration. They were a prayer, repeated in stone, for the water that kept the whole city alive.

You may wonder about climbing to the top. For many years visitors did climb the pyramid, but today it is closed to climbing. That protects the stone, and it protects people, because those stairs are dangerously steep. Instead, the best views often come from a short distance away. Step back toward the edge of the plaza and the whole curved mass fits into view, with the jungle stretching flat behind it.

Outsiders have been drawn here for a long time. In the 1840s, the American writer John Lloyd Stephens and the English artist Frederick Catherwood travelled through the Yucatán and recorded Uxmal in words and drawings. Their books helped introduce the Maya world to readers abroad. Much later, Uxmal was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognised as one of the finest examples of Maya architecture anywhere.

Before you move on, take one more look. Notice how the afternoon light falls differently on the rounded sides, so the shadows slide around the curve rather than breaking at a corner. Notice the iguanas resting on the warm steps, completely at home. Somewhere inside this mountain of stone, older temples still sit in the dark, sealed by the builders who came after. As you walk toward the rest of Uxmal, listen again to the quiet... and let the pyramid stay at your back a little while longer.

Practical tips
