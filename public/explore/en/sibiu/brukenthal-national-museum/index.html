The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the cobblestones of the Large Square and look at the long, pale facade in front of you. The wide arched gateway is the way in. Behind it, footsteps echo across a stone courtyard, and the noise of the square goes quiet. In this city of steep roofs and narrow lanes, the building feels formal and measured, the work of a man who knew the Habsburg court in Vienna well.

This is the Brukenthal Palace. Today it houses the main collections of the Brukenthal National Museum. The building is in the Baroque style, the ornate European style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and it went up in the late eighteenth century. It was never meant as a public gallery. It was the city residence of one man, and the art inside was his private collection. To understand the museum, you first have to meet him.

Samuel von Brukenthal was a Transylvanian Saxon. The Saxons were a German-speaking community who settled in this region during the Middle Ages and built towns like Sibiu, which they called Hermannstadt. He was a Lutheran and a lawyer, and he rose to remarkable heights. The Habsburg empress Maria Theresa trusted him, and he became Governor of Transylvania. For a Protestant in a Catholic empire, that was an unusual position to reach.

His career took him to Vienna many times, and there he became a serious collector. He bought paintings, books, coins, and minerals. He was not only decorating rooms. He was building something like an encyclopedia of European culture, here at the eastern edge of the empire. When you walk through the galleries, remember that every object first arrived here by road, over long distances, to the home of one curious man.

Here is the first surprise. Brukenthal planned for his collection to outlive him and to serve the public. After his death, his will kept the collection together, and the museum opened its doors in 1817. That makes it one of the oldest museums in this part of Europe. It is often called the first public museum on the territory of modern Romania. It opened decades before many better-known museums in western capitals.

Inside, the European art gallery is the heart of the visit. The rooms keep a sense of a private home. You will see painted ceilings, tiled stoves, polished parquet floors, and tall windows that look onto the courtyards. The paintings come from many schools: Flemish, Dutch, German, Austrian, and Italian. Flemish painting comes from the region of present-day Belgium. Many visitors expect a small provincial collection. They find something much deeper.

Look carefully for one small portrait. It shows a man in a vivid blue headdress, painted with almost impossible precision. This painting is attributed to Jan van Eyck, the fifteenth-century Flemish master who helped to develop oil painting in northern Europe. Move close and look at the stubble on the chin and the folds of the cloth. Then step back. It is a quiet, modest picture, and it is one of the treasures of the whole collection.

The second surprise is Italian. The museum holds a small Crucifixion by Antonello da Messina, a painter from Sicily who brought northern oil techniques to Italy. Behind the cross, he painted a wide, calm landscape. Few people imagine that a work by Antonello hangs in Transylvania. Take a moment with the small details in the background. They are easy to miss if you only look at the cross.

The collection has had its own troubled history. In the twentieth century, under the communist regime, some important works were moved away from Sibiu. Over time, many of them came back, and the museum returned to the care of the Lutheran Church, as Brukenthal had intended. When you walk through these rooms, you are looking at a collection that people fought to keep together.

The museum is larger than this one palace. It also includes several other branches around the city: a history museum in the old town hall, a pharmacy museum, a natural history collection, and spaces for contemporary art. In the palace itself, there is also Romanian art and Brukenthal's library, with old books and manuscripts. If you have time, these other branches show different parts of the same collector's world.

While you are inside, pay attention to the building itself. The rooms open into one another in a long line, so you can see through several doorways at once. This layout is typical of the Baroque palace. Floors creak gently underfoot. Light from the courtyards falls across the frames. The building still feels like a home, and that is part of its charm.

Sibiu itself fits this story well. In 2007 the city was a European Capital of Culture, and much of the old centre was carefully restored. The Large Square, with its pastel facades and attic windows that look like half-closed eyes, has been the civic heart of the town for centuries. The palace is not hidden away. It stands at the centre of public life, as Brukenthal wanted.

When you come back out through the gateway, stop for a moment. Look again at the facade and at the square around you. Notice the cafe tables, the church towers, and the roofs with their small watching windows. Somewhere behind you, in a quiet room, a man in a blue headdress is still looking out from his small panel... as he has looked out at visitors for more than two hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with the European art gallery while your energy is fresh.
Allow at least two unhurried hours for the palace.
Check current exhibitions before visiting, as galleries sometimes rotate.
Ask whether a combined ticket covers other museum branches.
Visit early in the day for quieter rooms.
