The guide

The story

Start with the ground beneath your feet. It is sand, fine and pale, warm even in the early morning. Now lift your eyes. Ahead of you rises a tower made of earth, the same colour as the ground, as if the desert simply decided to stand up. Wooden poles stick out from its sides like the spines of a great animal. The air smells of dust and dry wood. Somewhere nearby, a radio plays, a goat calls, and the wind moves the sand in small, patient waves.

This is the Tomb of Askia, in Gao, on the eastern bend of the Niger River in Mali. It is the burial place of Askia Mohammad, the ruler that later generations call Askia the Great. Under his rule, at the end of the fifteenth century, the Songhai Empire became one of the largest states in the history of Africa. Its lands stretched across the Sahel, the wide belt of dry grassland between the Sahara to the north and the forests to the south. And Gao was its capital.

To understand the tower, it helps to understand the man. Askia Mohammad was a military commander who took power in the 1490s. He was a devout Muslim, and soon after he came to power he made the pilgrimage to Mecca. That journey across the desert was long and dangerous, and it was also a statement. It told the wider Islamic world that Songhai was a serious, learned, and faithful kingdom. When he returned, he set about building a capital that showed the same ambition.

The tomb is the clearest result. Tradition says it was raised around the time of his return from Mecca, in the last years of the fifteenth century. Local tradition also says that some of the materials, or the inspiration for the design, came back with him from Arabia. Treat that as a story people tell with pride, not as a documented fact. What is certain is the result: a stepped, pyramid-shaped tower roughly seventeen metres tall, built almost entirely of mud brick.

Look closely at the surface. It is not smooth like concrete. It carries the marks of hands. Mud architecture in this region is a living thing. Rain softens the walls, wind wears them down, and every few years the community must replaster them with fresh earth. People mix clay, water, and other local materials, then climb the tower and spread the new layer by hand. The building survives because people return to it, again and again.

Those wooden poles have a practical job. Builders in the Sahel use them as permanent scaffolding. When the time comes to replaster, workers stand on the poles and climb the face of the structure. The beams also help the walls handle heat and movement. This style, with its tapering earthen forms and bristling timber, is often called Sudano-Sahelian architecture. You can see relatives of this tower in the great mud mosques of Timbuktu and Djenné, further up the river.

Here is something many visitors miss. The tower is not standing alone. It is the centre of a whole complex. Around it are two flat-roofed buildings that serve as a mosque, a cemetery, and an open-air space used for gatherings and prayer. This was never only a monument to one ruler. For more than five centuries, it has also been a working place of worship, where people of Gao come to pray, meet, and mark the important moments of their lives.

That living use is part of why UNESCO named the Tomb of Askia a World Heritage Site in 2004. UNESCO described it as one of the finest examples of the mud-building tradition of the West African Sahel. It is also seen as an early model for a style of mosque that spread across the region. When you look at the tower, you are looking at a design idea that travelled along trade routes and shaped buildings far from here.

Those trade routes made Gao powerful. Caravans crossed the Sahara with salt from the north. Boats moved along the Niger with grain, cloth, and gold. Scholars, merchants, and pilgrims passed through the city. Askia Mohammad used this wealth to build an administration, support Islamic learning, and connect his empire to North Africa and the Middle East. The tower stands above all of this as a kind of signature, written in earth.

The empire did not last forever. In 1591, an army from Morocco crossed the desert with firearms and defeated the Songhai forces. The great state broke apart, and Gao slowly lost its central role. Yet the tomb remained. Generations of local families continued to care for it, to pray in its mosque, and to bury their dead nearby. Empires come and go. A building made of mud survived because ordinary people decided it mattered.

In more recent years, the site has faced new pressure. Since 2012, conflict in northern Mali has placed the tomb on UNESCO's list of World Heritage in Danger. The fighting made regular maintenance harder and kept most foreign visitors away. During this period, residents of Gao worked to protect the site. That care is part of the story you are standing inside right now. The tower is not a ruin. It is a building that people chose to defend.

So take a moment to notice the details around you. The light changes the colour of the walls through the day. In the morning they look soft and pale. In the late afternoon they turn warm and golden. You might see small cracks where rain has run down the surface, or patches where newer plaster meets older layers. Each patch is a record of someone's work, a season of care added to the long life of the tower.

Before you leave, look up one more time. Follow the line of the tower from its wide base to its narrow top, against the open Sahel sky. Then look down again at the sand under your feet. It is the same material. The ruler, the builders, the worshippers, and the families who replaster these walls all worked with the earth of this place. As you walk away, listen to the sounds of Gao around you... the city that still keeps this tower standing.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current government travel advisories before planning any trip to Gao.
Travel with a trusted local guide who knows the area well.
Dress modestly, since the complex is an active place of worship.
Ask permission before photographing people praying or gathering.
Visit in late afternoon when the walls turn warm and golden.
