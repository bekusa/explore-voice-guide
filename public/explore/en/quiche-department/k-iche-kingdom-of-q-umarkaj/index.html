The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through tall pines at the edge of a high plateau. Somewhere nearby, a candle sputters in a stone hollow. The ground is uneven and grassy, with low mounds of rubble and pale plaster rising out of it. You are standing in what was once the capital of the most powerful Maya kingdom in the Guatemalan highlands. The people who built it called it Qʼumarkaj, which is often translated as the place of old reeds.

Take a moment to look at the edges of the site. The plateau ends in steep ravines on several sides, cut deep by water over thousands of years. That is the first thing to understand about this place. It was chosen for defence. An army climbing toward the city would have found very few easy ways in. The Kʼicheʼ rulers built their capital where the landscape itself did much of the work of a fortress wall.

The Kʼicheʼ are a Maya people. They still live across these highlands today, and their language is one of the most widely spoken Mayan languages in Guatemala. In the centuries before Europeans arrived, roughly the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the Kʼicheʼ expanded their rule over neighbouring peoples. Qʼumarkaj became the political and religious heart of that expansion. Several noble lineages governed together here, each with its own great houses around the central plaza.

Now turn toward the largest mound you can see near the plaza. This is generally identified as the temple of Tohil, the patron god of the Kʼicheʼ ruling lineages. Tohil was linked with fire and with the sun's power. What you see today is a worn core of stone and mortar. Try to imagine it complete. It would have been a stepped pyramid with a stairway climbing to a shrine at the summit, its surfaces covered in smooth plaster and paint.

Around the plaza stood other temples, dedicated to other patron deities of the founding lineages, such as Awilix and Jakawitz. There was also a ball court. The Mesoamerican ball game was far more than sport. Players kept a heavy rubber ball moving using their hips, and the game carried meaning about the cosmos, life and death. As you walk, look for the long, parallel lines of stone that mark where a ball court once stood.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The most famous book of Maya mythology is tied to this kingdom. It is called the Popol Vuh, often translated as the Book of the Council or the Book of the Community. It tells of the creation of the world, of hero twins who outwit the lords of the underworld, and of the origins of the Kʼicheʼ lineages. Its final sections trace the rulers who governed from this very city.

The Popol Vuh survives because Kʼicheʼ writers set it down in their own language, using the Latin alphabet, in the sixteenth century, after the Spanish conquest. Later, a Spanish friar named Francisco Ximénez copied that text in the nearby town of Chichicastenango, and his copy is the version that reached the world. So the stories you may know from that book were preserved by the descendants of the people who once walked this plaza.

The city's end came in 1524. Spanish forces under Pedro de Alvarado marched into the highlands with allied warriors from central Mexico. Those allies spoke Nahuatl, the language of the Aztec world, and they gave the city another name, Utatlán. You will still see that name on some signs and maps. After a series of battles, the Kʼicheʼ invited the Spanish into the capital. Alvarado suspected a trap.

According to the Spanish accounts, Alvarado seized the Kʼicheʼ rulers and had them burned, and then he set fire to the city. Qʼumarkaj never recovered as a capital. In the years that followed, the Spanish founded a new colonial town nearby, the place now called Santa Cruz del Quiché. Much of the city's cut stone was carried away over time. That is one reason the ruins today look so low and so broken.

Now for the second surprise. This is not a dead site. Look for the tunnel opening below the plaza, a dark passage cut into the earth. Inside, people still burn candles, incense and offerings. Kʼicheʼ spiritual guides, known as daykeepers or ajqʼij, keep the sacred Maya calendar and lead ceremonies here. The walls of the tunnel are black with the soot of countless fires. If a ceremony is happening, keep your distance, stay quiet, and do not take photographs without permission.

You may also see small fire altars on the open ground around the ruins, rings of ash and melted wax. Some people come with flowers, sugar, alcohol, or bundles of copal, a fragrant tree resin used as incense. Its smoke is thick and sweet. These ceremonies may mark a day in the calendar, a request for healing, or thanks for a harvest. For many families, this place connects the living to their ancestors in a direct, physical way.

If there is a small museum at the entrance, it is worth a few minutes before or after your walk. A model or a reconstruction drawing can help you picture the city at its height, when the plaza was crowded and the temples were bright with colour. Then come back out and stand among the mounds again. The gap between that image and the quiet grass around you is part of what makes this place so powerful.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the plaza. Notice the smell of pine and smoke. Notice how the ravines fall away beyond the trees. Five centuries ago, this was a crowded capital of lords, priests and traders. Today it is grass, stone and candle wax... and still, people come here to pray. As you walk back toward the entrance, listen for the wind in the pines. It is the same sound the city heard.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stay well back if a ceremony is underway in the tunnel.
Ask permission before photographing anyone performing a ritual.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, grassy ground around the mounds.
Bring a light torch if you plan to peer into the tunnel.
Visit the site museum first to picture the complete city.
