The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wind there is a low, steady boom. It comes from below your feet, where the Atlantic strikes the base of the cliff. The ground here is grey limestone, cracked into slabs and fissures, with small flowers growing in the gaps. Salt is in the air. And ahead of you, a curve of stacked stone rises out of the rock. This is Dún Aonghasa, a prehistoric fort on the island of Inis Mór, at the very edge of Europe.

Inis Mór is the largest of the three Aran Islands, which lie across the mouth of Galway Bay. It is an Irish-speaking island, so you will hear Irish in the shops, on the minibuses, and in the pubs. The landscape is unlike most of Ireland. There is little soil, almost no trees, and thousands of kilometres of dry-stone walls. Families built these walls over generations as they cleared stones from small fields. The fort uses the same basic method, but it is far, far older.

The walk up from the visitor centre at Cill Mhuirbhigh is part of the experience. The path climbs gently at first, then turns rough and uneven near the top. You walk over bare limestone, worn smooth in some places and sharp in others. Take your time here. With each step, the walls of the fort grow larger. Then the sky opens behind them... and you realise that there is nothing behind the fort except air and ocean.

So what exactly are you looking at? Dún Aonghasa is a series of stone enclosures, one inside the other. The outer walls sweep across the hillside in wide arcs. The innermost enclosure sits right at the cliff edge. Every wall is dry stone. That means no mortar holds it together — only careful placement and the weight of the stones themselves. The builders chose flat limestone from the island and fitted it piece by piece.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. This fort is much older than the castles and monasteries of medieval Ireland. Archaeological excavations in the 1990s found evidence that people were living and working on this hilltop in the Late Bronze Age, roughly three thousand years ago. Later generations rebuilt and extended the walls during the Iron Age. So the structure you see is not the work of one moment. It is the result of many centuries of building, repair, and change.

Those excavations also found traces of daily life inside the walls. Archaeologists recovered evidence of metalworking, including fragments of clay moulds used to cast bronze objects. There were also remains of simple houses and animal bones from meals. This suggests that the fort was not only a place of defence. It may have been a centre of power, craft, and gathering, where important people lived or met. Exactly how it was used is still debated by scholars.

Between two of the outer walls, look for a wide band of sharp stones set upright in the ground. They stand close together, pointing in every direction, like a field of stone blades. This is called a chevaux-de-frise, a defensive barrier that made it slow and dangerous to approach on foot. This type of defence is rare in Ireland. Walking through the narrow path that crosses it today, you can imagine how difficult it once was to cross quickly.

Now step through the low entrance into the inner enclosure. The walls here are thick and tall, much taller than a person. Look closely at their inner face. You will see terraces, like wide steps, built into the stone, and narrow stairways that lead up to them. People could stand on these levels and look down into the enclosure. Some writers have suggested they worked almost like seats around a stage, though this is only one interpretation.

And then there is the edge. The inner enclosure ends abruptly at a sheer drop of roughly one hundred metres to the sea. There is no railing. The second surprise is that some archaeologists think the fort was not always shaped like this. Over thousands of years the waves have eaten into the cliff, and part of the original enclosure may have fallen into the ocean. Others argue it was always built against the edge. Nobody knows for certain.

The name Dún Aonghasa means the fort of Aonghus. According to Irish legend, Aonghus was a leader of the Fir Bolg, a mythical people who arrived in Ireland long ago in the old stories. Locals and storytellers have connected the fort with him for centuries. But this is folklore, not documented history. The Bronze Age builders left no written record, and we do not know what they called this place, or what language they spoke.

Some of what you see today is also the result of repair. In the late nineteenth century, the state took the fort into care and restored sections of the walls, which had partly collapsed. If you look carefully, you may notice that some stretches of stone look neater and more regular than others. This restoration helped save the monument, but it also means that not every stone sits exactly where its prehistoric builders placed it.

Before you leave, find a safe spot well back from the edge and simply look outward. On a clear day you can follow the coastline of the island, the patchwork of tiny stone fields, and sometimes the mountains of Connemara and the hills of Clare across the water. Seabirds ride the wind below the cliff top. Lie flat on the rock if you want to look over — many visitors do, and it is far safer than standing.

As you walk back down the hill, turn around once or twice. The fort will shrink against the sky until it looks like part of the rock itself. Notice the walls of the fields beside the path, built by island families with the same stones and the same patience. Three thousand years separate those two kinds of wall. Yet the wind, the grey limestone, and the sound of the sea are the same ones the builders knew.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the rough limestone path uphill.
Bring a windproof layer, even on sunny days.
Keep well back from the unfenced cliff edge.
Lie flat on the rock to look over safely.
Arrive early or late to avoid ferry crowds.
