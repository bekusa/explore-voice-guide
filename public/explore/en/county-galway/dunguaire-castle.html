The guide

The story

Listen first. The water of Galway Bay laps against the rocks just below the walls, slow and steady. When the tide is out, the air carries the sharp, salty smell of seaweed drying on the stones. Gulls call over the shallows. And there, on its small rocky point at the edge of Kinvara Bay, stands a grey stone tower. It looks less like a fortress and more like something that has grown out of the shore itself.

This is Dunguaire Castle. You are standing on the southeastern edge of Galway Bay, a short walk from the village of Kinvara. To the south, the bare limestone hills of the Burren begin to rise. The castle is a tower house, a type of fortified home that Irish families built in large numbers in the late Middle Ages. Hundreds of them still stand across the country. Few have a setting quite like this one.

The tower you see dates from the early sixteenth century. It was built by the Hynes family, a local clan whose name in Irish is Ó hEidhin. But the name of the place is much older than the stones. Dún Guaire means the fort of Guaire. It refers to Guaire Aidne, a seventh-century king of Connacht, the western province of Ireland. Tradition holds that his royal fort stood on or near this spot, centuries before the present tower existed.

King Guaire is remembered above all for one quality... generosity. Irish storytellers made him a model of the open-handed ruler. One old tale says he gave away so much that his right arm, the arm he gave with, grew longer than his left. It is folklore, of course. But it tells you what people valued in a king. In early Ireland, a ruler who hoarded food and wealth lost respect. A ruler who shared it earned loyalty and fame.

Here is the best-known legend of all. According to the story, Guaire's cousin, Saint Colman, was fasting as a hermit in the Burren hills to the south. At Easter, the saint's servant was weak with hunger. Legend has it that the dishes of the king's Easter feast lifted from the table here, and flew across the countryside to the hungry hermit. Locals still point to a path in the Burren they call the Road of the Dishes.

Now look at the tower itself. Tower houses were built for a world of local raids and cattle feuds, not great sieges. The idea was simple — stack the rooms vertically, so the family could defend a narrow space. The stores sat low. The living quarters sat higher, where the windows could be larger and safer. A tight stone stair connects the floors. Narrow openings in the walls let in a little light, and let defenders watch the approaches.

Around the tower runs a lower defensive wall, enclosing a small yard. In Ireland this kind of enclosure is called a bawn. In times of trouble, it gave the household a protected space for animals and people. Notice how the castle uses the shore. The water guards one side naturally. From here, the Hynes family could watch the bay and the land route at the same time. That mattered on a coast where trade and danger often arrived by boat.

The castle did not stay with the Hynes family forever. Over the following centuries it passed to the Martyn family, landowners from Tulira, further inland. Like many tower houses, it slowly fell out of use as a home and drifted toward ruin. Then, in the early twentieth century, Dunguaire found a new life. It became connected with one of the most creative periods in Irish history — the Irish literary revival.

Edward Martyn, from that same family, was one of the founders of the movement that led to the Abbey Theatre in Dublin. And in the 1920s, the castle was bought by Oliver Saint John Gogarty. He was a surgeon, a poet, a senator, and a famous wit. Here is a detail most visitors miss. Gogarty is the real man behind Buck Mulligan, the mocking character who opens James Joyce's novel Ulysses. The man in the very first line of that book once owned this tower.

Gogarty wanted the castle as a retreat, and the literary revival came with him. Writers of that circle, including W. B. Yeats and Lady Gregory, knew this corner of Galway well. Lady Gregory's home at Coole Park lay not far away, and Yeats had his own tower house nearby, at Ballylee. So when you stand here, you are inside a small landscape of poets, playwrights, and stone towers — a whole generation of Irish writing within an easy drive.

Later in the twentieth century, the castle was bought by Christabel, Lady Ampthill, who carried out major restoration work. Thanks to that care, the tower you see today has floors, roofs, and rooms again, not just empty walls. Since then, Dunguaire has been run as a heritage site. In the summer months, it is known for its medieval-style banquets. Guests eat by candlelight in the old hall, and actors perform songs, poems, and stories from Irish literature.

There is a nice symmetry in that. A place named after a king famous for feeding people now hosts evening feasts. And a tower once owned by a poet now fills with poetry read aloud. If you go inside, take the stairs slowly. The steps are worn and uneven, and they turn tightly. At the top, step out and look across the water. On a clear day, the grey-blue line of the Burren and the open bay lie before you.

Before you leave, walk a little way along the shore toward Kinvara. Turn back and look at the tower from the water's edge. This is the view that fills so many postcards, and it is best when the tide is high and the castle doubles itself in the bay. Listen again to the water against the rocks. It is the same sound Guaire's people heard, and the Hynes family, and Gogarty's guests... and now you.
