The guide

The story

Listen first. A sheep calls somewhere up the slope. Water moves against the shore of the fjord. Under your feet the grass is short and springy, cropped close by animals, and here and there it lifts over a low line of stone. Those lines are not random. They are the outlines of buildings that stood here eight and nine hundred years ago. You are standing at Garðar, once the church capital of Norse Greenland, in the small village now called Igaliku.

It helps to picture where you are on the map. This is the far south of Greenland, in the region of Kujalleq, on a narrow neck of land between two arms of the sea. Ice caps lie inland. Mountains rise steeply on every side. Yet this patch of ground is green, sheltered, and gentle. Look at the meadows around you and you can understand quickly why medieval farmers chose this exact spot. It is some of the best grazing land in the whole country.

Those farmers came from Iceland. Near the end of the tenth century, Norse settlers followed Erik the Red west across the Atlantic and built farms along these southern fjords. Historians call this area the Eastern Settlement, even though it lies on the southwest coast. The name simply separated it from a second, smaller colony further north. For around four hundred years, these communities raised cattle, sheep, and goats, hunted seals and walrus, and traded with Norway and Europe.

Garðar appears in the old Icelandic sagas. In the Saga of the Greenlanders, this farm is the home of Freydís, a daughter of Erik the Red, a strong and difficult figure in the stories of voyages to North America. Remember that the sagas were written down long after the events. They mix history, family memory, and storytelling. So treat Freydís here as a figure of literature first, and a historical person only with caution.

The great change at Garðar came in the early twelfth century. Greenland received its own bishop, and this farm became his seat. According to a saga called the Tale of Einar Sokkason, the Greenlanders sent a man to Norway to ask the king for a bishop. The story says he carried a live polar bear as a gift. It is a good tale, but it is a tale. What is clear is that a bishop arrived, and Garðar became the religious centre of the colony.

Now look for the largest outline in the grass. This was the cathedral, dedicated to Saint Nicholas, the patron of sailors. Its walls were built from local stone, including blocks of red sandstone, and it was by far the biggest church in Norse Greenland. Today only the foundations remain, low and level with the turf. Walk slowly along them. You can still trace the long nave, the narrower chancel at the east end, and small side chapels.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the 1920s, Danish archaeologists excavated this site carefully. Inside the church they found a grave with a skeleton, a bishop's staff with a head carved from walrus ivory, and a gold ring. The burial is often linked to one of the medieval bishops, though nobody can be completely certain which one. Close to the church, excavators also found walrus skulls buried in the ground. Walrus ivory was Greenland's most valuable export, and these finds hint at how closely faith and trade belonged together here.

Beyond the church, look for the other ruins spread across the slope. There was a bishop's residence, a large hall, storehouses, and barns. One of the cattle byres was exceptionally large, with space for many animals through the long winter. Cattle were a sign of wealth in Norse society. A bishop with a big herd was a powerful man. Some of the stone stalls and partitions are still visible, and you can imagine the warmth of the animals in the dark months.

Think about the scale of this place in its time. The bishop collected church taxes, often paid in goods such as walrus ivory, sealskins, and wool cloth. Ships from Norway arrived only occasionally, sometimes with years between them. Every letter, every piece of church silver, every bell had to cross the open North Atlantic. Garðar was a small community. But it was connected, slowly and with great effort, to the courts and churches of Europe.

Then the colony ended. By the fifteenth century the Norse settlements in Greenland had disappeared. The last clear written record from the Eastern Settlement describes a wedding at Hvalsey church in 1408. Researchers still debate the reasons. A cooling climate, falling trade, isolation, and changes in hunting likely all played a part. No single dramatic event explains it. The farms were simply left, and the grass grew back over the walls.

The second surprise is all around you. Look at the older houses of Igaliku. Several are built from neat blocks of red sandstone. Much of that stone came from the Norse ruins. The modern village began in the late eighteenth century, when a Norwegian trader and his Greenlandic wife settled here and started farming again. Their descendants reused the medieval stones. So the old cathedral did not vanish completely. Part of it lives on in the walls of family homes.

In 2017, this landscape became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site called Kujataa. The listing covers several farming areas in southern Greenland. It recognises two stories together: the Norse farmers of the Middle Ages, and the Inuit and Greenlandic farmers of more recent times. Sheep farming still continues here today. When you see hay fields and fences around the village, you are looking at the same kind of work that brought people to this valley a thousand years ago.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the cathedral foundations. Look up at the mountains above the fjord, and then down at the stone lines in the grass. Notice how quiet it is, and how the light moves slowly across the slope. As you walk back through the village, glance at the red stones in the house walls. Each one has been handled twice — once by medieval builders, and once by the people who live here now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear waterproof boots; the meadows are often wet underfoot.
Stay on paths and never climb on the ruined walls.
Bring layers, as fjord weather changes quickly here.
Respect fenced fields; sheep farming is active in the village.
Walk the full site slowly to trace each building outline.
