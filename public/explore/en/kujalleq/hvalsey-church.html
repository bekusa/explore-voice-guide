The guide

The story

Listen first. There is the wind coming off the fjord, the soft clink of water against stones on the shore, and perhaps the call of a gull somewhere above the hillside. Under your boots the grass is thick and springy, with small hummocks that hide old foundations. In front of you stands a stone building with no roof, open to the sky. Its walls are grey and rough, patched with orange and pale green lichen. This is Hvalsey Church, and it has stood here, roofless, for more than five hundred years.

You are in southern Greenland, in the region now called Kujalleq, not far from the town of Qaqortoq. The name Hvalsey is Old Norse, the language of the medieval Scandinavians. It is usually translated as Whale Island, probably after a nearby island in the fjord. Most visitors arrive by boat, and that is fitting. For the people who built this church, the fjords were the roads. Every stone, every visitor, and every piece of news came across the water.

The builders were Norse settlers, the descendants of people who sailed from Iceland in the late tenth century. According to the Icelandic sagas, a man called Erik the Red led the first colonists here. They found green valleys at the heads of the fjords, protected from the open sea. They brought cattle, sheep, and goats, and they farmed this land for roughly four centuries. This part of Greenland became known as the Eastern Settlement, and it was the larger of the two Norse communities on the island.

Now look closely at the walls. The church is built from local fieldstone, fitted carefully together. Some of the blocks are very large, and you can see how the masons placed the biggest stones low and the smaller ones higher up. Scholars generally date the church to around the early fourteenth century. It is the best-preserved Norse ruin anywhere in Greenland. Both gable ends still rise to a point, and that shape tells you where the steep roof once sat.

Here is something many visitors miss. The building is small, but it is not simple. Look at the window openings and the doorways. Some have carefully shaped tops, and the walls are thick enough to keep out a Greenland winter. This was not a rough shelter. It was a statement of belonging to Christian Europe, built at the far western edge of the known world. The style follows churches in Norway and Scotland of the same period, so the builders clearly knew those traditions well.

The church did not stand alone. Walk around the site and you will notice low outlines in the grass. These are the remains of a large farm. Archaeologists have identified a great hall, storehouses, and byres, which are barns for cattle. The people who lived here were probably among the wealthier families of the settlement. In a place where winter fodder decided who survived, owning many animals meant power. The church sat at the heart of their estate, a sign of both faith and status.

And now the most famous moment in this ruin's story. In September 1408, a wedding took place here. A man named Thorstein Olafsson married a woman named Sigrid Bjornsdottir. We know this because Icelandic letters written a few years later confirm the marriage, and those documents survive. It sounds like a small, private event. But historians treat it as something much larger, because this wedding is the last written record we have of the Norse in Greenland.

Think about that for a moment. Guests gathered inside these walls, perhaps with candles, perhaps with the smell of wool, smoke, and seal oil in the air. The couple had arrived from Iceland, and they later returned there. After that... the written sources fall silent. No more letters, no more reports. Within the next few decades, the Norse settlements in Greenland appear to have come to an end. Nobody wrote down how or why.

The same Icelandic sources record a darker event from around that time. A man was burned at the stake here, accused of using witchcraft to seduce a married woman. The woman later lost her mind, according to the account, and died. It is a grim detail, and it reminds us that this was a real community, with real quarrels and harsh medieval justice. It was not a peaceful postcard. It was a society under pressure, far from the rest of Europe.

So what happened to the Norse Greenlanders? There is no single answer, and historians still debate it. The climate grew colder in this period, which made farming harder. Trade with Norway declined, and the ships came less often. European demand for walrus ivory, one of Greenland's main exports, also seems to have fallen. The Inuit, whose ancestors had moved into this region, adapted far better to the changing conditions. Most likely, several of these pressures worked together. Some settlers may simply have left.

In 2017, this site became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site called Kujataa. The listing covers farming landscapes in this region, both Norse and Inuit. That is worth remembering as you stand here. The story of this place does not end with the ruin. Sheep farming returned to southern Greenland in the twentieth century, and Greenlandic farmers work the land in these valleys today. The green fields around the fjords are, once again, in use.

Before you leave, step inside the walls if the site allows it, and stand where the floor once was. Look up. Instead of rafters, you see open sky and moving clouds. Then look out through one of the window openings toward the water. That view, the fjord and the hills beyond, is almost exactly what the people at that 1408 wedding would have seen. The grass and the weather have changed little in six centuries.

As you walk back toward the shore, notice how quiet it is. Only the wind, the water, and your own footsteps on the grass. Look back once at the grey gables against the hillside. They have outlasted the people who built them, the trade routes that supplied them, and the language that named them. And still they stand at the edge of the fjord, holding the shape of a church that no longer has a roof.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange a boat trip from Qaqortoq, the nearest town.
Bring windproof layers, as fjord weather changes quickly.
Wear waterproof boots for the soft, uneven grassy ground.
Walk the farm outlines after viewing the church itself.
Stay on the grass and never climb the fragile walls.
