The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The noise of Hyderabad, the horns and the traffic and the voices of the old city, has faded behind you. Up here the air moves more freely. Gravel crunches under your feet, and ahead of you a long pale facade rises against the sky. The light falls on white stucco, on columns, on rows of tall windows. It is quiet in a way that the streets below never are.

The name gives you a clue to this feeling. Falaknuma is usually translated from Urdu as "like the sky" or "mirror of the sky." The palace sits on a hilltop south of the old city, and its builders wanted it to seem to float above everything around it. On a clear day the city spreads out below in a haze of rooftops and minarets. Somewhere down there is the Charminar, the great four-arched gateway at the heart of old Hyderabad.

The palace was built in the late nineteenth century for Nawab Sir Viqar-ul-Umra. He was a nobleman of the Paigah family, one of the most powerful houses in the princely state of Hyderabad, and he served as Prime Minister to the Nizam. The Nizam was the hereditary ruler of Hyderabad, and for a time one of the wealthiest men in the world. Work began in 1884, and the palace took close to a decade to finish.

The design came from an English architect, and you can see it in every line. This is not a Mughal palace of domes and arches. It is a European dream placed on an Indian hill. Italian classical columns stand beside touches of English Tudor style. Inside, much of the floor and staircase is Italian marble. Even the furniture, the clocks, and the chandeliers were brought across oceans from Europe.

Here is the first detail that most visitors miss. If you could look down on this building from above, you would see the shape of a scorpion. The main block forms the head and body. Two long wings curve out on either side like the claws, or, in some descriptions, the sting. Locals say the shape was chosen on purpose. Nobody can quite agree on why, and it is best treated as one of the palace's stories rather than settled fact.

Another story follows the money. Building something this grand was ruinously expensive, even for a Prime Minister. According to a popular tale, Viqar-ul-Umra spent so much that he fell deep into debt. Then the sixth Nizam, Mahboob Ali Khan, came to stay. He liked the palace so much that he stayed far longer than planned. In the end, in the late 1890s, the palace passed into the Nizam's hands. Whether it was a gift, a sale, or a graceful way out of debt depends on who tells the story.

From then on, Falaknuma became a royal guest house. Its rooms received visiting princes, British officials, and important guests of the Nizams. Imagine the carriages coming up the winding drive at dusk, the lamps lit along the terraces, and the music drifting out from the halls. For a few decades this hilltop was one of the social centres of a kingdom about the size of a European country.

Now the second surprise. Somewhere inside is a dining table built to seat 101 guests at once. It is one of the longest dining tables of its kind anywhere. Diners sat on carved chairs along its length, and a single meal here was a carefully staged performance. It is said that the seating was arranged so that guests could hear one another along the whole table. Today the table is still laid, and it remains one of the rooms people most want to see.

The library is another quiet wonder. It is often said to be modelled on the library at Windsor Castle in England. The ceiling is carved from walnut wood, and the shelves hold a large collection of books and manuscripts. Stand in a room like this and think about what it meant. A noble in the Deccan, in southern India, was building a copy of an English royal library to show that his court belonged in the same world.

After the age of the Nizams ended, the palace slowly fell quiet. For many years it was closed and largely unused. Dust settled on the chandeliers, and the gardens grew wild. Then, in the early twenty-first century, the Taj hotel group began a long and careful restoration in partnership with the Nizam's family. The work took about a decade. The palace reopened as a luxury hotel in 2010, with much of its original furniture and decoration brought back to life.

This means that Falaknuma is not a museum in the usual sense. It is a working hotel, and its halls are kept for guests. Visitors who are not staying here can usually join a guided heritage tour or reserve a meal or afternoon tea. Either way, you will need to plan ahead and book. The reward is the chance to walk the marble corridors with a guide who can open doors that are otherwise closed.

As you move through the rooms, notice the mix of East and West. A European oil painting may hang above Indian textiles. A Venetian glass chandelier lights a hall where Hyderabadi dishes were once served. This blend was the whole point. The Nizams' Hyderabad sat between worlds, Persian and Indian, Mughal and British. The palace shows that position clearly, room after room, almost like a statement in stone and glass.

Before you leave, pause on the terrace or at the top of the drive. Look down again at the city below. The old Hyderabad of the Nizams is still down there, in its markets and mosques and crowded lanes. Up here it is quiet, and the wind moves through the palms. Listen to the gravel under your feet as you walk away, and look back once at the long white facade, standing high above the city like a ship that never left harbour.
