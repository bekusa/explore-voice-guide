The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond these walls, the old city of Hyderabad is roaring: scooter horns, traders calling out, the hum of a thousand small engines. Then you pass through the gateway, and the noise drops away. What replaces it is water moving in a long rectangular pool, the scrape of sandals on stone, and birds in the trees. The light here is soft and wide. It falls across cream-coloured walls and pale arches. You have stepped into the private world of the Nizams of Hyderabad.

The name gives you a clue. Chowmahalla comes from Persian and Urdu words. Chow means four, and mahalat means palaces. So this is, quite literally, the place of four palaces. For generations it was the formal seat of the Asaf Jahi dynasty, the rulers known as the Nizams. They governed one of the largest and wealthiest princely states in India. This complex was where their power was put on display, for guests, for officials, and for the city itself.

The palace did not appear all at once. Work is generally traced to the mid-eighteenth century, in the time of Nizam Salabat Jung. But much of what you see took shape a century later, under Afzal ud-Daulah, the fifth Nizam, in the eighteen-fifties and eighteen-sixties. That long timeline matters. It explains why the buildings around you do not all share one style. Each ruler added rooms, courtyards, and tastes of his own.

There is a story often told about the inspiration for this place. Many accounts say the complex was modelled on the palace of the Shah in Tehran, in Persia. That connection makes sense. Persian language and Persian court culture shaped the Nizams' world for centuries. Yet as you walk, notice how the design blends many sources. You will see Persian ideas of courtyards and water, Indian craftsmanship, and later, clear European touches in columns and windows.

The complex divides into two main parts, a northern courtyard and a southern courtyard. The southern side holds the four palaces that give the place its name. They are Afzal Mahal, Mahtab Mahal, Tahniyat Mahal, and Aftab Mahal. Mahtab means moon, and Aftab means sun. The grandest is Aftab Mahal, a two-storey building with a neoclassical face, meaning it borrows the columns and calm symmetry of ancient Greek and Roman design. Stand back from it, and see how it closes the view.

Now turn toward the heart of the whole complex: the Khilwat Mubarak. This was the great Durbar Hall, the ceremonial throne room. Here, Nizams held court, received honoured visitors, and marked important occasions. Inside, a raised marble platform once held the royal seat, known as the Takht-e-Nishan. Look up. Glittering chandeliers of Belgian crystal hang from the ceiling. When they catch the light, the room fills with small, scattered points of brightness, like frost on glass.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. Above one of the gateways sits an old clock, often called the Khilwat clock. Local tradition holds that it has kept time for well over two centuries, wound and cared for by the same kind of patient hands across many generations. Whether or not every year of that claim is exact, it is a lovely thought. While the world outside changed completely, this clock simply continued counting the hours.

For much of the twentieth century, the palace faced a slow decline. After Hyderabad joined independent India, the old court life faded, and grand buildings like this are expensive to keep. Plaster cracked. Rooms closed. Then, in the early two-thousands, a careful restoration began, supported by the family of Prince Mukarram Jah, the heir of the last ruling Nizam. The work was done with patience, using traditional materials where possible. That effort later earned recognition from UNESCO for heritage conservation.

The restoration also opened the palace to the public for the first time in its long history. Think about that for a moment. For most of its life, ordinary people of Hyderabad would never have stood where you are standing now. These courtyards were reserved for nobles, officials, and invited guests. Today, schoolchildren, families, and travellers from every continent walk the same paths. In a quiet way, the palace has changed from a closed court into a shared memory.

Inside several of the rooms, you will find displays of objects from court life. There are old photographs, robes and textiles, weapons, glassware, and household items. Take time with the photographs especially. They show faces, ceremonies, and courtyards full of people, and they help these empty rooms come back to life. Notice the formal poses and layered clothing. They tell you how seriously appearance and ritual were taken here.

And then there are the cars. The family collection includes vintage vehicles, among them an early Rolls-Royce Silver Ghost, kept in remarkable condition. It may seem strange to find motorcars in a palace of chandeliers and courtyards. But the Nizams were famous for their wealth, and they embraced modern luxuries as eagerly as old traditions. Standing beside these gleaming machines, you can feel the meeting point of two eras: the age of horses and the age of engines.

As you move around, pay attention to the gardens and the long pools between the buildings. Water was never just decoration in this kind of design. In hot weather, it cooled the air and calmed the mind. It also mirrored the white façades, doubling the architecture without adding a single stone. Find a spot at the edge of a pool, wait for the surface to settle, and watch the palace appear upside down beneath your feet.

Before you leave, pause in one of the courtyards and let the details settle. Feel the smooth stone under your feet, the warmth held in the walls, the shade under the arches. Somewhere outside, the old city is still roaring, and soon you will rejoin it. But for now, listen again to the water and the birds. When you walk back through the gateway, glance up at that old clock... still counting, as it has for so long.
