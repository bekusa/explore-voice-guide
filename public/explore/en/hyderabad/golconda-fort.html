The guide

The story

Start with your hands. Rest a palm against the granite near the entrance and feel how warm it is, even early in the day. The stone is rough, grey and ochre, patched with lichen. Above you, a hill of boulders rises in rough steps, and walls follow every fold of it. Pigeons move in and out of the old arches. Somewhere ahead, a guide claps, and the sound goes up the hill.

This is Golconda, on the western edge of Hyderabad. Today the city has grown around it, with traffic and apartment blocks near the outer walls. For a long time, though, Golconda was the city. Its kings ruled a large part of the Deccan, the high plateau of southern India. Their palaces, mosques, treasuries and gardens stood on and around this hill.

The fort is older than its famous rulers. Historians trace its origins to a mud fortification from the era of the Kakatiyas, a Telugu dynasty of the medieval Deccan. Later it passed to the Bahmani sultans, the first major Muslim kingdom of the region. When that kingdom broke apart in the early sixteenth century, a governor named Sultan Quli declared his independence here. He founded the Qutb Shahi dynasty, and Golconda became its capital.

Over the next several generations, the Qutb Shahi kings rebuilt the mud fort in stone. They raised thick walls, bastions for cannon, and heavy gates. The whole complex grew in rings, one defensive line inside another. As you walk uphill, you pass through these layers. The lower areas held soldiers, stables, storerooms and ordinary homes. Higher up stood the buildings of the court. At the very top was the citadel, the Bala Hisar.

Now, a small surprise at the main gate. Look up at the great wooden doors of the Fateh Darwaza, the Victory Gate. They carry large iron spikes. These were not decoration. In an age of war elephants, an enemy could drive an elephant against a gate to break it open. The spikes made that painful and dangerous. The gateway also turns at an angle, so an attacking force could not gain speed on a straight run.

Just inside this entrance is the place most visitors remember. Under the domed space near the gate, people stand and clap their hands. Legend has it that a clap here could be heard at the Bala Hisar, high on the summit. Locals say it worked as an alarm system, warning the court of visitors or danger. Try it yourself. Whether or not the sound carries all the way up, the echo inside the dome is sharp and clear.

The climb to the top takes a while, so go slowly. The path is uneven stone, with steps of different heights. As you rise, notice the channels and the remains of old water systems along the walls. The builders brought water up this dry granite hill and stored it in tanks. On a hill under siege, water meant survival. Some of these channels still trace their course through the ruins.

Golconda's name travelled far beyond India, mostly because of diamonds. The diamond mines lay in the region to the east, along the Krishna River. Golconda was the great market and trading centre for those stones. European travellers wrote about the diamond trade here, and for centuries the word Golconda meant fabulous wealth. Several of the most famous diamonds in history, including the Koh-i-Noor, are traditionally linked to the mines of this region.

Here is a second surprise. Near the upper levels you will find a small, dark chamber known as Ramdas's prison. According to a popular story, a Qutb Shahi official named Ramadas used state revenue to build a temple to Lord Rama at Bhadrachalam, a town on the Godavari River. He was imprisoned here as punishment. Devotees say he carved images of gods on the wall of his cell. The tale is part history, part devotion, and many pilgrims treat the cell with real reverence.

At the summit, the view opens in every direction. You can see the spread of modern Hyderabad, and the domes of the Qutb Shahi tombs a short distance away, where the dynasty's kings lie buried. Up here stands a small mosque. Close by is a temple to the goddess Mahakali, also called Jagadamba. Each year, the Hindu festival of Bonalu begins at this temple. Women carry decorated pots of cooked rice and offerings up the hill to the goddess.

In the late sixteenth century, the Qutb Shahi kings began to build a new city a few kilometres to the east. That city became Hyderabad, with the Charminar at its heart. Golconda remained the fortified stronghold. Its end came in 1687, when the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb besieged the fort for several months. The walls held. According to most accounts, the fort fell only when someone inside opened a gate. The last Qutb Shahi king was taken prisoner.

After the conquest, Golconda slowly lost its role. The court was gone, and many buildings fell into ruin. Roofs collapsed, and plaster fell from the walls. What you see today is mostly the stone skeleton of the old capital. Yet the bones are clear. You can still read the plan: the gates, the halls, the stairways, the terraces where gardens once grew. Look for traces of fine plaster decoration on some arches, small survivors of a richer surface.

As you come back down, slow your pace one more time. Listen to the sounds around you now: voices in many languages, children calling to each other, the clap of another visitor testing the echo. The granite under your feet is the same rock the Qutb Shahi soldiers walked. When you pass the spiked gate again, look back at the hill. The walls still follow every fold of the rock, just as they did when this was the heart of a kingdom.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb early to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Wear shoes with good grip for the uneven stone steps.
Carry water; shade is limited on the upper levels.
Test the famous hand-clap echo inside the Fateh Darwaza dome.
Combine your visit with the nearby Qutb Shahi tombs.
