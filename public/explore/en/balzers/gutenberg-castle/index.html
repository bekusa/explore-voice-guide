The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Down in the valley, a car hums along the main road. A dog barks somewhere among the houses. But up here, on this rocky hill, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through the trees. The stone walls around you feel cool even on a warm day. You are standing at the southern edge of Liechtenstein, in the village of Balzers, beneath Gutenberg Castle.

First, look at the hill itself. It rises out of the flat floor of the Rhine valley like a fist pushed up through the ground. That shape is the reason a castle stands here at all. A steep, isolated rock gives a clear view in every direction. It also makes any attacker climb. Long before anyone built a castle, people understood the value of this spot.

And here is the first surprise. This hill was home to people thousands of years before any knight arrived. Archaeologists have found traces of prehistoric settlement here, reaching back to the Stone Age and the Bronze Age. Among the finds were small bronze figures from the Iron Age, the period before the Romans. These little statuettes, including warriors, are thought to be offerings to the gods. So this rock was not only a home. It was probably a sacred place.

The castle you see grew on top of that ancient ground in the Middle Ages. Written records mention it in the thirteenth century. Its early owners were a local noble family. In the early fourteenth century, the castle passed to the Habsburgs, the powerful Austrian dynasty that ruled huge parts of Central Europe. For them, Gutenberg was a useful outpost. It watched the road through the Rhine valley and the border with the Swiss lands.

That border position was a blessing and a curse. In 1499, war broke out between the Swiss Confederation and the Habsburgs and their allies. Historians call it the Swabian War. The fighting reached this corner of the valley, and Balzers suffered badly. The castle held out, but the region around it was damaged. Living on a frontier meant that peace was never guaranteed here.

Over the following centuries, the castle lost its military purpose. Cannons and new kinds of warfare made small hilltop fortresses less useful. Gutenberg slowly fell into decay. For a long time it was a ruin. People from the village took stones from the walls to build their own houses. If you walk through older parts of Balzers, it is quite possible that some of those houses carry pieces of the castle inside them.

Now comes the second surprise. Much of what you see today is not medieval at all. In the early twentieth century, the ruin was bought by Egon Rheinberger, a sculptor and architect from Liechtenstein. He spent years rebuilding it, in the years before the First World War. Rheinberger did not copy the castle stone for stone. He rebuilt it as he imagined a castle should look. It is a romantic vision of the Middle Ages, shaped by an artist's eye.

So as you look up at the walls, try to read them in layers. Some stonework is old and rough, part of the original fortress. Other sections are smoother and more regular, the work of Rheinberger's rebuilding. The towers, the roofs and the overall outline owe a great deal to him. It is a medieval castle seen through the dreams of the early twentieth century. That mix is part of its charm.

In the late twentieth century, the castle passed into the ownership of the state of Liechtenstein. That makes it very different from the country's other intact castle, Vaduz Castle, a few kilometres to the north. Vaduz is the private home of the princely family, and its interior stays closed to the public. Gutenberg belongs to the nation. Today it is used for concerts, cultural events and guided visits, so its doors open more often to ordinary people.

Walk a little around the hill and look at the view. To the west and south, the mountains of Switzerland rise very close. The border is almost at your feet. To the north, the valley opens toward the rest of Liechtenstein. Across the Rhine lies Switzerland again. From here you can understand the whole geography of this small country in one turn of the head: a narrow strip of land between river and mountains.

Near the castle you will also notice a small chapel. Religion has long been woven into village life in Balzers, as in most of rural Liechtenstein. Look down, too, at the village spread around the foot of the hill. The large church with its tall tower is the parish church of Balzers. The castle above and the church below have watched over the same houses for generations.

If you come in the late afternoon, watch how the light changes on the stone. The walls turn warm and golden as the sun drops toward the Swiss peaks. In winter, snow on the mountains makes the castle look darker and sharper against the white. Each season gives the hill a different mood. Locals often walk up here simply for the air and the view, with no plan at all.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Put your hand on the stone if you can reach it. Under your palm is a mixture of medieval wall, twentieth-century craft and a hill that people have used for thousands of years. As you walk back down toward the village, look at the old houses along the way... and wonder which of them still holds a stone from up here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check ahead for guided tours, since the interior opens irregularly.
Wear sturdy shoes for the short, steep climb up the hill.
Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the walls.
Look for concert or event listings to see inside the castle.
Combine the visit with a slow walk through Balzers village.
