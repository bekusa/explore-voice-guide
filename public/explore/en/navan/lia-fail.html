The guide

The story

Feel the grass underfoot first. It is soft, springy, and often damp, even on a dry day. The wind comes across the open hilltop with nothing to stop it. Ahead of you, on a low grassy mound, a single grey stone stands upright, about the height of a child. It does not look like much at first glance. And yet, for many centuries, people believed this stone could tell a true king from a false one.

This is the Lia Fáil, often translated as the Stone of Destiny. It stands on the Hill of Tara, a short drive south of the town of Navan in County Meath. In Irish tradition, Tara was the seat of the High Kings of Ireland. It was a ceremonial centre more than a fortress, a place of gatherings, inaugurations and assemblies. The stone sits at the symbolic heart of that story.

Let us start with the legend, because the legend is what made this stone famous. Medieval Irish texts say the Lia Fáil was brought to Ireland by the Tuatha Dé Danann. They were a race of supernatural beings in Irish mythology, a kind of old divine people. According to these tales, the stone was one of four great treasures they carried with them, each from a mythical northern city.

The most famous part of the tale is this. When the rightful king of Ireland placed his feet on the stone, it was said to roar. The cry could be heard across the land. If the man was not the true king, the stone stayed silent. Some versions of the story go further. They say the stone also gave a long reign to the chosen king. This is folklore, of course, not documented history. But it tells us how seriously people took kingship here.

Now look at where you are standing. The mound beneath the stone is part of a larger earthwork. Archaeologists call it the Forradh, and it sits inside a wide enclosure known as Ráth na Ríogh, the Fort of the Kings. From the air, these earthworks form rings and banks across the hilltop. From the ground, they are gentle rises and dips in the grass. Walk slowly, and you will feel the slopes change under your feet.

Here is the first surprise. The stone is not in its original position. Tradition holds that it once stood near the Mound of the Hostages, a little to the north of here. It was moved to this spot in the nineteenth century. The reason was not royal at all. It was moved to mark the graves of rebels who died in a battle on this hill in 1798, during a rising against British rule.

That battle adds a second, much more recent layer to Tara. In 1798, a group of Irish rebels, inspired by the ideals of the United Irishmen, gathered on the hill. They were defeated here, and many were killed. Local memory holds that the dead were buried on the hilltop. So when you stand beside the stone, you are standing at a royal symbol and a memorial at the same time.

The second surprise concerns Scotland. For a long time, some writers claimed that the true Lia Fáil had been taken across the sea. They linked it to the Stone of Scone, the stone used in the crowning of Scottish and later British monarchs. Most historians today do not accept this idea. The stone in front of you has stood on this hill in local tradition for a very long time. Still, the old argument shows how much these symbols of kingship mattered.

Many visitors notice the shape of the stone before anything else. It is tall, rounded at the top, and smooth from many hands. Scholars have often pointed out that it looks like a symbol of fertility. In some older traditions, the bond between a king and the land was seen almost as a marriage. A healthy king meant a fertile land. Whether or not that was the intention, the shape fits the idea.

Turn now and look a little to the north. The low, grassy mound with a small opening is the Mound of the Hostages. It is a Neolithic passage tomb, which means a stone burial chamber covered by earth, built by farming communities thousands of years ago. It is far older than any High King. So Tara was already an ancient, sacred place long before the stories of royal inaugurations were written down.

The hill has drawn crowds in modern times too. In 1843, the political leader Daniel O'Connell held one of his so-called monster meetings here. These were huge open-air rallies calling for the repeal of the union between Ireland and Britain. Reports from the time describe an enormous gathering on these slopes. People chose Tara on purpose. Even then, the hill carried a strong meaning of Irish identity and self-rule.

The stone has had a difficult recent life as well. In the last decades it has been damaged by vandals more than once. Each time, local people and the authorities responsible for the site have worked to repair it. If you look closely at the surface, you may see small marks and patches on the stone. Please treat it gently. Touching it lightly is part of the tradition for many visitors, but it is a fragile and precious object.

Before you leave, take a moment to look outward. On a clear day, the land of Meath spreads around you in green fields, hedges and distant hills. The ancient kings chose this spot partly for this view. From here, a ruler could see a great part of the country around him. Even without a crown, you can understand the feeling of standing at a centre point of the land.

So stand beside the Lia Fáil for a moment. Listen to the wind, the distant sheep, perhaps a few voices from other walkers. The stone will not roar for you, or for anyone. But as you walk back down through the rings of grass, notice how the ground rises and falls beneath you. Those soft banks were shaped by people who came here thousands of years ago, and they are still here, under your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear waterproof shoes; the hilltop grass is often wet.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed open summit.
Come on a clear day for long views across Meath.
Walk the earthwork rings slowly to feel their shape.
Touch the stone gently, or simply admire it from beside.
