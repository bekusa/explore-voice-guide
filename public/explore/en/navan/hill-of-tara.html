The guide

The story

Start with the grass under your feet. It is soft and springy, cropped short by sheep, and it rises and falls in long, gentle ridges. Listen for a moment. You may hear wind, a distant car, the call of a rook from the churchyard trees. Not much else. At first glance, this is a quiet green hill in County Meath, a short drive from Navan. But those ridges under your boots are not natural. They are earthworks, shaped by human hands across thousands of years.

Welcome to the Hill of Tara. In Irish tradition, this was the seat of the High Kings of Ireland. It was the place where sovereignty was claimed, celebrated and tested. There are no towers here, no palace walls, no ruined great hall. Almost everything was built from earth and timber, and the timber is long gone. What remains is a pattern of mounds and ditches. You need a little imagination to see it, and that is part of the pleasure.

Let us begin with the oldest thing on the hill. Look for a small, rounded grassy mound with a low stone entrance on one side. This is the Mound of the Hostages. It is a Neolithic passage tomb, a stone chamber covered with earth. It was built around five thousand years ago, long before the Pyramids of Giza were finished. Archaeologists excavated it in the twentieth century and found the remains of many people buried over a long span of time. So Tara was already sacred ground long before any king stood here.

The name comes from a later tradition. Legend says that kings kept hostages from rival territories at Tara, as a guarantee of loyalty. Whether those hostages were ever held at this exact mound is uncertain. The name was attached by medieval writers who looked at an ancient landscape and gave it stories. That is a theme you will meet again and again on this hill. The monuments are real. Many of the names and tales are layers of later memory.

Now walk towards the largest earthwork, a wide oval enclosure ringed by a bank and ditch. This is Ráith na Ríogh, the Fort of the Kings, sometimes called the Royal Enclosure. Inside it sit two joined ring-forts, like a figure of eight drawn on the grass. One is known as the Forradh, or the Royal Seat. The other is called Teach Chormaic, or Cormac's House. Cormac mac Airt is one of the great kings of Irish legend, a wise ruler of a golden age. He sits somewhere between history and myth.

On top of the Forradh stands a single upright stone, a little taller than a person. This is the Lia Fáil, the Stone of Destiny. According to legend, the stone would roar aloud when the rightful king touched it. Kings were said to be inaugurated here, at a ceremony linked to a great feast called the Feis of Tara. In that tradition, the king was symbolically married to the land itself, often pictured as a goddess of sovereignty. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The stone did not always stand on this spot. It was moved here in 1798.

That year matters. In 1798, the United Irishmen rose in rebellion against British rule. A battle was fought here at Tara, and many rebels died. The Lia Fáil was placed on the Forradh to mark their grave. So when you stand beside this stone, you are standing at a royal symbol and a rebel memorial at the same time. Ireland's ancient past and its modern struggle meet in one granite pillar.

Tara has drawn crowds in modern times too. In 1843, the political leader Daniel O'Connell held one of his so-called monster meetings here. These were enormous open-air rallies calling for the repeal of the union between Ireland and Britain. An immense crowd gathered on these slopes. O'Connell chose Tara on purpose. He knew that this hill carried the weight of Irish kingship, and he wanted that weight behind his cause.

Not every visitor has treated the hill with care. Around the turn of the twentieth century, a group called the British Israelites dug into one of the earthworks, the Rath of the Synods. They believed that the lost Ark of the Covenant, from the Old Testament, was buried at Tara. They found no Ark. They did cause serious damage, and there were public protests against the digging. If the Rath of the Synods looks oddly disturbed, that is part of the reason.

North of the main hill, look for two long parallel banks running down the slope. This feature is called the Banqueting Hall. Medieval writers described a vast feasting hall where every guest had a set seat according to rank. Modern archaeologists are less sure. Many now think these banks may have formed a ceremonial approach to the summit. Either way, walk between them and notice how they guide you uphill. Whoever made them wanted people to arrive at the top in a particular way.

The church beside the car park is dedicated to Saint Patrick, and it now serves as a visitor centre. Nearby you will see a statue of the saint. A popular tale says that Patrick lit an Easter fire on the nearby Hill of Slane. He did this in defiance of the High King, Laoghaire, who had ordered that no fire burn before his own. This story is legend, told centuries after Patrick lived. But it shows how Tara became a stage for the meeting of the old religion and the new.

Now turn around and look outward. The hill is not very high, yet the view stretches a long way across the flat central plain of Ireland. On a clear day you can see far into the distance in almost every direction. This openness helps to explain why Tara mattered. From here, the land seems to spread out like a kingdom. In the 2000s, a new motorway was built through the valley below. It caused fierce debate about how Ireland should protect its ancient landscapes.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Feel the wind on your face, and the uneven ground under your feet. Watch the sheep move slowly between the mounds, as they have for generations. Notice how the light shifts across the banks and hollows as the clouds pass. Nothing here shouts for attention. The stories are quiet, held in grass and soil. As you walk back down the slope, look over your shoulder once more. The hill will look like an ordinary field again... and now you know it is not.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear waterproof boots; the grass is often wet and uneven.
Bring a windproof layer; the exposed hilltop catches strong wind.
Start at the visitor centre to get your bearings first.
Choose a clear day to enjoy the long views.
Keep dogs on a lead because sheep graze freely.
