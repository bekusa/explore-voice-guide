The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves across open water. Gulls call above the strait. Every few seconds a car passes overhead with a soft rush of tyres on concrete. Below you, the sea runs green and grey between two shores. On one side is the Scottish mainland, at Kyle of Lochalsh. On the other side is the Isle of Skye. Between them, a long pale curve of concrete links the two in a single smooth arc.

This is the Skye Bridge. It opened in 1995, and it ended a very old habit of island life. For generations, the only way to reach Skye by road was to wait for a ferry. Cars queued at the slipway in Kyle. Travellers watched the weather. In summer, the queues could stretch back along the road. The bridge turned that wait, often an hour or more, into a crossing of a minute or two.

The crossing does not happen in one leap. Look closely and you will see that the road touches down halfway, on a small low island. This is Eilean Bàn, which means the White Island in Scottish Gaelic, the old language of the Highlands. The bridge uses the island like a stepping stone. A shorter span links the mainland to the island. Then the taller main arch lifts the road high over the deeper channel, so that boats can pass underneath towards Skye.

That tall arch matters. The water below is a working sea route. Fishing boats, yachts and supply vessels move through here between the open Atlantic side and the sheltered lochs. The engineers built the main span in concrete, balanced out from its piers, and gave it enough height for masts to slide through. From the shore, the shape looks almost light. Up close, you can see how heavy and solid the concrete really is.

Now for something many visitors miss. On Eilean Bàn there is a small white lighthouse, built in the nineteenth century by the Stevenson family. This family of Scottish engineers designed lighthouses all around the coast of Scotland. The writer Robert Louis Stevenson, author of Treasure Island, came from the same family. He chose stories over engineering. His relatives stayed with stone, lamps and stormy headlands.

The island has a literary story of its own. In the late 1960s, the naturalist and writer Gavin Maxwell made his home in the old lighthouse keepers' cottages. Maxwell is best known for Ring of Bright Water, his account of living with otters on this coast. That book made otters famous to readers across the world. Maxwell died in 1969, not long after moving here. Today the island is looked after as a quiet wildlife refuge.

The otters themselves are still around. Wild otters hunt along these rocky shores, and they are shy and well camouflaged. They look like a dark, wet shape slipping between the seaweed. Seals also haul out on the rocks nearby. If you stand quietly on the shoreline in the early morning or the evening, your chances are better. Watch the edge of the water, not the open sea.

Next comes the part of the story that still makes local people sigh, or laugh. The bridge was paid for by private investment, an early example of that model in the United Kingdom. The private company recovered its money through tolls. For such a short crossing, the charge felt very steep. Many people considered it one of the most expensive bridge tolls in Europe, mile for mile.

Resistance began almost at once. Residents formed a campaign group called Skye and Kyle Against Tolls. Some people refused to pay, on principle. Hundreds of them faced prosecution, and the court cases dragged on for years. To islanders, the toll felt like a tax on simply getting home. Finally, in December 2004, the Scottish government bought the bridge back, and the tolls ended. The crossing has been free ever since.

There is an older layer here, and it sits mostly in legend. Look across to the Skye side, towards the village of Kyleakin. On a rocky point you may spot the broken walls of Caisteal Maol, a ruined castle. According to a popular local tale, a Norse princess once lived there. People called her Saucy Mary. Legend has it that she stretched a chain across the strait and charged every passing ship a toll. So, the locals joke, this was not the first toll on this water.

The name Kyleakin has its own tradition. In Gaelic, kyle means a strait or a narrow channel. Local tradition links the name to Haakon, a medieval king of Norway. The story says that he sailed his fleet through this channel. For centuries, Norse rulers controlled much of the Hebrides, the island chain off Scotland's west coast. You can still hear Norse traces in many place names around Skye.

Walking the bridge is worth the effort. A pavement runs along the side, and on foot you notice things that drivers never see. The concrete railings feel cold and rough under your hand. The wind grows stronger as the road climbs. At the highest point, the view opens up. On a clear day, you see the hills of Skye to the west, the mainland mountains behind you, and long silver channels of water running between them.

So take a last look before you move on. Notice the white lighthouse on its patch of rock. Notice the ruined castle walls across the water, and the slow traffic crossing the arch. A ferry queue, a protest movement, a writer and his otters, a princess and her chain... they all belong to this one short stretch of sea. As you walk away, listen once more for the wind, the gulls, and the soft sound of tyres overhead.
