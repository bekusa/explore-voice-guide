The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the water and runs through gaps in old stone. Gulls call somewhere below. Underfoot, the grass is soft and often damp, and the path slopes down toward a dark loch that stretches away in both directions. The water here is not blue. It is the colour of strong tea, stained by peat washed down from the hills. You are standing above Loch Ness, and in front of you sit the broken walls of Urquhart Castle.

Take a moment with the setting, because the setting is the whole reason this castle exists. The ruins sit on a rocky headland on the loch's western shore, near the village of Drumnadrochit. This headland pushes out into the water. From here, anyone holding the walls could watch traffic moving up and down the Great Glen. That glen is a long, straight valley that cuts across the Highlands, following an ancient geological fault line. For centuries, it was one of the main routes through the north of Scotland.

People understood the value of this spot long before the stone walls you see now. Archaeologists have found traces of an earlier fortification on the site, from the early medieval period. That was the era of the Picts, a people who lived in northern Scotland before the Scottish kingdom took its later shape. An early written life of Saint Columba, the Irish monk who carried Christianity into this region, describes him visiting a dying nobleman in a place called Airchartdan. Many historians connect that name with Urquhart.

The castle as we know it began to take shape in the thirteenth century. The Durward family, powerful landholders of that time, are generally credited with building an early stone castle here. Look at the shape of the ground as you walk. The site splits into two main parts — a higher area to the south and a larger, lower area to the north. A ditch was cut across the landward side to cut the headland off from attackers approaching overland. That ditch is still clearly visible as you enter.

Then came war. In the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, Scotland fought a long struggle for independence against the English crown. Urquhart changed hands more than once during those years. English forces under Edward the First took it in 1296. Scottish forces later took it back. The castle became a royal stronghold, and it was held for the Scottish crown in the decades that followed. A fortress on a lonely loch was, in fact, part of a national story.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The greatest threat to Urquhart in later centuries often came not from England, but from within the Highlands themselves. The MacDonald Lords of the Isles, a powerful clan dynasty based in the western islands, raided the castle and the surrounding glen more than once in the fifteenth century. Accounts of these raids describe cattle, grain, and household goods carried away. Life inside these walls was not only about siege and glory. It was also about protecting food and livestock.

In 1509, the castle and its lands were granted to the Grant family. They held Urquhart for a long time afterward, and they did a great deal of rebuilding. The tall tower house at the northern end of the site carries their name today — the Grant Tower. It is the most complete structure left standing. When you look at the ruins from a distance, it is this tower that gives the castle its familiar outline against the water.

So why is everything broken? The answer is not slow decay alone. In the late seventeenth century, government troops held the castle during a period of rebellion. When they left in the 1690s, the castle was deliberately damaged so that rebels could not use it. Much of the destruction you see was intentional. Later, in the early eighteenth century, a severe storm brought down part of the Grant Tower. After that, local people took stone from the ruins for their own buildings.

Now walk to the Grant Tower and climb it if you can. The stairs are narrow and the steps are worn, so take your time. From the top, the loch opens up in both directions. On a still day, the surface is smooth and dark. On a windy day, it breaks into short grey waves. Loch Ness is very deep — more than two hundred metres in places — and it holds an enormous volume of fresh water. Standing here, you feel how small a boat would look out there.

Of course, you cannot stand above Loch Ness without thinking of its famous monster. A surprising number of reported sightings have come from the stretch of water near this castle. Locals say the wide view from the headland is one reason. Many sightings have been explained as boat wakes, floating logs, or tricks of light on the water. No evidence has ever confirmed a creature. Still, people scan the surface here, half joking, half hoping. You may find yourself doing the same.

Before you leave the lower ground, look near the shoreline for the water gate. This was an entrance that opened directly onto the loch. Supplies and people could arrive by boat, without crossing the land approach at all. In a region with rough tracks and few roads, the water was often the easier highway. Nearby, you may also see a full-size replica of a trebuchet, a medieval siege engine that used a heavy counterweight to throw stones at castle walls. It helps you picture how attackers once worked.

The visitor centre sits up the slope, built low into the hillside so it does not block the view. Inside, there is an exhibition with objects found on the site, and a short film about the castle's history. At the end of the film, the screen lifts away to show the real ruins through a large window. It is a simple trick, and it works. Historic Environment Scotland, the public body that cares for many of the country's historic sites, looks after the castle today.

As you walk back up the path, turn around once more. Notice how the stone changes colour as clouds pass over — pale grey in sunlight, almost black in shadow. Notice the boats moving slowly on the water, the same way boats have moved along this glen for many centuries. The walls are broken, but the reason for building here is still in front of you. A headland, a long loch, and a view in both directions. Take one last look, and then let the wind carry you on.
