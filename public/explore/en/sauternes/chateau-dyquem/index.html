The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here on the hill above Sauternes, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through the vines. The gravel crunches underfoot. On an autumn morning the air feels damp and cool, and a pale mist may still hang over the low ground below. That mist matters more than almost anything else you will see today. It is the reason this quiet hilltop produces one of the most celebrated sweet wines in the world.

You are standing at Château d'Yquem, in the Sauternes wine region, south of the city of Bordeaux in southwest France. The house sits on one of the highest points in the appellation. From here, the vineyards fall away in long, even rows on every side. The château looks less like a palace and more like a small fortified manor. It has thick pale stone walls, round towers, and an enclosed courtyard. Parts of the building date back several centuries.

Now, back to that mist. Not far from here, a small river called the Ciron flows into the larger Garonne. The Ciron runs cold, shaded by forest along its banks. The Garonne is warmer. In autumn, when the two waters meet, fog rises in the early morning and drifts across the vineyards. Then the afternoon sun burns it away. That daily rhythm of damp mornings and warm afternoons creates the perfect conditions for a very unusual guest.

That guest is a fungus. Its scientific name is Botrytis cinerea, but winemakers call it noble rot. On most fruit, rot is a disaster. Here, it is a gift. The fungus pierces the grape skins, and water slowly evaporates from inside the berry. The grape shrivels, turns brown and purple, and looks frankly unappetising. But what remains inside is concentrated sugar, acidity, and flavour. Without noble rot, the wine of Sauternes would not exist.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not expect. The fungus does not arrive evenly. On a single bunch, some grapes may be perfectly rotted while others are still green and firm. So the harvest here is done by hand, in several passes through the vineyard. Pickers move along the rows again and again over many weeks. They select only the berries that are ready, sometimes one grape at a time. It is slow, careful, expensive work.

Because the grapes lose so much water, the yield is tiny. A figure often repeated at the estate is that one vine produces roughly a single glass of wine. Think about that as you look at the rows around you. Thousands of vines, each one tended for a whole year, and each one giving you a glass. The grapes are mainly Sémillon, a golden, thin-skinned variety that welcomes noble rot. A smaller share of Sauvignon Blanc adds freshness and lift.

The second surprise is about what happens in a bad year. If the weather does not cooperate, and noble rot fails to develop properly, the estate can decide not to release its sweet wine under the Yquem name at all. In some vintages, no Château d'Yquem has been bottled. For a business, that is a remarkable choice. It means an entire year of work is set aside to protect the reputation of the wine.

The history of this estate is closely tied to one family. For a long period it belonged to the Sauvage family. Then, in 1785, a marriage joined it to the Lur-Saluces family. They would hold the estate for more than two centuries. In 1999, after a long and public family dispute, the luxury group LVMH became the majority owner. The château has changed hands only rarely, and that continuity shows in how carefully traditions are kept.

There is also an American chapter to this story. In 1787, Thomas Jefferson travelled through the Bordeaux region. He was then serving as the American minister to France, and he was a passionate student of wine. Jefferson ordered bottles of Yquem, both for himself and, the story goes, for George Washington. His order is often cited as proof that the wine was already prized far beyond France more than two hundred years ago.

In 1855, Paris hosted a great world exhibition. For the occasion, the wines of Bordeaux were ranked in an official classification. Most of the sweet wines of Sauternes and its neighbour Barsac were placed in a first or second tier. But one estate was placed above them all, in a category of its own, called Premier Cru Supérieur. That single estate was Château d'Yquem. No other wine in that classification received the same title.

What does the wine actually taste like? Young Yquem is deep gold, with flavours often described as apricot, honey, citrus peel, and spice. With age, the colour darkens toward amber and copper. Thanks to its high sugar and strong acidity, it can age for an extraordinarily long time. Bottles several generations old are still opened and enjoyed. Few wines in the world can rest that long in a cellar and remain alive.

The estate also makes something many people never hear about — a dry white wine, sold under the name Y, pronounced like the French letter, ygrec. It uses the same grape varieties, picked in a different way, without the full sweetness. Inside the cellars, the sweet wine ages in oak barrels for a long period before bottling. Visits to the property are by appointment only, so if you are here on a tour, you are seeing a place that keeps a fairly closed door.

Before you leave, take one more look around you. Notice the slope of the land, and how the vineyards seem to wrap the hill. Look down toward the lower ground, where the river mist gathers on autumn mornings. Look at the plain stone walls of the château, which have watched over these vines for centuries. And remember that each glass of wine from this hill starts with fog, patience, and a single shrivelled golden grape.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book your estate visit well in advance; appointments are required.
Come in autumn to see pickers making several harvest passes.
Arrive early morning for a chance to see river mist.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and uneven vineyard ground.
Ask about the dry white Y alongside the sweet wine.
