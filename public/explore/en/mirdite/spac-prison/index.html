The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the slope, and loose gravel shifts under your shoes. A goat bell rings somewhere below. Rust-coloured stone and grey concrete rise in tiers against the mountainside. There is very little else here. No town, no traffic, no café. Just a steep valley in the mountains of Mirditë, in northern Albania, and the broken shells of buildings that once held people who were never meant to leave.

This is Spaç Prison. For more than two decades, it was one of the harshest labour camps of communist Albania. Today it is a ruin. Roofs have collapsed, window frames are empty, and grass grows through the floors. But the layout is still clear. As you walk, you can still read how the place worked: where men slept, where they were counted, and where they went down into the mountain to work.

Some background helps. After the Second World War, Albania was ruled by a communist government under Enver Hoxha. Over the following decades, the country became one of the most isolated states in Europe. It cut ties with Yugoslavia, then the Soviet Union, and later China. At home, the state treated criticism as a crime. People were imprisoned for a joke, a letter, a foreign radio broadcast, or simply for belonging to the wrong family.

Spaç opened in the late 1960s. Its purpose was both punishment and production. The mountain behind you holds copper and pyrite, a shiny mineral sometimes called fool's gold. Prisoners were sent underground to dig it out. Most of them were political prisoners. They worked in shifts, often with poor tools and little protection. Former inmates have described dust, cold, injuries, and quotas that were very hard to meet.

Now look at the setting itself. The valley is steep and narrow. The slopes close in on every side. This was a deliberate choice. Escape on foot through this terrain was close to impossible, and the nearest villages were small and closely watched. The landscape did much of the guards' work. Even today, the long and rough road that brought you here shows how far this place stands from ordinary life.

The buildings climb the hillside in rows. The larger blocks were dormitories, where prisoners slept crowded together. Other structures held kitchens, guard posts, and administration offices. Higher up, you can find the openings of the mine tunnels. If you stand among the dormitories and look uphill, you see the route prisoners walked every day — from their beds to the dark entrance of the mine.

Here is something many visitors miss. In 1973, the prisoners at Spaç rose up. It was one of the very few open revolts inside a prison in communist Albania. For a short time, inmates took control of parts of the camp. They raised the Albanian flag — the red flag with the black double-headed eagle — but without the communist star. That small change was a powerful statement. It said: we love this country, but not this regime.

The revolt did not last. Security forces restored control within days. Afterwards, the state punished the leaders severely. Several were sentenced to death and executed. Others received longer sentences. The revolt is remembered today as an act of great courage in a system that allowed almost no resistance. When you look at the open ground between the buildings, try to picture that flag above the camp.

Life here continued for many more years. The prison closed only around the end of the communist period, in the early 1990s, when the regime collapsed and political prisoners were released. Many former inmates returned home to families who had also suffered. In communist Albania, relatives of political prisoners were often sent into internal exile, lost their jobs, or were blocked from education.

After closure, nobody maintained the site. Local people took metal, wood, and other useful materials. Rain and snow did the rest. That is why Spaç looks the way it does today. In recent years, Albania has recognised the prison as a cultural monument, and European heritage organisations have warned that it is in danger. Survivors and their families have pushed for it to be protected as a place of memory.

As you move through the ruins, slow down. Look at the walls closely. In some rooms, visitors have found writing, scratched marks, and small traces of daily life. Notice the thickness of the walls and the size of the windows. Notice how little space each person would have had. These details tell you more than any number could.

There is also a quieter, human side to this place. Prisoners formed friendships here. Some taught each other languages, history, or literature, often in secret. Several former inmates later became writers and spoke publicly about what they lived through. Their memoirs are a large part of why the world knows about Spaç at all. Their accounts carry the voices that the state tried to silence.

A practical word. This is an open ruin, not a managed museum. Floors can be unstable, and stairs may be missing or broken. Stay on solid ground and keep away from tunnel entrances. Bring water, because there are no services nearby. And move with respect. For many Albanians, this is a place connected to their own families and their own losses.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look back at the hillside. The concrete is fading into the colour of the mountain. In a few decades, nature may cover much of it. For now, it is still here. Listen to the wind again, and the gravel under your feet. Then walk back down the valley, the same way the released prisoners once did.
