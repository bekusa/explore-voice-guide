The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Up here it comes straight off the Ionian Sea, cool and salty. It moves through the wild thyme and the low scrub that grow between the stones. Underfoot, the path is uneven limestone, worn smooth in places by centuries of boots and bare feet. Far below, the water changes colour as you watch, from deep navy to clear turquoise. You are standing on one of the highest coastal rocks on Corfu, at a castle named for the angels.

This is Angelokastro, which means the Castle of the Angels. It sits on a steep crag above the northwest coast, near the village of Krini and the bays of Paleokastritsa. The summit rises more than three hundred metres above the sea. From a ship offshore, the rock looks almost impossible to climb. That was exactly the point. Whoever held this peak could watch the sea for many kilometres in almost every direction.

The castle is Byzantine in origin. Byzantine means it belongs to the eastern Roman Empire, the Greek-speaking world that ruled from Constantinople for about a thousand years. Most historians date the fortress to the thirteenth century. It is usually linked to the Despotate of Epirus, a Byzantine successor state on the Greek mainland. Its rulers are thought to have built it, possibly under Michael the Second Komnenos Doukas, although the exact founding date is still debated.

Why build here, at the edge of the island? Think about the sea in the Middle Ages. Pirates, rival fleets and raiders all moved along these coasts. The town of Corfu sits on the eastern side, facing the mainland. The west was more exposed. A watchtower on this rock gave early warning. A signal fire on the summit could carry a message across the island long before an enemy ship reached the shore.

Over the following centuries, Corfu passed through many hands. Rulers from southern Italy took control, and later the Republic of Venice ruled the island for about four hundred years. Through all of these changes, Angelokastro stayed important. It became the administrative and military centre for this part of Corfu. Local officials worked from here, and the surrounding villages looked to the castle for protection.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. This was not only a place for soldiers. In times of danger, ordinary people climbed up here too. Farmers, families and their animals took shelter behind these walls. The most famous moment came in 1537, when a large Ottoman force invaded Corfu. Much of the countryside suffered badly. But the defenders of Angelokastro held out, and the castle was not taken. Later Ottoman raids also failed to capture it.

As you climb, notice how the walls follow the shape of the rock. The builders did not flatten the summit. Instead, they used the cliffs as part of the defence. Where the rock drops away sharply, there was little need for a wall at all. Where the slope is gentler, the stonework is thicker. Look closely and you can see different phases of building and repair, with rough rubble beside more carefully cut blocks.

Near the lower part of the site, look for the openings in the rock face. These are cave spaces cut or shaped into the cliff. One of them holds a small chapel dedicated to Saint Kyriaki. Tradition says that hermits once lived in these rock cells, praying in near silence above the sea. Some of the caves were also used for practical things, such as storage. Step inside if you can, and feel how the temperature drops.

Water was always the great problem on a rock like this. There is no spring on the summit. So the defenders collected rainwater in cisterns, which are underground stone tanks. During a long siege, these cisterns meant the difference between survival and surrender. As you walk, look for the low, vaulted structures and the channels in the stone. They are less dramatic than the walls, but they kept people alive.

At the very top stands a small church dedicated to the Archangels Michael and Gabriel. This church gives the castle its name. It is a simple, whitewashed building, modest in size. That modesty is part of its charm. After the hard climb, you reach a quiet place of worship at the highest point. Many Greek churches stand on hilltops in the same way, closer to the sky and visible from far away.

Now turn around and take in the view. To the south, you can see the coves of Paleokastritsa, with their green headlands and pale beaches. Inland, the hills roll away in olive groves and cypress trees. On a clear day, the horizon seems endless. Sailors once used this coastline as a landmark. Today, small boats trace white lines across the water, and the only sound may be a goat bell somewhere on the slope.

A few practical thoughts. The final climb is steep and rocky, with steps cut into the stone in places. Good shoes matter more than you might think. There is very little shade near the top, so the midday sun can feel fierce, especially in summer. Early morning or late afternoon brings softer light and cooler air. Bring water, take your time, and keep an eye on the edges, because the drops are real.

Before you go, pause for a moment where you stand. Feel the wind again, and the warm stone under your hand. For hundreds of years, people stood on this same rock, watching the same sea for sails on the horizon. Some were soldiers. Some were monks. Many were simply frightened families waiting for danger to pass. As you walk back down toward Krini, look back once over your shoulder. The castle will sit there above you, quiet and patient, just as it always has.

Stay well back from unfenced cliff edges near the summit.
