The guide

The story

Stand on the waterfront for a moment before you go in. Wind comes straight off the harbour here, cold and salty, and it pushes against your jacket. Gulls call over the water. In front of you rises a long, heavy building of stone, glass and pale panels. It sits at the edge of the sea like a ship that has just come in to dock. This is Te Papa, the national museum of New Zealand.

The full name is Te Papa Tongarewa. It is a phrase in te reo Māori, the language of New Zealand's Indigenous people. It is often translated as "container of treasures". Some people describe it as the place where the treasures of this land are held. That idea runs through the whole building. It is not only a store of objects. It is meant to hold the stories, the people and the land together, under one roof.

The museum opened its doors in 1998. It replaced an older national museum and art gallery that stood on a hill farther from the centre. Moving it down to the waterfront was a deliberate choice. The planners wanted a museum that ordinary people would walk past every day... a place for families, school groups and office workers on a lunch break, not only for specialists.

Here is the first surprise, and you cannot see it at all. Wellington sits on a major fault line, and earthquakes are part of life in this city. So the building does not rest directly on the ground. It sits on base isolators, large bearings made of layers of rubber and lead. In a strong quake, the ground can move beneath the museum while the building moves far less. The collections, and the people inside, are protected by engineering hidden under your feet.

That theme continues inside. Te Papa has long been known for exhibits that explain how this young, restless land was made. Volcanoes, earthquakes and shifting plates shaped these islands. Many visitors remember a small model house where you can feel what a tremor is like. Children often come out laughing. Adults sometimes come out quiet, because in Wellington the subject is very real.

Now, the heart of the museum. Te Papa was built on a bicultural idea. It aims to give equal voice to Māori, as tangata whenua, the people of the land, and to the people who arrived later, mostly from Europe. You can feel this in the way the spaces are arranged. Māori and European stories sit side by side. Neither one is treated as a footnote to the other.

The clearest expression of this is the marae on the upper levels. A marae is a Māori meeting ground, a place for welcomes, speeches, celebrations and grief. Te Papa's marae is called Rongomaraeroa, and its carved meeting house is Te Hono ki Hawaiki. The carvings are bright and modern, in colours you may not expect. The design was led by the artist Cliff Whiting. It is a living space. Ceremonies still take place here, so treat it with respect.

Nearby is something much older. Te Hau ki Tūranga is a carved meeting house from the Tūranga region, near present-day Gisborne on the east coast. It is regarded as one of the oldest surviving meeting houses of its kind. It was carved in the 1840s under the master carver Raharuhi Rukupō. In the 1860s it was taken by the colonial government. Its story is difficult, and the museum does not hide that. Look closely at the deep, curving patterns in the wood. Each figure carries ancestry and meaning.

Now for the second surprise, and this one is hard to miss. Te Papa holds a colossal squid, one of the largest ever studied. It was caught by a fishing boat in the icy waters of the Ross Sea, near Antarctica, in 2007. Colossal squid live in deep, dark water, and very few have ever been seen whole. Its eyes are among the largest of any animal known. Standing in front of it, many people simply go silent.

The museum also tells the story of war. One major exhibition looks at Gallipoli, the 1915 campaign in Turkey where soldiers from New Zealand and Australia fought in the First World War. It was made with Weta Workshop, the Wellington studio known for film effects. Giant human figures, far larger than life, show real people from that campaign. Every detail is there... the sweat, the stubble, the tired eyes. It makes the human cost very personal.

Art has its own home here too. Toi Art, the museum's art gallery, opened in 2018. It brings together historical paintings, contemporary work and pieces by Māori and Pacific artists. Wellington is a city of galleries and small theatres, and this space fits that character. You may find a quiet nineteenth century landscape on one wall and something loud and experimental around the corner. The mix is part of the point.

When your feet get tired, look for the outdoor terraces and the big windows. From the upper levels you can look out across the harbour to the hills beyond. On a clear day the water turns deep blue. On a grey day it turns silver, and ferries leave white lines behind them. Outside, the waterfront path runs toward the city in one direction and toward Oriental Bay and its sandy beach in the other.

So take one last look before you walk on. Notice the wind again, and the sound of the water against the edge of the wharf. Behind you, a building rests on hidden bearings, holding a giant squid, an old carved house and the voices of two peoples. Ahead of you is the harbour that brought so many of them here. As you walk away along the water, you are part of that story too.

Deep curving patterns carved into Te Hau ki Tūranga.
The colossal squid's enormous eyes in its display tank.
Tiny sweat and stubble on the giant Gallipoli figures.
Harbour views and passing ferries from the upper terraces.
Before you go

Practical tips

Pick two or three galleries rather than rushing everything.
Behave respectfully on the marae, a living ceremonial space.
Bring a warm layer for the windy waterfront walk.
Visit early on weekdays to avoid school group crowds.
Combine your visit with a walk to Oriental Bay.
