The guide

The story

Pine needles soften the ground under your feet. Then, quite suddenly, the forest floor turns hard and grey. Look closely and you will see a slab of broken concrete, half-swallowed by moss, with rusted steel rods curling out of it like dry roots. Around you there is only birdsong, wind in the treetops, and the smell of resin. It is hard to believe that this quiet woodland was once one of the most fiercely fought-over places in Europe.

You are standing on the Karelian Isthmus, the strip of land between the Gulf of Finland and Lake Ladoga. Today it belongs to Russia's Leningrad Oblast. Before the Second World War, however, this was Finnish territory. The ruins scattered through these woods are the remains of the Mannerheim Line, a chain of Finnish defences built to stop an invasion from the east. It ran right across the isthmus, from the sea to the lake, for well over one hundred kilometres.

Here is the first surprise. The Finns themselves did not originally call it the Mannerheim Line. To the engineers who built it, it was simply the main defensive position. The famous name became popular during the war itself. It honoured Carl Gustaf Emil Mannerheim, the former officer of the Russian imperial army who became Finland's commander-in-chief. Over time, the nickname stuck to the concrete so firmly that almost nobody remembers the plain original.

Construction began in the 1920s, when Finland was still a very young independent country. Money was always short. The work came in waves — a burst of building, then long pauses, then another push in the late 1930s as tension with the Soviet Union grew. The result was not one continuous wall. It was a patchwork of trenches, barbed wire, granite boulders, and concrete bunkers, placed wherever the lakes, swamps, and ridges made a natural barrier stronger.

As you walk, watch for rows of large rough stones set in lines across the ground. These were anti-tank obstacles. The idea was simple. A tank that tried to climb over a boulder would tilt, expose its thin underside, or get stuck entirely. Finland had plenty of granite and very little steel, so the defenders used what the land gave them. Many of these stone rows still sit exactly where the builders left them.

On 30 November 1939, the Soviet Union invaded Finland. This conflict is known as the Winter War. It was fought in one of the coldest winters in living memory, with deep snow and very short days. Finnish soldiers, often on skis and dressed in white, held these positions against a far larger army with far more artillery, aircraft, and tanks. For weeks, the attacks broke against the line.

The second surprise is about size. Soviet accounts at the time described the Mannerheim Line as a fortress on the scale of France's Maginot Line, the enormous chain of underground forts on the French-German border. The reality was much more modest. Many Finnish bunkers were small, and some were old and poorly protected. Describing the line as a vast fortress helped explain why the Soviet advance was so slow and so costly.

The hardest fighting came near a village the Finns called Summa. The area had some of the strongest bunkers on the whole line. One of them earned the nickname Millionaire, because it cost so much to build. Another was named Poppius, after an officer connected with it. In February 1940, after a massive bombardment, Soviet forces finally broke through in this sector. The Finnish army fell back to positions further west.

The Winter War ended in March 1940 with a peace treaty signed in Moscow. Finland survived as an independent state, but the price was heavy. It handed over most of the Karelian Isthmus, including the city of Vyborg, which the Finns knew as Viipuri. Hundreds of thousands of Karelians left their homes and moved west. For many Finnish families, this landscape remains a place of lost farms and family graves.

The story did not end there. In 1941, during the Continuation War, Finnish forces returned to the isthmus. By 1944 the land had changed hands again, and it has remained part of Russia ever since. After the fighting, many bunkers were blown apart on purpose, so that nobody could use them again. That is why so much of what you see today is tilted, cracked, or collapsed into its own foundations.

Look at the shattered concrete near you. Notice how thick the walls are, and how the steel reinforcement was laid in a dense grid. Some ruins still show firing slits, the narrow openings where machine guns once pointed across open ground. In places you can trace the shallow line of a trench, now just a soft dip in the earth, lined with ferns and blueberry bushes.

Today, visitors come here for many different reasons. Some are military history enthusiasts. Some are Finnish families looking for traces of grandparents. Others are Russian hikers who simply enjoy the forest. Small memorials appear in several places along the old line, some Finnish and some Soviet. Together they are a reminder that soldiers from both sides lost their lives in these woods, in temperatures far below freezing.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind in the pines and look at how quickly the forest has returned. Moss covers the concrete. Young birches grow straight out of the rubble. As you walk back along the path, notice the stones, the dips in the ground, the odd flat edges under the leaves. Once you know what they are, this forest will never look quite the same.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy boots for broken concrete and uneven forest ground.
Use insect repellent and check yourself for ticks afterwards.
Never touch rusted metal objects; old ordnance may remain.
Bring a flashlight if you plan to peer into bunker openings.
Download offline maps before leaving; mobile signal can be weak.
