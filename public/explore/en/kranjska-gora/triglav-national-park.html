The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere close, water is running over stone — cold, quick, and clear enough to see every pebble. The air smells of spruce resin and damp limestone. Above the rooftops of Kranjska Gora, grey walls of rock rise almost straight up, pale in the morning and pink at dusk. This is the northern doorway to Triglav National Park, the only national park in Slovenia. Everything you can see to the south, from the forests to the high ridges, belongs to it.

The park covers roughly 840 square kilometres of the Julian Alps. That is about four percent of the whole country. It is a big piece of a small nation. The mountains here are made of limestone and dolomite, old sea floor pushed upward over millions of years. Rain and snow have carved that soft rock into gorges, sinkholes, caves and sharp ridges. This is why the peaks look so jagged. And it is also why so much of the water here disappears underground, then appears again as sudden springs.

At the centre of it all stands Mount Triglav. At 2,864 metres, it is the highest point in Slovenia. Its name means three heads, and from some angles you can see why — the summit block looks split into three tops. But Triglav is more than a mountain. It appears on the national coat of arms and on the Slovenian flag, drawn as three simple white peaks. For many Slovenes, this mountain is a symbol of the nation itself.

Here is something many visitors miss. The protection of this landscape began a century ago. In 1924, a conservation area was created in the Valley of the Triglav Lakes, a high basin of small glacial lakes south of the summit. Later, in the 1960s, the protected zone grew and took the name Triglav National Park. In 1981, it was enlarged again to roughly its present size. So the park you are visiting grew step by step, over several generations.

Now a story — and this one is legend, not history. Locals tell of Zlatorog, a white chamois with golden horns. A chamois is a wild mountain goat, and you may spot real ones on the cliffs. Zlatorog guarded a garden of treasure high on the mountain. A greedy hunter shot him. Where the chamois bled, a red flower grew, and it healed him at once. In fury, Zlatorog tore the green garden apart, and the high country became the bare stone you see today.

You can meet Zlatorog just outside the village, at Lake Jasna. A bronze statue of the chamois stands on a rock beside the water. The lake itself is a pair of shallow pools, coloured turquoise and green by the stone beneath. Behind it, the road starts climbing toward the Vršič Pass. On a still day, the peaks reflect in the surface. Bring a jacket, even in summer. The water here comes straight from the mountains, and the air above it stays cool.

That road over Vršič carries a sad story, and this part is well documented. During the First World War, this region was close to the front line between Austria-Hungary and Italy. Russian prisoners of war were forced to build the road through the mountains. Many died in an avalanche during the winter of 1916. Their surviving comrades built a small wooden chapel in their memory. Today it is called the Russian Chapel, and it stands among the trees on the way up the pass.

The road itself has around fifty hairpin bends, each one marked with a number. Drivers count them. Cyclists suffer through them. At the top, the pass sits at about 1,611 metres. From there, the view opens south into the Trenta Valley, where the Soča River begins. The Soča is famous for its strange colour — a bright emerald green that looks almost painted. It is one of the rivers that make this park feel so different from one side to the other.

One more mountain tradition — half history, half ritual. Near the top of Triglav stands Aljaž Tower, a small metal shelter shaped like a cylinder with a pointed roof. A priest named Jakob Aljaž placed it there in 1895. He did this at a time when German and Austrian climbing clubs were active in these mountains, and he wanted a Slovene mark on the summit. Locals also say that anyone climbing Triglav for the first time should be gently whipped with a rope. It is meant as a joke, and a welcome.

You do not need to reach the summit to feel this place. From Kranjska Gora, easy paths lead into quiet side valleys. Near the village of Gozd Martuljek, a trail climbs through the forest to a series of waterfalls. West of town, the Tamar Valley ends in a ring of high cliffs, with a mountain hut at its head. These walks are gentle compared with the high routes. But they give you the same smell of pine, the same sound of water, the same sense of stone above you.

The park is also a living place, not only wilderness. Shepherds have used the high pastures for centuries. In summer, cattle graze on alpine meadows, and simple wooden huts serve sour milk, cheese and bread to walkers. Small farming villages sit inside the park boundaries. This is part of what makes Triglav unusual. The goal here is not to keep people out. The goal is to protect the land while people continue to live and work on it.

Watch the ground as you walk. In early summer, the meadows fill with alpine flowers — gentians in deep blue, and many small plants that grow nowhere else in the region. Look up, and you may see a golden eagle turning slowly above a ridge. Stay quiet near rocky slopes, and a chamois may stand still and watch you back. The park rules are simple. Stay on the marked paths. Pick nothing. Take your rubbish home with you.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look south. The forest climbs until it thins out. Then there is only grey stone, and above that, the sky. The water at your feet started its journey somewhere up there, in snow or rain, and slipped through the rock to reach you. Breathe in the resin and the cold air. When you walk back toward the village, listen for the river. It will follow you most of the way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer even on hot summer days.
Start early to avoid traffic on the Vršič Pass road.
Wear proper hiking shoes on rocky limestone trails.
Stay on marked paths to protect fragile alpine plants.
Try sour milk and cheese at a summer pasture hut.
