The guide

The story

Before any history, notice the light. Out here it is white and hard by mid-morning, and the sandstone takes on a warm, honey colour. A dry wind moves across the gravel. Somewhere behind you, the Nile runs slow and brown past a line of date palms. Ahead, a cluster of tall columns stands against an empty sky... a temple with most of its roof gone, still holding its shape.

First, an honest note about where you are. Soleb is not at Abu Simbel. It lies several hundred kilometres further up the Nile, across the border in northern Sudan, on the river's west bank. Many travellers link the two in one Nubian journey, so the connection makes sense. But if you are standing beside Ramesses the Second's colossal statues right now, this guide describes a place a long journey to the south.

This temple belongs to the reign of Amenhotep the Third, a pharaoh of Egypt's Eighteenth Dynasty in the fourteenth century before Christ. His reign was long, wealthy and mostly peaceful. Egypt controlled Nubia then, the gold-rich land along the Nile south of the First Cataract. A cataract is a stretch of rapids and rocks that breaks up the river. Soleb sits between the Second and Third Cataracts, deep inside that conquered territory.

Here is the first surprise. The temple honoured the god Amun, chief god of the Egyptian state. But it also honoured the king himself, in a deified form. In Nubia, Amenhotep the Third was presented as a living god, a divine lord of the land. A temple like this did religious work and political work at the same time. It told local people, in carved stone, exactly who ruled them.

Now look at the columns. They are thick, rounded and set close together, as columns often are in temples of this period. Some capitals are shaped like palm fronds. Others take the form of papyrus, the marsh plant that symbolised the Nile valley for Egyptians. Even in their worn state, you can still follow the curve of the stone where the carved plant opens at the top.

The second surprise sits much lower down, so drop your eyes to the column bases. Carved around several of them are figures of bound captives. Each captive's body ends in an oval ring, and inside the ring is a place name. These rings formed a list of foreign lands and peoples. The message was simple: every one of these places lies under the feet of the king.

One name on that list has drawn particular attention from scholars. It refers to the Shasu, a nomadic people of the lands east of Egypt, linked to a place written with letters close to Yahweh. Many researchers see it as one of the earliest known references to that name, centuries before the Hebrew Bible was written down. Its exact meaning is still debated. Even so, it is a remarkable line to find on a column base in the Nubian desert.

The walls also carried scenes connected with the Sed festival. This was a royal jubilee, a ceremony that renewed the king's strength after many years on the throne. Amenhotep the Third celebrated more than one in his later years. Much of the relief work here is faint and broken now. Wind-blown sand has worn the stone for more than three thousand years. Look closely, though, and you may still pick out the outlines of figures in procession.

Another thread connects Soleb to a museum far away. Two red granite lions, now known as the Prudhoe Lions, were carved for this temple. Later rulers moved them south to Gebel Barkal, another sacred site further up the Nile. In the nineteenth century they travelled to Britain, and today they rest in the British Museum in London. So part of this temple now sits in a gallery thousands of kilometres from the sand where it began.

Modern understanding of Soleb owes a great deal to an Italian-led expedition in the mid-twentieth century. The archaeologist Michela Schiff Giorgini directed years of careful work here. Her team recorded the temple, its reliefs and the cemeteries nearby. Their published volumes remain a key source for anyone studying the site. Much of what visitors understand today comes from that patient measuring, drawing and photography.

It also helps to see this place in a wider landscape. Not far to the north lies Sedeinga, where a temple was dedicated to Queen Tiye, Amenhotep's influential wife. Together, the two sites show a royal couple honoured side by side along the river. And about a century later, Ramesses the Second carved his great temples at Abu Simbel. Soleb shows an earlier stage of the same idea: Egyptian power cut into Nubian stone.

Take a moment simply to be here. Visitors are few, and it is often very quiet. You may hear only the wind, a donkey in a nearby village, or the call to prayer drifting over the fields. The ground underfoot is a mix of sand and loose stone, so walk slowly. Shade is scarce. The columns throw narrow shadows that move across the ground as the sun climbs higher.

Before you leave, stand back from the temple and look at the whole outline again. The columns rise against the pale sky, the palms line the river behind them, and the desert runs away on either side. A king once used this place to declare himself a god. Today it is calm and almost empty. As you walk away, look back once more and notice how the sandstone changes colour as the light shifts.

Practical tips

Check current Sudan travel advisories before planning any visit.
