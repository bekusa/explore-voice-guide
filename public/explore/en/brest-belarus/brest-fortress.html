The guide

The story

Before you see anything, you may hear it. Near the main entrance, a low sound comes from hidden speakers: a wartime song, and the crackle of an old radio voice. Underfoot, the concrete is cool and grey. Ahead, a huge five-pointed star is cut straight through a long wall of concrete. You walk into the fortress through the middle of that star, and the light changes as you pass through.

This is Brest Fortress, in the far southwest of Belarus, close to the Polish border. It stands where two rivers meet, the Bug and the Mukhavets. Water is the key to the whole design. The engineers used the rivers and a ring of canals to divide the ground into islands. Each island became its own defensive zone. The result is a fortress made as much of water as of brick.

The fortress was built in the nineteenth century, when this land belonged to the Russian Empire. Work began in the 1830s. Here is something many visitors never learn. An old town once stood on this exact ground. To clear space for the fortress, the authorities pulled it down, and the town was rebuilt a short distance to the east. The modern city of Brest grew from that move. So the streets you drove through to get here are, in a sense, the town that was relocated.

At the centre sits the Citadel, on the central island. Its long ring of barracks, built in dark red brick, once housed thousands of soldiers. Around it lie three more fortified islands. They carry the names of the directions they faced: Kobrin, Volhynia, and Terespol. Beyond those ran earthen ramparts and outer forts. Walk the perimeter, and you start to feel how carefully every approach was planned.

One of the best places to see the old brickwork is the Kholm Gate, on the side of the Citadel. Look closely at its face. The towers and the arches are decorated in a neo-Gothic style, which borrows the pointed shapes of medieval castles. But the surface is covered in pits and scars. Many of those marks come from bullets and shell fragments. The decoration and the damage sit side by side on the same wall.

Now, a surprise from a very different war. In March 1918, a treaty was signed inside this fortress. It is known as the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk. With it, the new Soviet government took Russia out of the First World War, and gave up large areas of territory. The signing took place in a building known as the White Palace. Today only its ruined foundations remain, near the centre of the Citadel. It is a quiet spot, easy to walk past.

Between the two world wars, this area belonged to Poland, and the fortress served as a garrison and also as a prison. In 1939, at the start of the Second World War, the region passed to Soviet control. The fortress changed flags several times in just a few decades. That history is layered into the walls, even where no sign explains it.

The story most people come here for begins before dawn on the 22nd of June, 1941. Germany launched its invasion of the Soviet Union, and this fortress stood almost on the border. It was among the first places hit. The garrison was caught by surprise. Many soldiers were asleep, and families of officers lived inside the walls. Yet groups of defenders fought on, cut off, for days and then weeks. Some pockets held out far longer than anyone expected.

Water became the cruel detail of that siege. The rivers were all around, but reaching them meant crossing open ground under fire. Defenders suffered terribly from thirst in the summer heat. One sculpture here shows exactly that. It is called Thirst. A soldier crawls toward the water, holding out his helmet to fill it. It is one of the most personal works in the whole memorial, and many visitors stop here longest.

Inside the barracks, people later found words scratched into the brick by the defenders. The most famous inscription says, in Russian, that the writer is dying but will not surrender, and it says farewell to the motherland. It carries a date in July 1941. Those few words became central to how the defence is remembered. The fortress museum, inside the old barracks, holds the personal objects and documents connected to that summer.

After the war, the Soviet state gave Brest the title of Hero Fortress, and in the early 1970s a large memorial complex opened. Its centrepiece is the monument called Courage. It is an enormous stone head of a soldier, with a furrowed brow, rising from a block of stone. Beside it stands a tall steel obelisk shaped like a bayonet. An eternal flame burns nearby. The scale is deliberate. It is meant to make you feel small.

There is also a church here. The Saint Nicholas garrison church once served the soldiers of the fortress. In the Soviet years it was used for other purposes. Today it is an Orthodox church again, with services held inside. You may see people lighting candles, or hear singing through the doors. It is a reminder that this is not only a museum. For many local families, it is a place of personal memory.

Before you leave, stand for a moment near the water. Listen to the rivers moving slowly past the brick, the birds in the trees, the footsteps on the paths. The quiet here was hard to earn. As you walk back out through the great concrete star, look once more at the scarred red walls behind you, and carry them with you into the town.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least half a day to walk the islands.
Wear comfortable shoes for long paths and uneven ground.
Visit the barracks museum before exploring the outdoor memorials.
Dress modestly if you plan to enter Saint Nicholas church.
Keep voices low near the eternal flame and graves.
