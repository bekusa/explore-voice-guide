The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through tall grass, and the stalks rub together with a sound like paper. Somewhere a bird calls twice, then stops. The ground under your feet is red laterite, hard and dusty, and the light here falls flat and golden across open woodland. This is Kainji National Park, one of the largest protected landscapes in Nigeria. It lies to the west of Kontagora, in the middle belt of the country, where the north's dry country meets the greener south.

Kainji is often described as Nigeria's first national park. It gained that status in the late 1970s, when two older game reserves were joined under a single protected name. Those reserves still shape how the park works today. Each sector has its own character, its own roads, and its own wildlife. Together they cover more than five thousand square kilometres of savanna, woodland, river, and lake shore. That is larger than many small countries' entire protected networks.

The two sectors are Borgu and Zugurma. Borgu is the larger one, lying to the west of the lake, toward the border with the Republic of Benin. Zugurma lies to the east, closer to Kontagora and the wider plains of Niger State. Between them sits a wide body of water. That water is the key to understanding this whole place. It looks natural from the shore. It is not. It is one of the largest artificial lakes in West Africa.

In the 1960s, engineers built a dam across the Niger River, the great river that runs from the highlands of Guinea through Mali and Niger before it enters Nigeria. The Kainji Dam was completed toward the end of that decade. Its purpose was hydroelectric power for a young, independent nation. Behind the concrete wall, the river spread out and filled the valley. The result was Kainji Lake, a long sheet of water that now divides the park in two.

Here is something most visitors never hear. The rising water covered more than fields and forest. It also covered the old town of Bussa, an ancient seat of the Borgu kingdom. Its people were moved to a new settlement, which is why the main town near the park is called New Bussa. And close to old Bussa, in 1806, the Scottish explorer Mungo Park died in the river's rapids. Those rapids now lie under the lake, out of sight and out of reach.

So when you look across that calm water, you are looking at a landscape with layers. Below the surface lie old homes, old paths, and the rocks where an explorer's journey ended. Above it, fishermen work from narrow boats, and birds hunt along the edges. The lake also supports a research institute at New Bussa, which studies fisheries and freshwater life. Science, history, and daily work all share this same shoreline.

Now turn your attention to the land. Much of Borgu is Guinea savanna, a mix of grassland and scattered trees. The trees are not tall and dense like a rainforest. They stand apart, with open ground between them. In the dry season the grass turns pale and brittle, and fires sometimes move through it. In the rains, everything turns green very fast. The same view can look like two different countries, depending on the month you come.

The Oli River runs through the Borgu sector, and it matters a great deal to the animals here. In the dry months, when pools shrink and streams vanish, the Oli holds water. Animals come to it in the early morning and late afternoon. Guides often plan routes along its banks for exactly this reason. If you want to see wildlife, you follow the water. That rule works almost everywhere in African savanna, and it works here too.

What might you see? Kainji is home to several kinds of antelope, including the roan, a large and powerful antelope with backward-curving horns, and the kob, a smaller reddish antelope that grazes near water. Hartebeest, with their long faces, also live here. Baboons move through the woodland in noisy groups. Hippos rest in the rivers and in parts of the lake during the heat of the day. Lions have been recorded in the park, though they are rare and seldom seen.

The birdlife is easier to find. Wetlands and lake margins draw herons, kingfishers, and many kinds of waterbirds, especially in the dry season. In the woodland you may hear hornbills before you see them, a hollow, rhythmic call from high branches. Even if large mammals stay hidden, a patient visitor with binoculars can fill a notebook with birds. That is one of the quiet rewards of this park.

It is honest to say that Kainji faces real pressures. Like many protected areas in West Africa, it has lost wildlife to poaching, grazing, and farming at its edges. Some animals that once lived here in good numbers are now scarce. Conservation groups and park staff continue to work on protection and monitoring. A visit here is not a guaranteed safari parade. It is a slower experience, closer to a walk through a working, fragile landscape.

It also helps to understand the people around the park. The Borgu area has a long royal history, and its traditional rulers still hold respect in local life. Towns near the park depend on farming, fishing, and trade. Many families have lived beside this river for generations, long before the dam and long before the park. When you pass through New Bussa or the villages near Kontagora, you are passing through their home, not only a gateway to wildlife.

Before you leave, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Notice the colour of the soil on your shoes. Notice how far the horizon sits, low and wide. If you are near the water, watch the light move on the surface of the lake that hides a drowned town. If you are in the grass, listen again for that dry rustle from the beginning. The park does not perform for visitors. It simply continues, and for a short while, you are part of it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current travel advisories before planning your visit.
Arrange an official park guide through park headquarters.
Go out early morning or late afternoon for wildlife.
Bring binoculars for birds along the lake shore.
Carry plenty of water, sun protection, and a hat.
