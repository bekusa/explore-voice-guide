The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is wind moving through birch leaves, a crow somewhere behind you, and the faint creak of metal settling in the cold. Underfoot, cracked concrete gives way to moss and young trees. Above all of it rises a yellow Ferris wheel, its paint faded and flaking, its cabins hanging still. No music plays here. No children shout. And yet everything around you was built for exactly that sound.

You are standing in the amusement park of Pripyat, a town in the north of Kyiv Oblast, close to the border with Belarus. Pripyat was founded in 1970 as a home for the workers of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant and their families. It was a young town, in every sense. The average resident was young, many families had small children, and the planners built schools, swimming pools, shops and a cultural centre to match.

This small park sits just behind the Palace of Culture, near the town's central square. It was meant to be a gift for the whole community. The rides were installed in the spring of 1986, and the opening was planned for the first of May. In the Soviet Union, May Day was a major public holiday, with parades, flags and family outings. A new Ferris wheel would have made a fine centrepiece for the celebration.

That celebration never came. In the early hours of 26 April 1986, reactor number four at the Chernobyl plant exploded during a safety test. The plant lies only a few kilometres from where you stand. At first, life in Pripyat continued almost normally. People went to work, children played outside, and many residents did not understand the danger in the air around them.

On the afternoon of 27 April, the authorities evacuated the town. Around fifty thousand people boarded long columns of buses. They were told to bring documents and a few belongings, and many believed they would return within days. Most never came back to live here again. The rides in this park stayed exactly where they were, waiting for a holiday that was cancelled by history.

Here is a detail many visitors do not know. Accounts differ about whether the park ever ran at all. Some former residents say the rides were switched on briefly on 27 April, perhaps to calm people before the evacuation. Others say the wheel never turned with passengers. There is no clear record either way. So when you look at the cabins, you are looking at a question that still has no firm answer.

Now turn your attention to the wheel itself. Its frame is simple and strong, made of steel painted in bright yellow. The cabins are small and open-sided, with seats for a few riders each. Decades of rain and frost have worn the paint down to rust in many places. Look at the joints and bolts — they show the slow, patient work of weather on metal that no one has maintained for a very long time.

Nearby you will find the bumper cars. Their shallow arena still has its metal roof frame and the low barrier around the edge. Several small cars sit inside, some tipped at odd angles, covered in leaves and dirt. Close by stand the swing boats and the remains of a carousel. Each ride is modest in size. This was never a grand theme park. It was a neighbourhood park for local families.

And this is the second surprise. The most dangerous spots here are often the quiet green ones. Moss is very good at collecting radioactive particles from rain and dust. Over the years, some patches of moss around the rides have shown much higher readings than the open concrete nearby. Guides with dosimeters often stop at these patches to show visitors how sharply the numbers can rise in a single step.

Look around at the trees. When the town was evacuated, this area was open and paved. Today birch, poplar and pine grow through cracks in the ground and crowd against the rides. Nature has moved back faster than many people expect. In the wider exclusion zone, animals such as wolves, deer and wild horses have been recorded. Here, the forest is slowly closing in around the yellow wheel.

Over the decades, this park became a symbol of the disaster around the world. Photographers, filmmakers and writers came here to capture it. The Ferris wheel even appeared in a popular video game set in Pripyat. For many people who never visited Ukraine, this wheel is the first image they think of when they hear the word Chernobyl. It speaks to them because it is so ordinary — a fairground ride, frozen.

It is important to know how much the situation here has changed. The exclusion zone has always required official permission and an authorised guide. In early 2022, during the Russian invasion of Ukraine, the zone was occupied for several weeks, and access for visitors has been heavily restricted since then. If you are here, you are here under strict conditions. Follow your guide closely, stay on the paths, and do not touch the rides.

Before you leave, take one more slow look. Notice the stillness of the cabins, the colour of the paint against the grey sky, the green moss along the edges of the concrete. Think of the families who expected to stand in this same spot on a sunny May morning. Then listen again to the wind in the trees — the only sound this park has really known — as you walk back toward the square.
