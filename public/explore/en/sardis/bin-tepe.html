The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through dry grass. A tractor hums somewhere far off. A dog barks from a farmhouse. Around you, the land rises and falls in soft, rounded swells, like a sea of earth that froze mid-wave. Some of these swells are low and easy to miss. Others climb high above the fields. None of them are natural hills. Every one was raised by human hands, over the body of someone who mattered.

This is Bin Tepe, a name that means "a thousand hills" in Turkish. The number is poetic, not exact. Archaeologists have counted more than a hundred burial mounds here, and many smaller ones have been ploughed flat over the centuries. The plateau sits north of ancient Sardis, across the valley of the Hermus River, which Turks today call the Gediz. To the north lies a lake. Together they frame the royal cemetery of the Lydians.

Who were the Lydians? They were a people of western Anatolia, the land that is now Turkey. Their capital was Sardis, and their kingdom rose to great power in the seventh and sixth centuries before the common era. Their wealth became legend. The Greek historian Herodotus wrote that the Lydians were the first people to strike coins of gold and silver. Whether or not they were truly first, their name became tied to riches for thousands of years.

The richest of all was Croesus, the last king of Lydia. People still say "as rich as Croesus" today. But the giant mound that dominates this landscape is not his. It belongs, by long tradition and strong evidence, to his father, Alyattes. Look for the largest hill on the horizon — the one that seems too big to be made by people. That is most likely it. Locals know it as Kocamutaf Tepe.

Herodotus visited this region, or at least spoke with people who knew it well. He described the tomb of Alyattes as one of the greatest works of human hands. In his view, only the monuments of Egypt and Babylon were larger. Stand near the base and you can understand him. The mound is so broad that walking around it takes real time. From a distance it looks like a part of the geology. Up close, it feels like a deliberate challenge to the sky.

Here is a detail most visitors never hear. Herodotus wrote that the mound was built by three groups: merchants, craftsmen, and young working women. He claimed that stone markers on top recorded how much each group had built, and that the women's share was the largest. He also wrote that Lydian girls earned their own dowries this way. Treat this as an ancient traveller's report, not a verified fact. But it shows how strange and remarkable this place seemed even to the Greeks.

So what lies inside these hills? In the 1850s, a Prussian consul named Spiegelthal dug tunnels into the great mound. He found a burial chamber built from carefully cut marble blocks. The workmanship was fine and precise. But the chamber was empty of treasure. Robbers had found it long before him, probably in antiquity. This is the quiet story of almost every tomb at Bin Tepe. The kings were buried with splendour, and the splendour did not stay.

In the twentieth century, a joint American team from Harvard and Cornell began long-term work at Sardis, and they turned to these mounds too. They studied another huge tumulus, known locally as Karnıyarık Tepe. Some scholars link it to Gyges, the founder of the dynasty that ended with Croesus. Researchers tunnelled deep inside and found a curving stone wall buried within the earth. Its exact meaning is still discussed. The central burial itself has remained elusive, and the mound keeps part of its secret.

Pause and think about how these mounds were made. There were no machines. Earth was carried in baskets, packed down in layers, and shaped. At the core lay a stone chamber, then the soil rose over it. Some mounds had a ring of cut stone around the base. The work must have taken huge numbers of people over long periods. A tumulus was not only a grave. It was a public statement of power, visible for many kilometres.

That visibility was the point. Turn south and look across the valley. On a clear day you can see the rugged ridge of the Sardis acropolis and the slopes of Mount Tmolus behind it. From the city, the people of Sardis could look north and see their royal dead on the skyline. The kings watched over the city, and the city watched over them. The river valley between them held the farms and roads that fed the kingdom.

Today, the scene is far more ordinary, and that is part of its charm. There are no grand gates or ticket halls at most of these mounds. Farmland runs right up to their sides. Shepherds move flocks between them. A village road may curve around a royal tomb as if it were any other hill. Many mounds show scars and holes from old looting. They are reminders that treasure hunting here did not stop with the ancient world.

The lake to the north adds another layer. In ancient times it was called the Gygaean Lake, a name connected to the same royal line as Gyges. Today it is known as Marmara Gölü. Its water level has changed over the years, and the shoreline you see may not match the ancient one. On calm days the light flattens across its surface. Birds gather along the edges. It softens the hard, dry outline of the burial plateau.

Now, look again at the mound nearest to you. Notice how grass and wildflowers have claimed its slopes. Notice the soft line where its base meets the field. Somewhere under that earth lies a room of cut stone, built for a person whose name may be lost. As you walk back to the road, listen to the wind once more. It moves across these hills the same way it did when the Lydian kings were laid to rest here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come by car; the mounds are spread across rural roads.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, grassy, sometimes muddy ground.
Bring water and a sun hat; shade is very scarce.
Respect farmers' fields and stay on existing tracks.
View the mounds from Sardis acropolis for the full scale.
