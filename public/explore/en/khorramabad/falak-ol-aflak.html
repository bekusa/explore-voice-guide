The guide

The story

Start with your hand on the wall, if you can reach it. The surface is rough and warm, a mix of fired brick, rubble stone and old mortar. Above you, round towers rise from the top of a steep hill. Below, the city of Khorramabad carries on with car horns, traffic and the call to prayer. Up here the sound softens. You are standing at the oldest working landmark in this valley, a fortress that has watched over it for well over a thousand years.

This is Falak-ol-Aflak. The name is usually translated as something like the heaven of heavens, or the sky above all skies. It is a grand name for a castle, but stand at its base and look up. The walls seem to grow straight out of the rock, and on a clear day the towers sit against open blue. The name makes sense from here. Older sources often call the site by another name, Shapurkhast, the fortress of Shapur.

That older name points to its origins. Most historians date the castle to the Sasanian era, the last great Persian empire before the arrival of Islam, which ruled from the third to the seventh century. The Sasanians built strongholds along the routes through the Zagros Mountains, the long range that separates the Iranian plateau from the lowlands of Mesopotamia. Khorramabad sat on one of those routes. A fortress on this hill could watch the road, the river and the valley at the same time.

Now look at the shape of the castle. It is an irregular outline that follows the edge of the hill rather than a perfect square. Eight round towers survive today, joined by thick curtain walls. Accounts often say there were once more towers, perhaps twelve, along an outer ring of walls that has mostly vanished. The towers you see are solid and broad at the base. They were built to resist siege, not to impress, and that plain strength is part of their power.

Here is a detail that many visitors walk past. A fortress on a dry hilltop has one great weakness, which is water. The builders solved it by cutting a deep well down through the rock to reach a spring below. During a siege, defenders could draw water without leaving the walls. That well is the quiet reason this castle could hold out. Without it, the towers and walls above would have meant very little.

After the Sasanian period, the fortress changed hands many times. In the medieval centuries it served as a seat of power for local rulers of Lorestan, the mountain province around you. Later governments used it as a garrison and an administrative centre. Each new owner repaired what was broken and added what they needed. So the walls you see are not from one single moment. They are layers of work from many centuries, stacked one on top of another.

One chapter surprises many people. In the twentieth century, this ancient fortress was used as a prison. Its thick walls and single approach made it easy to guard. For decades, people from the city looked up at the towers and thought of cells, not kings. Only later did the government turn the castle over to heritage authorities. After restoration, it opened to the public as a historic monument and museum complex.

Inside, the castle holds the museums of Lorestan, and they are worth your time. The archaeological galleries display the famous Lorestan bronzes. These are small cast objects made in this region roughly three thousand years ago, during the Iron Age. You will see horse bits, decorated pins, weapons and strange figures with animal heads and human bodies. Collectors around the world prize them, and this is one of the best places to see them close to their home.

Look closely at the bronze horse gear in particular. The people who made these objects lived with horses, and their artists shaped the animals with real care. Some pieces show creatures that seem to belong to a myth we no longer fully understand. Scholars still debate exactly who made them and what many of the symbols meant. Standing here, you are only a short drive from the valleys where many of these pieces were first found.

The castle also holds an ethnographic section about the life of the Lur people, the main community of this province. You can see traditional clothing, woven textiles, household tools and scenes of nomadic and village life. The Lurs have their own language and a long tradition of music and poetry. These rooms give you a sense of the people who have lived in the shadow of this fortress for generations. In a way, they are the castle's oldest neighbours.

When you reach the upper courtyards, walk slowly to the edge and look out. The city spreads across the valley floor, with the mountains of the Zagros on every side. The Khorramabad River runs below the hill, lined with trees. You can see how small the valley really is, and why one strong hill could control it. Few views explain military history so clearly in one glance.

The light here changes the castle more than you might expect. In the morning, the walls look pale and dusty. In the late afternoon, the brick turns a warm honey colour, and the towers throw long shadows down the slope. At night, floodlights wash the walls in gold, and the castle seems to float above the city. Many residents say the night view from the streets below is their favourite way to see it.

As you walk, notice the people around you. Families climb the path together, students sketch the towers, and older visitors rest on the benches. For the people of Khorramabad, this is not a remote ruin. It is the centre of their city, a place for weekend walks and photographs. That everyday use is part of its story too. A fortress built for war has become a place where people meet in peace.

Before you leave, stop once more and put your hand on the wall. Feel the rough brick and the small stones pressed into the mortar. Some of that material may have been placed by Sasanian builders, some by medieval masons, some by modern restorers. All of it still holds together. As you walk down the hill, turn back once or twice. The towers stay in view for a long time, as they have for every traveller who has passed through this valley.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb in late afternoon for warm light on the brick.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep approach path.
Return after dark to see the floodlit walls.
Allow extra time for the bronze galleries inside.
Walk to the river below for a full view upward.
