The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, waves turn over pebbles in a small bay. Behind you, the wind moves through olive trees and banana leaves. Under your feet, low walls of pale stone run between patches of reeds and dry grass. This is Kato Zakros, at the far eastern edge of Crete. It feels quiet and remote today, but for the Minoans this shore faced outward, toward the busiest sea routes of the ancient world.

You are standing in the ruins of a Bronze Age palace. The Minoans were the people who built the first great civilisation on Crete, long before classical Greece. Archaeologists usually speak of four major Minoan palaces. Knossos and Phaistos are the famous ones. Malia sits on the north coast. Zakros is the smallest of the four, and the most far-flung. But it holds a story that the others cannot tell.

That story begins with a near miss. In 1901, the British archaeologist David Hogarth dug in this valley. He uncovered Minoan houses close to where you stand. Then he moved on. The palace itself lay under the earth, just metres from his trenches. For around sixty more years, nobody knew it was here. Local farmers worked the land above it and grew crops on top of a buried royal building.

In the early 1960s, the Greek archaeologist Nikolaos Platon began excavating here. What he found surprised everyone. Most Minoan palaces had been searched, robbed or picked over in later centuries. Zakros had not. When the palace was destroyed, it seems nobody came back to dig through the rubble. So its storerooms and treasuries still held their contents — stone vases, bronze tools, pottery, and objects of rare beauty.

Take a moment to look at the layout. The palace is built around a central rectangular court, the open heart of every Minoan palace. Rooms cluster around it in wings. To the west were the ceremonial and religious spaces. To other sides were kitchens, workshops and storerooms. Try to picture walls standing two storeys high, plastered and painted, with light falling into the court below. What you see now are the foundations and the lower courses of stone.

Some of the most celebrated finds came from a small treasury in the western wing. Here, excavators found a vessel carved from a single block of rock crystal, clear as glass. It is a ritual pouring vessel, the kind archaeologists call a rhyton. Another stone rhyton was carved with a scene of a mountain shrine, with goats on its roof. Both pieces are now in the Archaeological Museum of Heraklion. If you travel west across the island, it is worth looking for them there.

The palace also kept records. Excavators found clay tablets written in Linear A, the script of the Minoans. Here is the surprise — nobody can read it yet. Scholars can recognise some signs and numbers, so these tablets were likely lists of goods. But the language behind the script is still unknown. Somewhere in these ruins, people kept accounts that we can see but still cannot understand.

Now look toward the water. Zakros was a port palace. Its bay faces east, toward Cyprus, the coast of the Levant, and Egypt. Ships could leave from here for the eastern Mediterranean. Among the finds were elephant tusks, raw material that must have come from overseas. Ivory, copper and luxury goods likely passed through this small harbour. That is probably why a palace was built so far from the rest of Crete.

Here is one of the strangest details of the site. Since Minoan times, the land and the water table have shifted. Parts of the palace now sit low and wet, especially around the old cistern and spring chambers. Water collects in these stone basins. Look closely and you may see terrapins — small freshwater turtles — swimming or resting among the ancient blocks. They have made a royal water system into their home.

That water holds another story. When Platon's team cleared a well inside the palace, they found a cup at the bottom. Inside it were olives. They had stayed under the water for thousands of years, and they still kept their shape. Perhaps someone left them there as an offering in the last days of the palace. We do not know. But it is a very human detail in a place of great ceremony.

The end of the palace came suddenly, in the fifteenth century before Christ. Zakros, like other Minoan centres, was destroyed and burned. Scholars still debate the causes. Some point to earthquakes, some to the after-effects of the great eruption on Santorini, some to invaders or unrest. The palace was never rebuilt. Its sudden end is the reason so much survived for archaeologists to find.

If you came here by road, you passed through the village of Zakros above. Between the village and the coast runs a deep gorge, known as the Gorge of the Dead. The name comes from the caves in its cliffs. The Minoans used them as burial places. A walking path follows the gorge down to this bay, through oleander and rock. Many visitors walk it and end their day here, at the sea.

Before you leave, stand still in the central court one more time. Notice the colour of the stone and the line of the hills. Notice how close the sea is, and how small the bay feels. Three and a half thousand years ago, people stood on this same ground and watched ships come in. As you walk back toward the shore, listen again to the pebbles in the waves. They are the same sound the Minoans heard.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring water and sun protection; shade on site is limited.
Walk the Gorge of the Dead down to the bay.
Visit Heraklion Archaeological Museum to see the Zakros finds.
Combine your visit with a swim in the bay.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone paths.
