The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves slap against limestone. Oars knock against wooden hulls. A boatman calls out in Neapolitan dialect to another boatman a few metres away. The cliff above you is pale and dry, and the sea at its foot is dark. Somewhere in that rock face there is an opening barely a metre high. In a few minutes you will lie flat in a small rowing boat and slide through it, into a cave where the water glows blue.

This is the Blue Grotto, the Grotta Azzurra, on the northwest coast of Capri. The cave sits within the territory of Anacapri, the island's higher and quieter town. From the outside it looks like nothing much. There is a narrow notch at the waterline, a metal chain fixed to the rock, and a cluster of boats waiting their turn. That plain exterior is part of the surprise. Almost nothing on the surface tells you what waits inside.

The entry has its own small ritual. You move from a larger boat into a little wooden rowing boat that holds only a few people. The boatman asks you to sit low, then lower, then lie back. He watches the swell. When the water drops, he grabs the chain above the opening and pulls hard. The boat shoots through the gap in one movement. For a second there is rock just above your face... and then there is space.

Your eyes need a moment. The cave is dark above, a dim vault of rock. But the water beneath you is lit from below, a clear and electric blue. It looks less like water and more like light that you could touch. The boatmen often sing here, usually old Neapolitan songs, and the voice bounces off the stone. The sound is close and warm, and the boat rocks gently while your eyes adjust.

So where does the blue come from? The answer sits under your boat. Below the small entrance there is a much larger opening, fully underwater. Sunlight passes into the sea outside, travels through the water, and enters the cave from beneath. Water absorbs red light quickly, so mostly blue light survives the journey. That filtered light then rises into the cave. The narrow upper entrance lets in very little direct daylight, so the glowing water is the brightest thing you see.

Watch the boatman's oar, or any hand that dips below the surface. Under the water, objects take on a pale, silvery shine. Tiny air bubbles cling to them and catch the light. Many visitors miss this detail because they are looking up at the vault. Look down instead. An oar blade can shine like polished metal just beneath the surface. Then lift it out, and it is plain wet wood again.

Long before tour boats, this cave belonged to the world of Rome. In the first century, the emperor Tiberius spent his final years on Capri and built several villas across the island. Historians believe the grotto was used in Roman times as a nymphaeum. A nymphaeum is a sacred grotto or fountain shrine dedicated to water spirits. It could also serve as an elegant retreat for wealthy people. Traces of Roman stonework have been identified inside the cave.

The strongest evidence came from the seabed. In the twentieth century, divers recovered Roman marble statues from the cave floor. They include figures linked to the sea, such as Neptune, the Roman god of the sea, and Triton, his son. Scholars think these statues once stood around the edge of the cave, perhaps half in the water. Some of these finds are now on display in Anacapri, in a building called the Casa Rossa, the Red House. You can visit them later, up on dry land.

After Rome, the cave drifted out of fashion. Local folklore filled the gap. Legend has it that island fishermen avoided the grotto because they believed witches or evil spirits lived inside. Whether people truly feared the cave or simply had no reason to row into it, we cannot say for certain. But the stories gave the place a dark reputation. For centuries the blue light shone here with very few people to see it.

That changed in 1826. A German writer and poet named August Kopisch and his friend, the painter Ernst Fries, were taken into the cave by a local fisherman. Kopisch wrote about the experience, and his account spread across Europe. Writers, painters and travellers on the Grand Tour began to come. The Grand Tour was a long educational journey through Italy that wealthy young Europeans made in that era. Soon the grotto became one of the most famous sights in the Bay of Naples.

Take a moment to look around the cave itself. The walls are rough, wet limestone, darker near the waterline. Toward the back, the cave narrows, and people often point to a passage that may link to Roman works in the rock. Keep in mind that the visit inside is short. Boats move in a slow loop, and then the boatman turns back toward the light. Use the quiet seconds well, and do not spend them all behind a phone screen.

The sea controls everything here. When waves are high or the tide is up, the entrance becomes too dangerous, and the grotto closes. This happens often, even on sunny days, so a visit is never fully guaranteed. Many people arrive by motorboat from Marina Grande, the island's main harbour. Others come by road from Anacapri and walk down stone steps to the water. Either way, you join the same line of little rowing boats at the cliff.

Now you are back outside, perhaps blinking in the bright sun. Look at the cliff again. It is just a pale wall of rock with a small dark gap at the bottom. Listen to the oars, the gulls, the low engines of the bigger boats. Underneath all of it, the light is still coming in through that hidden opening below the waves. As you move away, turn once more and look at the water near the entrance. Even out here, it carries a faint trace of that blue.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check sea conditions first; rough water closes the grotto.
Come late morning for the strongest blue light.
Bring small cash to tip your rowing boatman.
Follow the boatman's signal to lie flat at the entrance.
See the Roman statues afterwards at Anacapri's Casa Rossa.
