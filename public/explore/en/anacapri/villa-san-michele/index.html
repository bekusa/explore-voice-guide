The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, birds are calling from the pines. The air carries rosemary and warm stone. Underfoot, the path is smooth and cool, and ahead of you a white villa rises at the edge of a cliff. Beyond it there is only blue: the sky, the sea, and the long curve of the Gulf of Naples. You are standing at Villa San Michele, high above the island of Capri.

This house exists because of one man's stubborn dream. Axel Munthe was a Swedish doctor. He first came to Capri as a young man in the 1870s. He climbed up to Anacapri, the higher of the island's two towns, and he saw a ruined chapel on this ridge. He decided then that one day he would build a home here. Years later, he did exactly that.

Munthe was not a quiet country doctor. He worked in Paris and in Rome, and his patients included wealthy and powerful people. He later served as physician to the Swedish royal family. But he also treated the poor, sometimes without payment. People who knew him described him as generous, restless, and difficult to pin down. This villa is the place where all those sides of him came together.

Construction began in the late nineteenth century. Munthe did not hire a famous architect to design a grand palace. Instead, he worked closely with local builders from Anacapri. Many of them could not read plans. So the house grew room by room, arch by arch, following his ideas and their skill with stone and lime. That is why the villa feels personal rather than formal. Nothing here is quite symmetrical.

And here is the first surprise. Munthe built on top of the past. When workers dug the foundations, they found pieces of ancient Rome — marble fragments, carved stones, bits of columns. Capri was a favourite retreat of Roman emperors, and Roman villas once stood across the island. Munthe kept what he found. As you walk through the rooms, look for these old pieces set into walls, placed on tables, and lined along the garden paths.

Inside, the rooms are cool and dim after the bright sun. The walls are white. The ceilings are low and vaulted. Dark wooden furniture stands against the plaster, and old objects sit in niches and on shelves. Some are ancient. Some are medieval. Some came from antique markets. Munthe mixed them freely, with no strict museum logic. The result feels less like a collection and more like the home of a man who simply loved beautiful old things.

Now step outside to the pergola. This is the heart of the villa. A long walkway runs along the cliff edge, shaded by vines and supported by white columns. On one side, there are flowers and greenery. On the other side, there is a sheer drop and the whole bay below. On a clear day, you can see the Sorrento peninsula, and the dark shape of Vesuvius across the water. Walk slowly here. This view is what Munthe built the house around.

At the far end of the terrace, a stone sphinx sits on the low wall. It looks out to sea, its back turned to the house. It is an ancient Egyptian piece, carved from dark granite. Munthe wrote that the sphinx came to him almost by fate. Locals and guides share a popular tradition: place your left hand on the sphinx, make a wish, and keep it secret. Legend says the wish will come true. You will notice how many hands have already polished the stone.

The second surprise is a book. In 1929, Munthe published a memoir called The Story of San Michele. It mixed real memories with storytelling, and it was not always strictly accurate. But readers loved it. The book became an international bestseller and was translated into many languages. For decades, people around the world first heard of Anacapri through its pages. Many visitors came here simply because they had read it.

There is a sad irony in this place. Munthe built a house filled with light — and then his eyes began to fail. Bright sunshine became painful for him. In his later years, he moved to a darker tower elsewhere in Anacapri, where the light was softer. He died in Sweden in 1949. Before his death, he left the villa to the Swedish state. Today a Swedish foundation cares for the house and the gardens.

Munthe also loved animals, and especially birds. In his time, many migrating birds were trapped on Capri every spring. This troubled him deeply. He bought land on the mountain behind the villa, called Monte Barbarossa, to protect the birds. That hillside is still a nature reserve today. So when you hear birdsong in the garden, remember that it is part of his legacy too.

Before you leave, look down toward the coast. Not far from the villa, an old stairway called the Phoenician Steps runs down toward the harbour of Marina Grande. Its name is old, though its true origins are uncertain. For centuries, it was one of the main routes up from the sea. Munthe climbed those steps when he first arrived. In his book, he describes the climb, and the first sight of this ridge.

So take one more moment on the terrace. Feel the breeze off the sea. Notice the white columns, the dark sphinx, the red geraniums, and the deep blue far below. As you walk back through the garden toward the streets of Anacapri, listen again for the birds in the pines. They are still here, on the mountain one man chose to protect, above the house he dreamed of as a young man.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to enjoy the pergola before the crowds.
Take the bus up to Anacapri from Capri town.
Wear comfortable shoes for stone paths and garden steps.
Bring sunglasses; the white terrace reflects strong sunlight.
Choose a clear day to see Vesuvius across the bay.
