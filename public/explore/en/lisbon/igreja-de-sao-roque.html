The guide

The story

Step in from the bright little square and let your eyes adjust. The air is cooler here, and quieter. Light falls from high windows onto dark wood, cold marble, and a long run of gold. Outside, the façade looked almost severe, plain stone with very little ornament. Inside, the walls turn warm and glittering. That contrast is the first surprise of this church, and it is deliberate.

You are standing in the Igreja de São Roque, high on the slopes of Lisbon, in the parish of Misericórdia, at the edge of the Bairro Alto. This is the old upper town, a grid of steep streets that fills with voices after dark. The church sits a short walk from the Glória funicular and the viewpoint of São Pedro de Alcântara. Many visitors pass the plain front without stopping. They miss one of the most richly decorated interiors in Portugal.

The story begins with fear of disease. In the early sixteenth century, plague moved through Lisbon. The city turned to Saint Roch, a medieval holy man linked to healing the sick. Roch is often shown with a dog at his side and a wound on his leg. A modest shrine to him stood on this hill, outside the old city walls. A relic of the saint was brought from Venice to strengthen local devotion, and the name stayed with the site.

Then a new religious order arrived. The Jesuits, the Society of Jesus, came to Portugal in the 1540s with royal support. They took over this site and built a much larger church here. It became one of the earliest Jesuit churches anywhere in the world. Its plan was designed for one main purpose: preaching. Look around you. There is a single wide nave, with no rows of columns blocking the view. Every person in the congregation could see and hear the pulpit.

Now look up. The ceiling above you is painted wood, and it was painted to trick the eye. Arches, columns, and openings seem to rise into the sky. In fact the surface is almost flat. This technique is called trompe l'oeil, which means "deceive the eye" in French. Painted ceilings of this period rarely survive in Lisbon. Fire and the great earthquake destroyed most of them. This one stayed.

That earthquake is central to the story of the whole city. On the morning of 1 November 1755, a massive earthquake struck Lisbon. Fires and a tsunami followed. Churches collapsed during morning services across the city. São Roque stood on firmer ground than the lower districts. The church suffered damage, but much of its interior survived. That is why you can still see work here that vanished almost everywhere else in Lisbon.

Walk slowly along the side chapels. Each one is a small world of carved and gilded wood. In Portugal this gold-covered woodwork is called talha dourada. It was often paid for with gold from colonial Brazil. Look for the relic chapels too. They hold rows of small reliquaries, decorated containers for fragments of saints. The Jesuits collected relics with real passion. Displayed together, they once gave the faithful a sense of heaven gathered in one room.

In the chapel dedicated to Saint Roch himself, look closely at the tiles on the walls. These are azulejos, the painted ceramic tiles that cover so much of Portugal. These ones are older than the blue and white tiles you may know from the streets outside. Their colours are warmer, with yellows, greens, and blues. They are among the oldest dated tiles in Lisbon, from the late sixteenth century. Find the dates and the name of the tile painter worked into the design.

Now comes the chapel most visitors come to see. It is the Chapel of Saint John the Baptist, on the left side of the nave as you face the altar. It was commissioned in the 1740s by King João the Fifth. He was rich from Brazilian gold and he admired Rome above all things. The chapel was not built here at first. It was made in Rome, by Roman architects and craftsmen.

When the work was finished, a pope celebrated Mass in the chapel in Rome. Then the whole chapel was taken apart, packed into crates, and shipped by sea to Lisbon. Here it was rebuilt, piece by piece, on this spot. The materials include lapis lazuli, amethyst, alabaster, rare marbles, and gilded bronze. Step close to the large "paintings" inside the chapel. They are not paintings at all. They are mosaics, made from thousands of tiny stones, so fine they look like brushwork.

The Jesuits did not keep this church for long after the chapel arrived. In 1759, Portugal's powerful chief minister, the Marquis of Pombal, expelled the order from the country. A few years later, the church passed to the Santa Casa da Misericórdia. This is Lisbon's historic charitable brotherhood, founded to care for the poor and the sick. It still owns the church today. Next door, in the old Jesuit house, it runs the São Roque Museum, with many treasures from the chapel.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. For a long time, the Santa Casa has run Portugal's national lottery. Part of the money supports its charitable work. When you leave, look at the square in front of the church. A bronze statue there shows a lottery ticket seller, calling out to people passing by. A sixteenth-century Jesuit church and a modern lottery are linked in one charity. That is a very Lisbon story.

Before you go back outside, stand for a moment in the middle of the nave. Look from the flat painted ceiling, to the gold of the side chapels, to the cold blue glow of lapis by the Saint John chapel. Then turn toward the door and the plain stone frame of the daylight. Outside, the trams climb the hill and the cafés fill with conversation. Carry this quiet room with you as you step back into the street.

Good to know

Key facts

One of the earliest Jesuit churches built anywhere in the world.
Built on a plague-era shrine dedicated to Saint Roch.
Saint John the Baptist chapel was built in Rome first.
Largely survived Lisbon's great earthquake of 1 November 1755.
Owned by the Santa Casa da Misericórdia since the Jesuit expulsion.

What to look for

Painted wooden ceiling that imitates arches rising into the sky.
Mosaics in the Saint John chapel that look like paintings.
Deep blue lapis lazuli columns in the Saint John chapel.
Dated sixteenth-century tiles in the Saint Roch chapel.
Bronze lottery ticket seller statue in the square outside.
Before you go

Practical tips

Let your eyes adjust inside before studying the ceiling.
Visit the adjoining São Roque Museum for the chapel treasures.
Keep voices low; services may be held during your visit.
Combine with the nearby São Pedro de Alcântara viewpoint.
Ride the Glória funicular up to avoid the steep climb.
