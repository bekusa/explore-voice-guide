The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Far below, traffic hums through the Alcântara valley. Up here, the wind moves along a narrow stone walkway, and the only other sound may be your own footsteps on worn limestone. To one side runs a low parapet. To the other stands a long, closed stone gallery. Inside it, water once ran all the way to Lisbon. You are standing on one of the great engineering works of eighteenth-century Europe.

This is the Águas Livres Aqueduct, whose name means the Aqueduct of the Free Waters. It crosses the Alcântara valley here in Campolide, on the edge of central Lisbon. For centuries, the city had a simple problem. It sat beside a huge river, but the river water was salty near the sea. Fresh drinking water was scarce, and the old fountains could not serve a growing capital. Water carriers walked the streets selling it by the jug.

The answer came from King João the Fifth. In the first half of the eighteenth century, Portugal was wealthy from Brazilian gold, and the king liked large projects. A royal decree in 1731 set the work in motion. The plan was bold. Springs in the hills northwest of the city would be gathered into one channel. Then gravity alone would carry that water many kilometres to Lisbon, with no pumps at all.

Here is the first surprise. The project was not paid for with royal gold alone. Much of the money came from a special tax on everyday goods, including items such as meat, wine and olive oil. In other words, ordinary Lisbon residents paid for their own water, one purchase at a time. The aqueduct was a royal monument, but it rested on the coins of shopkeepers and households.

Several engineers shaped the work over the years. Manuel da Maia, a military engineer, was central to the early design. Custódio Vieira is closely linked to the great arches over this valley. Later, the Hungarian-born architect Carlos Mardel took a leading role in the decorative works inside the city. The first water reached Lisbon in 1748, but building, extending and finishing the wider network continued for many decades after that.

Now look along the line of the arches. The Alcântara section is the most dramatic part of the whole system. Here the channel must cross a deep valley without losing its gentle slope. So the builders raised a row of arches, and they grow taller as the ground falls away. The highest arch is often described as one of the tallest stone arches in the world, rising around sixty-five metres above the valley floor.

Look closely at the shape of those tallest arches. Many are not perfectly round at the top. They come to a gentle point, like the arches of a Gothic cathedral. This is the second surprise. Pointed arches were an old, medieval form by the eighteenth century. But engineers knew they push their weight down more steeply, and that makes them more stable at great height. The builders chose strength over fashion.

That choice mattered greatly. On the first of November, 1755, a huge earthquake struck Lisbon. Fires and a tsunami followed, and much of the old city was destroyed. Churches, palaces and whole districts fell. Yet the aqueduct across this valley stayed standing. Its survival gave the shaken city a working supply of fresh water during the long years of rebuilding. For many residents, the arches became a quiet sign of endurance.

As you walk, notice the covered channel beside you. Water did not flow in an open trench. It ran inside a protected gallery, which kept it cleaner and cooler. At intervals you will see small turrets and openings along the line. These let air move through the channel and gave workers a way to inspect it. The whole structure is a machine made of stone, built to move water slowly and steadily downhill.

The walkway also carries a darker story. In the nineteenth century, people used this path as a shortcut into the city. Lisbon folklore and old accounts tell of a criminal named Diogo Alves, who robbed travellers here and threw victims from the arches. He was later executed. The story says his preserved head was kept for scientific study. The tale is often told with dramatic additions, so treat some details with caution. Public passage along the walkway was later closed.

The aqueduct did not end here. Its water continued toward the city to a great stone reservoir called the Mãe d'Água das Amoreiras, which means the Mother of Water. From there, a network of smaller channels fed public fountains across Lisbon. Some of those fountains still stand in squares and streets today. The aqueduct served the city well into the twentieth century, before modern water systems finally replaced it.

Today the structure is cared for by Lisbon's water company and forms part of the city's water museum. Visitors can walk along this valley section and look out over Monsanto Forest Park, rooftops and busy roads. It is a strange contrast. Cars rush beneath arches that were designed for horses, carts and people on foot. The old structure and the modern city sit side by side, each still doing its daily work.

Before you go, pause at the parapet. Look down the long line of arches and follow them with your eyes as they shrink toward the far hillside. Feel the stone under your hand. It is rough, pale, and warmed by the sun. This simple wall has carried water, people and stories for nearly three centuries. As you walk away, listen again — beneath the wind, the city keeps moving below.
