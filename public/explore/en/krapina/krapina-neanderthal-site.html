The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, water runs over stones. Around you, wind moves through beech and hornbeam on a steep wooded slope. Underfoot the path is soft, and the rock face ahead is pale, crumbly sandstone. It looks ordinary. But this quiet hill on the edge of a small Croatian town holds one of the largest collections of Neanderthal remains ever found in one place.

You are on Hušnjakovo hill, above the little Krapinica stream, in the green Zagorje region north of Zagreb. Long ago, the stream ran much higher than it does today. Over thousands of years it cut into the soft sandstone and carved out a shallow rock shelter. This was not a deep cave, more of a sheltered overhang. For people living in the open landscape, it offered something precious: a dry, protected place close to fresh water.

Those people were Neanderthals, a human species closely related to us. They lived across Europe and western Asia long before modern humans arrived here. Researchers date the human remains at Krapina to roughly 130,000 years ago. That was a warm period between ice ages. Picture a different forest around you, with animals that are now gone from Europe, moving along this same valley.

The story of discovery begins in 1899. Local people were digging sand from this hill, and bones began to appear. Word reached Dragutin Gorjanović-Kramberger, a geologist and palaeontologist based in Zagreb. He came to Krapina and started careful excavations that continued for several years. What he found changed the scientific picture of early humans in this part of Europe.

The numbers are large. Kramberger's team recovered hundreds of human bone fragments and teeth. Today, scholars estimate that they come from several dozen individuals, men, women and children. Alongside them lay stone tools, traces of fire, and the bones of animals such as cave bears and rhinoceroses. Few Neanderthal sites anywhere have produced so many people from a single place. That is the first surprise of Krapina.

Here is the second surprise. Kramberger was not only a patient digger. He was also an early adopter of new technology. Within a few years of the discovery of X-rays, he used them to study the Krapina fossils. This made him one of the first scientists to look inside ancient human bones without cutting them. The quiet sand hill in front of you played a small part in the birth of a whole scientific method.

The bones also raised a darker question. Many fragments are broken, and some carry cut marks. For decades, some researchers argued that the Krapina Neanderthals practised cannibalism. Others disagreed. They pointed to rockfall, to the pressure of sediment, and to burial practices that might explain the marks. The debate is still open. Treat any confident story about it with care, because the evidence can be read in more than one way.

More recent research has looked at the Krapina finds with fresh eyes. In the 2010s, scientists studied a group of eagle talons from the site. The claws carry polished surfaces and cut marks. Many researchers believe they were worn together, perhaps as a necklace or other ornament. If that reading is right, the Neanderthals here were making jewellery long before modern humans reached Europe. It is a small find with a large meaning about how they saw themselves.

The teeth carry their own quiet stories. Some show grooves between them, the kind made by repeatedly poking with a thin stick or grass stem. In other words, these people may have cleaned their teeth. Scratches on the front teeth suggest that they held food or hides in their mouths while cutting with stone tools. Small details like these bring the people of Krapina very close to us.

Now look at the hillside itself. The original rock shelter has largely gone. The sand digging and the excavations removed much of it. What remains is a protected natural monument, among the first places in Croatia to receive that kind of protection. Along the paths you will see life-size sculptures of Neanderthals and the animals they lived with. They help you picture the scene, but remember that they are modern interpretations rather than exact portraits.

At the foot of the hill stands the Museum of Krapina Neanderthals. It opened in 2010, and its long, low form is set partly into the slope, as if the building were part of the hill. Inside, the exhibition follows the story of the Earth, the evolution of life, and the human journey, before arriving here at Krapina. The original fossils are kept in Zagreb for safekeeping, so the museum shows careful casts alongside reconstructions and displays.

As you walk between the museum and the hill, notice the town below. Krapina is small, with church towers, rooftops and green hills folding into one another. The Zagorje region is known for vineyards, castles and quiet country roads. It feels a long way from the deep past, and yet the same stream still runs through the valley. The same sandstone still shows under the trees. The landscape changed, but its bones are still visible.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the rock face. Feel the cool air under the trees and listen again for the water. Several dozen people once sheltered here, cooked here, and were remembered here in ways we are still trying to understand. They are not so distant. As you walk back down the path, look at the pale sand at your feet, and think about how much this hill has kept for so long.

Practical tips

Start inside the museum, then climb the hill trail.
Wear sturdy shoes for the sloped forest paths.
Visit on a weekday morning for a quieter hillside.
Allow extra time for the long museum exhibition.
Combine the visit with a drive through Zagorje villages.
