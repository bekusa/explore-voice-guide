The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wind there is a low, steady hush. That is the surf of the Northumberland Strait breaking against a long ribbon of sand. Closer by, marsh grass rustles and a gull calls over the lagoon. The air smells of salt, pine resin and warm boardwalk wood. You have driven south from Miramichi through quiet forest and farmland. Now the land flattens, the sky opens wide, and the sea begins.

Welcome to Kouchibouguac National Park, on the eastern coast of New Brunswick. The name comes from the Mi'kmaq language. It is usually translated as river of long tides. The Mi'kmaq are the Indigenous people of this coast, and they have fished, hunted and travelled these waters for thousands of years. Say the name slowly — Koo-shee-boo-gwack. Locals often shorten it with affection. Whatever you call it, the name points to the heart of the place: water moving slowly between river, lagoon and sea.

The park covers a little over two hundred square kilometres, and it is a landscape of layers. Out on the edge sits a chain of barrier islands. These are long, low dunes of sand, shaped by waves and wind. Behind them lie calm, shallow lagoons. Behind the lagoons come salt marshes, then peat bogs, then mixed forest of spruce, fir, birch and maple. Walk inland for twenty minutes and you pass through several different worlds. Each one has its own plants, birds and smells.

The dunes are the park's most famous feature. They run for roughly twenty-five kilometres along the coast. They are not fixed. Storms push them, winds reshape them, and over the years whole sections shift their position. Marram grass holds them together. Its long roots knit the loose sand into place. This is why you will see signs asking visitors to stay on the boardwalks. One footstep can crush the grass. Once the grass dies, the wind can carve away the dune behind it.

If you are at Kellys Beach, you are standing at the park's best-known doorway to the sea. A long wooden boardwalk crosses the lagoon and the dunes before it reaches the sand. Stop halfway and look down. In the shallow water you may see small fish, crabs, or eelgrass moving with the current. Then look ahead. The boardwalk rises and dips with the dunes, and the open strait appears all at once at the end.

Here is a surprise for many visitors. The water along this coast is often called some of the warmest saltwater north of Virginia, in the United States. That is a popular local saying rather than a precise measurement. But it is true that the strait is shallow, and in summer the sun warms it well. On a hot August afternoon, families wade far out from shore. The cold Atlantic that most people imagine in eastern Canada feels very far away.

The park is also a refuge for wildlife. Grey seals haul out on the sandbars, and on a calm day you can sometimes hear them before you see them. The beaches are nesting ground for the piping plover. It is a small, sand-coloured shorebird and an endangered species in Canada. Some beach sections close in early summer to protect the nests. On a group of small islands in the lagoons, common terns gather in one of the largest tern colonies in North America.

Now for a harder chapter. The park was created in 1969, but the land was not empty. Hundreds of families lived here, many of them Acadian. Acadians are French-speaking descendants of early settlers in the Maritime provinces. To create the park, the government expropriated their homes and farms. That means the state took the land by law, with compensation. Many residents felt the process was unjust. Their anger shaped how Canada created national parks afterwards.

One name is still well known in the region: Jackie Vautour. He was a resident who refused to leave. For decades he resisted eviction and stayed on land inside the park. His fight became a symbol for the displaced families. Today, the park acknowledges this history more openly than it once did. As you walk the trails, it helps to remember that some of these quiet clearings were once yards, fields and kitchen gardens.

Leave the coast for a moment and walk into the bog. A raised boardwalk crosses a peat bog, a wetland built from layer upon layer of slowly decaying moss. The ground here is soft and acidic. Few trees grow tall. Instead you will find sphagnum moss, low shrubs, and sometimes carnivorous pitcher plants. These plants trap insects to get the nutrients the poor soil cannot give them. An observation tower lets you look across the bog's open, rust-and-green surface.

The park is easy to explore at a slow pace. A network of gravel cycling paths runs through the forest and links many of the beaches and campgrounds. You can rent a canoe or kayak and paddle the rivers and lagoons. In winter, the same paths become routes for cross-country skiing and snowshoeing. Here is one more detail many visitors miss. The park is a designated dark sky preserve. On a clear night, far from city lights, the Milky Way stretches across the sky.

So what makes this place special? It is not one dramatic view. It is the way so many habitats sit close together, and the way the light changes over them. Morning mist rises from the lagoons. Midday sun turns the sand almost white. In the evening, the marsh grass glows gold and the water goes silver. The park rewards people who slow down, stay quiet, and look closely at small things.

Before you move on, pause where you are. Feel the wind on your face — it has crossed open water to reach you. Look at the ground beneath your feet, whether it is sand, boards or soft moss. Listen once more for the surf behind the dunes. As you walk away, notice the tracks in the sand and the tall grass bending in the wind. Some of those tracks will be gone by tomorrow. The dunes, slowly, will keep moving.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stay on boardwalks to protect fragile marram grass on dunes.
Bring a bicycle or rent one for the gravel trail network.
Respect closed beach sections during piping plover nesting season.
Stay after dark on clear nights to see the Milky Way.
Pack binoculars for grey seals on the offshore sandbars.
