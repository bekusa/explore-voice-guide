The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water runs somewhere below you, through channels cut into the hillside. The air carries the smell of oak, chestnut and damp granite. Above the trees rise the green slopes of the Sierra de Gredos. This is the Monastery of Yuste, a quiet religious house in the La Vera valley of Extremadura. Near the end of his life, the most powerful man in Europe chose this place to wait for death.

The monastery began as a small community of hermits in the early fifteenth century. They later joined the Hieronymite order. The Hieronymites were Spanish monks who followed the example of Saint Jerome, a scholar and hermit of the early Church. They valued prayer, silence and plainsong, which is unaccompanied chant. The site suited them well. It had shade, fresh springs and a mild climate. It also lay far from the noise of any royal court.

That distance is the reason the monastery is famous today. In the 1550s, Charles the Fifth gave up his crowns one by one. He was Holy Roman Emperor and King of Spain, and he ruled lands from the Netherlands to the Americas. Few rulers in history ever stepped down by choice. Charles did, worn out by decades of war and travel. He also suffered from severe gout. He wanted somewhere warm and still, close to God.

He arrived at Yuste in early 1557. Before that, he waited several months in the castle of nearby Jarandilla de la Vera while his rooms were finished. Today a walking route follows the path he was carried along through the hills. Locals call it the Emperor's Route. Its stony trail and mountain streams give a sense of how remote this corner of Spain felt then. It still feels remote today.

Now look at the building attached to the side of the church. This is the emperor's palace, though the word is generous. It is a modest house of a few rooms on two floors. It is built of stone and brick, with open galleries facing the valley. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The man who once commanded armies across a continent lived here in rooms that were not much bigger than those of a wealthy country gentleman.

The palace connects to the church by a sloping ramp. Charles could not climb stairs easily. The ramp allowed him to be carried, or to ride a small horse, up to his rooms. As you walk, notice how gently the floor rises under your feet. A practical solution for an old man in pain became part of the architecture. That ramp tells you more about his final years than any portrait.

The most moving detail waits inside the bedroom. One window opens directly onto the high altar of the church. From his bed, the emperor could see the priest and hear the Mass without moving. On his worst days, gout kept him lying down. Stand beside that window and look through. You see exactly what Charles saw in his last months — the altar, the candles, the stone vault above.

The rooms are hung in dark cloth. Charles kept them in mourning for his mother, Queen Joanna of Castile. History remembers her as Joanna the Mad. The furniture you see is mostly from the period rather than his own, but it gives the right feeling. Look for the special chair designed to support his painful leg. Its shape shows how much effort ordinary sitting cost him.

Charles brought a few treasures with him. One was a painting by Titian, the great Venetian master, known as La Gloria. It shows the emperor and his family kneeling before the Holy Trinity. The original now hangs in the Prado Museum in Madrid. A copy fills the church here. The emperor also loved clocks and mechanical devices. The Italian engineer Juanelo Turriano spent time with him, and together they worked on clockwork in this quiet retreat.

The second surprise involves a story you may hear in the village. Legend has it that Charles staged his own funeral before he died. In this tale, he lay in a coffin and listened to monks chant prayers for his soul. Historians still debate the story, and many doubt it. What is certain is simpler. Charles died at Yuste in September 1558, after a fever, less than two years after he arrived.

He did not stay here forever. His son, King Philip the Second, later moved his body to the great monastery of El Escorial near Madrid. Afterwards, Yuste had a hard life. French troops set fire to it during the Napoleonic wars in the early nineteenth century. In the 1830s, Spanish law took many monasteries away from the religious orders, and the monks left. For decades the buildings fell into ruin.

Restoration came in the twentieth century. Today Patrimonio Nacional, the body that cares for Spain's royal sites, looks after the palace. A small religious community lives in the monastery again. Walk through the two cloisters, an older Gothic one and a later Renaissance one, and compare them. One has pointed arches, the other has rounder, calmer lines. Yuste now also hosts a European foundation that gives an award in the emperor's name, linking this quiet valley to the idea of a united Europe.

Before you leave, step out onto the terrace below the palace. There is a stone pond here, fed by mountain water. Charles liked to sit nearby, and people say he fished in it. Look out over the oaks toward the valley. The same hills, the same sound of water, the same soft light filled his days. As you walk back down the path, listen again. The water is still running, just as it did for him.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk part of the Emperor's Route from Jarandilla de la Vera.
Visit early morning for quieter cloisters and cooler air.
Wear sturdy shoes for the stone ramps and paths.
Combine your visit with the village of Cuacos de Yuste.
See the original La Gloria later at Madrid's Prado.
