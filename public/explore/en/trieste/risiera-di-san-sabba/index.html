The guide

The story

Your footsteps change here. Between two high walls of rough grey concrete, the sound of the street falls away, and the corridor narrows the sky to a thin strip above you. The air feels cooler. The walls lean slightly, and they press in from both sides. This entrance was designed to make you feel enclosed before you see anything at all. Walk slowly. This is the Risiera di San Sabba, in the southern part of Trieste, and it asks for a quieter kind of attention than most places a traveller visits.

The name itself tells you how ordinary this place once was. Risiera means rice mill. In the early twentieth century, this group of brick buildings was built as an industrial plant for husking and processing rice. It stood among the warehouses, rail lines and factories that grew around the port of Trieste. Workers came and went. Sacks were loaded and unloaded. For decades, nothing about it suggested what it would later become.

That changed in September 1943. When Italy signed an armistice with the Allies, German forces occupied the north of the country. Trieste and the surrounding region became part of a zone under direct German administration, called the Operational Zone of the Adriatic Littoral. The SS took over the old rice mill. They turned it first into a camp for Italian soldiers who had been captured, and then into a police detention camp.

This is the detail that many visitors do not know before they arrive. The Risiera was the only Nazi camp on Italian soil to have a crematorium. The SS men who ran it included officers who had worked in the extermination camps of occupied Poland. They brought their methods with them. The crematorium was built inside one of the mill buildings, using the existing structure of the old drying plant.

The people held here came from many groups. There were partisans and anti-fascist resisters. There were Slovenes and Croats from the surrounding countryside, where the resistance against occupation was strong. There were political prisoners from Trieste and from the region. And there were Jews, rounded up in the city and beyond. For the Jewish prisoners, the Risiera was usually a holding point. From here, trains carried them north to Auschwitz and other camps.

Historians estimate that several thousand people were killed inside these walls. Many thousands more passed through the camp on their way to deportation, forced labour, or death elsewhere. Exact figures remain difficult to establish. The Germans destroyed much of the evidence. What survives comes from witness testimony, from the accounts of the few who were released, and from later investigation. Hold that uncertainty in mind as you walk. Behind every estimate stand individual names, many of which were never recorded.

Now step into the courtyard. It is wide, paved, and open to the sky. Look at the ground. You will see a dark steel plate set into the paving, and a line of metal tracing a shape across it. This marks the place where the crematorium and its tall chimney once stood. In April 1945, as Allied and Yugoslav forces approached the city, the retreating Germans blew up the crematorium and the chimney with explosives. They wanted to erase what had happened here. The imprint in the ground keeps the memory exactly where they tried to remove it.

The design you are moving through is the work of the Triestine architect Romano Boico. After the camp was declared a national monument in 1965, he was asked to turn the ruined mill into a place of remembrance. He chose not to rebuild or decorate. Instead, he removed the damaged structures, left the bare brick buildings, and added the concrete walls of the entrance. The result opened as a museum in the 1970s. It is austere on purpose. There is very little here to distract the eye.

Inside the main building, look for the small cells along one side. These narrow rooms were used to hold prisoners, often several people pressed into a space meant for far fewer. Some of them were held here before execution. Survivors later described the walls covered in names, dates and messages scratched or written by prisoners. Few of those original inscriptions survive, but the cramped size of each cell is still clear. Stand at a doorway for a moment. Notice how little room there is to turn around.

Near the cells is a space that witnesses called the cell of death. Prisoners who were to be killed were often brought here first. Upstairs, in the larger halls of the old mill, the museum displays photographs, documents and personal objects. You will find letters, portraits, and records of the people who passed through. The exhibition is not large. Give it time anyway. The faces in the photographs are the centre of this place.

For decades after the war, justice came slowly. Trieste itself spent years under Allied administration before it returned to Italy in the 1950s, and the politics of the border region made the past hard to discuss openly. It was only in the 1970s that a trial was held in Trieste concerning crimes committed at the Risiera. By then, most of those responsible were dead or beyond reach. The proceedings mattered all the same. They placed the testimony of survivors on the public record.

Today the Risiera is a site of ceremony as well as memory. Every year, on the anniversary of the city's liberation and on days of remembrance, people gather in the courtyard. Speeches are given in Italian and Slovene, the two languages of this borderland. Schoolchildren from across Italy and from neighbouring countries come on organised visits. On an ordinary weekday, though, you may find yourself almost alone. The silence is part of the experience.

Before you leave, turn back once in the courtyard. Look at the brick walls, darkened by weather and soot from the nearby industrial district. Look again at the steel outline on the ground. As you walk out through the concrete corridor, the noise of the city will return... traffic, a distant ship's horn, the ordinary life of Trieste. Let it come back slowly. You have stood in a place that its builders tried to erase, and by remembering it, you have helped to keep it.
