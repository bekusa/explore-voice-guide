The story

The water here is the color of jade. It lies flat and calm between walls of stone. Tall limestone cliffs rise straight up from the sea. They stand like dark green towers, hundreds of them, scattered across the bay. Your boat moves slowly between them. The air is warm and thick. It smells of salt and wet rock and green leaves.

This is Phang Nga Bay. It sits in the Andaman Sea, between the island of Phuket and the mainland of southern Thailand. The bay is wide and shallow. Across it rise islands of limestone, some small, some large. Many have steep sides that no person can climb. Trees grow from the cracks high above. They have stood here for a very long time.

The stone itself tells a slow story. This rock began as a reef, under a warm sea, many millions of years ago. Layer by layer, the shells of tiny creatures pressed into stone. Later the earth pushed it upward. Then rain and waves carved it. What remains are these strange and beautiful shapes, rising sheer from the water.

Look closely at the cliffs near the waterline. The rock is cut and hollowed there. The sea has eaten into it over the ages. In some places the stone curves inward like a roof. Stalactites hang down, pointed and gray. They drip slowly, even now, growing one drop at a time. Each drop adds a little more.

Much of the bay is protected. It became a national park in 1981. The park keeps the water, the islands, and the forests safe from building and harm. Because of this, the bay still looks much as it did long ago. Few lights. Few roads. Just stone and water and sky, and the slow passing of the boats.

Along the shore grow mangrove trees. Their roots stand in the salt water, lifted on thin legs. These forests are full of quiet life. Small crabs move across the mud. Fish hide among the roots. Birds rest in the branches and call to one another. The mangroves protect the coast and feed the whole bay.

Some of the islands hide a secret inside them. They are hollow. Long ago, caves formed in the rock, then their roofs fell in. What is left is a hidden lagoon, open to the sky but ringed by cliffs. Local people call these rooms hongs. You can reach them only by small boat, and only when the tide is right.

To enter a hong, you lie low in a canoe. The guide paddles you through a narrow, dark opening in the cliff. The ceiling is close above your head. Then the passage opens. Suddenly you float inside a quiet green world, walled by stone and hung with vines. The sound of the outside sea fades away to nothing.

One island in the bay is known around the world. It rises as a thin spike of rock near the cliffs. A film was made here in 1974, and since then visitors have come to see it. Boats gather near its small beach. People stand and look up at the narrow stone tower standing in the water.

Elsewhere in the bay, people live on the sea itself. There is a village built on tall stilts above the water, settled long ago by Muslim fishing families. Wooden walkways link the houses. Boats are tied at the doors. Children play between the buildings. A small mosque stands among them. Life here follows the rhythm of the tide.

The bay is alive with movement if you watch for it. Long wooden boats pass by with a low rumble. Eagles turn in slow circles high above the cliffs. Now and then a fish breaks the surface. The light shifts across the stone hour by hour. In the morning the cliffs are soft and pale; by afternoon they seem to glow.

The best way to know this place is to be still. Let the boat drift for a while. Trail your hand in the warm water. Watch the reflections of the cliffs break and reform on the surface. Listen to the small sounds: dripping stone, distant birds, the soft slap of water on rock. The bay rewards a patient, quiet eye.

Phang Nga Bay does not rush. It has waited here through ages we can barely imagine, and it will wait long after we are gone. When your boat turns back, look once more at the towers of stone behind you. They will stand there in the quiet, holding the sea between them, long after the sound of your engine fades.

Practical tips

Visit by longtail boat or kayak from Phuket piers
Enter the sea caves only at the correct tide
Bring water, sun protection, and a dry bag
Start early to avoid the midday boat crowds
