The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Up here, on this chalk spur above the Seine, it comes in steady from the river and pushes through the gaps in the old walls. Under your feet the ground is pale and crumbly, white chalk mixed with dark knuckles of flint. Below you, the river bends in a wide, slow curve, and the red roofs of Les Andelys gather along its bank. Before any king or siege, this is the first thing to notice. This rock is the reason the castle exists at all.

You are standing in the ruins of Château Gaillard, built in the late twelfth century by Richard the Lionheart. Most people remember Richard as an English king and a crusader. But he was also Duke of Normandy, and Normandy was where his real fight lay. His rival was the King of France, Philip Augustus, who wanted this rich duchy for himself. Rouen, the Norman capital, sat downstream. Whoever held this stretch of the Seine held the road to Rouen.

So Richard built fast. The castle rose in roughly two years, in the late 1190s, which was astonishing speed for a fortress of this size. Workers cut ditches straight into the chalk and raised walls of flint and stone on top. Chroniclers say Richard watched the work closely and took great pride in it. The name itself carries that pride. In old French, "gaillard" means something like bold, or cheeky. A saucy castle — a fortress that seemed to mock the French king across the border.

A famous story fits that mood. According to one chronicler, Philip Augustus boasted that he would take the castle even if its walls were made of iron. Richard is said to have answered that he could hold it even if the walls were made of butter. Nobody can confirm those exact words. But the tale has lasted for over eight centuries, because it captures two proud men and one very stubborn rock.

Now look at how the castle is laid out. It is not one single wall. It is a series of defences, one behind another, running along the spur. First came an outer work, a triangular enclosure facing the only easy approach. Behind it lay a middle ward. And behind that, the inner ward, with the great keep at its heart. An attacker had to break through each layer in turn, crossing deep ditches every time.

Here is a detail many visitors walk past. Look at the wall of the inner ward. It does not run flat and straight. It curves outward again and again, in a row of rounded bulges, almost like a scalloped edge. Builders of that period rarely did this. The curves left fewer blind spots for defenders, and they made the wall harder to batter or undermine. It is one of the most unusual walls of its age.

The keep itself is worth a long look. Its walls are very thick at the base, and one side pushes forward into a sharp, angled point, like the prow of a ship. That shape was meant to deflect missiles and make the stone harder to break. Notice too how the base flares outward. Stones dropped from above would bounce off it and fly into anyone crowding below.

Richard did not live to see his castle tested. He died in 1199, struck by a crossbow bolt during a siege in central France. His brother John became king and duke. Philip Augustus saw his chance. In 1203 the French army arrived beneath these walls, and a long, grim siege began. The English commander here was Roger de Lacy, and he refused to surrender.

The hardest chapter of that winter happened not inside the walls but between them. Many townspeople had fled into the castle for safety. As food ran short, the garrison sent out those who could not fight. The French refused to let them through their lines. Hundreds of civilians were trapped on the open slopes in the cold, with almost nothing to eat. Chroniclers describe terrible suffering before Philip finally allowed the survivors to pass. Pause for a moment and look down those slopes.

In the spring of 1204, the defences began to fall, one by one. One well-known account says French soldiers got inside the middle ward through a latrine chute, or through a chapel window, and opened the gate. The story is told by a chronicler close to the French king, so treat the colourful details with some caution. Either way, the garrison was pushed back to the inner ward, and in March 1204 the castle fell.

The loss mattered far beyond this hill. Within months, Rouen also surrendered, and Normandy passed to the French crown. A link between Normandy and England that had lasted since William the Conqueror was broken. That is the second surprise of this place. The fortress built to protect Normandy became the doorway through which the duchy was lost.

The castle had a later and quieter history. In the early fourteenth century it served as a prison. Among those held here was Marguerite of Burgundy, a French queen accused in a court scandal, who died in confinement. Centuries later, during the religious wars, the castle changed hands again. Around the turn of the seventeenth century, King Henry the Fourth of France ordered it partly dismantled, so that no rebel could use it. Much of the stone was carried away. What you see is what remained.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the inner ward and look out once more. Follow the Seine as it curves away toward Rouen, the same view Richard planned around. Listen for the boats, the birds over the cliff, the wind in the grass between the stones. On your way down, glance back at the keep's sharp prow against the sky. It still points toward the old French border, as if the argument were not quite over.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes; the chalk paths are steep and uneven.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed clifftop.
Walk the outer ditches first to follow the attackers' route.
Visit late afternoon for warm light across the Seine.
Combine the climb with a stroll through Petit Andely below.
