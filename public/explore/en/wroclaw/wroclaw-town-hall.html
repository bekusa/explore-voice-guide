The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the cobblestones of the Market Square. Feel the uneven stones under your shoes, worn smooth by centuries of carts, boots and bicycles. Around you, tall painted houses in pink, mint green and ochre frame the square. In the middle of it all stands a building that looks less like an office and more like a stone jewel box. Its gables are pointed and laced with carving. Its brick tower rises above the rooftops. This is Wrocław's Old Town Hall.

You are standing in one of the largest medieval market squares in Central Europe. Locals call it the Rynek. For hundreds of years, this open space was the commercial heart of the city. Merchants traded cloth, salt and grain here. Officials judged disputes, and townspeople gathered for news. The town hall was the place where all of that activity had a home. It held council meetings, courtrooms, offices and storerooms under a single roof.

The building did not appear all at once. Construction began around the late thirteenth century. Builders then extended and rebuilt it in stages for roughly two hundred and fifty years. That long timeline explains a lot about what you see. The town hall is mostly Gothic, the medieval style of pointed arches, steep roofs and stone tracery. But later hands added touches of the Renaissance, the style that brought back classical order and decoration. Walk around it slowly, and you can watch the centuries change from wall to wall.

Now make your way to the eastern facade, the side many people consider its finest face. Look up at the great stepped gable. It is crowded with pinnacles, slender pointed towers, and delicate stone patterns that look almost like lace. Below it sits a large astronomical clock. The clock dates from the late sixteenth century. It was made to show more than the hour. In an age before phones and printed calendars, a clock like this told the city about the sky as well as the time.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look closely at the carved figures and small sculptures set into the walls and around the windows. Some show saints and noble figures, as you might expect. Others are much more playful. You can find animals, odd faces, and scenes from ordinary town life. Medieval stonemasons often added a little humour to serious buildings. Take a minute to hunt for them. The longer you look, the more faces seem to look back at you.

Next, walk down below street level. Under the town hall lies the Piwnica Świdnicka, a vaulted cellar that has served food and drink for centuries. It is often described as one of the oldest restaurants in Europe still operating. Its name comes from Świdnica, a nearby town once famous for its beer. That beer was so popular that the cellar took its name. Ducking under the low brick arches, you can easily picture merchants closing deals over heavy mugs on a cold winter evening.

Inside the main floors, the town hall now houses the Museum of Burgher Art, part of the Wrocław City Museum. A burgher was a free citizen of a medieval town, usually a merchant or a craftsman. The museum tells their story. You will see objects from daily and civic life, from silverware to portraits of city leaders. The most impressive room is the great hall, with its ribbed vaulted ceiling. The stone ribs spread across the ceiling like branches, carrying the weight of the roof above.

The history of this city has many layers, and the town hall carries all of them. For centuries, Wrocław was known by its German name, Breslau. Over its long life, the city was ruled by Bohemian kings, by the Habsburg monarchy, and later by Prussia and Germany. After the Second World War, the borders of Europe moved. The city became part of Poland. Much of its pre-war population left, and new residents arrived, many of them from the eastern lands Poland had lost.

In 1945, during the final months of the war, the city was besieged and much of it lay in ruins. The town hall survived in far better condition than many buildings around it. That is a second surprise for many visitors. A large part of what you see in the square was carefully rebuilt in the following decades. The town hall, by contrast, still holds a great deal of its original medieval fabric. You are looking at real old stone, not only a copy.

Near the town hall, look for the statue of Aleksander Fredro, a Polish playwright and comic writer of the nineteenth century. The monument has an unusual journey behind it. It first stood in Lviv, a city that is now in Ukraine. After the war, it was brought here, along with many people who moved west. For new residents, the statue was a piece of home. Today it is a popular meeting point. People often simply say they will meet under Fredro.

In front of the town hall, you may also notice a stone pillory. In medieval towns, a pillory was a post where wrongdoers were tied up and publicly punished. Crowds could watch, mock, or throw things at them. The one you see is a reconstruction, but it marks the place where justice was once very public. It is a striking contrast. On one side are graceful Gothic carvings. On the other is a reminder of how harsh daily life could be.

As you circle the building, keep an eye on the ground as well as the walls. Wrocław is known for its small bronze dwarfs, hidden all over the old town. Several of them live around the Market Square. They climb lampposts, sleep on ledges, and carry tiny tools. Hunting for them has become a favourite game for visitors of every age. Their story also connects to the 1980s, when a protest movement used dwarf drawings to mock the communist authorities with humour.

Before you walk on, step back to the edge of the square and look at the town hall one last time. Notice how the light moves across the brick and pale stone. In the morning, the eastern gable glows. In the evening, the warm colours of the surrounding houses reflect onto its walls. Listen to the footsteps, the café chatter, and the bells. This building has watched over the square through trade, war and change. Today, for a few minutes, it is watching over you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the east facade for the finest gable view.
Visit early morning to photograph the square without crowds.
Go down into the vaulted cellar for a meal.
Walk a full circle around the building before going inside.
Bring a list or app to hunt for bronze dwarfs.
