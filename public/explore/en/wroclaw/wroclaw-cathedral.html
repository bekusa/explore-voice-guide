The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The trams and traffic of the modern city have faded behind you. Underfoot, the cobblestones are uneven and worn smooth, and the air smells faintly of river water and old stone. Ahead of you, two dark spires rise above a wall of deep red brick. You are standing on Ostrów Tumski, the Cathedral Island, and this is the oldest part of Wrocław.

The building in front of you is the Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist. Locals often call it simply the archcathedral. It is the seat of the Archdiocese of Wrocław, and it sits on ground where people have worshipped for more than a thousand years. Before the city had its great market square, before its merchant houses and town hall, there was this small piece of land in the river. The city grew outward from here.

Here is the first surprise. Cathedral Island is no longer really an island. For centuries it was surrounded by arms of the Oder River, and early settlers chose it because water made it easy to defend. In the nineteenth century, one branch of the river was filled in. Today the island is joined to the mainland on its northern side. Yet the old name stayed, and the quiet, separate feeling of the place stayed too.

The story of the bishops here begins around the year 1000. At that time, a new church organisation was created for the young Polish state, and Wrocław became one of its first bishoprics. Several churches stood on this spot before the one you see now. Some were made of wood, and later ones were made of stone. Each was rebuilt or replaced as the town grew richer and as fires and wars took their toll.

The present Gothic cathedral took shape from the thirteenth century onward. Builders began with the chancel, the eastern end that holds the main altar. It is considered one of the earliest Gothic structures in Poland. Look at the material. This is brick Gothic, a style common across northern Europe, where good building stone was scarce but clay was everywhere. The builders used fired brick to reach high, pointed vaults and tall, narrow windows.

Now look up at the two towers. Their sharp, dark spires are what most people picture when they think of this skyline. But the spires you see today are not medieval. They are the result of a long recovery. That story begins in 1945, in the last months of the Second World War, when Wrocław, then the German city of Breslau, was turned into a fortress and besieged.

The siege was devastating. Large parts of the city were destroyed, and the cathedral was badly hit. Much of the roof and vaulting collapsed, and the towers lost their spires. After the war, the city became Polish, and most of its German population was expelled. New residents arrived, many of them from lands further east. These newcomers rebuilt a cathedral they had not grown up with. Reconstruction took decades, and the spires were only restored in the early 1990s.

Walk inside, and your eyes will need a moment. The light is cooler and softer here. The nave, the long central space, stretches toward the altar between thick brick piers. Because so much was lost in 1945, the interior is a mix of rescued pieces, careful reconstruction, and later additions. Notice how plain some of the walls feel. That simplicity is partly a result of the war, not only of medieval taste.

The chapels behind the main altar hold the second surprise. Here the mood changes completely. Within this Gothic shell sit rich Baroque chapels, a style from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries known for colour, marble, and dramatic movement. One is the chapel of Saint Elizabeth. Another is the Electoral Chapel, designed by the Austrian architect Johann Bernhard Fischer von Erlach, who also worked on grand buildings in Vienna. It is rare to find his work this far north.

If your legs are willing, there is a viewing terrace in one of the towers, and a lift takes most of the effort out of the climb. From the top, the city opens up below you. You can trace the river as it divides around the islands. You can see the green bridges, the rooftops of the old town, and the many church towers that give Wrocław its outline. It is the best place to understand why the city grew where it did.

When you step back outside, look along the street lamps of the island. Many are still gas lamps. Each evening around dusk, a lamplighter in a dark cape and hat walks the streets and lights them one by one with a long pole. In the morning, the lamps are put out again by hand. It is a small, steady ritual, and it is one of the few places in Europe where this work is still done every day.

Nearby is the Tumski Bridge, painted green, which links the island to Sand Island. Its railings are covered with padlocks. Couples write their names on them, lock them to the bridge, and often throw the key into the river below. The city has sometimes removed the heavy locks to protect the bridge. Still, new ones keep appearing. It is a modern habit, but it fits the quiet, personal mood of this old district.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the cathedral doors. Look again at the red brick, darker in some places and brighter in others. The brighter patches often mark repairs, and they show how much was rebuilt by hand after the war. Listen to the bells, the footsteps on the cobbles, the river somewhere behind the houses. Then walk slowly toward the bridge, and watch the spires shrink behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the tower lift for a wide view over the river.
Arrive around dusk to watch the lamplighter at work.
Respect services inside; keep voices low and phones silent.
Walk over Tumski Bridge to continue toward Sand Island.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven cobblestones of the island.
