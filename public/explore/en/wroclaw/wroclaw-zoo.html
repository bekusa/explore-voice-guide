The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere behind the trees a bird is calling, sharp and unfamiliar. A child laughs near the gate. Gravel crunches under strollers, and the air carries a mix of cut grass, wet earth, and the warm, heavy smell of animals. You are standing at the entrance to Wrocław Zoo, one of the oldest zoological gardens in Poland. People have walked through gates on this spot for well over a century and a half.

The zoo opened in 1865. At that time, this city was not Polish at all. It was called Breslau, and it belonged to the Kingdom of Prussia, a powerful German state. Local citizens wanted a garden where people could study animals and simply enjoy them. The idea was very much of its time. Across Europe in the nineteenth century, growing cities were building zoos, museums, and parks as signs of education and civic pride.

Here is the first thing many visitors never realise. This zoo still stands on its original site. Many old European zoos moved, closed, or were rebuilt somewhere else. This one stayed. So the ground under your feet has been a home for animals since the 1860s. Some of the oldest paths still follow the lines of that first layout, curving gently between shade trees.

Look at the setting around you. The zoo sits on the east side of the city, close to the Oder River. Just across the road rises Centennial Hall, a huge concrete dome from the early twentieth century. It is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. Next to it lies Szczytnicki Park, with its old trees and a Japanese garden. Together, these places form one of the greenest corners of Wrocław.

The twentieth century was hard on this garden. The zoo struggled through economic crises, and the Second World War brought terrible damage. In 1945, the city was under siege for weeks. Buildings were destroyed, and most of the animals did not survive. After the war, the borders of Europe moved. Breslau became Wrocław, part of Poland, and almost the entire population changed. New residents arrived from other parts of the country, often from lands Poland had lost in the east.

Those new residents rebuilt the zoo, and it reopened under Polish administration in the late 1940s. Think about that for a moment. People who had just lost their own homes took the time to bring back a place of animals and play. For many families, a day here became a small sign that ordinary life was returning to a broken city.

Now for the second surprise. For decades, this zoo was famous across Poland because of television. Its long-serving directors, Antoni and Hanna Gucwiński, a married couple, presented a popular programme called With a Camera Among Animals. They brought animals into the studio and talked about them with warmth and patience. Generations of Polish children grew up watching the show. For many of them, Wrocław Zoo was the zoo they knew best, long before they ever visited.

As you walk, notice how the zoo mixes old and new. Some enclosures are simple and traditional, with fences, moats, and open lawns. Others are modern, with glass walls, planted slopes, and rock work that copies natural landscapes. The change shows how ideas about zoos have shifted. Early zoos focused on showing as many animals as possible. Today the focus is on space, welfare, education, and breeding programmes for species at risk in the wild.

The largest modern addition is the Afrykarium, which opened in 2014. It is a large building dedicated entirely to Africa, and especially to its water environments. Inside, the route takes you through different habitats, from coastal waters to rivers and rainforest. It is the kind of place where you lose track of time. The light is dim and blue in some halls, and the sound of moving water follows you from room to room.

Inside the Afrykarium, look for the hippopotamuses. Through thick glass you can sometimes watch them underwater. On land they look heavy and slow. Under the surface they seem almost weightless, pushing off the bottom and gliding forward. Nearby, a tunnel runs beneath a large tank, so sharks and rays pass right over your head. And in another part of the building, you can watch penguins from the southern coast of Africa — a reminder that not all penguins live on ice.

Outside, the zoo is home to animals from many continents. You may see big cats, primates, hoofed animals, reptiles, and many kinds of birds. Wrocław Zoo is known for keeping a very large number of species, among the highest in Poland. That variety means every visit feels a little different. One day the giraffes are close to the fence. Another day the monkeys are the noisiest neighbours in the park.

Take a moment to watch the other visitors, too. Grandparents walk with grandchildren, sometimes on the same paths they walked as children. School groups move in noisy lines with clipboards. Couples sit on benches with ice cream. For people in Wrocław, the zoo is not only an attraction for tourists. It is a family habit, a place for birthdays, first dates, and quiet Sunday afternoons.

Before you go, pause somewhere with a view between the trees. Listen again to the mix of sounds — birds, voices, footsteps, perhaps a distant tram on the street outside. This garden has survived empires, a war, and a complete change of nation. Through all of it, people kept coming here to look at animals and feel curious. As you walk out toward the dome of Centennial Hall, notice how quickly the city returns... and how the calls from behind the trees follow you a little way down the road.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see animals during their most active hours.
Visit the Afrykarium midweek to avoid weekend crowds.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks on gravel paths.
Combine your visit with nearby Centennial Hall and Szczytnicki Park.
Take a tram to avoid looking for parking nearby.
