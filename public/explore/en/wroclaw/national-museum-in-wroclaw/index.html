The guide

The story

Listen for the leaves first. On a breezy day the whole front of this building moves. A thick coat of climbing vine covers the brick and stone, and the wind runs across it like a hand across fur. In summer the wall is deep green. In autumn it turns copper, then a red so strong that people cross the square just to stand in front of it. You are looking at one of the most photographed walls in Wrocław.

This is the main building of the National Museum in Wrocław. It stands on Powstańców Warszawy Square, a short walk from the Oder River and the old heart of the city. Before you go inside, step back a little. The vine hides a lot of the detail, so look at the edges where it thins out. Gables, tall windows and decorated corners show through the green.

Here is the first surprise. This building was not designed as a museum at all. It was built in the 1880s as an administrative headquarters. At that time Wrocław was a German city called Breslau, and the building served the Prussian government of the province of Silesia. Officials worked in these rooms. Files moved along these corridors. The galleries you are about to walk through began life as offices.

The style is often called Dutch Neo-Renaissance. In simple terms, the architects borrowed the look of grand merchant buildings from sixteenth-century Holland and Flanders. That means red brick with lighter stone trim, stepped and curved gables, and a sense of solid civic pride. The message was clear. This was a place of order, authority and permanence, built to impress anyone who came here on official business.

The twentieth century changed everything. The city suffered heavy damage at the end of the Second World War. After 1945, the borders of Europe moved, and Breslau became Polish Wrocław. Most of the German population left, and new residents arrived, many of them from the east, from lands that Poland had lost. A whole city had to rebuild its memory from the ground up. A museum was part of that work.

So the former government building became a home for art. The collections came together from many sources. Some objects survived from the old museums of the city. Others came with the people who moved here, including parts of collections linked to Lviv, a city that had been an important centre of Polish culture before the war. This museum carries both stories at once — the Silesian past of the region and the memory of the communities who resettled it.

Inside, many visitors start with the medieval art of Silesia, and for good reason. These rooms hold Gothic sculpture and painted altarpieces from churches across the region. Look at the faces of the carved saints and the Madonnas. Many still carry traces of their original colour and gold. Some figures smile very slightly, a gentle expression that sculptors of the late Middle Ages liked to give the Virgin Mary. Stand close, then step back, and the mood of the figure changes.

Here is a second surprise for many visitors. One of the most important pieces here is a medieval tomb. It belongs to Henry the Fourth Probus, a Duke of Wrocław in the thirteenth century. His stone effigy lies on top, and small mourning figures line the sides. The tomb originally stood in the Church of the Holy Cross, across the river on Cathedral Island. Today you can study it at eye level, which is impossible in most churches.

Upstairs, the story moves forward in time. The museum holds a large collection of Polish painting, from older portraits to works of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. You will see changes in fashion, in faith, and in the way artists looked at their own country. There are also galleries of decorative arts: silver, glass, furniture and objects of daily life. These small things often tell you more about how people lived than the grand canvases do.

The museum is also strong in art made after the Second World War. Wrocław became a lively centre for Polish artists in the decades after 1945. This collection shows how painters and sculptors responded to a country under communist rule, sometimes openly, sometimes in quieter, coded ways. If you prefer modern work, the museum runs a separate branch for contemporary art in the Four Domes Pavilion, near Centennial Hall.

The museum family does not end there. Just across the park from this building stands a round rotunda that holds the Racławice Panorama. It is a huge painted canvas that wraps all the way around the viewer and shows a battle from the late eighteenth century. The Panorama is a branch of this same museum. Many visitors see both on the same day, and the short walk between them is pleasant under the trees.

A few practical thoughts. The building is large, and the floors are spread out, so choose two or three sections rather than trying to see everything at once. The medieval rooms reward slow looking, so give them real time. Bring a light layer, because the old stone interiors can feel cool. And if the vine is red when you visit, take your photograph from the far side of the square, where the whole facade fits into one frame.

Before you leave, come back outside and look at the wall once more. Notice the windows set deep in the leaves, like eyes in a face. Behind them, saints and dukes and painted landscapes wait in quiet rooms that were once full of clerks and paperwork. The building has served an empire, survived a war, and changed its purpose completely. Listen again to the leaves moving... and take that sound with you as you walk back toward the river.
