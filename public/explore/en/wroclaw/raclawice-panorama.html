The guide

The story

Step into the dim corridor and let your eyes adjust. The air is cool and still, and the noise of the city fades behind you. You climb a short spiral stair toward a soft, even light. Then the walls open out on every side, and you are standing on a hill in the Polish countryside, on a spring day more than two centuries ago. Smoke drifts across green fields. Horses rear. Men run toward cannon with scythes raised.

This is the Racławice Panorama. It is one enormous painting, hung in a continuous circle around the viewing platform. The canvas is roughly fifteen metres high and more than a hundred metres long. There is no frame, no edge, and no corner where your eye can rest. Wherever you turn, the battle continues. That is the whole idea of a panorama: you do not look at the picture. You stand inside it.

The scene shows the Battle of Racławice, fought on 4 April 1794. Poland at that time was being divided up by its powerful neighbours, Russia, Prussia, and Austria. A rising began in the spring of that year, led by Tadeusz Kościuszko. He was a military engineer who had already fought in the American War of Independence. At Racławice, near Kraków, his forces met a Russian army. Against the odds, the Poles won the day.

What made the victory famous was not the regular soldiers. It was the peasants. Kościuszko brought in farmers armed with war scythes, the blades turned upright on long poles. Poles call these men kosynierzy, which simply means scythemen. They charged the Russian guns directly. For a nation without its own state, that image became very powerful. It showed nobles and peasants fighting side by side for the same country.

Look for the figure most visitors search for first. One peasant became a legend after the battle: Wojciech Bartosz Głowacki. According to the popular story, he put out the fuse of a Russian cannon with his cap before it could fire. Historians treat some of the details with caution, but the tale spread widely. Kościuszko rewarded him and made him an officer. In the painting, the charge of the scythemen against the guns is the dramatic centre of the action.

The painting itself was made a century after the battle, in 1893 and 1894. Two artists led the work, Jan Styka and Wojciech Kossak. Kossak was known for painting horses and cavalry, and you can see his skill in the riders and mounts. Styka designed much of the overall composition. They did not work alone. A team of other painters helped them, each handling landscape, sky, or groups of figures. The canvas was finished in under a year.

Here is the first surprise. This painting was not made for Wrocław at all. It was created for the city of Lwów, which is Lviv in today's Ukraine. In the eighteen-nineties, Lwów was a major Polish cultural centre. The panorama was unveiled there in 1894, timed for the hundredth anniversary of the battle. It stood in its own rotunda in a city park and drew huge crowds for decades.

Then came the Second World War. The rotunda in Lwów was damaged, and so was the canvas. After the war, the borders of Europe moved. Lwów became part of the Soviet Union, and many Poles from the east were resettled in the west, often here in Wrocław. The painting travelled with that movement. It arrived in Wrocław in rolls in 1946, torn in places and in need of serious care.

Now the second surprise. For almost forty years, the painting stayed rolled up, out of public view. The reason was politics as much as money. Poland was then in the Soviet sphere, and this canvas shows Poles defeating a Russian army. For the authorities, that was a sensitive image to put on display. Restorers worked on it quietly over the years, and plans for a new building moved slowly.

The round building around you was built for the painting here in Wrocław. Construction began in the nineteen-sixties, but the panorama only opened to the public in 1985. By then, many people saw its return as a symbol in itself. Look at the rotunda from outside when you leave. It is a modern concrete drum, plain and practical. Its job is to hold the canvas in a perfect circle and control the light.

Now look down, just below the painted horizon. The space between the platform and the canvas is filled with real objects: earth, grass, fences, broken carts, and pieces of equipment. This is called the faux terrain, a French term meaning false ground. It hides the bottom edge of the painting. Try to find the exact line where the real soil ends and the painted soil begins. Most people cannot.

The lighting adds to the effect. Daylight enters from above, and the roof over the platform hides its source. You never see a lamp or a window. That is why the sky in the painting seems to glow on its own. Walk slowly around the platform and notice how the scene changes. One side shows quiet villages and church towers. Another shows smoke, wounded men, and the heaviest fighting.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and turn one full circle. Notice how much the painters asked of you. They give no single point to look at and no beginning or end. You have to choose where to look, just as a witness would. When you go back down the stairs and out into the park, the trees and the city noise may feel, for a few seconds, slightly less real than the fields you just left.
