The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the wind in the pines, there is the soft knock of water against stone. Somewhere out on the lake, a loon may call. It is a long, wavering cry that carries across the surface. The air smells of resin, wet moss, and cold granite. This is La Mauricie National Park, a stretch of forest, rock, and water just north of Shawinigan. It is one of the quietest corners of southern Quebec.

The ground under your feet is very old. This park sits on the Canadian Shield, a vast plate of ancient rock that forms the core of the North American continent. Here it rises into the southern edge of the Laurentian Mountains. These are not sharp peaks. They are low, rounded hills, worn down over enormous spans of time. Look at any bare rock face by the road and you are looking at some of the oldest stone on Earth.

The lakes came much later. During the last Ice Age, thick glaciers moved across this land. They scraped the rock, carved out long valleys, and dropped rubble as they melted. When the ice retreated, water filled the hollows it left behind. The result is the landscape around you today. The park holds well over one hundred and fifty lakes. Many of them are long and narrow, and they follow the lines the ice cut into the rock.

Long before this was a park, it was home to Indigenous peoples. The Atikamekw, whose territory reaches up into the upper Saint-Maurice valley, travelled these waterways for generations. Rivers and lakes were roads. Canoes carried families, furs, and goods between camps. Portages, the short trails where travellers carried their canoes overland, linked one lake to the next. When you see a canoe glide past today, it is following a very old way of moving through this country.

Then came the timber era. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the forests of the Mauricie region fed a large logging industry. Each spring, cut logs were floated down the rivers toward the Saint-Maurice River, which marks the eastern edge of the park. This practice was called log driving. Crews rode the floating timber and broke up jams by hand. It was cold, loud, and dangerous work. Shawinigan itself grew strong on wood, paper, and hydroelectric power from the river.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Before it became public land, much of this territory was leased to private hunting and fishing clubs. Wealthy members, many from the cities and from the United States, came up for trout and game. They built lodges along quiet lakes. Some of that history survives. On the shores of Lac à la Pêche, the Wabenaki and Andrew lodges carry on that old club tradition. Today they serve groups of visitors instead of private members.

The park was created in 1970. A local figure played a key part. Jean Chrétien, who represented the Shawinigan area in Parliament, was then the federal minister responsible for national parks. He pushed to protect this piece of his home region. Years later, he became Prime Minister of Canada. So this forest is tied, in a small way, to one of the best-known political careers in the country.

The park covers more than five hundred square kilometres. A long scenic parkway crosses it and links the two main entrances, one near Saint-Jean-des-Piles in the east and one near Saint-Mathieu-du-Parc in the southwest. The road climbs and dips over the hills. It has many lookouts along the way. From several of them, you can see lakes laid out below like pieces of dark glass, with ridges of forest rolling away behind them toward the horizon.

One of the best-known views is over Lac Wapizagonke. This long, slender lake runs through a deep valley, with steep wooded slopes on both sides. From above, its shape looks almost like a river. Canoeists paddle its length, and some camp at sites that can only be reached by water. On a calm morning, mist often lies on the surface. The far shore appears slowly as the sun climbs and the air warms.

Now a second surprise. The forest here is a meeting zone. Southern hardwoods such as maple and birch grow beside northern conifers such as spruce and fir. This mix is why autumn here is so striking. The maples turn deep red and orange, the birches go gold, and the dark evergreens hold their colour. The contrast is sharp. Many Quebec families make a special trip in late September or early October just to drive the parkway and see it.

Wildlife moves quietly through all of this. Moose feed in wetlands at dawn and dusk. Black bears live in the forest, so food must always be stored properly. Beavers build dams and lodges of sticks and mud, and you can often spot their work at the edge of small ponds. Along the shore, look for chewed stumps cut into neat points. And on the larger lakes, the common loon raises its young each summer.

Water also falls here. Waterfalls such as Waber Falls drop over shelves of granite deep in the forest. Reaching them means a walk on a marked trail, past ferns and over roots and stones. The sound grows before you see anything. Then the trees open, and white water pours over dark rock into a cool pool. Trails of many lengths cross the park, from short loops near the road to long routes into the backcountry.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. Notice the colour of the water, which is often a dark tea-brown from the forest soils around it. Notice the lichen on the rocks, and the way the light falls through the branches. Listen again for wind, for water, for a bird you cannot name. When you leave, the parkway will carry you back toward town. But this quiet will stay a little while longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Drive the scenic parkway slowly and stop at the lookouts.
Visit in late September for peak autumn colour.
Store all food securely because black bears live here.
Rent a canoe to explore Lac Wapizagonke from the water.
Bring layers since lakeside mornings can be cold and misty.
