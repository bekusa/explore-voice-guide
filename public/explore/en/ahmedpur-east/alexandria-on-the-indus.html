The guide

The story

Feel the ground under your feet for a moment. It is soft, pale, and fine, the kind of soil that rivers leave behind. Around you, the flat land of southern Punjab runs out toward a low horizon. The air often carries dust and the green smell of irrigated fields. Somewhere beyond the haze, great rivers meet. This is the landscape where an army from Macedonia once stopped and gave an order to build a city.

That city is known today as Alexandria on the Indus. Here is the first surprise, and it is an honest one. No one knows exactly where it stood. There is no gate to walk through and no labelled ruin. What you have instead is a question that historians have argued about for a very long time. So this guide will walk with you through the evidence, the guesses, and the land itself.

Let us begin with Alexander the Great. In the fourth century before Christ, he led his army out of Greece and across the Persian Empire. By the late 320s BCE, he had reached the Indus valley. His soldiers were exhausted and far from home. They refused to march further east. So Alexander turned south, and he followed the rivers toward the sea.

This journey down the rivers matters for the place where you stand. The army travelled partly by boat and partly along the banks. The ancient historian Arrian, writing several centuries later from earlier sources, describes how Alexander reached the meeting point of a great tributary with the Indus. There, according to Arrian, he ordered a city to be founded. He also ordered dockyards. He hoped the city would grow large and important, because so many waters met there.

The rivers in question are worth understanding. The five rivers of Punjab gather together before they reach the Indus. Today that combined stream is called the Panjnad, which simply means five rivers. The Greeks knew these rivers by other names. The Chenab, for example, appears in Greek texts as the Acesines. The confluence that Alexander saw was a place of enormous moving water, and a natural point for trade and control.

Now comes the second surprise. The rivers of this plain do not stay still. Over many centuries, the Indus and its tributaries have shifted their courses, sometimes by many kilometres. A riverbank in Alexander's time might lie under farmland today. A confluence might have moved downstream or upstream. This is the main reason the city is so hard to find. The water that defined it has wandered away.

Scholars have proposed several candidate sites. One of the most discussed is Uch, today often called Uch Sharif, a short drive from Ahmedpur East. Uch is very old, and it lies near the region where the rivers once joined. Other researchers point to places further south along the Indus, such as the area around Mithankot. None of these identifications is proven. Each one is a reasonable argument built on old texts, geography, and the long memory of settlement.

If you travel to Uch, you will find a town with deep layers of history, even if the Greek city cannot be confirmed there. Uch became a major centre of Sufi learning in the medieval period. Its tombs rise above the plain in brick and tile. The best known is the tomb of Bibi Jawindi, decorated with glazed tiles in blue and white. Part of that tomb has collapsed, and the broken side shows the inner structure of the brickwork.

Those tombs belong to a much later chapter than Alexander. Still, they help explain why Uch is part of this story. A place that people chose again and again, across many centuries, is often a place with good water, good ground, and good routes. Settlements tend to return to the same advantages. In that sense, the medieval town may sit close to older foundations that are now buried or washed away.

What happened to Alexander's city after he left? Here the record becomes very thin. Alexander continued south toward the sea, and he died a few years later in Babylon. His empire broke apart among his generals. Many of the cities he founded faded, changed names, or merged into local towns. Alexandria on the Indus seems to have followed that path. It does not appear as a great city in later history.

It helps to remember how many Alexandrias there were. Alexander gave his name to a long list of foundations across his empire. The one in Egypt became famous for its library and lighthouse. Others, in Central Asia and the Indus region, became garrison towns or trading posts. Some are known only from a single line in an ancient text. This Alexandria belongs to that quieter group, known mostly through words rather than stones.

Ahmedpur East itself carries a different chapter of the region. The town grew in the territory once ruled by the Nawabs of Bahawalpur, a princely state of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Nearby, at Dera Nawab Sahib, stands the Sadiq Garh Palace, a large residence of that dynasty. So within a short distance, you can trace a long arc, from Greek soldiers to Sufi saints to princely rulers.

For a visitor, this means the experience here is mostly about imagination and landscape. Look at the flatness of the plain and the way water channels cut through the fields. Notice the date palms, the mud brick walls, and the dust that rises behind carts and motorcycles. Listen for the call to prayer drifting across from distant villages. These are the same broad conditions, water and heat and open ground, that drew settlers here for thousands of years.

Before you move on, take one more look at the horizon. Somewhere out there, perhaps under a field or beside an old river channel, the remains of a Greek foundation may still lie. Or they may be gone completely, carried off by floods. Either way, the soil under your feet is river soil, laid down by the same waters Alexander saw. As you walk away, notice how quietly the land holds that history.
