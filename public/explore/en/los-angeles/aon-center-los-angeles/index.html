The guide

The story

Stand at the base of this tower and tilt your head back. The building does not taper or step. It rises as one dark, straight shaft, and the vertical lines pull your eyes upward until the top seems to lean toward you. Down here, at street level on Wilshire Boulevard, you hear traffic, footsteps, and the hum of air vents. Up there, the facade turns quiet and almost black against the bright Los Angeles sky.

This is the Aon Center, at 707 Wilshire Boulevard in downtown Los Angeles. It was completed in the early 1970s, and it stands sixty-two stories tall. For years it was the tallest building in the city. It was also, for a time, the tallest building in the western United States. Today newer towers crowd around it. But this one still has a presence that is hard to ignore, because of its simple shape and its dark, serious colour.

The tower did not begin life with the name you see today. It opened as the United California Bank Building, the headquarters of a major regional bank. Later, after bank mergers, it carried the name of First Interstate. The current name comes from Aon, the insurance and professional services company, which became a major tenant. Office towers in American cities often change names like this. The steel stays the same, but the sign on the door follows the money.

The design came from the office of Charles Luckman, an architect with a remarkable life story. Before he became one of the busiest architects in postwar Los Angeles, Luckman was a business executive. He rose to lead a large soap and consumer goods company. Then he returned to architecture and built a firm known for big commercial and civic projects. That background makes sense here. This is a building designed to look efficient, confident, and very corporate.

Look closely at the skin of the tower. The main surface is dark, a deep bronze-black finish of metal and tinted glass. Along the edges and corners, pale vertical bands run from the bottom to the top. Those light stripes act like pencil lines on a drawing. They outline the building and make it look even taller and thinner than it is. Notice also that the corners are cut at an angle, not square. This small detail softens the block and catches the light differently through the day.

The early 1970s were a turning point for this part of the city. For decades, Los Angeles had a strict height limit for most buildings. City Hall was allowed to stand out, and most other buildings stayed relatively low. When the limit was lifted in the late 1950s, developers began to build upward. This tower belongs to that first wave of real skyscrapers in downtown. In a sense, you are looking at the moment Los Angeles decided to have a skyline.

Building tall here carries a special challenge — earthquakes. Southern California sits among active faults, so engineers must design towers that can move and flex without failing. This one uses a steel frame, which can bend under stress rather than crack. You will not see that engineering from the sidewalk. But it is a quiet part of every tall building in this city. The structure must be ready for a day that may never come.

Now here is the story that most visitors walk past without knowing. In May 1988, a serious fire broke out inside this tower. It started on one of the lower office floors and spread upward through several levels. Firefighters fought it for hours, climbing stairs with heavy equipment. Flames broke through windows, and the night sky over downtown glowed orange. One person, a building maintenance worker, lost his life.

The fire changed the law. At the time, the building did not have a complete sprinkler system on its office floors, and that was legal for older towers. After the fire, Los Angeles required existing high-rise buildings to install sprinklers. So the safety systems in many towers around you today trace back to that night. It is a hard lesson, written into the rules of the city. And it began right here.

The tower also changed how fire departments think about tall buildings. Fighting a fire many floors above the street is very different from fighting one in a house. Water must be pumped up through the building. Crews must climb, carry gear, and manage smoke in stairwells. Experts studied this fire closely afterward, and it became a reference case in training and planning. A building that looks so calm from outside became an important classroom.

Take a moment to look at the neighbourhood around you. This stretch of Wilshire Boulevard sits at the edge of the financial district, close to Bunker Hill. The street itself is one of the great roads of Los Angeles. It runs west for many kilometres, through Koreatown and Beverly Hills, almost all the way to the ocean. Starting here, among glass and steel, it is hard to imagine palm trees and beaches at the other end. But they are there.

Nearby, you can see how downtown has kept growing upward. Newer towers use lighter glass, curved tops, and bright lighting at night. Next to them, this tower looks more serious, almost old-fashioned in its dark colour. Yet that contrast is part of its appeal. Many people who know the skyline well say this building gives the cluster of towers its weight. It is the dark note in a row of pale and shining shapes.

This is a working office building, so the inside is mainly for tenants and their visitors. The best experience is outside. Walk across the street and look back to see the whole height at once. Then walk to the corner and watch how the angled edges catch the light. In the late afternoon, the pale bands turn warm, while the dark panels hold the reflection of the sky.

As you leave, look back one more time. Think of the bankers who opened this tower, the firefighters who climbed its stairs, and the office workers who still ride its elevators every morning. Above the noise of Wilshire Boulevard, the dark shaft rises, steady and straight. And the next time you see the downtown skyline from a freeway or a hill, look for the darkest tower. It will probably be this one.
