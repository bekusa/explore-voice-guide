The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the plaza and look up. Above you, a vast pale form rests on its supports, and its curved underside casts a broad pool of shade across the ground. The paving feels cool, even on a warm Los Angeles afternoon. Voices from the park echo softly beneath the overhang. Somewhere nearby, palm fronds rattle in the breeze. This is the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art. Before you learn a single date, notice how the building makes you slow down and tilt your head back.

The name is a promise. Narrative art means pictures that tell stories. A painting can show one decisive moment. A comic strip moves panel by panel. A storyboard maps a film scene before a single camera rolls. Most art museums keep these forms in separate rooms, or leave some of them out entirely. This museum puts them side by side. The idea is simple. Illustration, comics and film art belong in the same conversation as painting, and each one can teach us how the others work.

The museum was founded by George Lucas, the filmmaker behind Star Wars and American Graffiti, together with his wife, Mellody Hobson, a business leader from Chicago. Lucas has collected illustration and popular art for decades. He started long before many critics took these forms seriously. He is known as a major collector of Norman Rockwell, the American painter whose magazine covers showed everyday life in small, careful scenes. That personal collection became the seed of this institution.

Here is something many visitors do not know. Los Angeles was not the first choice. Early plans looked at the Presidio in San Francisco, close to Lucas's own film companies. Then the project moved to Chicago, Hobson's home city, with a site on the lakefront. A parks advocacy group challenged the use of that public land in court, and the plan stalled. In the late 2010s, the founders turned to Los Angeles instead. In the end, the city of cinema became the home.

Look around, and you will see why this site fits. Exposition Park is a cultural campus in South Los Angeles. The Natural History Museum is close by. So is the California Science Center, home of the Space Shuttle Endeavour. The Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum rises nearby, and the University of Southern California sits just across the road. Families, students and school groups move between these buildings all day. The new museum joins a neighbourhood that was already built around learning.

The building is the work of MAD Architects, the studio led by the Chinese architect Ma Yansong. MAD is known for soft, flowing forms that look shaped by wind or water rather than drawn with a ruler. Here, the main body rises above the ground on supports, so the structure seems to hover. People have compared it to a cloud, a ship, or a creature from science fiction. Whatever you see, notice one thing. There are almost no sharp corners on the outside.

The lifted form is more than a visual gesture. It creates a large, shaded public space at ground level. In a city with long, bright summers, shade is a real gift. This covered area works like a town square. It is open, walkable, and has no walls to stop you. You can pause here without entering the galleries at all. It is a quiet reminder that a museum can also serve the people who simply pass through.

Around the building, old asphalt has given way to gardens. The landscape design comes from Studio-MLA, the practice led by the Los Angeles landscape architect Mia Lehrer. Before construction, much of this ground was surface parking. Now there are paths, lawns and trees, planted with the local climate in mind. Walk the edges slowly. The planting changes the sound of the place. You may hear more birdsong and less traffic than you expect this close to a freeway.

Inside, the collection covers a wide range. You may find paintings by Norman Rockwell, original comic art, children's book illustration, photography, and material from the history of film. Some works connect to Lucas's own long career in cinema. Concept art and production pieces show how a story is built before it reaches the screen. The galleries change over time, so the exact works on view will vary. Read the labels closely. They often explain how a single image carries a whole story.

Here is a second surprise. For much of the twentieth century, illustration was treated as commercial work, not serious art. Comic pages were often thrown away after printing. Many original drawings survive only by chance — rescued from an office, a drawer, a garage. A museum that collects and studies them makes a quiet argument. The images that millions of people grew up with deserve the same care as anything hanging in a grand gallery.

The building also includes theatres for film screenings, and spaces for talks and education. That fits the founding idea. Moving images are part of the same story as paintings and drawings. Give yourself time here. The galleries spread across several levels, and the scale can surprise first-time visitors. Comfortable shoes help. So does a plan. Choose a few rooms to see properly, rather than rushing through everything in one breath.

Exposition Park is easy to reach by public transport. The Metro E Line stops at the park, which saves you from hunting for parking. That matters on event days, when football games or concerts fill the Coliseum or the nearby stadium. On those days, expect crowds on the surrounding streets. Weekday mornings tend to be calmer. If you have time, pair your visit with a walk through the Rose Garden, a formal garden that sits in the heart of the park.

Before you leave, step out from under the overhang and look at the building one more time. Watch how the light moves across its curved surface as clouds pass. Notice the people around you... a child pointing up, a student cutting across the plaza, someone resting in the shade. Each of them is part of a small story playing out right here. That is what this place is about. Pictures, and the people who stop to look at them.
