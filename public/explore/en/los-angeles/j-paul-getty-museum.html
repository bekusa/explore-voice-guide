The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the roar of the freeway fades into a soft hum far below. Water runs somewhere nearby. Under your feet, the stone is pale, warm, and slightly rough. Run your hand along a wall and you will feel tiny pits and grooves. This is travertine, cut from quarries in Italy. Welcome to the Getty Center, the hilltop home of the J. Paul Getty Museum in Los Angeles.

Most visitors arrive the same way. They leave the car or bus at the foot of the hill and board a small, driverless tram. It climbs slowly through brush and oak, and the city opens up behind you. The ride takes only a few minutes. It works like a gentle airlock between the traffic of the basin and the quiet campus above. By the time the doors open, the pace of your day has already changed.

The museum carries the name of Jean Paul Getty, an American oil businessman of the twentieth century. He became one of the richest people in the world. He was also a serious collector, with strong tastes in Greek and Roman antiquities, French furniture, and European paintings. Here is the first surprise. Getty spent most of his later life in England, and he never saw this hilltop campus. It was built many years after his death in 1976, using the fortune he left to the trust that bears his name.

That fortune made the Getty trust one of the best-funded art institutions on Earth. The money paid for more than a museum. This campus also holds research, conservation, and education programmes. Scholars study here. Conservators repair fragile objects in labs you will never see. The galleries you walk through are only the public face of a much larger operation working quietly behind the stone walls.

The architect was Richard Meier, known for crisp, white, geometric buildings. The Getty Center opened to the public in December 1997, after many years of planning and construction. Neighbours in the wealthy hills around here worried about height, noise, and views. So Meier's design keeps the buildings fairly low, spread along two ridges of the hill. The result feels less like a single museum and more like a small hilltop town, with plazas, courtyards, and walkways between them.

Look again at that pale stone. Meier mixed two main materials. Some walls are clad in light metal panels with a soft, off-white finish. Others use the rough Italian travertine. The stone was split along its natural grain, so the faces stay uneven and textured. Here is the second surprise. If you look closely, some blocks contain fossils — the faint outlines of leaves, twigs, and even feathers, trapped when the stone formed. Keep your eyes open as you walk. Finding one feels like a small private reward.

Now to the collection itself. The galleries are split across several linked pavilions. Each one has its own focus. You will find European paintings, sculpture, drawings, decorative arts, and photographs. One famous highlight is Vincent van Gogh's painting of irises, with its thick, curling strokes of blue and green. Visitors often gather in front of it, so be patient. The crowd usually moves on within a minute or two.

Do not skip the decorative arts rooms. Many of them show French furniture and objects from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — gilded cabinets, clocks, tapestries, and porcelain. Some rooms are panelled and lit to suggest the grand homes these pieces came from. Getty himself loved this kind of craftsmanship. Stand close and look at the inlaid wood. Tiny pieces of different timbers form flowers, scrolls, and patterns, all fitted together by hand.

For something quieter, look for the illuminated manuscripts. These are handwritten books from the Middle Ages, decorated with paint and real gold. Because pages fade in light, the museum shows only a few at a time and changes them regularly. That means what you see today may be gone next visit. The rooms are dim on purpose. Let your eyes adjust, and the colours will start to glow from the page.

The photography galleries are another strong part of the collection. Photographs are also sensitive to light, so these shows rotate too. Then step outside again. Between the pavilions, the architecture keeps framing views. A gap between two walls might hold a slice of ocean. A staircase might end at a window full of mountains. Meier designed these moments deliberately. The art is not only on the walls inside.

Below the main plaza lies the Central Garden, designed by the artist Robert Irwin. He treated it as a work of art in its own right, not a decoration. A path zigzags down the slope beside a stream. Water runs over stone, and the sound changes as you descend. At the bottom, a pool holds a maze of clipped azaleas. The planting shifts with the seasons, so the garden never looks quite the same twice.

On a clear day, the views from the terraces stretch a long way. To one side, the city spreads in a grid toward downtown. To the other, you may see the Pacific Ocean and the Santa Monica Mountains. On the southern edge, a cactus garden sits on a promontory, with spiky desert plants set against the sky. Afternoon light here turns the travertine gold, and the white panels take on a soft peach tone.

One more thing to know. The J. Paul Getty Museum has two homes. This hilltop campus is one. The other is the Getty Villa, near the coast in Pacific Palisades. The Villa is modelled on an ancient Roman country house and focuses on Greek, Roman, and Etruscan art. The Etruscans were an ancient people of central Italy. If you love the ancient world, the Villa is a separate trip worth planning.

Before you leave, find a quiet bench on one of the terraces. Listen again. The water is still running. The freeway hum is still there, faint and far away. Look down at the stone under your hand, and maybe you will spot one last fossil leaf. Then take the tram back down, and watch the white buildings shrink into the hillside behind you.
