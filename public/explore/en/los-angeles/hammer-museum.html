The guide

The story

Start at the corner of Wilshire and Westwood. Traffic moves in steady waves, buses sigh at the curb, and office workers cut past with coffee in hand. Then you notice the building itself. It is wrapped in horizontal bands of marble, pale grey and white, stacked like layers in a cake. On a bright Los Angeles afternoon the stripes almost glow. Behind them sits one of the most unusual art museums in the city. It is part private collection, part university laboratory, and part public living room.

This is the Hammer Museum. Its name comes from Armand Hammer, a businessman whose life sounds almost invented. He was a physician by training. As a young man in the 1920s, he did business in the Soviet Union, at a time when very few Americans did. Later he became the head of Occidental Petroleum, the oil company whose tower still rises right beside you. Hammer collected art for decades. Near the end of his life, he decided that his collection needed a home with his own name on it.

That decision caused a stir. For years many people expected his paintings to go to the Los Angeles County Museum of Art, across town. Instead, Hammer chose to build his own museum here in Westwood, attached to his company's headquarters. Critics at the time were not always kind. Some saw the project as a monument to one man. But the story has a quiet twist. Hammer died in December 1990, only weeks after the museum opened its doors.

After his death, the young museum faced an uncertain future. The answer came from just up the street. In the mid-1990s the University of California, Los Angeles took over its operation. UCLA's campus begins only a short walk north of here. That partnership changed everything. A private collector's museum slowly became a public institution with a university's curiosity. Today the Hammer belongs to UCLA, and you can feel that academic energy in the programs, the lectures, and the art on the walls.

Walk inside and the city noise drops away. The heart of the building is an open courtyard, surrounded by galleries on the upper level. Light falls straight down into this space. People sit at tables, read, talk, and drift between exhibitions. The architecture here is calm and geometric. The original building was designed by the architect Edward Larrabee Barnes. In more recent years, a renovation by the Los Angeles firm Michael Maltzan Architecture opened the museum outward, giving it a much stronger face on Wilshire Boulevard.

Now to the art Hammer himself gathered. His collection leans toward European painting, especially French work from the nineteenth century. Look for the canvases of the Impressionists and the artists who came after them. They were painters who tried to catch light, weather, and movement with quick, broken brushstrokes. One of the treasures here is a painting by Vincent van Gogh. It shows the hospital at Saint-Rémy in southern France, where he stayed during a very hard period of his life.

Stand in front of that van Gogh for a moment. The trees twist upward. The paint sits thick on the surface, almost like a relief. You can see the direction of every stroke. Van Gogh painted it during his voluntary stay at the asylum, not long before his death. Knowing that, the bright colours feel more complicated. There is energy here, but also a kind of restlessness. It is a small window into a mind working at full speed.

The collection reaches further back, too. There is a painting by Rembrandt of the goddess Juno, queen of the gods in Roman myth, dressed in rich fabric and jewels. There is also a portrait by John Singer Sargent of a doctor in a flowing scarlet robe. Many visitors walk past that red figure without realizing the sitter was a famous Paris surgeon. The colour is so intense that the painting almost fills the room by itself.

Here is something most visitors never hear. Armand Hammer once owned a notebook by Leonardo da Vinci. It was filled with Leonardo's handwriting, sketches, and ideas about water, rivers, and the moon. Hammer bought it in 1980 and renamed it after himself. After his death, the notebook was sold at auction, and the buyer was Bill Gates. So for a short time, a piece of Leonardo's mind belonged to the man whose name is on this building.

Yet the Hammer today is far more than its founder's collection. It has become one of the most important places in Los Angeles for contemporary art. That means art made by living artists, often right now, often in this city. The museum's Made in L.A. exhibition, which began in 2012, surveys artists working across the region. It is one of the best ways to understand what Los Angeles looks like through the eyes of the people who make art here.

The university connection also brings works on paper into the story. The Hammer cares for the Grunwald Center collection, a large UCLA holding of prints, drawings, and photographs. Paper is fragile and cannot sit in bright light for long. So these works appear in rotating shows rather than on permanent display. If you see a quiet gallery of etchings or drawings, slow down. These are often intimate pieces, where you can follow the artist's hand line by line.

The museum is also a place for conversation and film. The Billy Wilder Theater, named for the Hollywood director, sits inside the building. It hosts screenings from the UCLA Film and Television Archive, one of the great film collections in the country. Evenings often bring talks, readings, and performances. On those nights the courtyard fills with voices, and the museum feels less like a temple to art and more like a town square for ideas.

Before you leave, step back out toward Wilshire. Look once more at those marble stripes, and at the office tower rising next to them. One man built this place to hold his name and his paintings. A university turned it into something wider and more open. Listen to the traffic again, feel the warm air, and notice how the art inside and the city outside sit only a few steps apart.
