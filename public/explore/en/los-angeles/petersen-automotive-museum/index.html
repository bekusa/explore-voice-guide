The guide

The story

Stand on the corner of Wilshire Boulevard and Fairfax Avenue for a moment. Traffic rolls past in both directions, and the sound of engines never quite stops. Then look up. A wall of deep red rises in front of you, wrapped in long ribbons of brushed stainless steel. In the Los Angeles sun, the metal flashes silver one second and soft grey the next. Few buildings in this city look like they are moving. This one does.

This is the Petersen Automotive Museum, and it sits in exactly the right place. Los Angeles is a city shaped by the car. Its freeways, its drive-ins, its sprawling neighbourhoods all grew around the idea that people would drive everywhere. A museum about cars could have been built in many cities. Here, it feels less like a museum and more like a family portrait.

The name belongs to Robert Petersen, a publisher who built a magazine empire around American car culture. In 1948 he launched Hot Rod magazine, aimed at young men who rebuilt and raced their own cars. Motor Trend and many other titles followed. Petersen and his wife Margie later decided that the cars and the culture behind them deserved a permanent home. The museum opened here in 1994.

Here is the first surprise. The building behind those steel ribbons was not designed for cars at all. It began life in the early 1960s as a department store, a branch of the Ohrbach's chain. Shoppers once came through these doors for coats and kitchenware. When the museum moved in, it inherited a big, plain, boxy structure. For about twenty years, that is what visitors saw. It was practical, but nobody stopped on the pavement to stare.

That changed with a major redesign by the architecture firm Kohn Pedersen Fox. The museum closed, and when it reopened in December 2015, the old box was gone from view. The firm painted the facade a strong red and wrapped it in those flowing steel bands. Many people see the shape of a speeding car in them, or the lines of air moving over a body in a wind tunnel. Others find it too loud for the street. Either way, nobody walks past without an opinion.

Notice how the ribbons are placed. They curve over the corner and pull your eye along Wilshire, almost like a car turning into the boulevard. This fits the neighbourhood's own history. This stretch of Wilshire is known as the Miracle Mile. In the 1920s, a developer named A. W. Ross promoted it as a shopping district built for drivers, not walkers. Buildings here were designed to catch the eye of someone passing at speed. The red facade continues that tradition, nearly a century later.

Inside, the museum is spread across three main floors, and each one has its own mood. The exhibitions change regularly, so the exact cars you see today may not be here next year. What stays constant is the approach. The Petersen treats cars as design objects, as engineering puzzles, and as pieces of social history. You will find gleaming vintage machines next to race cars, prototypes, and vehicles with very strange ideas behind them.

One floor usually focuses on history, on how the automobile changed daily life and the shape of cities like this one. Another leans toward industry and design, with displays on how cars are styled, tested, and built. A third celebrates artistry, which in Los Angeles often means custom cars, hot rods, and lowriders. That last tradition matters here. Lowriders grew out of Mexican American communities in Southern California, and they are as much about family pride and craftsmanship as about the car itself.

This is Los Angeles, so Hollywood is never far away. The museum has a long connection with film and television cars. Over the years, visitors have seen famous vehicles from the screen, including Batmobiles and cars from well-known movie franchises. There is something odd about seeing a film car up close. On screen it looked enormous and perfect. In person you notice the welds, the scuffs, the small tricks that made it work for the camera.

Now for the second surprise, and many visitors leave without knowing it exists. Below the public galleries is the Vault. It holds a large part of the collection that is not on display upstairs, several hundred vehicles parked in long rows under the building. Access is by guided tour, booked separately from general admission. If you love cars, this is often the part people remember most. The rows feel less like an exhibition and more like the garage of a very obsessive collector.

The Petersen is also a good place for younger visitors. The museum has run interactive spaces where children can learn how engines and design work by trying things with their hands. Even without those, kids tend to react to the cars in a direct way. They point at colours, at oversized wheels, at doors that open upwards. Watching a child see a strange car for the first time is part of the experience here.

When you step back outside, look along Wilshire. The Petersen is part of a cluster of cultural buildings often called Museum Row. Across the street is the Los Angeles County Museum of Art. A short walk away are the La Brea Tar Pits, where Ice Age fossils were pulled from natural asphalt seeps. Asphalt, oil, and cars... in this small corner of the city, the connections are hard to miss. Few places tell the story of fuel and motion so neatly in one walk.

You might take a few minutes before moving on. Cross to the opposite corner and look back at the facade as the light changes. Late afternoon sun turns the red warmer and makes the steel glow. At night, lights pick out the curves of the ribbons. It is a building that was meant to be seen from a moving car. Seen from the pavement, with time to stand still, it shows its layers more clearly.

So as you leave, listen again to the traffic on Wilshire. Every car passing this corner is part of the same story the museum tells inside. Some are ordinary commuter cars. A few might be lovingly restored classics. One day, some of them may sit in a gallery like the ones you just walked through. For now, they keep moving... past a red building that remembers where they came from.

Practical tips

View the facade from the opposite corner of Fairfax.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the facade.
Pair your visit with LACMA across the street.
Allow extra time if visiting with car-loving kids.
