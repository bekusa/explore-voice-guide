The guide

The story

Stand at the base of one leg and lay your palm flat against the steel. On a sunny afternoon it feels warm. On a winter morning it is cold enough to make you pull your hand back. The surface is brushed stainless steel, with fine grain lines running upward. Now tilt your head back and follow that grain with your eyes. The curve rises, narrows and disappears overhead. Somewhere far above you, it bends down to meet the other leg.

This is the Gateway Arch, on the west bank of the Mississippi River in Saint Louis. It stands 630 feet tall, roughly 192 metres. Here is the first surprise. It is exactly as wide at its base as it is tall. Your eye does not believe this. Almost everyone who stands here thinks the arch is taller than it is wide. The narrowing legs and the sweep of the curve play a quiet trick on the mind.

The arch is the tallest arch in the world. It is also the tallest human-made monument in the Western Hemisphere. Yet the arch does not feel heavy. It has no columns, no statues and no inscription carved across its face. It is a single line of steel drawn against the sky. That simplicity was deliberate. It came out of a design competition held in the late 1940s.

The winner of that competition was Eero Saarinen, a Finnish-born American architect. He later designed famous airport terminals and furniture, including chairs you may have sat in without knowing his name. His idea for Saint Louis was bold for its time. Other entries proposed buildings, plazas and colonnades. Saarinen proposed one curve. Sadly, he died in 1961, a few years before construction began. He never saw his arch rise above the river.

The shape is not a simple semicircle. It is based on a catenary — the curve a chain makes when you hold it by both ends and let it hang. Turn that hanging chain upside down and you get a shape that carries its own weight very efficiently. Saarinen and his engineers used a weighted version of this curve. The legs are thicker at the bottom and slimmer at the top. That is why the arch looks so light up there.

If you could slice through one leg, you would see a triangle. Each leg is made of stacked triangular sections. They are hollow, with an outer skin of stainless steel and an inner skin of carbon steel. Between the two skins, the lower sections are filled with concrete. The foundations also reach deep into the ground below you. All of that hidden mass is what keeps this slender shape standing against the wind.

Construction began in 1963. Workers built the two legs at the same time, section by section, using cranes that crept up the outside of the steel as it rose. The work was slow and exposed. Crews worked hundreds of feet in the air with the river wind pushing at them. Then came the hardest moment of all — fitting the final piece at the very top, where the two legs had to meet.

That final section was placed in October 1965. Here is the second surprise. The sun nearly stopped it. Steel expands in heat, and the sunlit south leg had grown slightly. The gap at the top was too narrow. To fix it, workers sprayed the leg with water from fire hoses to cool the metal. Jacks then pushed the legs apart just enough. The last section slid into place, and the arch was complete.

Why build it here? The arch is a memorial to the westward expansion of the United States. Saint Louis sat at the meeting point of great rivers, and in the nineteenth century it became a starting place for people heading west. The expedition of Lewis and Clark set out from this region. The arch was meant as a gateway — a symbol of the door to the West. Today the grounds form Gateway Arch National Park.

That story is not a simple one, and the site does not pretend it is. Look west across the park toward the Old Courthouse, with its green dome. In that building, an enslaved man named Dred Scott first brought his lawsuit for freedom. His case later reached the Supreme Court and helped push the country toward civil war. Westward expansion also meant the loss of land and life for Native nations. The museum beneath the arch addresses these stories too.

If you want to go up, you will ride a tram that is unlike any elevator. Small pod-shaped capsules hold just a handful of people. They climb the curved leg slowly, tilting to stay level as the angle changes. The ride is snug, so people who dislike tight spaces should know before they board. At the top, a narrow observation area runs along the curve. Small windows look east over the river and west over the city.

From up there, the view changes how you understand the place. To the east, the Mississippi River runs brown and wide, with Illinois on the far bank. Look north and you may spot the old Eads Bridge, one of the first great steel bridges, crossing the water. To the west, the Old Courthouse sits below you like a model. On a clear day, the city spreads out in a long grid of streets and rooftops.

Back on the ground, take a few steps away from the leg and watch the steel change colour. In the morning it can look silver and cool. At sunset it often turns gold, then pink, then grey. The surface reflects the sky, so no two hours look the same. Clouds slide across it. As you walk toward the river or the city, turn around once more. From every new angle, the curve looks slightly different — and it is still the same single line of steel.

Before you go

Practical tips

Reserve tram tickets ahead, as capsule rides often sell out.
Skip the tram if tight enclosed spaces make you uncomfortable.
Return near sunset to watch the steel change colour.
Walk to the riverfront for a full view of both legs.
Visit the museum beneath the arch before riding up.
