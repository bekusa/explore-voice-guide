The guide

The story

Start at the bottom of the grassy slope. The lawn rises ahead of you in a long green sweep, and at the top a pale stone building with tall columns waits. Behind you, water lies flat and still in a long basin. If the day is quiet, you can hear geese, a jogger's footsteps on the path, and the wind moving through old trees. This is Art Hill in Forest Park, and the climb is part of the visit.

At the crest stands a bronze rider on a horse, his sword raised and turned so that the hilt forms a cross. This is Saint Louis himself, King Louis the Ninth of France, the medieval king who gave the city its name. The statue is called the Apotheosis of Saint Louis. An apotheosis means a figure lifted up to glory. For many decades, this rider has served as a symbol of the city, appearing on seals, logos, and postcards.

To understand why a grand museum sits in the middle of a park, go back to 1904. That year, Saint Louis hosted a World's Fair called the Louisiana Purchase Exposition. It marked one hundred years since the United States bought a vast territory from France. Forest Park filled with palaces, lagoons, and crowds from around the world. Most of the fair's buildings were temporary, made of plaster over wooden frames. They were meant to last one summer, and almost all of them were torn down.

This building was different. It was designed by the architect Cass Gilbert as the fair's Palace of Fine Arts, and it was built to stay. Gilbert later became famous for skyscrapers and government buildings, but here he worked in a classical style, drawing on the architecture of ancient Rome. So when you look at the façade, you are looking at one of the few permanent survivors of that huge fair. The temporary city around it disappeared. This museum remained.

Look up at the stone above the entrance. Carved words state the museum's purpose: Dedicated to Art and Free to All. It is a simple sentence, but it carries a strong civic idea. Art here was meant for everyone in the city, not only for wealthy collectors. Today the museum is supported partly by local taxpayers through a public district that also helps fund other cultural institutions in and around Forest Park. That inscription is a promise the city still talks about.

Step inside, and the first big room is Sculpture Hall. The ceiling rises in a long, curved vault, and daylight falls from above. Gilbert took inspiration from the great public baths of ancient Rome, where huge vaulted halls gave ordinary citizens a sense of space and dignity. Stand in the centre for a moment. Listen to how sound travels here — voices soften and float upward. Statues line the hall, and the scale makes even a large group of visitors feel small.

The collection spreads out from this hall in many directions, across thousands of years and many cultures. You can walk from ancient Egypt to medieval Europe, from African masks to Asian ceramics, from Indigenous art of the Americas to modern painting. The variety is the point. This is an encyclopedic museum, which means it tries to show the art of the whole human story under one roof, rather than focusing on a single period or place.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The museum holds one of the most important collections in the world of works by Max Beckmann, a German painter of the early twentieth century. Beckmann painted strong figures, dark outlines, and scenes full of tension and myth. He lived through both world wars, and his art carries the weight of that era. He spent time in Saint Louis late in his life, teaching at a local university. If his name is new to you, his rooms are a good reason to slow down.

Another surprise waits among the Impressionist paintings. Look for a long canvas of water lilies by Claude Monet, painted late in his life at his garden in Giverny, in France. The surface is loose and layered, with blues, greens, and soft violets. This panel was originally part of a larger set. The other parts ended up in two different American museums, in Cleveland and Kansas City. So the painting in front of you is one piece of a much wider view of a pond.

As you move toward the back of the museum, the mood of the building changes. In 2013, a new East Building opened, designed by the British architect David Chipperfield. Where Gilbert used carved stone and columns, Chipperfield used dark, polished concrete and clean lines. Coffered ceilings — grids of deep square panels — let in soft, even light from above. The two buildings were made more than a century apart, yet they connect without either one trying to copy the other.

The East Building often shows modern and contemporary art, and it is a calm place to walk. The floors are quiet, the walls are open, and the light is steady across the day. Notice how the new wing sits low beside the old one. It does not rise above Gilbert's building or block the view from the hill. Many visitors pass through it quickly, but its restraint is part of its design.

When you have seen enough, step back outside and walk to the edge of Art Hill. The view runs down the lawn to the Grand Basin, a long pool from the fair years, with paths and trees on each side. In winter, after snow, this slope fills with families on sleds, and the hill becomes one of the liveliest places in the city. In summer, people spread out blankets and watch the light change across the water.

Before you go, look once more at the bronze king on his horse, and at the stone columns behind him. A great fair came and went here, and its palaces vanished. This one building held on, and the city kept its promise of art for everyone. As you walk down the hill, listen to the park around you — the geese, the voices, the wind in the trees — and let the museum stay in view behind you a little longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb Art Hill from the Grand Basin for the full approach.
Pause in Sculpture Hall's centre to hear the vaulted acoustics.
Save time for the Beckmann galleries if you like modern art.
Visit Art Hill after snowfall to watch the sledding crowds.
Walk to the East Building to compare old and new architecture.
