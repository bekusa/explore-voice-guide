The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beneath the traffic of south Saint Louis there is a softer layer of sound. Gravel shifts under shoes. Water drips from leaves. Somewhere ahead, a fountain runs over stone. The air here feels heavier and greener than the street you just left. You are standing in the oldest botanical garden in the United States that has stayed open without a break. It began in the middle of the nineteenth century and has never closed its gates for good since.

The garden began as the private project of one man, Henry Shaw. He was born in Sheffield, England, and arrived in Saint Louis as a young man. At that time the city was a river town on the edge of the American frontier. Shaw sold hardware and supplies to settlers heading west, and he became very wealthy. Then, while he was still fairly young, he did something unusual. He retired from business and gave the rest of his life to plants.

Shaw travelled in Europe and visited the great gardens there, including the royal gardens at Kew outside London. He came home with an idea. Saint Louis should have a garden that did more than look beautiful. It should also collect, study, and name plants from around the world. The garden opened to the public in 1859. Locals still call it Shaw's Garden, even though the official name is much longer.

Here is the first surprise. The man who built all of this is still here. Shaw died in 1889, and he chose to be buried on these grounds. His stone mausoleum stands among the trees, close to the house where he once lived. Inside, a marble effigy shows him lying at rest. Very few public gardens contain the grave of their founder. Here, the founder never really left.

That house is called Tower Grove House. It was Shaw's country home, built in the Italianate style, which was popular in the nineteenth century and borrowed from Italian villas. Look for the square tower and the wide, overhanging roof edges. When Shaw lived here, this land was open countryside. The city had not yet reached it. Today the house sits inside the garden as a museum, and its rooms help explain the private life behind the public project.

Now look for the shape that dominates the skyline of the garden. It is the Climatron, a large geodesic dome that opened in 1960. A geodesic dome is built from a network of triangles. This structure spreads its weight evenly, so it needs no columns inside. The idea came from the work of the American inventor Buckminster Fuller. When the Climatron opened, it was a bold piece of modern engineering. It was among the first greenhouses of its kind in the world.

Step inside and the climate changes at once. Your glasses may fog. The air is warm and wet, like a lowland rainforest. Paths wind past palms, giant leaves, orchids, and small waterfalls. Fish move in the pools below the walkways. Listen to the water and the hum of the climate system working overhead. Everything you see under that dome would die outdoors in a Missouri winter. The building exists to keep a small piece of the tropics alive.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it. The Missouri Botanical Garden is not only a pleasure ground. It is one of the leading centres of plant research in the world. Its scientists work in many tropical countries, with long-running programmes in places such as Madagascar and Latin America. The garden also keeps a very large herbarium. A herbarium is a library of dried and pressed plant specimens. Researchers around the world use its records to identify and protect plants.

Now walk toward the quieter part of the grounds, where the ground opens out around a lake. This is the Japanese Garden, called Seiwa-en. The name is often translated as the garden of pure, clear harmony and peace. It was designed by the landscape architect Koichi Kawana and opened in the late 1970s. It is one of the largest gardens of its kind in North America. Stone lanterns, carefully placed rocks, and raked gravel all follow traditional Japanese design ideas.

In a Japanese garden, every path is planned. Notice how the route bends so that you never see the whole lake at once. A new view appears around each turn. Look at the bridges, especially the curved one that arches high over the water. Below the surface, large orange and white koi move slowly. Koi are ornamental carp, and in Japanese culture they often stand for patience and strength. Children love to watch them gather near the edges.

There is more to explore beyond these two famous areas. The Linnean House is a long brick greenhouse from the late nineteenth century, built in Shaw's lifetime. It is named after Carl Linnaeus, the Swedish scientist who created the modern system of naming plants. There is also a Chinese Garden, a formal Victorian district with hedges and gravel walks, and a large home gardening centre. Each part shows a different idea of what a garden can be.

Shaw's generosity also reached outside these walls. He gave the city the land for Tower Grove Park, a large public park just to the south. He wanted ordinary people in Saint Louis to have green space, fresh air, and a place to walk. In his will, he left the garden to be run for the public good. That decision is the reason you can walk here today. A wealthy man's private hobby became a public trust.

Before you leave, find a bench and sit for a few minutes. Look at the trees around you. Some of them were planted long before anyone alive today was born. Others went into the ground only last season. A garden is never finished. It is planted, pruned, and replanted, year after year. As you walk back toward the gates, listen again for the gravel under your feet, the water, and the birds. This is the same sound Henry Shaw heard here, more than a century and a half ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early on summer days before the Climatron gets crowded.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks on gravel paths.
Wipe foggy glasses before photographing inside the Climatron.
Visit Seiwa-en in the morning for calm water reflections.
Bring water and sun protection for the open lake paths.
