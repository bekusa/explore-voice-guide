The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves over bare rock. Somewhere below, water runs through a stone channel. A mule's bell rings once, then again, further off. The air up here is thin and dry, and it smells of juniper and warm dust. Underfoot, loose scree shifts with every step... small grey stones that click against each other like coins. This is Toubkal National Park, high in the Atlas Mountains of Morocco, and you are standing on the roof of North Africa.

The park takes its name from Jbel Toubkal, the tallest mountain in North Africa. Its summit rises to about 4,167 metres above the sea. That is higher than any peak in the Alps outside the Mont Blanc and Swiss high ranges. Most visitors expect Morocco to mean desert and palm trees. Few expect snow. Yet for part of the year, these upper slopes are white, and the Sahara lies only a few days' travel to the south.

The park was created in 1942, which makes it the oldest national park in Morocco. Its borders reach across more than one province. Many travellers arrive from the north, through the valleys above Marrakech. But you are here on the southern flank, in Taroudant Province, where the mountains drop toward the Souss plain. This side feels quieter. The trails are emptier, the villages smaller, and the views open south toward a softer, drier land.

These mountains belong to the High Atlas, a long chain that crosses Morocco from the Atlantic coast toward Algeria. The range formed over many millions of years, as the African and European plates pressed slowly against each other. Look at the rock around you. You will see dark volcanic stone, reddish layers, and bands of grey. Each colour marks a different chapter in the making of this land. The peaks are sharp and broken, because frost splits the rock every winter.

The people of these valleys are Amazigh, often called Berbers in older books. Amazigh is the name they use for themselves, and it is usually translated as free people. They lived in the Atlas long before Arab armies arrived in the seventh century. Many families here still speak Tashelhit, one of the Amazigh languages. You may hear it in the villages — a soft, quick language, full of sounds that do not exist in English.

Now look at the valley floors. Small green fields climb the slopes in narrow terraces, held up by dry stone walls. There is no mortar in many of these walls. Farmers stack each stone by hand. Water reaches the fields through open channels that follow the contour of the hillside. Families share this water by custom, sometimes by the hour. It is an old and careful system, and it still feeds barley, vegetables, and orchards today.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The big trees near the villages are often walnut trees. Their wide branches give shade in summer, and their nuts are an important crop. In some valleys, apple and cherry trees grow higher than you might expect. Above the villages, the green ends suddenly. From there, only juniper, thorny cushion plants, and bare rock continue toward the peaks. The line between cultivated land and wild mountain is often just one stone wall.

The park is also home to wild animals, though most of them keep their distance. Barbary sheep, sometimes called aoudad, live on the steep cliffs. Their sandy coat blends almost perfectly with the rock. Look up, too. Large birds of prey ride the warm air above the ridges, and choughs — black crows with bright red or yellow beaks — call from the cliffs. In the evening, the bird sounds change, and the valleys fall quiet very fast.

The first recorded ascent of Toubkal by European climbers took place in 1923. Of course, local shepherds knew these slopes long before any map named them. Today, the summit route is popular, and most climbers take two days. They sleep at a mountain refuge below the peak, then start before dawn. At the top stands a metal frame shaped like a pyramid. It is often covered in small flags and scraps of cloth left by climbers.

The mountain asks for respect. The altitude is real, and many people feel it — a headache, short breath, a heavy tiredness in the legs. Weather can change within an hour. Even in summer, nights at the refuge are cold. In winter, the upper slopes need ice axes, crampons, and real mountain experience. The local mountain guides know these risks well. Many of them grew up in these villages, and they read the sky the way a farmer reads a field.

On the southern side of the massif, there is another surprise. Lac d'Ifni is a mountain lake that sits in a deep, bare basin of rock. Its water is dark blue-green, and the slopes around it are almost empty of plants. Few roads come near it. Walkers who reach it often say the silence there is the strongest thing they remember. It is a long walk, but it shows a side of the park that most summit climbers never see.

This landscape has also known hardship. In September 2023, a powerful earthquake struck the High Atlas. Many villages in and around these mountains suffered heavy damage, and many lives were lost. Rebuilding has taken time, and in some places it continues. As you walk through the valleys, you may see new walls beside old ones, or homes still under repair. Visitors who stay in local guesthouses and hire local guides help these communities directly.

So take a moment before you move on. Feel the cool air on your face, even under a strong sun. Look at the terraces below, at the water moving in its small stone channel. Listen for the mule bells, or a voice calling across the valley. Every path here was made by feet, not machines. As you walk away, notice how the colours of the rock change with the light — and how small the villages look against these old, broken peaks.

Before you go

Practical tips

Hire a licensed local mountain guide for summit routes.
Pack warm layers even on hot summer days.
Climb slowly and drink water to ease altitude effects.
Start summit walks before dawn to avoid afternoon weather.
Stay in village guesthouses to support local families.
