The guide

The story

Stand in the square for a moment and feel the stone under your feet. It is smooth and pale, and in the afternoon it holds the warmth of the sun. Above you rises a wall of carved figures, columns and curling stone, the colour of honey in strong light. Pigeons cross the square. Café cups clink behind you. This is the Cathedral of Saint Mary in Murcia, and its Baroque front is the first thing most people see.

The façade looks like one huge altarpiece turned inside out. Instead of hiding inside a chapel, the saints, angels and columns face the open square. It was built in the eighteenth century and is widely linked to the architect Jaime Bort. The front curves gently in and out, so the light moves across it all day. In the morning it looks flat and soft. By late afternoon the deep shadows make every statue stand out.

Here is the first surprise. This Baroque face is a late addition to a much older building. Behind it stands a Gothic cathedral, started in the late fourteenth century. And even that was not the beginning. Before the Christian conquest of Murcia in the thirteenth century, this was a Muslim city, and the cathedral was raised on the site of its main mosque. So under one square you have layers of Islamic, Gothic and Baroque Murcia.

Now look up at the bell tower. It rises high above the rooftops of the old town, and it is often described as one of the tallest church towers in Spain. Builders worked on it in stages over more than two centuries. That is why it changes style as it climbs. The lower levels are calm and Renaissance in feeling. The upper levels become lighter and more decorative, ending in a dome and a lantern at the top.

The tower holds a second surprise. Inside, people climb it by a series of ramps rather than a simple stone staircase. Locals like to say that the ramps were wide enough for a bishop to ride to the top on horseback. Treat that as a popular tale, not a documented event. Near the top corners, look for small structures called conjuratorios. From these, priests once said prayers against storms and hail that threatened the crops of the surrounding farmland.

Walk around the side of the building before you go in. On the side facing the narrow streets, you will find older doors with pointed Gothic arches. One of them, the Door of the Apostles, carries carved figures that have been softened by centuries of weather. The contrast is striking. On one side, smooth eighteenth century theatre. On the other, worn medieval stone that has watched the city change from a frontier town into a regional capital.

Step inside, and the temperature drops. The noise of the square fades almost at once. Your eyes need a moment to adjust. The interior is Gothic, with tall pointed arches and a long central nave. Light comes in through high windows and falls in pale columns onto the floor. Side chapels open off the aisles, and each one was paid for by a different family, bishop or guild over several centuries.

The most famous of these is the Chapel of the Vélez family, from the late Gothic period. Look closely at the outside wall of the chapel. The stone is carved into the shape of a thick chain, wrapping around the structure. The detail is so fine that the links almost look like real metal. Inside, the vaults form a star pattern overhead. This chapel shows how rich and powerful noble families wanted to be remembered, right inside the cathedral.

Not far away is another chapel, known as the Junterón Chapel. It is Renaissance in style, which means it looks back to the art of ancient Rome and Greece. Here the lines are calmer and more balanced than in the Vélez chapel. Walking from one to the other is like moving between two ways of seeing the world, side by side, in the same building. Many visitors pass them both without noticing the change.

Now for a detail most visitors miss completely. Near the main altar, the cathedral keeps the heart and inner organs of King Alfonso the Tenth, known as Alfonso the Wise. He was a thirteenth century king of Castile, famous for his work on law, astronomy and music. He asked that his heart rest in Murcia, a city he loved. Look for the urn near the high altar. It is a quiet object for such a remarkable story.

In the choir and around the altar, take time with the woodwork. Carved stalls, gilded surfaces and painted panels fill these spaces. Some areas of the cathedral also house a museum, with religious art, silverwork and sculpture. If you enjoy Baroque sculpture, look out for works linked to Francisco Salzillo, the eighteenth century Murcian sculptor whose figures are still carried through the streets during Holy Week. His influence is everywhere in this city.

When you step back outside, turn and face the opposite side of the square. You will see a modern building of plain stone and regular openings. It is an extension of the town hall, designed by the architect Rafael Moneo and completed in the 1990s. It was designed to face the cathedral without competing with it. It has no grand entrance and no tower. Its flat front works almost like a frame for the Baroque façade across the square.

Before you leave, find a spot at the edge of the square, perhaps at a café table. Watch how people use this space. Families cross it on their way home. Children chase pigeons. Old men sit on benches in the shade. The cathedral is not only a monument. It is still the background to daily life in Murcia, as it has been for centuries.

So take one last look. Follow the line of the tower up to the sky. Notice how the light has shifted on the façade since you arrived. Then walk into the narrow streets around you, and listen. You will hear the bells mark the hour behind you, the same sound that has carried over these rooftops for hundreds of years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the square in late afternoon for deep façade shadows.
Walk around the sides to see the older Gothic doors.
Check whether a separate ticket is needed for the tower.
Dress modestly; the cathedral is an active place of worship.
Sit at a square café to take in the full façade.
