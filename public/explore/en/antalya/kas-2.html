The guide

The story

Evening in Kaş smells of jasmine, grilled fish, and sea salt. Whitewashed houses with timber balconies line lanes barely wide enough for a scooter, and bougainvillea pours over the walls in waves of purple and crimson. Down at the harbour, mast lines tap in the breeze and swimmers dry off on the rocks. Across the water, close enough to count its houses, sits an island that belongs to another country. Kaş is full of such quiet surprises.

The town rises on the site of Antiphellos, an ancient Lycian harbour. Lycia — the seafaring region of this coast — organised itself around pairs of cities, and Antiphellos began as the port serving Phellos, a town up in the hills behind. In time the port outgrew its parent, as ports often do. Ships wanted timber, sponges, and passage, and all of that moved through here. The modern streets sit directly on the ancient plan, and the past keeps poking through the pavement.

The best example stands in the middle of a shopping street. At the top of Uzun Çarşı, the long market lane, a Lycian sarcophagus rises above the shopfronts on a tall stone base — a tomb more than two thousand years old, with a pointed lid shaped like an upturned boat. Carved lion heads guard the lid, so locals call it the Lion Tomb. Shoppers brush past it with ice cream. Cats sleep on its steps. Antiquity, here, is furniture.

A short walk west brings you to the ancient theatre, and it may be the most pleasant surprise in town. Its stone rows are largely intact, and it faces not a stage wall but the open sea. The Mediterranean is the backdrop. People drift up in the late afternoon with water bottles and sit on the warm stone as the sun drops toward the water. Concerts and events still use it. Twenty-some centuries on, the theatre remains in service.

Above the town, cut into the cliffs, you can find rock tombs left by the Lycians, including a monumental chamber tomb reached by a short, steep climb through the back streets. Go up in the cooler hours. The reward is double — carved doorways from a vanished culture, and a rooftop view of the whole bay, the peninsula, and that foreign island floating offshore.

The island is Kastellorizo, called Meis in Turkish, and it is Greece — the far eastern outpost of it, anchored just across a narrow strait. Ferries cross from Kaş harbour in well under an hour, and day-trippers go over with passports for lunch among pastel Greek houses. At night its lights hang on the horizon like a string of lanterns. Border and neighbour, all in one view.

Kaş itself once had a Greek voice. Under the name Andifli, the town was home to a Greek Orthodox community until 1923, when the population exchange between Greece and Turkey emptied it — the same treaty that emptied Kayaköy near Fethiye. Some families crossed only that narrow strait to Kastellorizo. The old houses they left behind, with their bay windows and shutters, are now the pensions, galleries, and bars that give Kaş its character.

Below the waterline, Kaş has a second reputation. Divers rank this stretch among the best scuba territory in Turkey — clear, deep water, walls, reefs, sea turtles, amphora fragments, and several wrecks, including aircraft and ships placed as artificial reefs. Dive shops cluster near the harbour and run boats out daily. Beginners can try a first dive in sheltered coves. Snorkellers get their share too, right off the rocky platforms at the town's edge.

The sea shapes gentler days as well. Sea kayak tours paddle the nearby coast, including trips toward the sunken ruins at Kekova. Small beaches dot the peninsula road, some pebbled, some platforms over rock, each with its own loyal defenders. In town, the day moves at cafe speed — long breakfasts under vines, an afternoon swim, then the slow evening promenade around the harbour as the fish restaurants light their lamps.

Market logic still rules the centre. Uzun Çarşı and the lanes around it hold workshops and small shops — sandal makers, silversmiths, spice sellers, soap stalls, galleries. Prices are friendlier a street or two back from the water. A weekly market draws villagers in from the hills with cheese, honey, herbs, and mountains of produce. Kaş has grown fashionable, but it still shops like a small town.

Even the name is small and human. Kaş means eyebrow in Turkish, and locals say the town earned it from the curve of land arching around the harbour — a thin peninsula raised over blue like a brow over an eye. Whether or not that is the true origin, it fits. This is a town on an intimate scale, built for strolling, permanently arched over the sea.

Walkers pass through town as well. The Lycian Way, the long-distance footpath running along this coast, threads through Kaş on its route between Fethiye and Antalya, and hikers with dusty boots share cafe tables with sailors and divers. Day walks lead out onto the peninsula or up toward the old hill villages. In spring the slopes around town turn green and briefly soft, and wild herbs scent the trails. Even without a full trek, an hour on the path buys you the whole coast from above.

End the day back at the harbour with something cold. Watch the ferry come in from Greece, the dive boats washing down their decks, the theatre crowd drifting home along the shore road. Twenty-five centuries of sailors have tied up in this exact curve of coast, and the evening routine — boats in, lamps lit, dinner served — has scarcely changed its shape. Sit still long enough and you become part of it.
