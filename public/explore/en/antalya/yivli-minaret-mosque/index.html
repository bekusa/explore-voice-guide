The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic hums somewhere above you, near the square. Down here, the sound softens. Pigeons scuffle on warm stone, and a breeze carries a faint smell of salt up from the old harbour. Now lift your eyes. A tall tower of reddish brick rises in front of you. Its surface is not smooth. It is ribbed, like a column of folded cloth, and the ribs catch the light differently as the sun moves.

This is the Yivli Minaret, and the mosque that sits at its foot. The Turkish word yivli means fluted or grooved. That one word describes what you are looking at. The minaret is built from a cluster of half-round shafts, pressed together around a central core. From a distance it reads as a single tower. Up close, it becomes a bundle of brick ribs that run upward and pull your gaze toward the sky.

To understand why it is here, go back to the early thirteenth century. At that time, the Seljuks ruled much of Anatolia. They were a Turkish dynasty that had moved west from Central Asia and Persia. Antalya was a valuable prize, a port on the Mediterranean that opened trade routes by sea. After the Seljuks took the city, they began to mark it as their own. Building a minaret was one of the clearest ways to do that.

The minaret is traditionally linked to Sultan Alaeddin Keykubad the First. He was one of the most powerful Seljuk rulers, and his name appears on buildings across Anatolia, from caravanserais to fortresses. Here, his builders chose brick over stone for the tower. That was a Persian and Central Asian habit, not a local Mediterranean one. So this tower carries a building tradition from far to the east, set down beside a sea the Seljuks had only recently reached.

Look closely at the brickwork now. Between the reddish bricks, you may notice small pieces of glazed tile in shades of turquoise and deep blue. Many have faded or fallen away over the centuries. But where they survive, they flash when the sun hits them. Imagine the tower when it was new, with those blue accents bright against the brick. It would have been a striking sight for sailors arriving in the harbour below.

Here is something many visitors miss. The minaret does not grow straight out of the ground as one shape. It stands on a square stone base. Above that, the form shifts to an octagon, and only then do the fluted shafts begin. This layering, from square to eight-sided to round, is a careful piece of geometry. It gives the tower a steady footing and a graceful rise at the same time.

Now turn to the mosque itself. The building you see is younger than the minaret. It is generally dated to the late fourteenth century, when the Hamidids controlled the region. They were one of several Turkish principalities that rose after Seljuk power faded. Their builders gave the prayer hall a roof of several small domes, carried on columns inside. From outside, the domes sit low and rounded, a calm contrast to the tall, ribbed tower beside them.

There is an older layer still. According to local accounts, an earlier religious building stood on this spot before the Seljuks arrived, possibly a Byzantine church. That would fit the pattern of Antalya, a city where Romans, Byzantines, Seljuks and Ottomans each built on top of what came before. Some reused columns inside the mosque may come from older structures. Treat the details as uncertain, but the idea of layers is true of the whole old town around you.

The minaret is only one part of a wider complex. Around it stand a former religious school, known as a medrese, and small tombs of local rulers and notable figures. There is also a former Mevlevi lodge. The Mevlevi are the Sufi order famous for the whirling ceremony, a form of prayer through turning movement. In recent years, the restored lodge has been used for art exhibitions, so its quiet rooms now hold paintings instead of dervishes.

If the mosque is open and you would like to step inside, remember that it is still a working place of prayer. Shoes come off at the door. Shoulders and knees should be covered, and women usually cover their hair. Inside, the light is soft, filtered through small windows. Carpets cover the floor, and the air feels cooler than the street. Move quietly, and avoid the times when people gather for prayer.

Step back again and notice how the minaret sits in the city. It stands just inside Kaleiçi, the old walled quarter of Antalya. Above you is the modern square and the main road. Below you, narrow lanes run down toward the harbour, lined with old Ottoman houses with wooden upper floors. The tower stands at the meeting point of these two worlds, the busy new city and the slow old one.

That position is one reason the minaret became the symbol of Antalya. You will see its fluted outline on signs, souvenirs and city logos. For many people here, it means home in the way a single shape can. It has watched the harbour for around eight centuries. Earthquakes, fires and changes of empire have come and gone, and the brick ribs are still here, repaired in places but standing.

Before you walk on, take one last look at the tower. Follow a single rib with your eyes, from the stone base to the balcony near the top. Notice where the brick is darker from repair, and where a fleck of blue tile still holds on. Then listen again to the pigeons, the breeze, the distant traffic. As you head down into the lanes of the old town, glance back once. The minaret will still be there, rising above the rooftops.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly with covered shoulders and knees to enter.
Avoid visiting the prayer hall during daily prayer times.
View the tower from the square above for full height.
Come in late afternoon when low sun lights the ribs.
Continue downhill into Kaleiçi lanes toward the old harbour.
