The guide

The story

Listen for the water first. A shallow stream runs down the middle of this valley and spreads across the pebbles before it reaches the sea. In summer it slows to a trickle you can step over. The stones on the beach are grey and rounded, and they shift and click under your feet. Oleander grows along the banks. Above you, the valley walls close in and the pine shade drops the temperature by several degrees. The sea here is cold and very clear.

Two places share this bay, and people mix up their names constantly. Olympos is the southern end, the narrow valley with the ruins and the small pensions built among trees. Çıralı is the northern end, a flatter strip of orange groves, guesthouses and quiet lanes. One long pebble beach joins them. You can walk from one to the other in a few minutes. They feel different, and locals will tell you they are different.

The ancient city of Olympos took its name from the mountain that rises behind the coast. It was a Lycian city — Lycia was the ancient region of this shoreline, made up of independent towns that governed themselves together. Olympos belonged to the Lycian League and had voting rights in it. Coins were struck here. The city sat on both banks of the stream, with the harbour where the water meets the beach. Ships came right into the valley mouth.

Its most notorious period involved pirates. In the second and first centuries before the common era, this coast was a base for raiding fleets. A pirate leader named Zeniketes controlled Olympos and nearby strongholds. Rome eventually moved against him. A Roman commander campaigned along the Lycian coast and broke the pirate hold. Olympos came under Roman control, and later grew again as a normal provincial town with baths, a theatre and public buildings.

What survives is unusual, because the forest took it back. Ruins here are not laid out on cleared ground. You find them by walking a dirt path and noticing a wall through the bay leaves and wild fig. A carved doorway stands almost complete on the south bank, with a smooth marble frame and an inscription across the lintel. Nearby, a bath building keeps parts of its floor. Vines grow over cut blocks that once belonged to a colonnade.

The city did not stop at the Roman period. A Christian basilica was built here, and you can still trace its apse and part of its brickwork. Later, in the Middle Ages, Italian merchants used the harbour and fortified the rocky ground on either side of it. Walls and a small tower survive above the north bank. Trade routes between Italy and the eastern Mediterranean passed along this coast, and a sheltered inlet like this one was worth holding.

Walk to where the stream meets the sea. In spring the channel can be knee-deep and you will need to wade. In late summer it often disappears into the shingle before reaching the water. Small fish gather in the pools. Ducks and terrapins live in the reeds upstream. The mix of fresh water, salt water and shade makes this one of the greener valleys on a coast that is mostly dry rock and pine.

Olympos became known among travellers for its treehouses. Wooden cabins were raised on posts among the trees, and cheap beds brought backpackers here from the 1980s onward. The site is protected, so permanent concrete building is restricted in the valley. That restriction is the reason there are no large hotels behind this beach. Most places are still small, family-run, and made of timber. The style has grown more comfortable over the years, but the scale has not changed much.

Çıralı developed differently. Families farmed citrus on the flat ground behind the beach, and many of the guesthouses grew out of those farms. You will pass orange and pomegranate trees between the lanes. In winter the fruit hangs heavy and the smell reaches the road. There is no promenade and no line of clubs. Most evenings the loudest sounds are the sea on the pebbles and the generators of a few beach restaurants.

This beach matters for another reason. Loggerhead turtles come ashore here at night in summer to lay eggs in the sand and gravel. The species is protected, and volunteers mark and cage the nests to keep them safe from animals and feet. Hatchlings find the sea by moving toward the brightest horizon, so artificial light confuses them. That is why beach lighting is limited, why umbrellas cannot be driven deep into the sand, and why you should keep torches pointed down at night.

Above the northern end of the valley, a rocky slope carries small permanent flames — the site known as Yanartaş, or the Chimaera. It is a steep walk uphill from Çıralı, best done in the evening. Many visitors combine the two in one day: ruins and swimming in the afternoon, then the flames after dark. Bring water and a torch for the descent, because the stone path is uneven and there is no lighting on it.

Before you leave the beach, turn and look inland. The valley narrows into shadow, and the mountain stands above it with its upper slopes bare. Somewhere in that undergrowth are the streets of a city that traded, minted coins and sheltered pirates. Nobody rebuilt over it. The pebbles are still moving under the water at your feet, exactly as they did for the sailors who steered in here. Sit down for a while before you go.
