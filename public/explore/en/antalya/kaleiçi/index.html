The guide

The story

Step off the wide modern avenue and the noise falls away. The streets narrow. Stone underfoot replaces pavement. Wooden houses lean toward each other overhead, almost touching, and the air smells of orange blossom and sea salt. This is Kaleiçi, the old town at the heart of Antalya, and the oldest part of the whole city.

The name means inside the castle. For most of its history this district sat behind defensive walls, a tight knot of houses, mosques, and churches set above a small harbor. Parts of those old walls still stand among the buildings. The streets inside keep their early shape, winding and uneven, never quite straight for long.

Many people enter through Hadrian's Gate. This marble archway was built for the Roman emperor Hadrian, who visited the city in the year 130. Three arches rise on tall columns, finely carved. Look down as you pass beneath them: deep grooves worn into the stone floor were cut by cart wheels over many centuries of steady use.

Beyond the gate the houses begin. Most date from the Ottoman period, built of stone on the lower floor and timber on the floors above. The upper rooms often jut out over the street on wooden brackets, shading the lane below. Many have been restored and now serve as small hotels, but their old bones are easy to read.

The streets lead gently downhill toward the old harbor. For centuries this was the working heart of Antalya, the place where trading ships loaded and unloaded their cargo. The harbor is small, held inside a tight curve of stone. Today it shelters wooden tour boats rather than freight, but its shape has hardly changed at all.

Above the rooftops you will see the Yivli Minaret, the fluted tower that has become the symbol of the city. Its red-brick shaft is grooved into deep vertical channels, and it was raised in the thirteenth century, in the Seljuk period. It stands beside one of the oldest working mosques in all of Antalya.

The layers of faith here run deep. Near the center stands the Kesik Minare, the broken minaret. The building beneath it began as a Roman temple, became a Byzantine church, and was later turned into a mosque. Fire damaged it long ago. In a single structure you can read the changing hands the city has passed through over the centuries.

Walk slowly and let yourself get lost. Kaleiçi is small enough that you cannot go far wrong, and the real pleasure of it lies in the wandering. Each turn brings a hidden courtyard, a climbing vine heavy with flowers, a cat asleep on warm stone, or a sudden glimpse of blue sea at the far end of a lane.

Near the southern edge stands the Hıdırlık Tower, a round stone tower set above the cliffs. Its first purpose is uncertain; it may have served the city defenses, or stood as a tomb for someone of importance. From the small park beside it you look out over the open sea and along the long curving line of the coast.

The district is full of small trades. Carpet sellers, jewelers, and makers of soap and spices keep their shops in the old houses. Tea is offered freely, and there is no need to buy anything in return. Sitting with a glass of tea and watching the lane pass by is part of how the place is meant to be used. Many of the makers are glad to show how their goods are made if you show real interest.

Listen as you walk. The call to prayer carries out over the rooftops several times each day. Wheels of luggage clatter on the worn stones. Somewhere nearby a fountain runs steadily. In the evening the harbor restaurants fill, and the sound of talk and clinking glasses rises gently from the water's edge below.

Much of what you see has been rebuilt and repaired. Kaleiçi was a poor and crowded quarter for much of the twentieth century, and careful restoration over recent decades has brought it back to life. The result is part open museum, part living neighborhood, where people still live behind the painted wooden shutters.

Stand for a moment at the harbor wall as the light begins to fade. The moored boats rock gently against their lines. The old houses climb the slope behind you, window above window. Above them the fluted minaret catches the last of the sun. Layer upon layer of the city's long past sits here in a few hundred meters, quiet and close enough to touch.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear flat, sturdy shoes for the uneven stone streets.
Accept offered tea while browsing without pressure to buy.
Visit the harbor at sunset for the best light.
Let yourself wander; the district is small and safe.
