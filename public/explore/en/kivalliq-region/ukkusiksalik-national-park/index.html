The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves across bare rock and low tundra, and almost nothing interrupts it. There is no traffic here. There are no engines, no voices from another tour group, and no hum of power lines. Underfoot, the ground is a mix of lichen, moss and loose stone. It gives slightly with each step. The light in this part of the Arctic is long and low for much of the year, and it turns the hills a soft grey-gold. You are standing inside one of the most remote national parks in Canada.

Ukkusiksalik National Park sits in the Kivalliq Region of Nunavut, on the northwestern side of Hudson Bay. Its centre is Wager Bay, a long saltwater inlet that reaches deep inland from Hudson Bay. The park wraps around this inlet, and it covers more than twenty thousand square kilometres of land. That is a large area, but very few people ever see it. In most years, only a small number of visitors arrive. Many of them are researchers, Inuit families with ties to the land, or travellers on carefully planned expeditions.

The name comes from Inuktitut, the language of the Inuit. Ukkusiksalik is usually translated as the place where there is stone to carve pots and oil lamps. The stone is soapstone. It is soft enough to shape with simple tools, and it holds heat well. For generations, Inuit used soapstone to make the qulliq, the shallow oil lamp that gave light and warmth inside snow houses and tents. So the park's name does not describe a view. It describes a resource, and the people who knew where to find it.

That human connection runs through the whole park. Archaeologists have recorded more than five hundred sites here. They include tent rings, which are circles of stones that once held down the edges of skin tents. There are stone caches for storing meat, fox traps built from piled rock, and the remains of older winter houses. Some sites belong to the Thule people, the ancestors of today's Inuit. Others are much more recent. Together, they show that this land was never empty. It was a home, a hunting ground and a route between seasons.

Here is something many visitors miss. A tent ring can look like a random scatter of rocks at first. Look again, and you may see the shape — a rough circle or oval, with larger stones on the side that faced the wind. Once your eyes learn the pattern, you start to notice these rings in many places. They are fragile records. Parks Canada asks visitors to leave every stone exactly where it lies. Moving even one rock can erase information that has survived for centuries.

The English name of the bay has a different story. In the eighteenth century, British expeditions sailed into Hudson Bay looking for the Northwest Passage, a sea route to Asia across the top of North America. One of these expeditions named the inlet after Sir Charles Wager, a senior officer of the Royal Navy. The sailors hoped the inlet might lead west to open ocean. It did not. It ends in the land, surrounded by hills. But the name stayed on European maps.

In the twentieth century, the outside world arrived in a more lasting way. The Hudson's Bay Company, the old fur-trading company, operated a trading post on Wager Bay for a period in the first half of the century. Inuit families traded fox furs there for goods such as tea, flour, ammunition and cloth. The post later closed, and the families moved toward larger settlements. Today, the remains of the post still stand in the park. They are a quiet reminder of a short but important chapter.

Now, a second surprise. Wager Bay has some of the strongest tides in this region. Where the water squeezes through narrow passages, the tide creates fast, turbulent currents. At one place, known as the Reversing Falls, the water rushes in one direction as the tide rises, and then turns and rushes the other way as it falls. The flow can be powerful and dangerous for boats. For visitors on shore, it is a rare chance to watch the ocean change direction in real time.

The animals here live on the edge of what is possible. Polar bears travel along the coast, especially when the sea ice is absent. Barren-ground caribou cross the tundra. Arctic wolves, Arctic foxes and Arctic hares live on the land. In the water, there are ringed seals and Arctic char, a cold-water fish related to salmon. Birds such as peregrine falcons nest on the cliffs in summer. Every species here must handle long winters, short summers and very little shelter.

Polar bears change how everyone moves in this park. They are curious, strong and fast, and the open landscape gives them a clear view of people. For this reason, Parks Canada requires visitors to register and to complete an orientation before any trip. Many groups travel with experienced local guides or bear monitors. Camps are planned carefully, and food is stored with great care. Here, safety is not an extra step. It is part of the journey from the very first hour.

The land itself is old and hard. Much of the park sits on ancient bedrock, smoothed and scraped by glaciers during the last ice age. When the ice melted, the land slowly rose. Along some slopes, you can see raised beaches — lines of rounded stones that were once the edge of the sea and now sit well above it. There are no tall trees here. Plants stay low to the ground, out of the wind. In summer, small flowers appear between the rocks in white, yellow and purple.

The park also has a living meaning for Inuit today. It was created through an agreement with Inuit, under the land claim that also led to the creation of Nunavut. Inuit can continue to hunt, fish and travel here, and families from nearby communities such as Naujaat, Baker Lake and Rankin Inlet keep strong ties to the area. For them, this is not wilderness in the empty sense of the word. It is a place with names, stories and memories attached to many hills and shorelines.

So take a moment where you stand. Look at the stones near your feet, and ask whether a hand once placed them there. Watch the water in the bay, and notice which way it is moving right now. Listen to the wind again — it is the same wind that hunters, traders and explorers felt here long before you. When you walk on, walk lightly. Leave the land as you found it, so the next visitor can read it too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Register and complete the Parks Canada orientation before arriving.
Travel with an experienced local guide or bear monitor.
Plan charter flights or boat transport well in advance.
Pack layers; Arctic weather changes quickly even in summer.
Carry everything in and out; there are no services.
