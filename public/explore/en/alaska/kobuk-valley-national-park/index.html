The guide

The story

Listen first. Sand is moving under your boots and hissing softly in the wind. Up here, north of the Arctic Circle, you might expect snow, ice, or tundra moss. Instead, a long golden ridge of sand runs off toward the horizon. Beyond it, a dark line of spruce marks the edge of the forest. The air is dry and clean, and the light comes in low and sideways, even in the middle of the day.

This is Kobuk Valley National Park, in the far northwest of Alaska. It lies a short distance above the Arctic Circle, in a wide basin between two mountain ranges. The Baird Mountains rise to the north, and the Waring Mountains rise to the south. Through the middle runs the Kobuk River, slow and broad. The river's name is often translated from the Inupiaq language as "big river". The Inupiaq are the Indigenous people of this region.

Here is the first surprise. There are no roads into this park. None at all. You cannot drive here, and there is no entrance gate or paved trail. Most visitors arrive in a small bush plane, usually from the town of Kotzebue on the coast, or from the village of Bettles to the east. Others come by boat along the river. Because of this, Kobuk Valley is one of the least visited national parks in the United States. On many days, you may be the only person standing here.

The area became a national monument in 1978. Two years later, in 1980, it became a national park under a major federal law that protected large areas of wild land across Alaska. The park was created to protect three things that meet in this valley: the sand dunes, the river corridor, and the migration of a great caribou herd. Each one is unusual on its own. Together, they make this valley unlike any other place in the American Arctic.

Let's start with the sand under your feet. The Great Kobuk Sand Dunes are the largest active dunes in the Arctic of North America. There are two smaller dune fields nearby as well, the Little Kobuk Sand Dunes and the Hunt River Dunes. Some of the great dunes rise around thirty metres high. On a warm summer afternoon, the surface of the sand can feel surprisingly hot to the touch. It is a strange feeling to stand in desert heat while you are in the Arctic.

So where did all this sand come from? During the last Ice Age, glaciers moved across the mountains around this basin. As the ice ground over rock, it crushed the stone into fine grains. When the glaciers melted, rivers and streams carried this material down into the valley. Then the wind went to work. Over thousands of years, steady winds piled the grains into dunes. Most of that sand is now held in place by plants, but the great dunes are still moving.

Now look at the edges of the dune field. Here, sand and forest are in a slow contest. In some places, the dunes are moving into the trees and slowly burying them. In other places, grasses, low shrubs, and small flowers are taking hold on the sand and holding it still. If you walk toward the treeline, you can see this meeting point clearly. Some spruce trees stand half buried, with only their upper branches above the sand.

The second treasure of this valley is alive, and it walks. Every year, the Western Arctic caribou herd migrates across this part of Alaska. It is one of the largest caribou herds on the continent. In late summer and autumn, long lines of animals move south and cross the Kobuk River. In spring, they move north again toward their calving grounds. If you are lucky enough to see a crossing, you will hear it before you understand it — the splash of hooves, and a low clicking sound. That click comes from the caribou's ankles as they walk.

And this is where the third story begins. The caribou have crossed the river at certain places for a very long time. People learned these places, and they waited there to hunt. One of the most important sites is called Onion Portage, a bend in the Kobuk River. The name comes from the wild onions that grow there. In the 1960s, the archaeologist J. Louis Giddings and later researchers excavated this site. They found layer after layer of human camps, one on top of another.

Those layers carry one of the longest records of human life in the Arctic. The oldest traces at Onion Portage go back many thousands of years. Different cultures came and went, but they returned to the same river bend for the same reason — the caribou. Onion Portage is now a National Historic Landmark. For archaeologists, it works almost like a calendar written in soil. For the Inupiaq communities of the region, it is part of a living tradition, not only a story from the past.

That tradition continues today. Local Inupiaq people still hunt, fish, and gather berries in and around the park. This activity is called subsistence, which means living from the land for food and daily needs. The law that created the park protects this right. So if you see a camp, a fish rack, or a boat on the river, please give it space and respect. The valley is wild, but it has never been empty of people. It is a homeland, not only a landscape.

It also helps to remember that this valley played a part in a much larger story. During the Ice Age, a land bridge connected Asia and North America. Parts of this region stayed free of thick ice, even when glaciers covered much of the continent. Plants and animals survived here through the cold. Some plants that grow on the dunes today are rare, and a few are found almost nowhere else. Look closely at the small flowers on the sand. They are survivors of a much colder world.

Before you leave, take a moment to stand still on the crest of a dune. Turn slowly in a circle. On one side, you see open sand with rippled patterns from the wind. On the other side, you see the river, the dark spruce, and the far blue mountains. There are no buildings, no power lines, and no roads. The only sounds are wind, water, and perhaps a raven calling. This is what wild land sounds like.

As you walk back toward your plane or your boat, look down at your footprints. By tomorrow, the wind will probably erase them. The dunes will shift a little, and the caribou will keep moving along their old paths. The river will keep running west toward the sea. You were here for only a short time, but this valley has seen thousands of years of travellers like you. Take that quiet with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange a bush plane flight from Kotzebue or Bettles.
Bring sun protection; dune sand reflects strong Arctic light.
Pack insect repellent for summer mosquitoes near the river.
Give local subsistence camps and fish racks plenty of space.
Visit in late summer for a chance at caribou crossings.
