The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the silence has weight. A breeze moves through low willow and dwarf birch, and somewhere a ground squirrel gives a sharp whistle. The air smells of wet moss, cold rock and spruce. The ground under your boots is soft and springy. Beneath that thin carpet of tundra, much of the earth stays frozen all year. Welcome to Denali, a place where the land feels far larger than any human scale.

The park and preserve together cover more than six million acres. That is larger than some entire countries. Yet only one road leads into it. Most of this ground has no trails, no signposts and no buildings. Visitors walk across open tundra and choose their own route. This is not an oversight. It is the plan. Denali was managed from the beginning as a wild place first and a destination second.

At the centre of it all stands the mountain. Its summit rises a little over 20,300 feet, about 6,190 metres, above sea level. That makes it the highest point in North America. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Measured from base to summit, the vertical rise is greater than that of Mount Everest. Everest starts from the high Tibetan Plateau. This mountain rises from lowlands only a few thousand feet above the sea. So the wall of rock and ice in front of you is taller from its foot than almost anything else on Earth.

The name Denali comes from the Koyukon Athabascan language. Koyukon people are one of the Indigenous groups of interior Alaska. The word is usually translated as "the high one" or "the tall one." For many years, official maps used the name Mount McKinley, after an American president. The federal government has changed the mountain's official name more than once in recent years. But the park itself carries the name Denali, and so do the voices of most people you will meet here.

Now, a gentle warning. You may not see the summit at all. The mountain is so large that it creates its own weather. Moist air rises against its slopes and turns into cloud. Rangers often say that only a minority of summer visitors see the full peak. If the clouds clear for you, take a long look. If they do not, you are in good company... and the land below still has much to show.

The story of the park begins with a man named Charles Sheldon. He was a hunter and naturalist who spent time here in the early twentieth century. He watched market hunters kill Dall sheep and caribou to feed gold miners. He worried that the wildlife would vanish. Sheldon campaigned in Washington for protection. In 1917, Congress created Mount McKinley National Park. Its first purpose was to protect animals, not scenery. That idea still shapes the park today.

In 1980, a major Alaska lands law expanded the park to roughly three times its original size. The law also renamed it Denali National Park and Preserve. The word "preserve" matters. In the preserve areas, rural residents can continue traditional hunting and gathering. So this is not an empty museum of nature. People have lived with this land for thousands of years, and some still depend on it.

Here is a second surprise. The first people to stand on the true summit reached it in 1913. The group was led by Hudson Stuck, an Episcopal archdeacon, and Harry Karstens. The first person to step onto the very top was Walter Harper, a young Alaska Native man. Years later, Karstens became the first superintendent of the new park. So one of the first climbers also became one of its first guardians.

The park road is the thread that ties a visit together. It runs west from the entrance area for about ninety miles, or close to 150 kilometres. Private cars can only drive the first section, up to the Savage River area. Beyond that point, most people ride buses. This rule keeps traffic low and gives animals space. In recent years, a landslide at a place called Pretty Rocks has closed part of the road. Ask at the visitor centre how far buses currently travel.

From a bus window, watch the land change. Near the entrance, you pass through taiga, a northern forest of thin black spruce and white spruce. As the road climbs, the trees grow shorter and then disappear. Open tundra takes over. In late summer, the tundra turns red, orange and gold. Braided rivers spread across wide gravel beds. Their grey colour comes from rock ground into fine powder by glaciers higher up.

This is also a place to watch for animals. Many visitors hope to see grizzly bears, moose, caribou, wolves and Dall sheep. Dall sheep look like small white dots high on green slopes. Caribou often travel alone or in small groups across the open ground. Bus drivers usually stop when someone spots wildlife. Keep your voice low and stay inside. Remember that every animal here is wild, and distance protects both you and them.

One more detail sets this park apart. Near the entrance, there is a kennel of working sled dogs. In winter, rangers use dog teams to patrol the wilderness. Motorised vehicles are not allowed in much of the backcountry, so the dogs do real work. In summer, visitors can often meet the dogs and watch a demonstration. It is a living link to the way people first travelled through this country.

In summer, the light changes how time feels. Near midsummer, the sun stays up for most of the night. Evenings stretch on in a soft, low glow. In winter, the opposite happens. Days grow very short, temperatures fall far below freezing, and the land goes quiet under snow. The park you see today is only one season of a much longer cycle.

Before you move on, pause and look around once more. Notice the colours of the tundra at your feet, the grey braids of the river, and the line where the trees give up. Feel the cool air on your face. Whether the mountain shows itself or hides in cloud, it is still there, rising above all of this. As you walk away, listen again for that silence. It is one of the things this park was made to protect.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a park bus in advance for the interior road.
Ask rangers how far the road is open today.
Bring layers and rain gear, even in midsummer.
Carry binoculars to spot Dall sheep on high slopes.
Visit the sled dog kennels near the park entrance.
