The guide

The story

Take off your shoes for a moment, if you can. The sand here is fine and pale, almost the colour of milky coffee. In the morning it is cool under your feet. By midday it is hot enough to make you hop. Listen, too. The wind from the sea never really stops, and you can hear grains of sand hiss as they slide across each other along the ridges.

You are standing in Médanos de Coro National Park, on the northern coast of Venezuela, in the state of Falcón. The word médanos means dunes in the Spanish of this region. This is one of the few true dune fields in the Caribbean world. It sits just at the edge of the city of Coro, so close that houses and streets run almost to the sand. Few deserts in the world begin this near to a colonial town.

Look at the shape of the land around you. The park covers a narrow strip of ground, an isthmus, which links the mainland to the Paraguaná Peninsula to the north. An isthmus is simply a thin neck of land with water on both sides. On a clear day you can sense the sea on either hand, even when the dunes hide it from view. The whole landscape is a bridge made of sand.

So where does all this sand come from? The answer is the wind. Steady trade winds blow from the northeast, off the Caribbean, for most of the year. They carry sand from the beaches and push it inland across the isthmus. Grain by grain, the wind builds ridges, then shapes them into curves. The dunes are not fixed. They move, slowly, every single day.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look closely at any dune and you will see two different faces. The side facing the wind is long and gentle, and the sand there feels firm. The far side is short and steep, and the sand is loose. That steep face is where the wind drops its load. Grains roll over the crest and tumble down. Over months, this process walks the whole dune forward, like a slow wave on land.

Now look down at the surface. The wind draws thin ripples across the sand, often only a few centimetres apart. They look like the lines on a fingerprint, or the pattern the sea leaves on a beach at low tide. Walk a little way, then turn around. Your own footprints may already be softening at the edges. By tomorrow, the wind will have erased them completely. Very little here stays the same for long.

The area was declared a national park in the 1970s. The aim was to protect this unusual landscape, and the fragile life that has adapted to it. That life is easy to overlook at first. But at the edges of the dunes, where the sand meets harder ground, you will find plants that have learned to live with almost no rain. Tall columnar cacti rise like green pillars. Low, twisted thorn trees bend away from the wind.

Those trees are worth a second look. Many of them grow at a strong angle, their branches all pointing in the same direction. They are not damaged. They are simply shaped by years of wind blowing from one side. In a way, each tree is a compass. If you ever lose your sense of direction out here, the trees can tell you where the sea breeze comes from.

Animals live here too, though most of them are shy in daylight. Lizards dart between patches of shade. Small birds work the thorny scrub for insects and seeds. Early in the morning, before the wind smooths everything, you may notice tiny tracks across the sand. They are the night's record of who passed by. Some visitors find these tracks more exciting than the dunes themselves.

The desert has also shaped the city beside it. Coro is one of the oldest cities on the South American mainland, founded by the Spanish in the sixteenth century. Its historic centre, together with its old port of La Vela, is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. Many of its colonial houses are built of earth, a mix of clay and straw. Dry air from this coast has helped those earthen walls survive for centuries. The sand and the old town share the same climate.

That closeness has a practical side too. The road that crosses the isthmus to the Paraguaná Peninsula runs beside the dunes. On windy days, sand drifts across the asphalt, and crews must clear it away. It is a quiet, constant contest between people and the landscape. Watch the edge of the road as you arrive or leave. You may see thin tongues of sand reaching across the pavement.

For many local families, the dunes are not a remote wilderness but a place to play. On weekends and holidays, children climb the ridges and slide down the steep faces. Some people bring boards and try sandboarding, which is like snowboarding on sand. Others simply sit on a crest at sunset and talk. You may hear music from a car parked nearby, or the laughter of kids racing to the top. This is a shared, everyday place.

If you climb to the top of a dune, take it slowly. The loose sand gives way under each step, so the climb is harder than it looks. At the crest, pause and turn in a full circle. On one side, you may see the low line of the city. On the other side, only ridges of sand, folding into the distance. The light changes fast here. In late afternoon, the sand turns gold, then orange, and the shadows grow long and blue.

Before you go, stand still for one more minute. Feel the wind on your face, and the sand against your ankles. Everything you see around you is moving, even when it seems perfectly still. The dune under your feet will be a little further inland next year. As you walk back toward the road, look over your shoulder once. The shape you climbed today is already beginning to change.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Bring plenty of water, a hat, and strong sunscreen.
Climb on the gentle windward side, not the steep face.
Protect your camera and phone from blowing sand.
Combine your visit with a walk through Coro's colonial centre.
