The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the steps for a moment. The stone under your shoes is warm from the Sicilian sun. Scooters rasp along the road behind you, a waiter calls across the piazza, and pigeons settle on the balustrade. Above you a wide staircase climbs toward a row of tall columns, and above the columns a great bronze dome sits against the sky. This is the Teatro Massimo, the opera house of Palermo, and it is very large.

The full name is Teatro Massimo Vittorio Emanuele, after the first king of a united Italy. The word massimo simply means the greatest, and the name is not boasting. It is usually described as the largest opera house in Italy and one of the largest in Europe. The building fills its whole block on Piazza Verdi, at the end of Via Maqueda, one of the old main streets of the city.

To understand why Palermo wanted something so big, think about the decades after Italian unification in the eighteen-sixties. Palermo was an ancient capital, once a seat of Arab emirs and Norman kings. Now it was one city among many in a new nation. A grand modern theatre was a statement. It said that Palermo still mattered, that it was cultured, ambitious, and part of Europe. The city held a design competition, and an architect from Palermo won.

His name was Giovan Battista Filippo Basile. Building work began in 1875, but progress was slow. There were money troubles, political disputes, and long pauses when nothing moved at all. Basile died in 1891 with the theatre still unfinished. His son, Ernesto Basile, took over and completed the work. Ernesto later became one of the leading architects of the Italian Art Nouveau style, so the theatre connects two generations and two very different tastes.

The opera house finally opened in May 1897, more than twenty years after the first stone. The opening opera was Falstaff by Giuseppe Verdi, then the most celebrated Italian composer alive. Here is a detail many visitors miss. To clear this ground, the city demolished older buildings, including churches and a convent. That act left a mark on local memory, and it gave birth to one of the theatre's best-loved stories.

Locals say the ghost of a nun still walks the building. According to the popular tale, she was buried in the convent that once stood here, and she never accepted its destruction. Some people speak of a particular step on the staircase where unbelievers stumble. It is folklore, not history, but you may hear Palermitans mention it with a half-smile. Notice how carefully people place their feet on the way up.

Now look at the facade itself. Basile chose a neoclassical style, which means he borrowed the language of ancient Greek and Roman temples. The columns at the top of the stairs are Corinthian, with capitals carved like bundles of acanthus leaves. This choice made sense in Sicily. The island holds some of the finest Greek temples anywhere, at sites such as Agrigento and Segesta. The theatre was meant to feel like a temple, but a temple dedicated to music.

Read the long inscription across the front, above the columns. It is in Italian, and it carries the purpose of the whole building. In English it says, roughly, that art renews peoples and reveals their life, and that the pleasure of the stage is empty if it does not aim to prepare the future. It is a serious message for a place of entertainment. The builders believed opera could shape a better society, not just fill an evening.

On either side of the staircase sit two bronze lions, each carrying a seated female figure. These are allegories, figures that stand for ideas. One represents Tragedy and the other represents Lyric Poetry, the two great traditions of the musical stage. Children love to point at the lions, and photographers often frame the staircase between them. Stand back toward the piazza to see how the lions, the columns, and the dome stack into one picture.

The theatre has another life on film. The final scenes of The Godfather Part Three, directed by Francis Ford Coppola, were shot here. In the film, a performance takes place inside the opera house, and the story ends tragically on these very steps. Many visitors climb the staircase because of that film. It is a strange layer to add to a building about art and renewal, but Palermo has learned to live with it.

The twentieth century was not always kind to the theatre. In 1974 it closed for restoration and safety work, and the doors stayed shut for more than twenty years. A whole generation of Palermitans grew up without opera in their own grand house. The reopening came in 1997, close to the hundredth anniversary of the first performance. For many people in the city, that return felt like a symbol of Palermo recovering its civic life.

If you go inside on a guided tour, you will find a horseshoe-shaped auditorium lined with tiers of boxes in red and gold. Guides often show the royal box and explain how the ceiling was designed with movable sections, so the hall could breathe in the Sicilian heat. Many tours also visit a small round room with curious acoustics, where a voice behaves unexpectedly. Listen carefully if you get the chance, because the effect is easy to miss.

When you come back out, pause at the top of the stairs before you go down. Look across Piazza Verdi toward the old city, with its domes, balconies, and laundry lines. The noise of traffic rises again, and the light changes on the pale stone. This building took more than two decades of effort to finish and more than two decades of silence to restore. As you walk away, turn once and look back at the dome above the trees.
