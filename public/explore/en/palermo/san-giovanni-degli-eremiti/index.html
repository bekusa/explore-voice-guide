The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Palermo is only a few streets away, yet here the air is softer. Palm fronds rustle overhead. Somewhere a bird calls from the citrus trees. Underfoot, the path is warm and dusty, and above the garden wall five rounded domes rise against the sky, the colour of baked terracotta. This is San Giovanni degli Eremiti, the Church of Saint John of the Hermits. It is one of the strangest and most beautiful buildings in Sicily.

Look up at those domes first. They do not look like the domes of a typical European church. They look like something from North Africa or the Middle East. Low, smooth and plain, they sit on the building like upturned bowls. For many first-time visitors, this is the moment of confusion. Is this a church? Was it a mosque? The honest answer is that the building belongs to a very particular moment, when Sicily stood between several worlds at once.

To understand that moment, we need to go back to the twelfth century. Sicily had been under Arab rule for more than two centuries. Then Norman knights, originally from northern France, conquered the island. Under King Roger the Second, Palermo became the capital of a rich and unusual kingdom. Roger did not erase what came before. Instead, his court employed Arab craftsmen, Greek scholars and Latin clergy side by side. The result was a mixed style that historians now call Arab-Norman.

This church was built during Roger's reign, around the middle of the twelfth century. It was linked to a Benedictine monastery, a community of monks who followed the rule of Saint Benedict. Roger gave the monastery royal favour, and it held an important place in the life of his court. Sources also say that the site had older religious buildings beneath it. Some accounts describe an earlier monastery, and later a mosque, on this ground. The details are debated, so treat that layered story with some caution.

Now walk closer and look at the walls. They are built from blocks of local stone, cut into neat, square shapes. There is almost no decoration on the outside. No statues. No carved saints. Just clean geometry and small, high windows. This plainness is part of the Islamic tradition of design, where beauty often comes from proportion rather than ornament. The builders here understood that a simple cube, crowned by a dome, can feel calm and complete.

Here is something many visitors miss. The bright red colour of the domes was not necessarily what twelfth-century Palermo saw. In the late nineteenth century, restorers worked on the church. They removed later additions and tried to return the building to its medieval form. The striking red finish dates from that period of restoration. Scholars still discuss what the domes originally looked like. So when you photograph them, you are capturing both a medieval design and a nineteenth-century interpretation of it.

Turn now toward the bell tower. It is slim and square, with arched openings near the top. And at its very summit sits another small red dome, like a cap. A Christian bell tower topped with an Islamic-style dome — that single detail sums up the whole building. Two traditions, one structure, no sense of conflict between them. Stand back far enough to see the tower and the domes together. The composition is quietly perfect.

Step inside the church itself, if it is open to visitors. Prepare for a surprise. The interior is almost completely bare. The walls are plain stone. The floor is simple. Light falls from the small windows and gathers under the domes. Many churches in Palermo are covered in gold mosaics or rich Baroque marble. This one is empty and silent. Some visitors feel disappointed. Others find the emptiness moving. You can sense the shape of the space without any distraction.

That contrast is worth thinking about. Only a short walk from here stands the Norman Palace, with its famous Palatine Chapel, glowing with gold mosaics from the same century. Both buildings come from the same royal world. One dazzles you. The other asks you to be still. Seeing them on the same day helps you understand how wide the range of Norman Palermo really was.

Now return outside and find the cloister. This is a small square garden surrounded by a covered walkway. Its arches rest on pairs of slender columns, standing close together like companions. The cloister is generally dated to a slightly later period than the church, toward the end of the twelfth century or the early thirteenth. Monks once walked here in quiet prayer. Today it is one of the most peaceful corners in the city.

The garden around the cloister and the church is a pleasure in its own right. Look for palms, citrus trees, and climbing plants against the old stone. In spring and early summer, flowers soften the walls with colour. The sound of the city fades. Lizards sometimes rest on the warm stones. This garden is not medieval in its exact planting, but it gives you a sense of what the monks valued — shade, order, and a place to think.

In 2015, this church became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing. The listing covers a group of Arab-Norman monuments in Palermo, together with the cathedrals of Cefalù and Monreale. UNESCO recognised these buildings as evidence of a rare coexistence between Western, Islamic and Byzantine cultures. Byzantine here means the Greek-speaking Christian world of the eastern Mediterranean. San Giovanni degli Eremiti is one of the smallest members of that group, but it is also one of the most memorable.

Before you leave, find a shady spot and look once more at the domes. Notice how the light changes their colour. In bright noon sun they look almost orange. Later in the day they turn deeper, closer to rust. Listen again to the palms, the birds, the distant hum of the city. Nearly nine hundred years have passed since this place was built. As you walk back out through the gate, you carry with you a small piece of that quiet.
