The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the air. It is cool, dry, and faintly dusty, with a smell that is hard to name — old fabric, old wood, old stone. Your footsteps sound soft on the floor. Then your eyes adjust to the light, and you see them. Rows and rows of people, standing in niches along the walls, dressed in their best clothes. They have been waiting here for a very long time.

Welcome to the Catacombe dei Cappuccini, the Capuchin Catacombs of Palermo. You are underneath a monastery of the Capuchin friars, a branch of the Franciscan order known for simple brown habits and a life of poverty. Above you, the friars still pray and work. Below, in these long corridors, rest the remains of thousands of people from Palermo's past. Before we walk further, take a breath. This is a burial place first, and a visitor attraction second.

The story begins in the late sixteenth century. The friars had outgrown their original cemetery, so they began to dig new burial chambers beneath the church. According to the traditional account, when they moved the bodies of earlier friars, they found something unexpected. Many of the bodies had not decayed in the usual way. They had dried out naturally, keeping their skin and features. To the friars, this felt like a sign.

The first friar to be placed here on display is usually named as Brother Silvestro of Gubbio, at the very end of the sixteenth century. After him came other friars, laid out in the corridors so their brothers could visit and remember them. At first this was a private practice. It was a way for a religious community to stay close to its dead, and to keep in mind that every life on Earth ends.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The preservation was not only luck. The friars developed a careful process. Bodies were placed in special drying rooms, laid on racks of terracotta pipes, and left for many months while the fluids drained away. Afterwards, the bodies were washed, often with vinegar, then dressed and placed in the corridors. Some later bodies were treated with chemical solutions instead. If you look closely, you can see the difference in how faces have aged.

Over time, being buried here became a mark of status for people outside the monastery. Wealthy families of Palermo asked for their relatives to be preserved and displayed. Doctors, lawyers, officers, and noble women joined the friars underground. Families paid donations to keep their loved ones here, and relatives would come to visit, to pray, and sometimes to change the clothes of the dead. For many Palermitans, these corridors were less a place of horror and more a family room.

As you walk, notice that the catacombs are organised into sections. There is a corridor for men and one for women. Priests and friars have their own area. Professionals were grouped together too. There is a chapel area for young unmarried women, and a small space for children. The layout shows how the society of the time saw itself — divided by gender, by role, and by stage of life — even after death.

The clothing tells its own story. Look at the faded jackets, the lace collars, the long dresses, the hats. Some of these garments are centuries old, and the fabrics have turned brown and grey with age. Many people chose their burial clothes themselves before they died. Some bodies stand upright, held by wires or supports. Others lie in coffins or rest on shelves. Each position was a choice made by someone who loved them.

Officially, the practice of placing new bodies here ended in the late nineteenth century. Yet there were a few exceptions after that. The most famous is in a small chapel at the end of one corridor. Her name is Rosalia Lombardo. She died in 1920, when she was not yet two years old. Her grieving father asked an embalmer, Alfredo Salafia, to preserve her. The result is so lifelike that visitors often call her Sleeping Beauty.

For decades, nobody knew exactly how Salafia did it. He took the method with him when he died. Then, in the early twenty-first century, a researcher studying the catacombs found Salafia's handwritten notes. They listed a chemical mixture that included formalin, alcohol, glycerin, and zinc salts. This is the second surprise of this place. A child who died over a century ago is now also a subject of modern science, studied with scanners and careful research.

You may hear a story that Rosalia's eyes seem to open and close during the day. Locals and visitors repeat it often. The explanation is an optical effect. Her eyelids are not fully closed, and as the light from the windows changes through the day, a thin line of her eyes can appear or disappear. It is a reminder of how much the light down here shapes what we see, and what we think we see.

The catacombs are now protected as a place of study as well as remembrance. Researchers have catalogued the remains to learn about health, diet, and disease in past centuries. To protect the bodies and to show respect, photography is not allowed inside. That rule can feel strange at first. But it also means you look with your own eyes, without a screen in between, and that is a different kind of attention.

For some visitors this is a dark place. For others it is strangely peaceful. In Sicilian culture, the relationship between the living and the dead has always been close. Families remember their dead in daily life, with visits, prayers, and stories. Standing here, you are part of that tradition for a few moments. You are another visitor among the many who have walked these corridors to look, to remember, and to think.

Before you climb back into the warm Palermo air, pause once more. Look at one face. Not the most famous one — any face. Someone sewed that collar. Someone chose that coat. Someone came here, year after year, to stand where you are standing now. When you step outside into the sunlight and the noise of the street, notice how bright everything feels. The friars wanted visitors to leave with exactly that feeling.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly; this remains an active religious burial place.
Bring a light layer for the cool underground corridors.
Keep your camera away; photography is not permitted inside.
Prepare children beforehand; the displayed remains can be upsetting.
Walk slowly and keep voices low out of respect.
