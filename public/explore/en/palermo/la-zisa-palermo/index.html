The guide

The story

Start with the heat. On a summer afternoon in Palermo, the air outside La Zisa feels thick and still. Then you step through the entrance, and the temperature drops. The stone around you is cool to the touch. A faint movement of air crosses your face, even though no window seems to be open. That small change is the first thing this building wants you to notice. It was designed, more than eight hundred years ago, to keep a king comfortable.

What stands in front of you is a tall, almost box-like block of pale golden stone. The outside is quiet and plain. Shallow blind arches run up the walls in long vertical lines, like the outlines of windows that were never cut. There are few openings, and they are small. From a distance, La Zisa looks closed and serious. Inside, it is a very different place.

The palace belongs to the Norman period of Sicily. The Normans were knights from northern France who took the island in the eleventh century, after a long period of Arab rule. They did not erase what they found. Instead, they hired Arab craftsmen, Greek mosaic workers and Latin builders, and mixed their skills. Work on La Zisa began under King William the First in the 1160s, and it was completed under his son, William the Second.

The name comes from Arabic. It is usually traced to the word al-Aziz, which means something like the splendid or the glorious. Near the top of the building, an Arabic inscription once ran along the facade. Today only parts of it survive. Think about that for a moment — a Christian king, ruling from Palermo, chose an Arabic name and Arabic writing for his summer palace. In twelfth-century Sicily, this was not strange. It was the language of luxury and learning.

La Zisa was never a fortress or a seat of government. It was a pleasure palace, a place for rest during the hottest months. It stood inside a large royal park outside the city walls. Sources call this park the Genoardo, a name linked to the Arabic phrase for paradise on earth. Picture orchards, fishponds, pavilions and running water across what is now a dense district of apartment blocks and busy streets.

Now the surprise. Much of the cool air you felt is not an accident. The builders used ideas known across the Islamic world. Thick walls held the cool of the night. Openings and internal shafts were placed to pull air through the rooms. Water added moisture and freshness. The whole building worked like a slow, natural air conditioner, with no machines at all. Many visitors walk through without realising the structure itself is a climate system.

The heart of the palace is the Fountain Room, on the ground floor. Walk to its centre and look up. The upper corners and the vault are filled with muqarnas — a type of Islamic ceiling decoration made of many small carved niches, stacked like honeycomb cells. They catch the light in tiny pieces, so the ceiling seems soft rather than solid. This room was meant to be the coolest and most beautiful space in the building.

On the back wall, water once flowed from a fountain and down a sloping marble panel. The marble is carved with a zigzag pattern. This shape broke the water into thin ripples, so it made a gentle sound and cooled the air as it fell. From there, the water ran along a channel in the floor, out through the entrance, and into a fishpond in front of the palace. Follow that line with your eyes, from the wall to the doorway.

Above the fountain, look for a band of gold mosaic. It shows peacocks, archers and palm trees, arranged in careful, mirrored pairs. The style is close to the mosaics in other Norman buildings in Palermo, and it carries strong influences from Byzantine and Islamic art. Peacocks were often linked with paradise and with royalty. Here, in a room built around water and shade, the image of a garden continues onto the walls.

The palace did not stay royal. Over the centuries it passed through many owners and uses. In the seventeenth century it belonged to the Sandoval family, who changed parts of the building to suit their own tastes. You can still see their coat of arms. In the early 1970s, part of the structure collapsed after years of neglect. The long restoration that followed saved the palace, and the joins between old stone and new repairs are still visible.

Today La Zisa houses a museum of Islamic art. Upstairs, the rooms hold objects from across the Mediterranean Islamic world — metalwork, ceramics, and carved wooden screens called mashrabiya, which let air and light in while keeping the inside private. Climb slowly. Notice how the rooms on the upper floors open onto each other, and how the light changes as you go higher. Since 2015, the palace has been part of a UNESCO World Heritage site that covers Arab-Norman Palermo.

Before you leave the entrance hall, look up at the painted vault. Locals talk about the devils of La Zisa. According to a popular tale, the painted figures there cannot be counted — every attempt gives a different number. Another legend says a treasure lies hidden somewhere in the palace, guarded by those same figures. Nobody has found it. But Palermo families have told these stories to their children for generations, and many still try to count.

When you walk back out, stand by the long pool in front of the palace. Turn around and look at the facade one more time. The traffic is louder now, the air is warmer, and the city presses close on every side. Yet the stone still holds the same quiet lines it held for a Norman king. Listen for the water, feel the heat return, and notice how much of this place was built simply to make a summer afternoon gentle.
