The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind does most of the talking. It moves across low, sun-baked mounds the colour of milky tea, and it lifts a fine dust that settles on your shoes and in the folds of your clothes. Underfoot the ground is crusted and pale, and it breaks with a soft crunch. Look down and you may see small fragments of fired clay among the dirt. They are easy to miss. They are also the reason anyone ever came here.

This is Jemdet Nasr, an archaeological tell in the flat country of Babylon Governorate, in central Iraq. A tell is a mound that people built without meaning to. Generation after generation lived in mudbrick houses, and when those houses collapsed, new ones went up on top of the ruins. Over centuries, the layers rose into a hill. What looks like a natural rise in the plain is really a stack of abandoned homes, workshops and storerooms.

There are no grand columns here, no carved gateways. That surprises many visitors. The importance of Jemdet Nasr is quieter than that. Its name is now attached to an entire stage of human history. Archaeologists speak of the Jemdet Nasr period, a span of roughly the late fourth and early third millennium before the Common Era. That is around five thousand years ago, when the first cities of southern Mesopotamia were taking shape.

Mesopotamia, for anyone unfamiliar with the word, means the land between the rivers. It refers to the region between the Tigris and the Euphrates, which together water most of modern Iraq. In this flat, hot landscape, people learned to dig canals and grow barley on a large scale. Farming surpluses supported temples, officials and craftspeople. And to keep track of all that grain and livestock, they needed a new tool. That tool was writing.

Here is the first thing most visitors do not expect. Some of the earliest written records in the world came out of this ground. In the 1920s, excavators working with the Oxford and Field Museum expedition, which was based at the nearby ancient city of Kish, dug at this mound. They recovered clay tablets marked with signs that scholars call proto-cuneiform. These are early forms of the wedge-shaped script that later spread across the ancient Near East.

Do not imagine poems or royal boasts on those tablets. As far as scholars can tell, they are mostly accounts. They list quantities of goods, numbers of animals, rations, and fields. In other words, some of the first writing humans produced was bookkeeping. There is something very human in that. Before anyone wrote down a story, someone needed to know exactly how much barley went where, and who was responsible for it.

The second discovery was colour. The excavators found a distinctive style of painted pottery, decorated in shades of red and dark paint over a pale clay surface. Designs often include geometric bands, checkerboards and other repeating patterns. This pottery became a marker. When archaeologists find the same style at other sites, they can recognise that those layers belong to the same broad era. That is how a single dusty mound gave its name to a whole period.

The early excavations were quick by modern standards, and records from that time were not as careful as archaeologists would want today. Decades later, in the late 1980s, a British team led by the archaeologist Roger Matthews returned to the site. They aimed to understand its layers more precisely and to re-examine what the first diggers had found. Among their interests were clay sealings, small lumps of clay pressed with carved seals to mark goods and containers.

Those seal impressions open a window onto how people organised themselves. A cylinder seal is a small carved stone that you roll across wet clay, leaving a continuous picture. It worked like a signature or an official stamp. Some seal impressions linked to this site appear to refer to several communities together. Scholars debate exactly what that means, but it hints that early settlements in this region were connected through shared administration, trade, or religious ties.

The ancient name of this settlement is not known with certainty. Jemdet Nasr is a modern Arabic name for the mound, used by local people and adopted by the excavators. So as you stand here, you are on a place whose residents had a name for it, a name they spoke every day, and which history has not clearly preserved. Their accounts survived. Their name for home, so far, has not.

Now look around at the landscape itself. The plain stretches out almost perfectly level, broken by palms, irrigation channels, and the occasional village. In ancient times the course of the Euphrates and its canals shaped where people could live. Water meant fields, and fields meant food. Many of the faint lines and dips you see across the countryside today are linked, in one way or another, to the long human effort to move water through this dry land.

Take a moment with the light. In the middle of the day it is hard and white, and it flattens the mounds into a single pale shape. Early in the morning or late in the afternoon, the low sun throws long shadows. Then the uneven surface of the tell becomes easier to read. Hollows, old trenches and slopes appear. If you can visit at those hours, the ground shows its shape far more clearly.

The finds from here are no longer on the site. Tablets and pottery from the excavations went to museum collections, and scholars around the world still study them. What remains in place is the mound itself, the scattered sherds, and the silence. That is worth respecting. Please leave every fragment where you find it. Each piece belongs to the record of the site, and removing it takes away information that future researchers may need.

Before you leave, stand still for a final minute. Feel the dust on your hands and the heat rising from the ground. Somewhere beneath your feet, people once pressed reeds into soft clay to count their barley and their sheep. They could not have known that their simple records would help start the long story of writing. As you walk back across the crunching earth, look down once more. Those small shards of fired clay are where that story began.
