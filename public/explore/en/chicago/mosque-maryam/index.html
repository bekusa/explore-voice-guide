The guide

The story

Listen first. Stony Island Avenue is a wide road, and the traffic here rarely stops. Buses sigh at the curb. Cars move north toward Jackson Park and the lake. Then you look up, and a large dome rises above the street. It sits calmly over the noise. This is Mosque Maryam, on Chicago's South Side, the national headquarters of the Nation of Islam.

Before we go further, a surprise. This building did not begin as a mosque. It was built in the middle of the twentieth century as a Greek Orthodox church, dedicated to Saint Constantine and Saint Helen. At that time, a Greek American community worshipped here. That origin explains the dome. In the Eastern Orthodox tradition, the dome often stands for heaven above the faithful. Its form traces back to Byzantium, the eastern Roman Empire centred on Constantinople.

So the shape you see came from one faith and now serves another. That is not rare in a city like Chicago. Neighbourhoods here change, and their buildings change hands with them. Churches become synagogues. Synagogues become churches. Theatres become places of prayer. The South Side especially carries these layers. Many families moved away in the decades after the Second World War, and new communities took their place. This building followed that same path.

Now, the community that worships here today. The Nation of Islam is a religious and political movement. It began in Detroit in the 1930s and soon moved its centre to Chicago. For decades, it was led by Elijah Muhammad. He taught a message of Black self-reliance, discipline, and separation from what he saw as a hostile society. The movement ran schools, farms, bakeries, and small businesses. For many Black Americans in the mid-twentieth century, it offered dignity and structure.

In the early 1970s, Elijah Muhammad bought this former church for his movement. It was a powerful statement. A Black religious organisation was buying a large, prominent building on one of the South Side's main roads. A school connected to the movement also operated nearby. Then, in 1975, Elijah Muhammad died. His son, Warith Deen Mohammed, took over. He led most of the community toward mainstream Sunni Islam, the largest branch of the Muslim faith worldwide.

Not everyone followed that change. Louis Farrakhan, a minister in the movement, chose to rebuild the Nation of Islam on Elijah Muhammad's original teachings. Over the following years, he gathered a new following. In the late 1980s, after a long renovation, he dedicated this building as Mosque Maryam. It became the headquarters of the revived Nation. So, in a way, this building marks two endings and a new beginning.

Here is the second surprise for many visitors. The name Maryam is Mary — the mother of Jesus. In the Quran, the holy book of Islam, Mary holds a deeply honoured place. A whole chapter carries her name. Christians and Muslims both revere her, though in different ways. So a building first raised to Christian saints now carries the name of a woman honoured in both faiths. It is a quiet thread of continuity through the change.

It is also fair to say that this place is debated. The Nation of Islam has drawn admiration for its work in prisons, its focus on family and self-discipline, and its role in Black American history. It has also drawn strong criticism. Louis Farrakhan's statements, in particular, have been widely condemned as antisemitic and divisive. Many visitors come here with mixed feelings. That is understandable. This building is a place where you can see American religion, race, and politics meet in one address.

The movement has also touched famous lives. Malcolm X rose to national attention as a minister of the Nation before he broke with it. The boxer Muhammad Ali joined the movement in the 1960s and later lived in Chicago. Both men later moved toward Sunni Islam. Their stories are not told through this exact building, but they belong to the same history. The ideas that grew in this city travelled far beyond it.

Now look again at the architecture. Notice how the dome dominates the whole composition. The walls below are solid and steady, built to support that weight. Look for the arched forms and the clear symmetry of the main front. These are signs of the building's Orthodox origins. Then look for the changes made for its Islamic use. Signs, lettering, and symbols added over time mark the building's new identity. The two layers sit together in the same stone.

If you go inside, remember that this is an active place of worship, not a museum. Members of the community guard it closely, and visitors may pass through a security check. Modest dress is expected. Women may be asked to cover their hair, and men and women may sit in separate areas. Ask before you take any photographs, inside or out near the entrance. A polite, respectful approach is the best way to be welcomed here.

Step back for a moment and take in the neighbourhood. Stony Island Avenue runs straight and wide, with low buildings and open sky on both sides. To the north lies Jackson Park, with its lagoons and wooded paths. Beyond it, Lake Michigan stretches toward the horizon. The wind here often carries the cool smell of the lake. The flat land makes the dome even more visible, rising clearly over the street.

Before you leave, stand on the far side of the avenue and look at the whole building once more. Picture the Greek families who first gathered under this dome. Picture the crowds who later came to hear the Nation's ministers. Then notice the traffic again, the buses, the people walking past without looking up. The dome stays where it is, above all of it. As you walk away, look back once. It is still there, over Stony Island Avenue.
