The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, students hurry between concrete university buildings. Traffic rolls along Halsted Street. And in front of you stands something older and quieter: a red-brick house with a wide front porch, tall windows, and white trim. It looks almost out of place here. That feeling is a good place to begin, because this house was always a little out of place. That was the whole point.

This is Hull House. In 1889, two young women, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr, moved into this building with a simple, radical idea. They would not help the poor from a distance. They would live among them, as neighbours. Over the following decades, this house became one of the most influential places in American social reform. Much of what modern cities take for granted was tested, argued over, or championed inside these walls.

First, the house itself. It was built in 1856 as a country home for Charles Hull, a Chicago real estate developer. At that time, this was the edge of the city. There were open fields and a sense of space. Look at the style. The tall, narrow windows and the decorative brackets under the roof belong to the Italianate fashion of the mid nineteenth century, a style that borrowed from rural villas in Italy. It was a gentleman's retreat.

But Chicago grew fast. By the time Addams arrived, the fields were gone. The Near West Side had become one of the most crowded districts in the city. Immigrants filled the streets: Italians, Greeks, Russian and Polish Jews, Irish, Germans, Bohemians, and many more. Families lived in crowded wooden tenements. Garbage piled up in the alleys. Children worked long hours. The old Hull mansion, once a country home, now stood in the middle of it all.

Where did the idea come from? Addams had travelled in Europe. In London, she visited Toynbee Hall, a settlement house in the poor East End. There, educated young men lived in the neighbourhood and offered classes and support. Addams brought the model home and changed it. Here, women led the work. And here, the residents did not just teach. They listened, they studied, and they pushed for new laws.

Here is something many visitors miss. Hull House was not one building. At its peak, it grew into a complex of thirteen buildings, covering most of a city block. There was a gymnasium, a theatre, a coffee house, a music school, an art gallery, a nursery, and residences. There was even one of the first public playgrounds in Chicago. Today, only two of those buildings survive: the mansion in front of you, and the Residents' Dining Hall beside it.

What happened to the rest? In the 1960s, the city chose this neighbourhood as the site for a new campus of the University of Illinois. Many local residents fought hard against the plan. They lost. Most of the settlement complex was torn down, along with much of the surrounding neighbourhood. The two remaining buildings were saved and restored. So when you look at the university buildings around you, you are looking at the answer to that old argument.

Now, the people. Hull House drew a remarkable group of residents. Florence Kelley investigated child labour and sweatshops, and she became a leading voice for workplace laws. Alice Hamilton, a physician, studied industrial poisons and dangerous working conditions, and she helped found a new field of medicine. Julia Lathrop later became the first head of the federal Children's Bureau. These were not volunteers dropping in for an afternoon. They lived here, sometimes for decades.

The residents believed in facts. In 1895, they published a book called Hull-House Maps and Papers. It included detailed maps of the surrounding blocks, coloured house by house to show the nationalities and wages of the families living there. It was careful, street-level research, and it helped shape the new science of sociology. Their work also fed into major reforms of the era — and Hull House residents played an important role in the creation of the first juvenile court in Cook County, in 1899.

Hull House also cared about beauty and dignity, not only about laws. Addams started a Labor Museum here. Older immigrant women showed traditional spinning and weaving from their home countries. The idea was gentle and clever. Children who felt embarrassed by their parents' old-world ways could see those skills honoured and respected. Art classes, plays, and concerts filled the evenings. For many neighbours, this was the first place they saw a painting on a wall.

Now a story that sits between history and folklore. In 1913, a strange rumour spread through the neighbourhood. People said a 'Devil Baby' had been born and was hidden somewhere inside Hull House. Crowds came to the door asking to see it. There was no such baby. Addams later wrote about the episode with curiosity rather than scorn. She was interested in why older women, especially, held on to such tales. Locals still tell ghost stories about the house today. Treat them as legend, not record.

Jane Addams herself became famous, and also controversial. During the First World War, she spoke out for peace, and many Americans called her disloyal. Her reputation suffered badly. Years later, opinions changed. In 1931, she was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize. She was the first American woman to receive it. Today the house is a museum run by the University of Illinois at Chicago, and it is recognised as a National Historic Landmark.

Before you move on, take one more look. Notice the porch, where neighbours once waited with questions about rent, work, and sick children. Notice how the house faces the street, open and close to the sidewalk. Then turn around and look at the busy campus and the city beyond. Many of the ideas tested here — playgrounds, safer factories, special courts for children — are now simply part of ordinary life. As you walk away, you are walking through a city this small brick house helped to change.
