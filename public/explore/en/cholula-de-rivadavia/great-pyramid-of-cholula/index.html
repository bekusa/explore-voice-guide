The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is a grassy slope, with dry earth, scattered stones, and paths worn smooth by walking. It looks like an ordinary hill. A church sits on top with yellow walls and a bell tower, and the morning sun is already warm on your shoulders. But this hill is not natural. Under the grass and soil lies one of the largest structures that human hands have ever built in the Americas.

The Nahuatl name for this place is Tlachihualtepetl. Nahuatl was the language of the Aztecs, and many people in central Mexico still speak it today. The name is usually translated as the made-by-hand mountain. That is a fair description. Measured by volume, this is often called the largest pyramid in the world. It is bigger than the Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt. It is not taller, though. It is wider, spreading across the landscape instead of rising to a sharp point.

The base is commonly described as roughly four hundred and fifty metres on each side. Picture several city blocks covered by a single monument. The pyramid also was not built all at once. Archaeologists have found several construction phases, each one wrapped around the one before it. Over many centuries, starting a few hundred years before the common era, builders added new layers of adobe and stone. Adobe is sun-dried mud brick. Each new layer made the structure larger and buried the old temple inside.

So when you stand here, you are standing on top of a set of nested pyramids, like layers inside a shell. Every generation of builders covered the work of the generation before. This was common in ancient Mexico. Temples were renewed, rebuilt, and enlarged as rulers and beliefs changed. Cholula was an important city for a very long time, and this pyramid grew with it.

Many scholars connect the later life of the site with Quetzalcoatl, the feathered serpent. He was one of the most important gods in central Mexico, linked with wind, learning, and priesthood. By the time the Spanish arrived, Cholula was a major place of pilgrimage. People travelled long distances to worship here. The city was also a busy trading centre, and its pottery was well known across the region.

That brings us to 1519, the darkest chapter in this town's story. Hernán Cortés and his Spanish forces came through Cholula on their way to the Aztec capital, Tenochtitlan. They were joined by Indigenous allies. Here, the Spanish and their allies killed a large number of Cholula's people in what is remembered as the Cholula massacre. Historians still discuss the exact numbers and the reasons. What is clear is that the event shocked the region, and it marked the start of a new and painful era.

Now look up at the church on the summit. This is the Sanctuary of Our Lady of the Remedies, built by the Spanish in the late sixteenth century. Placing a Christian church on top of a sacred Indigenous site was a deliberate act. It was a message about who now held religious power. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. By the time the church was built, the pyramid was already covered in earth and plants. It may have looked like a natural hill, and some accounts suggest the Spanish did not fully understand what lay beneath.

Locals tell their own stories about the pyramid's origins. According to one popular legend, a giant named Xelhua built it after a great flood, as a mountain to reach the sky. In the tale, the gods were angered by this ambition and sent fire to stop the work. Some listeners hear echoes of the Tower of Babel in this story. Treat it as folklore, not history, but it shows how long this place has held people's imagination.

Another story you may hear in town is that Cholula has a church for every day of the year. Locals often say there are 365. That number is more legend than careful count, but walk around and you will understand why the saying exists. Domes and bell towers appear in almost every direction. From the summit, you can see many of them rising above the low rooftops, tiled in bright colours.

Here is the second surprise. The pyramid is full of tunnels. In the twentieth century, archaeologists began digging passages into the structure to study it without destroying the church above. Over the decades, they created several kilometres of tunnels. A short section is usually open to visitors. Walking inside, you feel the cooler air, smell the damp earth, and see the walls of older buildings that were hidden for centuries. The tunnels are narrow and low in places, so take your time.

On the south side of the site, you can see excavated areas that show what the pyramid looked like before it was buried. There are stepped platforms, stone facings, and a large plaza often called the Patio of the Altars. Some of the original painted surfaces have survived in small sections. Archaeologists also found murals inside the structure, including one famous scene of figures drinking together. It is a rare look at daily life and ritual, not only war and gods.

Near the entrance, a small site museum helps you make sense of it all. It shows models of the different construction phases, pottery from Cholula, and copies of murals found inside. Spending a few minutes there before you climb will change how you see the hill. Suddenly the grassy slopes are not just slopes. They are the outer skin of a structure that people kept rebuilding for more than a thousand years.

If the air is clear, turn to the west from the top of the hill. You may see the snowy peak of Popocatepetl, an active volcano. It sometimes sends up a thin column of smoke or ash. Beside it stands its sleeping partner, Iztaccihuatl, whose long ridge looks a little like a woman lying down. The view puts everything in its place. There is the volcano, the ancient pyramid, the colonial church, and the modern town, all in one frame.

Before you walk down, pause for a moment. Listen to the church bells, the street vendors calling below, and the wind moving across the grass. You are standing on a hill that is really a building, on top of older buildings, in a town that has been sacred to many people over many centuries. As you leave, look back at the slope. It still looks like a hill... and now you know it is not.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the site museum first to understand the layers.
Come early for cooler air and clearer volcano views.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven summit climb.
Expect low, narrow passages inside the visitor tunnels.
Bring water and sun protection for the exposed slope.
