The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water is running over stones. The Ichijōdani River moves quietly through this narrow valley, and the air carries the smell of wet grass and cedar. Under your feet the ground is soft and level, almost too level for a mountain valley. That flatness is your first clue. For centuries, this was rice paddy. Beneath the mud lay an entire lost city.

You are standing in the old heart of the Asakura family's domain, a few kilometres southeast of central Fukui. The valley is long and thin, closed in on both sides by wooded hills. In the late fifteenth century, the Asakura made this place their capital. They ruled the province of Echizen, roughly the northern part of today's Fukui Prefecture, for about a hundred years and five generations.

Why hide a capital in a slot between mountains? The answer is defence. The valley works like a natural fortress. At its northern and southern ends, the Asakura built earthen and stone barriers called kido, a word that simply means gate. Anyone travelling up the valley had to pass through them. Inside those gates, a whole town grew up along the river... samurai houses, temples, merchants, craftsmen, and the lord's own residence.

This was not a rough frontier camp. Japan at that time was breaking apart. The Ōnin War, a long civil war that began in the 1460s, left the old capital of Kyoto badly damaged. Many court nobles, poets, and monks fled the city. A number of them came here. They brought tea ceremony, linked verse poetry, flower arranging, and refined taste in gardens. For a while, people spoke of Ichijōdani as a little Kyoto in the north.

Then, in 1573, it all ended. The warlord Oda Nobunaga, the most powerful man in Japan at the time, defeated the Asakura army. The last lord, Asakura Yoshikage, fled and later took his own life. Nobunaga's forces set the town on fire. Accounts describe the valley burning for days. The capital was never rebuilt, and over time farmers turned the ash and ruins into rice fields.

Here is the surprise. That disaster is the reason the site survived so well. Because no later city was built on top, the streets, foundations, and drains stayed buried and undisturbed. When large-scale excavation began in the late 1960s, archaeologists found the plan of a medieval town almost intact. They uncovered house lots, wells, stone paths, and a huge number of everyday objects. Many of those finds are now protected as Important Cultural Properties of Japan.

Walk toward the lord's residence area and look for the low stone foundations set into the grass. Each flat stone once held a wooden pillar. You can trace the outline of rooms, corridors, and kitchens with your eyes. In front of the residence site stands a wooden gate with a curved, gabled roof. This style is called a karamon. Local tradition says Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Nobunaga's successor, donated it to honour Yoshikage, though the gate itself dates from after the Asakura fell.

Now turn to the gardens. The stone gardens of Ichijōdani are among the oldest surviving warrior gardens in Japan. Four of them carry a national designation as places of scenic beauty. There are no flowers to speak of. Instead, large rough rocks stand upright on the hillsides, set by hand five centuries ago. Some remain exactly where the garden builders placed them. Water once flowed between them. Today moss fills the gaps.

The garden at the Suwa residence site holds one of the largest standing stones. It belonged to a woman close to Yoshikage. Stand in front of it and notice the scale. Moving rocks this size without machines took planning, ropes, and many workers. Gardens like this were a statement of power. A lord who could reshape a hillside could also command an army.

Across the river lies the reconstructed town street. For about two hundred metres, you can walk between rebuilt samurai residences and townspeople's houses. They follow the exact lot lines found during excavation. Peer inside and you will see earthen floors, wooden walls, a well, a toilet pit, even the tools of a dyer or a bead maker. These details come from objects found in the ground on this very spot. Nothing here is a guess dressed up as fact.

Pause by one of the wells. The archaeologists found that many households had their own. Some estimates suggest the town held around ten thousand people at its height, a large number for a valley this narrow. Picture the noise... hammers, voices, carts, temple bells, the river under it all. Then listen again to the quiet that surrounds you now.

If you raise your eyes to the east, you will see a steep wooded peak. That hill held Ichijōdani Castle, a mountain fortress for last-ditch defence. A hiking trail climbs to the old earthworks and terraces near the top. The climb is steep and muddy after rain, but the view down the valley shows the whole shape of the Asakura capital. Near the station, a modern prefectural museum displays finds from the excavations and a full-scale model of part of the lord's residence.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on one of the stone paths. The stones under your shoes were laid for people who walked here five hundred years ago. The hills you see are the same hills they saw. The river makes the same sound. As you walk back toward the gate, look at the rice fields beyond the site. Somewhere under them, perhaps, more of the city is still waiting.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and grassy slopes.
Visit the prefectural museum near the station first.
Allow at least half a day for the whole valley.
Skip the castle trail after heavy rain.
Bring insect repellent for summer visits near the river.
