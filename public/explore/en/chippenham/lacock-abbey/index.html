The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Your footsteps echo softly under the stone vaults of the cloister, and the air carries a cool, faintly damp smell of old limestone. Light falls through the arches in pale bars across the worn floor. Outside, rooks call from the trees, and somewhere beyond the walls a village goes about its quiet day. This is Lacock Abbey, a few miles south of Chippenham in Wiltshire. It began as a medieval nunnery, became a family home, and became one of the birthplaces of photography.

The story begins with a remarkable woman. Ela, Countess of Salisbury, was one of the most powerful landholders in thirteenth-century England. After the death of her husband, William Longespée, she founded this abbey in 1232 for nuns of the Augustinian order, a community that followed rules of prayer, work and shared life. Some years later, Ela became abbess here herself. She chose a quiet site in the meadows near the River Avon, and the place still has that quiet today.

As you walk the cloister, look closely at the stone. These walkways are some of the most complete medieval rooms in the building. The nuns walked here between prayers, read here, and kept the rhythm of the day. Off the cloister you will find rooms that served the community, such as the chapter house, where the nuns met to discuss business, and the sacristy, where sacred objects were kept. The ribbed vaults above you date largely from the later Middle Ages.

One room tells a small, human story. The warming room was one of the few places in a nunnery where a fire was allowed. In a cold stone building, in a Wiltshire winter, this was a precious space. Today a great metal cauldron stands here. It gives you a sense of the scale of cooking for a large household. Stand in this room for a moment and imagine the warmth after hours of prayer in the cold church.

For about three hundred years, the nuns lived here. Then, in the sixteenth century, everything changed. Henry the Eighth broke with the Church of Rome and closed the monasteries across England. Lacock was one of the last to go, in 1539. The abbey and its lands were sold to a courtier named William Sharington. He did not destroy the whole building, as many new owners did. Instead, he pulled down the church and turned the cloister buildings into a grand private house.

Sharington was ambitious, and the house shows it. He added an octagonal tower, a tall, eight-sided structure that held small private rooms. He also built the stable courtyard you can visit today. His work mixed new Renaissance ideas, which came from Italian classical design, with the older Gothic shapes of the abbey. Sharington's life had its own drama too. He was later involved in a scandal over coin-making at the royal mint, but he managed to survive it.

Here is a story you will hear from almost every guide at Lacock — but treat it as legend, not record. Sharington's niece, Olive, fell in love with a young man named John Talbot, and her uncle did not approve. According to the tale, Olive jumped from the battlements to reach him. Her skirts filled with air and slowed her fall, and he caught her below. In one version, the impact knocked him out. Whatever the truth, the couple did marry, and the house passed to the Talbot family.

The Talbots stayed for centuries, and each generation changed the house. In the eighteenth century, the family added a new Gothic entrance hall, designed in the 1750s by the architect Sanderson Miller. Gothic Revival was a fashion for bringing back medieval styles, and this hall is an early English example. Look up at the walls. Terracotta figures stand in the niches. They are playful, theatrical, and quite strange. Visitors often stop and stare at their faces.

Now comes the surprise that brings many people here. In the nineteenth century, the house belonged to William Henry Fox Talbot, a scientist, mathematician and inventor. He wanted to capture the images he saw through a camera lens. Here at Lacock, he experimented with paper treated with chemicals that reacted to light. In 1835, he pointed a small camera at one of the windows in the south gallery. The result is the oldest surviving camera negative in the world.

That window is still here. It is a latticed oriel window, a window that projects out from the wall, with small diamond-shaped panes. You can stand in the gallery and look at the same view that Talbot recorded. His tiny image is barely larger than a postage stamp, but its importance is huge. Talbot's process created a negative, and from one negative, he could print many positive copies. That idea shaped photography for more than a century.

Here is a second detail many visitors miss. Lacock Abbey once held one of the copies of Magna Carta, the great charter that limited the power of the king. Ela's family connection to the charter is part of this story, because her husband was present at its sealing. The Lacock copy, from 1225, stayed with the house for centuries. In the 1940s the family gave it to the nation, and it is now kept in the British Library in London.

In 1944, Matilda Talbot gave the abbey and much of the village to the National Trust, the charity that cares for historic places in England. Because of that gift, the village around you has changed very little. Its stone and timber houses have appeared in many period dramas. The abbey cloisters have appeared on screen too, including as classrooms and corridors in the early Harry Potter films. Many families come here looking for those scenes.

Before you leave, spend some time in the grounds. There is a botanic garden, a stretch of lawn, and the old trees that frame the abbey walls. Near the entrance, a museum in an old barn tells the story of Fox Talbot and his work, with cameras and early prints on display. Walk slowly. Look back at the house from the lawn, and you can see the different centuries side by side — medieval, Tudor, Georgian.

As you go, take one more look at the windows. Somewhere in that wall is the window that Talbot photographed, nearly two hundred years ago. The glass catches the same light today. The rooks are still calling, and the stone is still cool to the touch. Nuns, courtiers, inventors and film crews have all passed through these rooms. Now, for a little while, so have you.
