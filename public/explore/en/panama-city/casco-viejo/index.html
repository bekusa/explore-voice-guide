The guide

The story

The light here is different — softer, filtered through centuries of salt air and the shade of wrought-iron balconies overhead. You are standing in Casco Viejo, Panama City's colonial heart, where narrow cobbled streets meet the Pacific and the old stones still hold the memory of three empires. The air smells faintly of the sea, mixed with coffee brewing somewhere just out of sight.

This peninsula was born from catastrophe. In 1671, the Welsh privateer Henry Morgan sacked the original Panama City — known today as Panama Viejo — burning it to ash. The Spanish viceroy decided to rebuild on this rocky outcrop to the southwest, easier to defend, surrounded on three sides by water. By 1673, the new city was taking shape. For two centuries, Casco Viejo was the entire city, the beating heart of Spain's Pacific trade route, where silver from Peru passed through on its way to the Atlantic and onward to Seville.

Walk slowly here. The architecture layers three distinct periods like sediment. Spanish colonial buildings from the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries sit low and thick-walled, their facades painted in ochres and deep reds, balconies enclosed with wooden latticework. After independence in 1821, French influence swept in — mansard roofs, delicate ironwork shipped from foundries in Paris, tall shuttered windows. Then came the Americans during the Canal years, adding neoclassical banks and art deco apartment blocks in the early twentieth century. The result is a kind of beautiful chaos, no two streets quite matching.

The Cathedral of Our Lady of the Assumption anchors the main plaza. Construction began in 1796 but took nearly a century to finish. Its twin bell towers are clad in mother-of-pearl inlay, shimmering faintly in the afternoon sun — a detail you notice only when standing close. Inside, the altar is carved wood overlaid with gold leaf, brought from Spain in the seventeen hundreds. The floor is worn smooth by generations of footsteps, the stone cool even on the hottest days.

Just behind the cathedral, the Presidential Palace faces the waterfront. Built in 1920 on the site of an older colonial structure, it is painted brilliant white with Moorish arches and a courtyard filled with herons. The birds are wild, not kept — they simply arrived decades ago and stayed, wading in the fountain, nesting in the eaves. Locals call them the palace guardians.

Casco Viejo nearly disappeared. By the nineteen fifties, wealthier families had moved to newer neighborhoods, and the old quarter slid into neglect. Buildings crumbled, entire blocks stood empty, roofs collapsed into courtyards overgrown with vines. Squatters moved into the shells of mansions. For decades, it was considered dangerous, a place to avoid after dark. Then in 1997, UNESCO declared it a World Heritage Site, and restoration began in earnest. Today, the transformation is ongoing — some streets gleam with fresh paint and boutique hotels, others remain half-ruined, laundry strung between balconies, plaster peeling in great sheets to reveal older layers beneath.

The sea wall along Avenida A is where the city meets the Pacific. Waves crash against the stones during high tide, sending spray over the pavement. Fishermen still cast lines here at dawn. From this vantage, you can see the Bridge of the Americas arching across the Canal entrance to the west, and the glass towers of the modern financial district rising to the east — a skyline that did not exist forty years ago. The contrast is startling, old and new pressed together with no buffer.

Plaza Bolívar is smaller, quieter, shaded by ancient fig trees. A bronze statue of Simón Bolívar stands at the center, commemorating the 1826 Congress of Panama he convened here, an early attempt at Latin American unity. The plaza is ringed by low buildings with arcades, their columns chipped and stained. In the mornings, vendors sell empanadas and fresh coconut water. Pigeons gather in the shade, and elderly men play dominoes at folding tables.

The Church of San José, a few blocks north, holds one of the city's most famous objects — the golden altar. According to local legend, when Morgan's raiders approached the original city in 1671, a priest painted the altar black to disguise its value. Whether true or not, the altar survived and was moved here during the rebuild. It rises floor to ceiling, an explosion of baroque carving — saints, vines, cherubs, all covered in gold leaf that glows in the dim interior light.

Casco Viejo is not a museum. People live here, in apartments above the restaurants and galleries. Laundry hangs from balconies. Children kick footballs in the plazas after school. The neighborhood hums with the friction between preservation and daily life — a grandmother selling lottery tickets from a plastic chair beside a newly opened wine bar, a colonial courtyard converted into a co-working space, graffiti tagged on a three-hundred-year-old wall.

The best time to walk these streets is late afternoon, when the heat breaks and the light turns golden. Shadows lengthen across the cobblestones. The bells of the cathedral ring the hour, echoing off the old stones. Somewhere a radio plays salsa, the music drifting from an open window. The air cools slightly, carrying the smell of frying plantains and salt.

As you leave, glance back at the skyline one more time. The contrast — colonial balconies in the foreground, glass towers rising behind — captures the entire story of this city. Old trade routes and new finance, Spanish stone and American steel, all compressed into a few square kilometers. Casco Viejo holds its ground, weathered but still standing, the peninsula where Panama began.
