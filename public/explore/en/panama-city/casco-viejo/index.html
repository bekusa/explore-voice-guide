The story

Step off the wide avenues and into a maze of narrow lanes. The noise of the traffic fades behind you. Stone walls rise close on either side. Balconies hang above, so near that they almost touch. This is Casco Viejo, the old town of Bilbao. These streets were made for walking, not for cars. Let yourself slow down and simply wander. There is no single right path through these lanes.

Bilbao began here, on this side of the river. The town was founded in the year 1300. At its core lay just seven streets. In the Basque language they are called Zazpi Kaleak. In Spanish, they are the Siete Calles. For many centuries, these seven lanes held the entire town within them. Everything a person needed could be found inside this small space.

Walk slowly and read the names on the walls. Somera was the very first street of all. Then came Artekale, and then Tendería. Each lane once had its own trade and its own daily life. Merchants sold wool, iron, fish, and salt. The river carried their goods down to the sea. From there, ships took them out to the wider world. This trade made the young town grow rich and busy.

The seven streets still follow their old medieval lines. The buildings are tall, and they stand close together. Many hold small shops on the ground floor and homes above. Laundry hangs out to dry from some of the windows. The smell of coffee and fresh bread drifts from open doors. Here, life is still lived down at street level. Look closely, and you will find old doorways and carved dates.

Soon you will reach the Cathedral of Santiago. It is the oldest church in the old town. Long ago, pilgrims passed through Bilbao on their journey. They were walking west toward the tomb of Saint James. The cathedral gave them shelter and rest along the way. It served them then, and it serves the city still. Its bells still mark the hours for the streets around it.

Look up at the cathedral's pointed arches. The style is Gothic, with tall, narrow lines. Inside, slim stone columns rise toward the roof. Light falls through high windows onto the worn floor. A quiet courtyard with a covered walk sits to one side. Sit there for a while, away from the lanes outside.

Leave the church and find your way to the Plaza Nueva. This is a large square framed on all sides by arches. It was built in the nineteenth century. Rows of stone columns run around the edge. Under the arches sit cafés and small old shops. People meet here for little glasses of wine and small plates of food. In the evening, every table under the arches fills.

On Sunday mornings, the square fills with stalls. Sellers lay out old coins, stamps, books, and records. Some bring plants and small caged birds. Collectors lean over the tables in search of treasure. Families stroll, talk, and meet their neighbors. The square becomes a gathering place for the whole city, as it has for many generations.

The food here follows its own gentle custom. Small snacks are called pintxos. Each one usually sits on a slice of bread, held in place by a stick. People move slowly from one bar to the next. At each stop they take a single pintxo and one small drink. This unhurried crawl is part of daily life. No one is in a hurry, and neither should you be.

Make your way down to the Ribera Market by the river. It is a long covered hall, full of food and noise. Beyond its doors, the water moves slowly past the old quarter. Bridges link this bank to the newer city across the way. From here you can see how the old and the new face each other. The river has always been the center of the city.

As the day ends, the lanes grow busy once more. Voices echo off the stone walls. Glasses clink softly under the arches. The seven streets, so old and so narrow, hold the warmth of the crowd. People spill from the bars out into the cool night air. This is the heart from which all of Bilbao first grew.

Stand still in one of the lanes for a moment. Above you, a thin strip of sky runs between the rooftops. Around you, seven hundred years of daily life press close. The old town does not shout for your attention at all. It simply keeps on living, one quiet street at a time.
