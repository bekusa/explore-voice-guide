The guide

The story

Listen first. Out beyond the ruins, the Pacific moves across wide grey mudflats. At low tide the water pulls far back and leaves a shining shelf of wet sand, where white herons step slowly between the stones. Under your feet the ground is soft grass and old rubble. And behind you, almost close enough to touch, rise the glass towers of modern Panama City. Few places hold two worlds this close together.

You are standing in the ruins of the first Panama City. Spanish colonists founded it in 1519, under the governor Pedro Arias de Ávila. It became the first permanent European settlement on the Pacific coast of the Americas. For about a hundred and fifty years, this was one of the most important crossroads in the Spanish empire. Today it is a quiet field of broken walls and grey stone, open to the sky.

The Spanish did not arrive in an empty land. Indigenous communities lived along this coast long before 1519, fishing these rich waters. Archaeologists working at the site have found pre-Hispanic burials beneath the colonial layers. So the story here is older than the ruins you can see. One popular explanation says the name Panamá comes from an Indigenous word meaning something like an abundance of fish. Scholars still debate the true origin, but the idea fits this shoreline well.

Why build a city here, on a muddy, low coast? The answer is the narrow isthmus itself. Panama is the thinnest strip of land between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans. Silver from the mines of Peru came north by ship and landed on this shore. From here, mule trains carried it across the jungle on stone and earth trails to ports on the Caribbean side. Then fleets carried it to Spain. This city was a doorway for one of the greatest flows of wealth in history.

Now look toward the tallest ruin on the site. That square stone tower is the bell tower of the old cathedral. Builders raised the cathedral in the early seventeenth century, after earlier churches of wood. The tower has become the symbol of the whole site. It has been stabilised and restored, and visitors can often climb inside. From the top, you see the full shape of the old city laid out below — and the modern skyline running along the bay.

Walk slowly between the walls and you will notice their texture. Much of the stone is rough and dark, mixed with mortar and pieces of brick. Some walls still hold the square holes where wooden beams once rested. Imagine roofs of clay tile, wooden balconies, and narrow streets full of merchants, priests, enslaved Africans, sailors, and officials. Enslaved people did much of the hard labour that kept this trade city running. Their story is part of these stones too.

The city was full of religious houses. Ruins of several convents and churches still stand across the site, built by orders such as the Franciscans, the Dominicans, the Jesuits, and the nuns of La Concepción. Each one had cloisters, gardens, and chapels. Today some of them are only a corner of wall or a single arched doorway. But together they show how much of city life turned around the church. Bells from these towers once marked the hours of every day.

All this wealth attracted attention. Pirates and privateers watched the silver routes for decades. In 1671, the Welsh privateer Henry Morgan led a large force across the isthmus from the Caribbean side. His men fought the Spanish defenders on the plains outside the city. The defenders lost. Within days, the city that had stood for a century and a half was in ruins, and much of it was burned.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The fire that destroyed the city may not have been started by the pirates at all. Some historical accounts say the Spanish themselves set fire to parts of the city, or blew up stores of gunpowder, to keep the wealth out of Morgan's hands. Other accounts blame the attackers. Historians still argue about it. What is certain is the result: by the time Morgan left, the old city was blackened stone.

The Spanish did not rebuild here. In 1673 they founded a new city a few kilometres to the southwest, on a rocky point that was easier to defend with walls. That new city survives today as Casco Viejo, the historic old quarter. There is a well-loved legend connected to the move. Locals say a golden church altar was painted black to hide it from Morgan, and then carried to the new city. You can still see a golden altar in Casco Viejo's Church of San José — but treat the story as legend, not proven history.

After the move, this place slowly returned to nature. Trees grew through the walls. The tide kept washing the shore. For a long time, people took stones from the ruins for other buildings. Only in the twentieth century did serious efforts begin to protect what remained. In 1997, UNESCO listed Panamá Viejo as a World Heritage Site, and the listing was later extended to include Casco Viejo. The two old cities now share one story.

Before you leave, take time in the small museum near the entrance if it is open. It shows objects found during excavations — pieces of pottery, tools, and models of the city as it once looked. Seeing the model helps you understand what you have just walked through. Those single walls suddenly become houses, plazas, and streets again. It turns a field of ruins back into a living city, at least in your mind.

Now stand still for one more moment. Look at the cathedral tower, then at the towers of glass behind it. One skyline grew from silver and the other from the canal and modern trade. Both depend on the same narrow strip of land. As you walk away, listen again for the sea on the mudflats, and watch the herons. They were here long before the first stone was laid... and they are still here now.
