The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath the steady hum of traffic, there is a deeper sound — the low rumble of a ship's engine somewhere below. The air here is heavy and warm, and it carries the green smell of wet forest. Ahead, two tall concrete towers rise from the hills. White cables fan out from them like the strings of a harp. This is the Centennial Bridge, and it crosses one of the most important stretches of water on Earth.

The water below is the Panama Canal. To be exact, it is the Culebra Cut, also called the Gaillard Cut. This is the narrow channel where the canal slices through the mountains of the continental divide. On one side, rain flows toward the Atlantic. On the other, it flows toward the Pacific. The bridge stands almost exactly where the two great oceans of the Americas are separated by a ridge of rock and earth.

The name of the bridge tells you why it exists. Panama became an independent nation in 1903, when it separated from Colombia. One hundred years later, the country was preparing to celebrate that centennial. A new crossing over the canal was part of that celebration. The bridge carries the name of the anniversary, although the work ran a little behind schedule. It opened to traffic in 2004, the year after the centennial itself.

But the bridge was not built only for ceremony. It was built out of real need. For decades, the main road link across the canal near the capital was the Bridge of the Americas, an arched steel bridge that opened in 1962. As Panama grew, that single crossing filled with cars and trucks. Traffic jams became part of daily life. A second bridge, further north along the canal, gave the region a much-needed second route between east and west.

Now look at the design. The Centennial Bridge is a cable-stayed bridge. In simple terms, the road deck hangs from straight cables attached directly to the towers. This is different from a suspension bridge, where the deck hangs from one long, curving main cable. Cable-stayed bridges look lighter and more geometric. Here, the cables form clean, angled lines against the sky. On a clear day, they shine almost silver in the tropical sun.

The central span is long — more than four hundred metres between the towers. This was a deliberate choice. Engineers placed the towers on land, away from the water's edge, so that no supports stand in the shipping channel. The canal below must stay open for vessels day and night. The deck also sits high above the water, so that large ships can pass underneath without difficulty.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Culebra Cut has a difficult history with its own ground. When the canal was built in the early twentieth century, this section was the hardest to dig. The soil and rock kept sliding back into the channel. Landslides buried equipment and undid months of work. Thousands of workers laboured here in heat, rain, and disease. Building a bridge over this unstable ground required careful study of the slopes on both sides.

The second name of this channel honours one of those builders. David Gaillard was an American army engineer who led the excavation of the cut. He became ill and died in 1913, before the canal opened the following year. The channel was later named after him. So when you look down at the water, you are looking at one of the great engineering struggles of its age — and at the human cost behind it.

Now turn your attention to the ships. If you are lucky, a vessel is passing right now. Watch how slowly it moves. Container ships, tankers, and car carriers glide through this narrow passage at a careful pace. Small tugboats often stay close beside them. From the height of the bridge, even a large cargo ship can look surprisingly calm and quiet. It is one of the few places where you can see global trade in motion, almost within reach.

The surroundings are worth a moment too. Unlike many big bridges, this one does not stand in the middle of a city. Thick tropical forest covers the hills around it. This greenery is not an accident. The forests around the canal help protect the rivers and lakes that supply the canal with fresh water. Every ship that passes through the locks uses a large volume of that water. So the trees you see are part of how the canal works.

Another surprise is in the morning and evening sky. Panama sits on a major migration route for birds. Each year, large numbers of birds of prey travel over the isthmus between North and South America. In certain seasons, watchers in the region can see hawks and vultures circling on the warm air above the hills. Even on an ordinary day, look up. Frigatebirds, pelicans, and vultures often drift over the canal on long, slow wings.

The bridge is also part of a much longer road. It carries a section of the Pan-American Highway, the network of roads that runs through most of the Americas. Drivers crossing here may be heading home to the western provinces of Panama, toward Costa Rica, or simply to the other side of the city. In 2019, a third crossing, the Atlantic Bridge, opened near the city of Colón. Together, the three bridges now connect the two halves of the country across the canal.

Think for a moment about what this crossing means for Panama. For much of the twentieth century, the canal and the land around it were under American control. Full control passed to Panama at the end of 1999. A few years later, the country built this bridge over its own canal, named for its own independence. Some people see the bridge as a quiet symbol of that change — a structure that belongs fully to the nation it serves.

Before you move on, take one more look. Follow the white cables from the deck up to the tops of the towers. Notice the green slopes on each side, and the brown-green water of the cut below. If a ship is in view, watch it until it disappears around the bend. Listen again for that low engine sound under the traffic. It is the sound of the world passing through Panama, as it has for more than a century.

Before you go

Practical tips

Watch from a canal viewpoint rather than stopping on the bridge.
Check ship transit schedules to see a vessel pass beneath.
Visit in early morning for softer light and cooler air.
Bring a zoom lens to photograph ships and cables.
Carry water and sun protection in the tropical heat.
