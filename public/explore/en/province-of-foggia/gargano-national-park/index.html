The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below you, the Adriatic breaks against pale limestone. Behind you, the wind moves through pine and holm oak, and the air smells of resin, salt, and wild herbs warmed by the sun. The ground underfoot is rough and chalky. This is the Gargano, the rocky promontory that juts out from the eastern coast of Italy. On a map, it is the spur on the heel of the Italian boot.

The Gargano feels different from the flat land around it. To the south and west, the plain of the Tavoliere stretches out in fields of wheat and olive groves. Then the land rises suddenly into a block of hills, forests, and cliffs. That contrast is the first thing to notice here. You are standing on a kind of island of high ground. Over many centuries it developed its own landscapes, its own shrines, and its own way of life.

Italy protected this landscape as a national park in the early 1990s. It is one of the larger national parks in the country. Its boundaries cover the inland forests, the coastline, two large coastal lagoons, and even a small archipelago out at sea. A single day here can take you from a dark beech wood to a white beach to a cave church. Few protected areas in Europe pack so much variety into such a compact space.

Start with the rock itself. Almost all of the Gargano is made of limestone. Rainwater slowly dissolves this stone. Over a very long time it has carved caves, underground channels, and round hollows in the ground called dolines, which are natural sinkholes. One of the best known is the Dolina Pozzatina, a vast bowl in the land near San Marco in Lamis. When you see these hollows, remember that the hills are full of hidden water and empty spaces beneath your feet.

Now move inland, to the heart of the promontory. Here is the Foresta Umbra, the shaded forest. The name fits. Tall beech trees close over the paths, and on a summer afternoon the light turns green and soft. Most visitors miss a surprising detail. These beeches grow at a fairly low altitude, close to a warm southern sea, where you would not normally expect them. Since 2017, parts of this forest have belonged to a UNESCO World Heritage listing for Europe's ancient and primeval beech forests.

The forest is not only beech. You will also find oak, maple, yew, and holly. Deer move through the undergrowth, and woodpeckers drum on the trunks. The Gargano is also well known among naturalists for its wild orchids. In spring, dozens of species flower in the meadows and along the edges of the paths. Many are small and easy to overlook. Bend down close to the grass, and you may find one shaped like a tiny insect.

The coast is a completely different world. Between Mattinata, Vieste, and Peschici, white limestone cliffs drop straight into clear, blue-green water. Sea caves open at the waterline, and small coves sit between the headlands. Aleppo pines grow almost to the edge of the rock and lean out over the sea. Boats leave from the harbours to visit the caves, and the colour of the water changes as you pass from sunlight into shadow.

Along this stretch of coast, look for the trabucchi. These are old fishing machines made of wood, rope, and wire. Each one stands on a rocky point, with long wooden arms that hold a large net over the water. The fishermen lowered the net, waited for a shoal to pass, and then lifted it quickly. The structures look fragile, almost like skeletons. Several have been restored, and today they are among the most recognisable sights of this coastline.

Near Vieste, a tall white sea stack rises from the beach. Locals call it Pizzomunno. According to a popular local tale, Pizzomunno was a young fisherman who loved a girl named Cristalda. Jealous sirens dragged her into the sea, and in his grief he turned to stone. Legend has it that every hundred years the lovers meet again for one night. It is folklore, of course... but people in Vieste still tell it with real affection.

The Gargano is also a land of pilgrims. High on a ridge stands Monte Sant'Angelo, a town built around a cave sanctuary dedicated to Saint Michael the Archangel. Tradition says the archangel appeared here at the end of the fifth century. For more than a thousand years, pilgrims have walked down the steps into the cave to pray. The sanctuary is part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing that honours the Lombards, a Germanic people who ruled much of Italy in the early Middle Ages.

Not far away is San Giovanni Rotondo. This town became a major place of pilgrimage in the twentieth century because of Padre Pio, a Capuchin friar who lived and died here and was later declared a saint. Large crowds still arrive each year. So, in one small corner of Italy, you can follow a medieval path of devotion and a modern one. Both traditions shape the roads, the towns, and the small shrines you pass along the way.

On the northern side, the park includes two wide coastal lagoons, the Lake of Lesina and the Lake of Varano. Here the landscape becomes flat and quiet. Reeds line the shore, and herons and other water birds feed in the shallows. Out at sea lie the Tremiti Islands, a small group of rocky islands with clear water and an old abbey. Inland, farmers still raise the grey Podolica cattle, a hardy breed whose milk makes a local cheese called caciocavallo.

As you stand here now, take one more look around. Notice the colour of the stone, the sound of the wind in the trees or the waves below, and the scent of the herbs. Every part of the Gargano follows the same simple pattern: limestone, water, forest, and people who have adapted to all three. When you walk on, listen for small things, like a bird in the branches or a bell from a distant town. They belong to this place as much as any monument.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in spring to see wild orchids in flower.
Take a boat trip from Vieste to explore sea caves.
Wear sturdy shoes for rocky limestone forest trails.
Bring a light layer for cooler air in Foresta Umbra.
Allow several days to cover forest, coast, and towns.
