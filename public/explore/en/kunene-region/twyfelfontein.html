The guide

The story

Feel the heat rising off the rock beneath your feet. The sandstone here glows a deep rust red, almost the colour of dried chillies. It is cracked into huge blocks that lean against each other, as if a giant had stacked them and walked away. The air smells of dust and warm stone. Somewhere, a bird calls once and then goes quiet. You have arrived at Twyfelfontein, in the dry heart of Namibia's Kunene Region.

The name sounds strange at first. Twyfelfontein is Afrikaans, and it means "doubtful spring." A farmer who settled here in the 1940s gave it that name. The small spring in this valley was unreliable, and he could never be sure it would flow. But this place had a much older name long before him. In the local Khoekhoe language, it is known as Ui-Ais, a name usually translated as "jumping waterhole."

That single spring explains almost everything you are about to see. In a landscape this dry, water is life. For thousands of years, a source of water drew animals, and animals drew people. Hunter-gatherers came to this valley again and again. They rested here, they hunted here... and they left their mark on the stone. What they left behind is one of the largest collections of rock engravings in Africa.

There are thousands of them. Most estimates place the number at well over two thousand individual figures. The great majority are engravings, or petroglyphs. This means they were cut, pecked, or scraped into the rock surface, not painted onto it. The artists used harder stones as tools. They chipped away the dark outer skin of the sandstone to expose the paler rock underneath. That contrast is what makes the images visible to you today.

Most of this work is credited to the San, the hunter-gatherer peoples of southern Africa. Researchers generally believe the bulk of the engravings were made over the past two thousand years, though some may be older. Later, Khoekhoe herders also lived in this region and added images of their own. In 2007, UNESCO listed Twyfelfontein as a World Heritage Site. It was the first place in Namibia to receive that recognition.

Now look closely at the animals. You will see giraffe, with long necks stretched upward. You will see rhinoceros, elephant, ostrich, and antelope. Many figures are drawn in clean outline, with real confidence in the line. Alongside them are animal tracks and human footprints. For a hunting people, reading tracks was a vital skill. Some researchers think these prints may have worked almost like a teaching tool, or a record of knowledge.

Here is the first surprise. Among the desert animals, some panels appear to show seals and penguins. These are sea creatures, and the Atlantic coast lies more than one hundred kilometres to the west. How did they end up carved into rock in the interior? One explanation is that the people who made them travelled widely and knew the coast well. The images suggest a world of movement and contact far beyond this valley.

The most famous figure is often called the Lion Man. At first glance, it looks like a lion. But look again. The animal has five toes on each paw, not four. And its long tail ends in what appears to be a paw print, or perhaps a hand. Nobody knows for certain what the artist intended. But that small, strange detail is exactly what makes visitors stop and stare.

That question of meaning is where the stories begin. Many scholars connect the art to the spiritual life of the San. In this view, some images relate to the trance experiences of healers, sometimes called shamans. During trance, a healer might feel that they turned into an animal or crossed into a spirit world. A lion with human features could reflect that idea. It is important to say this is an interpretation, not a proven fact. But it shows that these were never simple pictures.

You cannot wander here alone, and that is a good thing. Visits are led by local guides, many from the surrounding communities. They will walk you along marked routes between the rock panels. Listen to them closely. A good guide will point out faint engravings that most eyes miss completely. The rule also protects the site. The art has survived for centuries in open air, and it is fragile.

That brings us to the second surprise. The greatest danger to these engravings is not the sun or the wind. It is human hands. Over the years, some visitors touched the rock, traced the lines, or even scratched their own marks beside the ancient ones. The oils from skin can damage the surface over time. So please keep your hands to yourself. Look closely, take photographs, but let the stone rest.

The wider landscape is worth your attention too. This is the region many still call Damaraland. It is open, dry, and wide, with flat-topped mountains on the horizon. Nearby, you can find a dark hill known as Burnt Mountain, and a formation of tall rock columns called the Organ Pipes. And in this harsh country lives a population of desert-adapted elephants. They have learned to survive with very little water, just as the ancient artists once did.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the warm stone under your shoes once more. Look at the slope of red boulders around you, and the small dark figures cut into them. Someone sat here, perhaps on a rock very close to where you are standing now. They chose this surface, and they chose this animal. As you walk back toward the path, watch the rocks on either side. There may be one more giraffe looking back at you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Carry plenty of water and wear a wide-brimmed hat.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven rocky walking paths.
Ask your guide to point out the faintest engravings.
Never touch the rock surfaces, even lightly.
