The guide

The story

Gravel crunches under your boots, and the air smells of dry grass and warm granite. Above you, the boulders glow a deep rust red, almost the colour of embers. The walk up the ravine is quiet except for the click of insects and the occasional bird calling from a thorn tree. Then the path narrows, a shaded rock overhang appears... and on its wall, a small pale figure strides across the stone. This is the White Lady.

The painting sits in the Brandberg, a huge granite massif rising out of the desert in northwestern Namibia. It is the highest mountain in the country, and its name means "burning mountain" in German and Afrikaans. The Damara people call it Dâures, a name usually translated the same way. Watch it in late afternoon and you will understand why. As the sun drops, the rock seems to catch fire from within, turning orange, then copper, then a deep, smoky red.

The White Lady is not alone up here. The Brandberg holds one of the largest concentrations of rock art in southern Africa. Researchers have documented tens of thousands of painted figures across hundreds of shelters, hidden in gullies and under overhangs all over the mountain. Most visitors see only this one panel. But as you walk, remember that the rocks around you may carry paintings that few people have ever looked at closely.

The route to the shelter follows a dry riverbed known as the Tsisab Ravine. The name is often said to mean "leopard" in the Damara language. Leopards do still live in these mountains, though you are very unlikely to see one. What you will notice instead are the tough little plants that survive here — shrubs with grey leaves, and trees that hold on to cracks in the rock. Everything in this landscape is built to save water.

Now look closely at the figure itself. It is painted mostly in reddish brown, with legs and parts of the body in white. That white colouring gave the painting its famous name. The figure appears to carry objects in its hands, possibly a bow and arrows, and something that some observers have read as a cup or a flower. Around it, other human figures and animals move across the rock, including a large antelope that looks like an oryx.

Here is the first surprise. The White Lady is very probably not a lady at all. Most archaeologists today believe the figure is a man. Many think he may be a healer or ritual specialist, perhaps painted in body paint for a ceremony. The white legs may show decoration or clay, not a woman's clothing. The name stuck long before this reading became accepted, and it is now simply part of the place.

The painting first came to wider attention in 1917. A German surveyor, Reinhard Maack, was mapping the Brandberg when he came across this shelter and copied the scene into his notes. That is why the overhang is often called the Maack Shelter. For decades after his visit, few outsiders came here. The terrain was remote, the heat was intense, and reaching the ravine took real effort.

The second surprise involves a famous French priest and prehistorian, Abbé Henri Breuil. In the years after the Second World War, he studied the painting and made it known around the world. Breuil argued that the figure showed a woman of Mediterranean origin, perhaps linked to ancient Crete or Egypt. It was a dramatic theory, and it brought the White Lady great fame. But later research rejected it completely.

Today the painting is understood as the work of the ancestors of the San, the hunter-gatherer peoples of southern Africa. Their art appears across rock shelters from Namibia to South Africa. Breuil's idea reflected a belief, common in Europe at the time, that local African communities could not have produced such skilled work. That belief was wrong. The figure in front of you belongs firmly to this land and to the people who lived here.

How old is the painting? Nobody knows exactly. Dating rock paint is difficult, and estimates vary. Many researchers suggest the art of the Brandberg was made over a very long period, and that this panel is likely at least a couple of thousand years old. The pigments came from the earth — ochres, clays and minerals ground into powder and mixed with a binding liquid. The colours you see were literally taken from the ground around you.

You will notice a protective barrier in front of the painting, and a guide standing beside you. Visits here are made with local community guides, and this is for good reason. In the past, some visitors splashed water on the rock to make the colours brighter for photographs. Over time, this damaged the paint and dulled the image. The barrier keeps hands, water and dust away, so the figure can survive for future generations.

Take a moment to look beyond the main figure. Let your eyes move slowly across the whole wall. Small animals, running people and faded shapes appear the longer you look. Some are clear, others are almost gone. The overhang also frames a view back down the ravine, toward the open desert. Imagine the people who sat in this shade, mixing paint, and looking out at the same horizon you see now.

As you walk back down, the heat rises off the stone and the light changes on the boulders. Listen to the gravel under your feet, the wind moving through the dry grass. The painting stays behind you in its shelter, quiet and small against the size of the mountain. Before you leave the ravine, turn around once more. Somewhere up there, on rocks you did not visit, other painted figures are still waiting in the shade.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the ravine walk early to avoid the midday heat.
Carry plenty of water for the walk up and back.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel and boulder sections.
Visit with a local community guide at the site.
Stay for late afternoon to see the mountain glow red.
