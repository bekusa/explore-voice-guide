The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead, water moves over stone. The air is cooler than it was a few minutes ago, and it smells of wet rock and damp earth. Above you, a ragged opening in the cave roof lets in a column of daylight. Leaves turn slowly in that light. This is the Peruaçu valley, and you are standing inside a canyon that a small river cut through limestone over a very long stretch of time.

The park sits in the far north of Minas Gerais, in southeastern Brazil, in a dry and open landscape. São João das Missões, Januária and Itacarambi are the towns closest to it. Brazil created the national park in 1999 to protect the river, its caves and the painted rock walls of the valley. For many years few outsiders came here. Today small groups walk the trails with local guides, and the place still feels quiet and far from any city.

The river gives the park its name. The Peruaçu runs toward the São Francisco, one of Brazil's great rivers. Along the way it does something unusual. In places it disappears underground, flows through caves, and comes back into daylight further downstream. Over thousands of years the water dissolved the limestone. Cave roofs fell in. What remains is a chain of huge caverns, natural bridges and collapsed openings, all joined by the same thread of moving water.

Geologists call this kind of terrain karst. The word describes land made of soluble rock, usually limestone, that water slowly carves from the inside. Look at the walls around you. You can see grooves and channels where water once ran. You can also see smooth curves where the river polished the stone. Every surface carries a record of time and water, written in shape rather than in words.

The best-known cave here is the Lapa do Janelão. The name means roughly the cave of the big window. It comes from the great openings in its roof, where sections of rock collapsed long ago. Through those gaps, sunlight falls straight down onto the cave floor. Trees grow in the light, so a small forest lives inside the cave. Few visitors expect to find green leaves and birdsong underground.

Inside the Janelão hangs a very large stalactite. Guides often call it the Ballerina's Leg. It is frequently described as one of the largest stalactites known anywhere in the world. Stalactites grow drop by drop, as water leaves behind tiny amounts of mineral. A formation of this size needs an enormous amount of time. Stand beneath it for a moment and think about how slowly each drop worked.

Now comes the part that surprises many people. This park protects nature, but it also protects human history. The valley holds many archaeological sites. People lived in these rock shelters thousands of years ago. They left tools, traces of fires and burials. Above all, they left paintings. Researchers count the Peruaçu among the most important places in Brazil for the study of early human life.

The rock art covers walls and overhangs across the valley. You will see figures in red, yellow, black and white. Some shapes look like animals. Others are geometric patterns, grids, circles and lines whose meaning is still debated. The painters made their colours from minerals in the earth. In many places, newer images were painted over older ones. One wall can hold the work of different peoples from different periods.

No one knows exactly what these images meant to the people who made them. Some may have marked territory. Others may have belonged to rituals or stories. Archaeologists study the style, the layers and the location of each panel. Even so, much remains uncertain. That uncertainty is part of the experience here. You look at a hand-drawn line, and you know a real person stood where you stand.

The landscape outside the caves matters too. This region lies where different Brazilian ecosystems meet. There is the cerrado, a dry savanna with twisted trees and tough grasses. There is also dry forest that loses many leaves in the dry season. Near the river, the vegetation is greener and denser. Watch for lizards on warm rocks, and listen for parrots and other birds in the trees above the trail.

People still live close to this land. Near São João das Missões is the territory of the Xakriabá, an Indigenous people of northern Minas Gerais. Many families in the surrounding towns depend on farming and, more and more, on visitors. The local guides who lead groups through the park often grew up in the area. Ask them about the valley. Their knowledge goes beyond facts on a sign.

Visits here follow rules. You walk only with an authorised guide, on set trails, and in small groups. These limits protect fragile formations and painted walls. A single touch can leave oils from your skin on a painting that survived for thousands of years. So keep your hands back, stay on the path, and use the guide's eyes as well as your own. The slow pace helps you notice more.

Before you leave, stop for a minute and look around once more. Notice the colour of the light where it falls through the roof. Notice the cool air against your skin and the sound of water somewhere below. Then think of the painters, the river and the long patience of the stone. As you walk back toward the trailhead, the canyon will close behind you, but the quiet will stay with you a little longer.
