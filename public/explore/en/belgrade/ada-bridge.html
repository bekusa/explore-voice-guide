The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the hum of traffic, there is a low, steady rush of water. That is the Sava River, moving slowly towards the Danube a short distance downstream. Above you, white steel cables rise in long, straight lines, all of them meeting at one tall needle of a tower. On a clear day, the light catches each cable separately. They look almost like the strings of a huge instrument stretched across the sky.

This is Ada Bridge, one of the newest landmarks in Belgrade. It opened at the start of 2012, so many locals still remember the city before it existed. For decades, traffic between New Belgrade and the southern districts had to squeeze across older bridges closer to the centre. This crossing was built to take some of that pressure away. It also gave the city a new shape on its skyline.

The design is called a cable-stayed bridge. In simple terms, the road deck does not rest on many supports in the water. Instead, it hangs from cables, and those cables run up to a single pylon. The pylon carries the weight down into the ground. Here, there is only one pylon, and that is what makes the silhouette so clean. From almost any riverbank in Belgrade, you can spot that lone white spike.

Now for the first surprise. That pylon does not stand on either riverbank. It stands on the very tip of an island — Ada Ciganlija, the long green strip of land that gives the bridge its name. The word ada means river island in Serbian. So the bridge uses the island as a stepping stone. Its tower rises from the point where the water divides around the land.

The pylon reaches roughly two hundred metres into the air. That makes it one of the tallest structures in the city. The main span, the stretch between supports over the open water, runs for more than three hundred and seventy metres. When the bridge opened, engineers and journalists pointed out that this was one of the longest spans in the world for a bridge with a single pylon. It is a quiet record. Most people who drive across never hear about it.

The bridge was designed by a team led by the Slovenian engineer Viktor Markelj. Building it was not simple. The river is wide here, and the soft ground near the water needed deep foundations. Large sections of the steel deck were floated along the Sava and lifted into place. For a while, residents watched the bridge grow outwards from the tower, piece by piece, like two arms reaching for the shore.

Look along the deck and you will notice something unusual. The middle of the roadway has space set aside for a future rail line. Belgrade has long discussed a light rail or metro system, and the bridge was planned with that possibility in mind. So this structure was built for a city that does not fully exist yet. It carries the traffic of today and leaves room for the transport of tomorrow.

If you are on foot, you share the bridge with cyclists and runners. There are paths for pedestrians and bikes on the deck, and from here the view opens in every direction. Upstream, the river curves away towards the south and west. Downstream, you can see the older bridges and the towers of New Belgrade. On the far horizon, the old city sits on its ridge above the meeting of the two rivers.

Below you lies Ada Ciganlija itself. Locals often call it the Belgrade sea. The island was joined to the riverbank by dams long ago, and the water trapped inside became a long, calm lake. In summer, thousands of people come to swim, sunbathe, row, and cycle around it. From the bridge, you can often see the green tree line and, in warm months, the bright colours of beach umbrellas through the leaves.

Here is the second surprise. The bridge changes after dark. A lighting system runs along the pylon and the cables, and at night the whole structure glows. The colours can shift, and on special occasions the bridge has been lit to mark holidays and public events. Many Belgraders know this bridge best as a line of light reflected in the black water. If you can, come back after sunset and see it again.

People in Belgrade have mixed feelings about big modern projects, and this bridge has had its share of debate about cost and planning. But over time, it has become part of everyday life. Couples take photographs here. Runners use it for long training loops. Families cross it on their way to the island beaches. A structure that once looked new and strange now simply belongs to the view.

Stand still for a moment near the middle of the span. You may feel a very slight movement under your feet as heavy trucks pass. That is normal. Long cable-stayed bridges are built to flex a little with wind and weight. Listen too. The wind can make a soft sound around the cables, and the river below never fully goes quiet. Seagulls and river birds often circle the pylon, especially in the cooler months.

As you leave, take one last look up at the tower and follow a single cable down to the deck with your eyes. Then turn towards the island and the trees, or towards the city and its older bridges. You are standing on the newest line drawn across a very old river. The Sava has carried boats and armies past this point for centuries. Today, it carries the reflection of a white tower and the people walking across it — including you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Return after sunset to see the pylon and cables lit.
Walk the pedestrian path for wide views of both riverbanks.
Combine the crossing with a stroll around Ada Ciganlija lake.
Bring a windproof layer; the open deck gets breezy.
Photograph from the riverbank to fit the whole fan of cables.
