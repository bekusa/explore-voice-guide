The guide

The story

Feel the ground change under your feet. Smooth city pavement gives way to old stone, worn uneven by centuries of boots, carts and hooves. A breeze comes up from below, carrying the damp, green smell of moving water. Somewhere a tram rattles, a dog barks, and a group of chess players argue quietly on a bench. You are standing on a ridge above the meeting of two great rivers, and people have been fighting to stand exactly here for more than two thousand years.

Walk toward the edge of the walls and look down. On your left, the Sava River flows in from the west. Ahead, it joins the Danube, one of the longest rivers in Europe. That meeting point is the whole reason this fortress exists. Whoever held this ridge could watch both rivers, control trade, and block armies moving between Central Europe and the Balkans. For empire after empire, this hill was a key that had to be held.

The story begins long before any of the walls you see now. A Celtic tribe called the Scordisci settled here in the centuries before Christ. Then the Romans arrived and built a military camp named Singidunum. It housed a legion and guarded the Danube frontier of the empire. Most of the Roman stonework is now buried beneath later layers, but fragments still appear across the site. Keep an eye out for old foundations and carved stones resting in the grass.

After Rome, the hill passed through many hands. Byzantines, Bulgarians, Hungarians and Serbs all ruled here at different times. The fortress was destroyed and rebuilt again and again. Each new owner reused the rubble of the last. That is why the walls look patched together, with different stones, different colours, and different building styles side by side. You are looking at one structure built by many peoples who never met each other.

In the early fifteenth century, Belgrade became the capital of the Serbian state under Despot Stefan Lazarević. He was a ruler and also a writer. He strengthened the Upper Town and turned the fortress into a real seat of power, with a palace, a harbour and new towers. Very little of his palace survives today. Still, his time is remembered as a short golden age, a brief bright moment before a long storm.

That storm came from the Ottoman Empire. In 1456, Sultan Mehmed the Second, the conqueror of Constantinople, laid siege to Belgrade. The defenders were led by the Hungarian commander John Hunyadi. Against the odds, they held the city. Here is a detail most visitors miss. During the siege, the Pope ordered church bells across Europe to ring at noon, calling the faithful to pray for the defenders. Many churches still ring their bells at midday, and that habit is often linked to this battle.

The victory did not last forever. In 1521, Suleiman the Magnificent captured Belgrade, and the Ottomans held it for most of the next three centuries. They gave the park around you its name. Kalemegdan comes from two Turkish words: kale, meaning fortress, and meydan, meaning field or open square. So when locals say they are going to Kalemegdan, they are really saying they are going to the fortress field. The name has stayed long after the empire left.

Much of what you see today comes from an eighteenth-century chapter. Austria seized Belgrade in 1717 and rebuilt the defences in the modern style of that era. Look for the low, angled walls, deep ditches and star-shaped bastions. These shapes were designed to deflect cannon fire, not arrows. The Austrians also built the so-called Roman Well. Despite its name, it is not Roman at all. Locals have long told dark stories about it, but its real origin is Austrian military engineering.

Now look for the Victor monument, a tall bronze figure on a high column at the edge of the Upper Town. The sculptor was Ivan Meštrović, and the statue was raised in 1928 to mark Serbian victories in the wars of the early twentieth century. The naked man holds a falcon in one hand and a sword in the other. Here is the second surprise. The statue was first planned for a square in the city centre. Some citizens found the nudity improper, so it was placed here instead, facing out over the rivers.

From the Victor, the view opens wide. Across the Sava, you can see New Belgrade, with its long blocks and broad avenues built in the twentieth century. To the right, the Danube bends past the flat green land of the old border with Austria. On a clear afternoon, the water turns silver and then gold. This is the spot where couples linger, students sit on the wall, and older residents come to watch the sun go down.

If you walk down toward the Lower Town, you will find two small churches. Ružica is a modest church built into the old walls. Inside, the chandeliers were made by soldiers from spent bullets, shell casings and sword parts. Nearby is the Church of Saint Petka, built over a spring. Many people come here to collect the water, which they believe has healing power. You may see visitors filling bottles and lighting thin candles in silence.

On your way through the fortress, notice the gates and towers. The Stambol Gate, named for the road to Istanbul, was once a place of public executions. The Clock Tower rises above the Upper Town and has kept time for the garrison since the eighteenth century. Down by the river stands Nebojša Tower, whose name roughly means fear nothing. The Greek revolutionary poet Rigas Feraios was killed there in 1798. Today the tower holds a small museum about his life and that era.

Before you leave, pause and listen again. The chess players are still at their boards. Children run along the paths where soldiers once marched. Tennis balls thud on courts below the walls, and boats move slowly on the water. This hill has been a camp, a capital, a prison and a battlefield. Now it is mostly a park, a place for ordinary afternoons. As you walk back toward the city, look at the stone under your feet... and remember how many people have crossed it before you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon for sunset over the river confluence.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone paths and slopes.
Visit Ružica and Saint Petka churches together in Lower Town.
Dress modestly and stay quiet inside the working churches.
Bring a bottle if you want spring water at Saint Petka.
