The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the paving of Republic Square. Pigeons lift off around the bronze rider at its centre. Trams and buses hum along the edges, and voices drift in from the cafés of the pedestrian street nearby. In front of you rises a pale, ornate facade, heavy with carved stone and tall windows. This is the National Museum of Serbia, the oldest museum in the country, and one of the oldest public institutions in Belgrade.

Before we go inside, look at the rider behind you. That is Prince Mihailo Obrenović, a nineteenth-century ruler of Serbia, on horseback. Locals rarely use his name. They simply say they will meet "at the horse." For generations this spot has been the city's default meeting point. Students, couples and old friends gather here. So the museum sits at the exact place where Belgrade comes to wait for someone.

The museum's story begins in 1844. At that time Serbia was a young principality, still emerging from centuries of Ottoman rule. The writer Jovan Sterija Popović, then working in education, pushed for a national collection. The idea was simple and ambitious. A new state needed a place to keep the evidence of its past. Coins, manuscripts and antiquities were gathered, and a museum was born before the country even had full independence.

For its first century, the collection moved between several buildings. Wars interrupted its work more than once. During the First and Second World Wars, objects were packed, moved, lost and sometimes recovered. That instability shaped the museum's character. It is not a calm, untouched treasure house. It is a collection that has survived repeated crises, and the staff who protected it are part of its story too.

The building you see now was not designed as a museum at all. It was built in the early twentieth century for a state lottery administration, and later used by a financial institution. You can still sense that origin. The facade has the confident, decorated look of a bank — columns, sculpted details, a strong sense of order. The museum moved into these rooms only in the 1950s, after the Second World War.

Here is something many visitors do not know. For about fifteen years, this museum was closed. Renovation began in the early 2000s and dragged on through funding problems and delays. A whole generation of Belgrade children grew up without visiting it. When the museum finally reopened in 2018, people queued around the square. For many residents, walking back inside felt like getting back a part of the city they had almost forgotten.

Inside, the journey starts long before Serbia existed. Some of the most remarkable objects come from Lepenski Vir, a prehistoric settlement on the Danube, in the gorge known as the Iron Gates. The people there lived by the river many thousands of years ago. They carved large river stones into strange, fish-like human faces. Look closely at their round eyes and wide mouths. Nobody knows exactly what these figures meant to the people who made them.

Not far from these, you will meet the Vinča culture. Vinča is a site just outside Belgrade, on the bank of the Danube, and it gave its name to a Neolithic culture — a farming society of the late Stone Age. Its clay figurines have triangular, masked faces and large almond eyes. They look almost modern. Archaeologists still debate whether they show gods, ancestors or people wearing ritual masks.

Then comes Rome. The land of modern Serbia was an important part of the Roman Empire. Several emperors were born in this region, including Constantine the Great, who came from Naissus, today the city of Niš. The museum holds a bronze head of Constantine. Pay attention to his eyes, which look upward. That gaze was a deliberate image of a ruler connected to the heavens. Nearby, gilded helmets show the wealth of late Roman officers.

The medieval rooms bring you to the heart of Serbian identity. The greatest treasure here is Miroslav's Gospel, an illuminated manuscript from the late twelfth century. It was made for Prince Miroslav, a brother of the founder of the Nemanjić dynasty, the medieval royal family of Serbia. Its pages are decorated with colourful initials, birds and animals. The original is extremely fragile, so it is shown only rarely. It is listed by UNESCO as part of the world's documentary heritage.

Upstairs, the mood changes again. The museum holds a large collection of Serbian and Yugoslav painting from the eighteenth to the twentieth centuries. You can follow how local artists moved from church icons to portraits, then to landscapes, and finally to bold modern styles. The collection also includes works by European masters, including French Impressionist and modern painters. Many visitors are surprised to find such names in Belgrade at all. Take your time here. The rooms are rarely crowded.

As you walk, notice the building itself. The stairs, the high ceilings and the tall windows let daylight fall across the floors. Wood and stone surfaces carry the marks of a full restoration. Old bank interiors and modern display cases sit side by side. Sometimes the contrast is quite sharp. That mix is honest. It shows a historic building adapted, with some effort, for a very different purpose.

It helps to think of this museum as a kind of long conversation. Prehistoric river people, Roman emperors, medieval monks and modern painters all speak across the same rooms. Each group lived in this land, and each left something behind. The national story here is not a single straight line. It is layered, interrupted and often surprising — much like the city outside the doors.

When you step back out onto Republic Square, pause for a moment by the steps. Look across at the bronze prince on his horse, and at the people waiting beneath him. Listen to the trams and the conversations. Somewhere behind you, those carved river faces are still watching from their cases. The past and the present share this square every day. You are now part of it too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with the prehistoric rooms to follow the chronology.
Check ahead whether Miroslav's Gospel is currently displayed.
Allow at least two hours for all the floors.
Meet friends beforehand at the horse statue outside.
Visit on a weekday morning for quieter galleries.
