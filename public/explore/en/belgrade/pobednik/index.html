The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes up from the rivers below. It moves across the old stone walls and carries the faint sound of water traffic and distant city noise. Under your feet, the paving of the Upper Town is worn smooth by countless visitors. Above you, on a tall pale column, a bronze man stands against the open sky. He looks out over the place where two great rivers meet. This is Pobednik — in English, the Victor — and he has watched over Belgrade for nearly a century.

You are standing inside the Belgrade Fortress, high on the ridge that locals know as Kalemegdan. For most of its long history, this hill was not a park. It was a military stronghold, fought over again and again by armies from the east and the west. The position explains why. From here, a defender could see far across the flat land to the north and watch every boat on the water. The Victor stands at the edge of that view.

The monument was unveiled in 1928. It marked the tenth anniversary of a turning point in the First World War. In 1918, Allied forces, with the Serbian army at their centre, broke through the enemy line on the Salonika Front in northern Greece. Salonika is the older name for the Greek city of Thessaloniki. That breakthrough led to the liberation of Serbia and the end of the war in the Balkans. For people here, it meant the return home after years of exile and loss.

The sculptor was Ivan Meštrović, an artist from Dalmatia, on the Croatian coast. He became one of the best-known sculptors of the twentieth century in this region. His work often combines classical forms with a strong, almost architectural sense of the human body. In Belgrade, he also created the Monument to the Unknown Hero on Avala hill, south of the city. If you have seen that memorial, you may notice the same solemn, heavy style here.

Now look closely at the figure. In his right hand, the Victor holds a sword, pointed downward. That detail matters. A lowered sword usually means the fighting is over. It is a sign of peace that has been won, not of a battle still to come. In his left hand, he holds a falcon. The bird is a symbol of freedom, and in South Slavic folk poetry falcons often appear beside heroes. Together, the two objects tell a simple story: war finished, liberty kept.

Here is the first surprise. The Victor was never meant to stand on this hill. The original idea was to place him in the city centre, at Terazije, as part of a fountain. The project began earlier, connected with Serbian victories in the Balkan Wars before the First World War. The war interrupted everything. When the statue was finally ready, the plan for the city square no longer seemed right, and the figure needed a new home.

The reason is easy to see. The Victor is completely nude. In the 1920s, that was a serious problem for many citizens. The story told in Belgrade is that groups of residents, including some women's associations, objected to a naked male figure in the main square. Whether the protests alone decided the matter is hard to say. But the result is clear. The monument went up here, in the fortress, away from the busy shopping streets.

The second surprise is in the direction he faces. The Victor does not look toward the city behind him. He looks outward, across the rivers, toward Zemun and the wide plain to the north. Before the First World War, that land belonged to Austria-Hungary, and the border ran along these rivers. So the figure watches over the old frontier. Some visitors read his pose as a warning to the former enemy. Others see it as a guardian looking out for the city.

Take a moment to study the column itself. It is plain and tall, and it lifts the bronze figure high above the walkway. Because of this height, you almost never see the Victor at eye level. You look up at him, and the sky becomes his background. On a clear day, the dark bronze stands sharp against blue. On grey days, the figure looks heavier and more serious. The same monument can feel very different from one hour to the next.

What began as a war memorial slowly became something else. Over the decades, the Victor turned into one of the main symbols of Belgrade. You will see his outline on postcards, posters and souvenirs in the shops below. Many residents treat him the way Parisians treat their tower — as a landmark that simply means home. Couples meet here. Families bring visiting relatives. Students sit on the walls nearby and watch the light change over the water.

Look down from the edge of the viewpoint. Below you, the Sava River flows in from the left and joins the Danube. On a calm day, you can sometimes see a difference in the colour of the two rivers where they meet. Across the water lies Great War Island, a green and mostly wild stretch of land. Further out, the towers of New Belgrade rise on the far bank. Old fortress, quiet island, modern city — all in one view.

The area around the monument is good for a slow walk. Paths lead along the walls, through gates and down toward the lower part of the fortress near the river. You will pass old cannons, stone steps and small groups of chess players in the park. In the evening, many people gather near the Victor to watch the sunset. The sun often drops behind New Belgrade, and the rivers turn gold and then grey.

Before you leave, look up one more time. The bronze figure is still there, sword lowered, falcon held close, face turned to the north. He was too bold for the city square, so the city gave him the edge of its oldest hill instead. As you walk back through the park, listen again to the wind over the walls. It is the same wind that has crossed this ridge for centuries, and it still moves past the Victor every day.
