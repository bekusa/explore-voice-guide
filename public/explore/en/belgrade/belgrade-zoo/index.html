The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees of Kalemegdan Park, the city traffic fades into a softer layer of sound. You hear birds calling from behind wire, gravel moving under strollers, and children laughing near a fence. Somewhere above you, old stone walls rise against the sky. This is Belgrade Zoo, and it sits in an unusual place: inside the grounds of one of the oldest fortified sites in the Balkans.

Most zoos are built on empty land at the edge of a city. This one is different. It lies within the park that surrounds Belgrade Fortress, on the high ridge above the meeting of two great rivers, the Sava and the Danube. For centuries, armies fought over this ridge. Romans, Byzantines, Serbs, Hungarians, Ottomans and Austrians all held it at different times. Today, on part of the same ground, flamingos stand on one leg and big cats doze in the afternoon sun.

The zoo opened in 1936. Belgrade was then the capital of the Kingdom of Yugoslavia, a young state that was trying to look modern and confident. A public zoo was part of that picture. A city with museums, boulevards and a zoological garden looked like a European capital. The site in the fortress park made sense. It was central, green, and already a place where families came to walk on Sundays.

As you walk, look at the stone around you. In several places, the enclosures sit against old walls and earth ramparts from the fortress. The builders of the zoo used the slopes and the defensive lines that were already there. So the masonry next to an animal enclosure may be far older than the zoo itself. It is an easy detail to miss, but it changes how the place feels. Military history and animal life share the same ground here.

The zoo's early years were short and peaceful. Then came the Second World War. In April 1941, German aircraft bombed Belgrade, and the zoo was hit. Enclosures were damaged, and many animals died. Belgrade was bombed again later in the war. Accounts from that period describe animals loose in the streets around the park. Some of these stories have grown in the retelling, so it is fair to treat the details with care. But the damage was real, and the zoo had to rebuild almost from the beginning.

One survivor of those years became a legend in the city. His name was Muja, an American alligator who arrived in Belgrade in the late 1930s. He lived through the wartime bombing, and he went on to live here for decades after it. Over time he became one of the oldest alligators known to live in captivity anywhere in the world. Generations of Belgrade children grew up visiting him. For many locals, Muja was less an exhibit and more a fellow citizen.

Think about that for a moment. The same animal was here before the war, and still here when the grandchildren of those first visitors came with their own children. Kingdoms and governments changed. The country changed its name more than once. The city around the park was rebuilt and grew. And in a warm pool at the zoo, a quiet reptile simply continued.

After the war, the zoo slowly found its feet again. In the later decades of the twentieth century, it became closely linked with one long-serving director, Vuk Bojović. He promoted a gentle name for the place: the Garden of Good Hope. The name was a statement of attitude. It suggested a refuge, not only a display. It also matched the mood of a city that went through hard times in the 1990s, when sanctions and conflict made daily life difficult.

Those difficult years also shaped the zoo. Money for food and care was short. Local people helped, and the zoo became a small symbol of survival in a struggling city. Many residents still remember it this way. For them, a visit here is not only a family outing. It is a memory of keeping something kind and ordinary alive when the world outside felt uncertain.

One feature surprises many first-time visitors. Belgrade Zoo has long been known for its white animals. Over the years, its collection has included pale or white forms of species that are usually darker, such as lions and tigers. These animals carry a rare colour trait. Their fur looks almost like cream in soft light. They are a favourite with photographers and with children, who often stop and stare in disbelief.

Locals also enjoy stories of a more mischievous kind. A chimpanzee named Sami became famous in the city for slipping out of his enclosure more than once. Belgrade newspapers followed his adventures with clear affection. These stories are part of the zoo's popular memory. They show how people here think of the animals. They see them as personalities with names, habits and a sense of humour, not only as species on a sign.

The zoo is not large, and that is part of its character. Paths turn quickly, and the next enclosure is always close. Tall trees from the park give shade on hot summer days. In autumn, fallen leaves gather along the fences and the light turns gold through the branches. On weekdays the place is calm. At weekends it fills with families, school groups and grandparents with ice cream in their hands.

As you leave, take a few steps up toward the fortress walls. From the ridge, you can look out over the two rivers and the wide flat land to the north. Listen once more. Behind you, the calls of birds and animals mix with voices on the paths. In front of you, the city moves on as usual. Both belong to this hill, and both have survived a great deal to be here today.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a weekday morning for quieter paths.
Combine your visit with a walk around Belgrade Fortress.
Wear comfortable shoes for the sloping park paths.
Climb to the ridge afterwards for river views.
Bring water on summer days despite the shade.
