The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic on Gospodar Jevremova Street hums past, a tram bell rings somewhere downhill, and then, behind a low wall, there is a small garden and a quiet that feels almost deliberate. In front of you stands a compact stone building with a single dome and one slender minaret. It is modest in size. It does not compete with the city around it. And yet this small mosque carries one of the longest stories in Belgrade.

This is the Bajrakli Mosque. It is the only mosque still standing in Belgrade from the long centuries of Ottoman rule. That fact surprises many visitors. During the Ottoman period, the city had a great many mosques. Travellers of the time described a skyline full of minarets above the rivers. Today, only this one remains. When you look at it, you are looking at the last surviving piece of that older Belgrade skyline.

The mosque was built in the late sixteenth century, when Belgrade was an important Ottoman city on the frontier with Central Europe. It was founded as a charitable endowment. In the Islamic world, this was a common practice: a wealthy patron paid for a public building as an act of faith and service. Mosques, fountains, schools and baths across the Balkans began this way. This building was one of many such gifts to the city.

Now, the name. Bajrakli comes from the word bajrak, which means flag or banner. According to the most common explanation, a flag was raised on this mosque to tell the other mosques of the city when it was time to call the faithful to prayer. In other words, this building helped to keep time for the whole town. It is a lovely idea... a small stone building setting the rhythm of daily life for an entire city.

Look at the shape of the building. The plan is simple: a square prayer hall covered by a single dome. A slim minaret rises beside it, with a small balcony near the top. From that balcony, the muezzin — the man who calls Muslims to prayer — once sang the call by voice alone. This square-and-dome form is typical of smaller Ottoman mosques across the Balkans. There is no grand courtyard here, and no cluster of domes. The design is clear and quiet.

The neighbourhood around you has its own Ottoman roots. This district is called Dorćol. The name comes from Turkish words meaning four roads, or a crossroads. For centuries, this was a mixed quarter. Muslim, Orthodox Christian, Jewish and other families lived in these streets, close to the Danube. Merchants, craftsmen and boatmen passed through. Much of that old fabric is gone now, but the street pattern and the name still carry traces of it.

The mosque's own history was not calm. Belgrade changed hands several times between the Ottoman and Habsburg empires. During one period of Austrian rule in the eighteenth century, the building is said to have been used as a Catholic church. When the Ottomans returned, it became a mosque again. So the same walls have held two different faiths. That small detail tells you a great deal about the history of this frontier city.

In the nineteenth century, Serbia gained its independence step by step, and the Ottoman presence in Belgrade came to an end. Many mosques fell out of use. Some were abandoned, some were taken down, and others were lost to new street plans and modern buildings. The Bajrakli Mosque survived this period. It was repaired and kept in use for the Muslims who remained in the city. That is the main reason it is still here today.

This is not a museum. It is a working place of worship, and it still serves the Muslim community of Belgrade. On Fridays, at midday, people come for communal prayer. You may see men washing their hands and feet before they enter. You may hear the call to prayer drift over the rooftops. Treat the space with the same respect you would give any church or synagogue, and the experience becomes much richer.

The building carries scars from recent history as well. In March 2004, during unrest linked to violence in Kosovo, rioters attacked the mosque and set it on fire. The interior was badly damaged. Many people in Belgrade, from different backgrounds, spoke out against the attack. The mosque was later repaired and reopened. When you look closely at the walls and the windows, remember that this survival is not only old history. It is also recent.

If you are allowed inside, notice how the light changes. Small windows let in soft daylight, and the space under the dome feels calm and open. Carpets cover the floor, because worshippers pray close to the ground. On one wall, you will find the mihrab — a niche that shows the direction of Mecca. Nearby stands the minbar, a raised pulpit with steps, where the imam gives the Friday sermon.

Step back to the street for one more view. From here, you can see how closely the city has grown around the mosque. Apartment blocks, shops and cafés press in on every side. A short walk away, Gospodar Jevremova Street continues past old houses and small museums, and the green slopes of the Belgrade Fortress rise towards the rivers. The mosque is one quiet stop in a dense and layered part of the city.

Before you leave, look once more at the minaret against the sky. Think of the many other minarets that once stood above Belgrade, and of the flag that, according to tradition, once flew from this one to mark the hours of prayer. Today, the city keeps time with clocks and phones. But this small building is still here, still in use, and still part of daily life in Dorćol.

As you walk away, listen again. The traffic returns, the voices of the street return, and the garden behind you falls quiet once more. You have just stood beside the last mosque of Ottoman Belgrade. Carry that thought with you as you explore the old streets nearby... and notice how much of the past still sits, quietly, between the modern buildings.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees.
Remove your shoes before entering the prayer hall.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers.
Ask permission before photographing people inside.
Combine your visit with a walk through Dorćol.
