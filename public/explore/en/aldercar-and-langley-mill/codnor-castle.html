The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across an open hillside, sheep call from a nearby field, and somewhere below you a road hums faintly in the valley. Under your feet the grass is uneven, full of hidden bumps and hollows. Those bumps are not natural. They are the buried outlines of walls, rooms and ditches. Ahead of you, broken stone rises out of farmland. This is Codnor Castle, and its story starts long before the houses of Aldercar and Langley Mill filled the valley.

Stand still for a moment and look east. The ground falls away towards the River Erewash, which has long marked the border between Derbyshire and Nottinghamshire. The builders of this castle chose the ridge for good reason. From here, a lord could watch the valley, the roads and the movement of people below. A castle on a high place was a message as much as a defence. It told everyone in sight who held power over this land.

The stone castle you see today dates mainly from the thirteenth century. Historians believe an earlier timber stronghold may have stood here first, but the stone walls belong to a later age. For most of the medieval period, this was the seat of the Grey family. They held the title Lord Grey of Codnor, and for generations they served English kings in war and in government. This hilltop was their home, their estate office and their statement of rank.

The Greys were not a small local family. Members of the line fought in royal campaigns in Scotland and France, and they sat among the barons of the realm. A castle like this one had to host visitors, feed servants, store weapons and keep records. Picture the courtyard full of horses, carts and voices. Smoke rises from kitchen fires. Messengers arrive from distant places. Today you hear only wind, but this hillside once held a crowded working household.

Now look at the layout of the ruins. The castle was arranged in two main parts. There was an upper court, where the lord and his family lived, and a lower court, which held stables, stores and working buildings. The lower court wall is the most complete stretch you can see. Notice the rounded towers set along it. These towers let defenders look along the face of the wall, so attackers could not hide close beneath it.

Here is something many visitors miss. The castle was surrounded by a moat, and it was in that muddy ground that a small treasure came to light. In 2007, the archaeology television programme Time Team excavated here. During the dig, the team found a gold coin from the reign of King Henry the Fifth, the king of the Battle of Agincourt. Someone, perhaps six centuries ago, lost a small fortune in the mud. It is a very human moment, preserved by accident.

The male line of the Greys of Codnor ended in the late fifteenth century, with the death of the last baron, Henry Grey. After that, the estate passed through other families by inheritance and sale. Castles like this slowly lost their military purpose. By the seventeenth century, owners wanted comfortable houses, not draughty stone fortresses. Over time, Codnor was abandoned as a residence and allowed to decay. Much of its stone was taken away and reused in local buildings.

So here is a second surprise. Parts of Codnor Castle may still stand today, but not on this hill. Across the surrounding area, old farm buildings and walls likely contain dressed stone carried away from these ruins. It was a common fate for abandoned castles across England. People saw a ready supply of good, cut stone and put it to practical use. The castle did not vanish in one great collapse. It was carried away, one cartload at a time.

The title itself had an unusual afterlife. After the last medieval baron died, the barony of Grey of Codnor fell into abeyance, a legal state where a title sleeps because no single heir has a clear claim. It stayed that way for roughly five hundred years. Then, in 1989, the abeyance was ended and the title was revived for a descendant of the old family. A name tied to this hillside returned to the House of Lords after half a millennium.

The valley below also tells a newer story. In the nineteenth century, this part of the Erewash Valley became a landscape of coal mines, ironworks and canals. Codnor Park, just nearby, became known for industry rather than for medieval lords. Langley Mill grew around transport and trade. If you look out from the ruins, you see two ages side by side. One is the age of knights and barons. The other is the age of furnaces, pit heads and railways.

In recent years, local volunteers have worked to protect and study the site. A heritage trust has helped to record the ruins, encourage research and share the story with the community. The castle is a protected monument, and the remaining walls are fragile. Keep to the paths, and resist the urge to climb on any stonework. Every loose stone that falls is a piece of evidence lost for good. The ruins have survived this long partly because people cared.

Take a closer look at the stone itself. It is a local sandstone, weathered to soft browns, greys and warm yellows. Run your eye along the wall and you will see different textures. Some blocks are neatly squared. Others are rough rubble packed between them. Look for patches where the stone changes colour or style. These often mark later repairs or areas where original facing stone was stripped away. The wall carries traces of every century it has lived through.

As you prepare to leave, turn slowly in a full circle. Behind you are the broken towers of a family that once stood close to the throne. In front of you are fields, hedgerows and the roofs of valley towns. Listen again to the wind and the sheep. When you walk back down the hill, watch the old stone walls of nearby farms. You may be looking at a small piece of Codnor Castle, still doing useful work today.
