The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Outside, the Pacific keeps its slow rhythm against the shore, and the air carries salt and dust in equal measure. Underfoot is some of the driest ground on Earth, the edge of the Atacama Desert. This dryness is the reason you are here. In this soil, the people of the Chinchorro culture laid their dead to rest, and the desert kept them. What you are about to see is not a royal tomb. It is something far older, and far more personal.

The Chinchorro were fishers, hunters and gatherers. They lived along this coast of what is now northern Chile and southern Peru. They harpooned sea lions, gathered shellfish from the rocks and caught fish with hooks made from shell and cactus spine. They did not build pyramids or palaces. They lived in small groups close to the shoreline, where freshwater streams met the sea. And yet these modest fishing communities created one of the most remarkable funeral traditions in human history.

Here is the first surprise. The Chinchorro began to mummify their dead artificially around seven thousand years ago. That is roughly two thousand years before the ancient Egyptians developed their own famous methods. When most visitors think of mummies, they picture pharaohs wrapped in linen. But the oldest known artificial mummies in the world were not made beside the Nile. They were made here, beside this ocean, by people who lived from the sea.

The technique was careful and complex. Specialists took the body apart, removed the organs and often the skin. They then rebuilt the person. Sticks reinforced the bones and the spine. Reeds, plant fibre and other materials filled the body to give it shape. The skin was placed back over this frame, sometimes in pieces. Finally, the body was covered in a paste and painted. A face was modelled over the skull, often with a calm, simple mask.

Archaeologists describe several styles that changed over the centuries. In what they call the black mummies, the body was coated with a dark paint made with manganese. In the later red mummies, the body was painted with red ochre, a natural earth pigment. Other bodies were covered in a thick coat of mud. Some mummies wear wigs made of human hair. Each style shows a long tradition that people passed down and adapted over many generations.

Now the second surprise, and perhaps the most moving one. The Chinchorro did not reserve this care for powerful leaders. Men, women, the elderly and children all received this treatment. Many of the most careful mummies are of infants, and even of babies who were never born. In other ancient cultures, mummification often marked status and wealth. Here, it seems to have marked something closer to love, or to a refusal to let anyone go.

Why did the Chinchorro begin this practice? No one knows for certain. Researchers have proposed several ideas. One theory links the practice to the natural arsenic in some local water sources. Arsenic can cause miscarriages and harm to infants, and so the community may have faced many early deaths. According to this theory, mummification grew partly as a response to grief. It is a hypothesis, not a settled fact, but it gives the tradition a deeply human face.

The mummies also had a life after death. Some show signs of repair and repainting, as if people kept them for a time and looked after them. Some researchers think the bodies may have been displayed or kept within the community before burial. Again, this is interpretation, not certainty. But as you look at them, it becomes easy to imagine a family returning to the person they lost, touching up the paint, keeping their face whole.

Modern science began to study these burials in the early twentieth century, when the German archaeologist Max Uhle described them. Since then, many more have been found. Some appeared by accident, as builders dug foundations in Arica itself. In parts of this city, the past is almost literally under the pavement. Construction workers and residents have uncovered remains in their own neighbourhoods, and the city has had to learn how to live on top of its ancestors.

You can see this tradition in two main places. The Museo de San Miguel de Azapa, run by the University of Tarapacá, sits a short drive inland in the green Azapa Valley, among olive groves. It holds a large collection and explains the methods in detail. In the city, near the foot of the Morro de Arica, the large headland above the port, a small site museum shows burials where they were found, beneath a glass floor.

In 2021, UNESCO added the settlement and artificial mummification of the Chinchorro culture to its World Heritage List. This recognition brought new attention. It also brought new worries. These bodies survived thousands of years in dry earth, yet modern conditions threaten them. Scientists have reported that rising humidity on this coast helps microbes damage some of the mummies in museum collections. Conservators now work with careful climate control to protect what the desert once protected on its own.

As you move through the galleries, take your time. The rooms are often quiet and dim, kept that way to protect the remains. Look closely at the hands, the small clay faces, the threads of hair. Read the labels about the tools as well, the fishhooks and harpoon points. They remind you that these were ordinary people with ordinary lives. Their days were full of tides, nets and cold mornings when the coastal fog drifted in from the sea.

When you step back outside, notice the light on the dry hills and the grey line of the ocean beyond the city. This is the same coast the Chinchorro knew. The same salt wind moved across their camps. Thousands of years ago, someone stood near here, holding a child they had lost, and decided that the child would not simply disappear. Carry that thought with you as you walk on. The ground of Arica still holds their memory.

Avoid flash photography to help protect the fragile remains.
