The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the southern edge of Tehran, the city's traffic gives way to a wide, dry plain. Wind moves across open pavement. Somewhere a loudspeaker carries a recitation, the voice rising and falling. Then you look up and see the golden dome, bright against a pale sky, with four slender minarets standing at its corners. Underfoot the stone is warm in summer and cold in winter. You are standing at the resting place of the man who led Iran's 1979 revolution.

Ruhollah Khomeini was a Shia cleric. In Shia Islam, a senior cleric can hold great religious authority over his followers. He spent many years in exile, criticising the Shah, Iran's monarch. In early 1979 the monarchy fell, and Khomeini returned to Tehran. He became the founder and first Supreme Leader of the Islamic Republic. He held that role for about a decade. He died in June 1989, and work on this mausoleum began soon after his death.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The site is not random. It stands beside Behesht-e Zahra, the vast cemetery of southern Tehran. When Khomeini came back from exile in February 1979, he travelled from the airport to this cemetery. There he gave one of his first speeches on Iranian soil, among the graves of people killed during the protests. A decade later, he was buried close to that same ground. The beginning and the end of his public story sit side by side.

His funeral is remembered as one of the largest in modern history. Huge crowds filled the streets of Tehran. Accounts and film from the day show mourners pressing so close that the burial was very difficult to carry out. Many Iranians who lived through it still describe that week in strong, personal terms. That emotion helps explain the scale of what you see now. The mausoleum was built for crowds, not for a quiet family visit.

Now take in the shape of the complex. The central building is wide and low, with the golden dome at its heart. The four minarets frame it from a distance, so you can read the outline from far down the highway. Around it spread large courtyards and parking areas. The design follows a long Persian and Islamic tradition of shrine architecture, where a domed tomb chamber sits inside a larger enclosure for pilgrims.

And here is the second surprise. The mausoleum is still, in a sense, unfinished. Construction has continued for decades. You may notice scaffolding, cranes, or areas closed to the public. Plans for the wider complex have included religious schools, a library, and facilities for visitors. So you are not looking at a completed monument from the past. You are looking at a project that is still being built, and that keeps changing shape over the years.

Visitors enter through separate doors for men and women. Shoes come off before the main hall, and you leave them at a shoe counter. Women are expected to cover their hair, and a chador, the long cloak worn by many Iranian women, is often available to borrow. Inside, the floor is covered with carpets. The space is large and high, supported by tall columns, and lit by hanging lamps. The air is usually calm, with a low murmur of prayer.

At the centre stands the tomb itself, enclosed in a metal lattice. This kind of enclosure is called a zarih, and it is common at Shia shrines. Pilgrims often touch the lattice, pray beside it, or simply sit on the carpet nearby. Some people bring children. Some come alone and sit in silence. Watching how people behave here tells you as much as any plaque about what this place means to them.

Khomeini does not rest here alone. His son Ahmad, who worked closely with him, is buried within the complex. His wife was later buried here too. In 2017, the former president Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, one of the central figures of the revolution, was also buried at the mausoleum. Step by step, the shrine has become a burial place for the inner circle of the republic's founding generation, not only for its first leader.

The site has also seen violence. In June 2017, attackers struck the mausoleum on the same day as an attack on Iran's parliament. The group known as Islamic State claimed responsibility. Security here is visible as a result. You will likely pass through checks at the entrance, and guards watch the halls. It is worth keeping this history in mind. It explains the careful atmosphere, and why staff may ask questions about bags or cameras.

It is also honest to say that Khomeini's legacy is contested. For many Iranians, he is a revered religious and national figure, and this is a place of pilgrimage. For others, inside Iran and abroad, his rule is linked to repression, executions, and a long war with Iraq. Both views exist, often within the same families. As a visitor, you do not need to settle that debate. Simply notice the many different people who come through these doors.

The busiest day of the year is the anniversary of his death in early June. Large official ceremonies take place, and crowds travel from across the country. On ordinary days the mood is very different. The courtyards can feel almost empty. Families picnic in shaded corners. Pilgrims arriving from distant provinces rest after long journeys. The contrast between those two moods, the packed anniversary and the quiet weekday, is part of the place's character.

Before you leave, walk back out into the open courtyard. Turn and look at the dome once more. Notice how the light changes on the gold as clouds pass. Listen to the wind, the distant highway, perhaps a recitation drifting from inside. Beyond the walls, the graves of Behesht-e Zahra stretch away across the plain. Take a moment there, between the shrine and the cemetery, and think about how a single life can still shape a whole country.

Before you go

Practical tips

Women should bring a headscarf; chadors are often lent.
Expect security checks and bag screening at the entrance.
Ask staff before photographing inside the main hall.
Avoid the June death anniversary unless you want crowds.
Wear shoes that are easy to slip off and on.
