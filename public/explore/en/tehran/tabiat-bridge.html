The guide

The story

Feel the boards under your feet. They are wood, laid over steel, and they give back a soft, hollow sound with every step. Below you, traffic moves in a steady roar. Around you, people walk slowly, talk, and lean on the railings. In northern Tehran the air often carries a dry edge from the mountains. Look up, and on a clear day those mountains fill the horizon.

This is Tabiat Bridge. In Persian, the word tabiat means nature. The name fits, because the bridge was built to join two pieces of green across a gap of concrete. On one side is Taleghani Park. On the other is Ab-o-Atash Park, whose name means water and fire. Between them runs the Modares Expressway, one of the city's major roads.

For years, these two parks sat on opposite sides of a river of cars. Crossing was difficult, and the expressway cut the hillside in two. The city decided to build a pedestrian bridge to reconnect the slope. But the bridge that rose here in the 2010s is not a simple walkway. It is a place that people visit for its own sake, often with no plan to reach the other side.

Here is the first surprise. The bridge was designed by a young architect named Leila Araghian, who was still in her twenties when her design won the competition. She worked with the firm Diba Tensile Architecture. A project of this scale, in a city of many millions, went to someone near the start of her career. The bridge opened in 2014, and it quickly became one of Tehran's favourite public spaces.

Now look at the structure itself. The bridge does not run in a straight line. It curves gently as it crosses, so the view changes as you walk. The supports below are not plain columns. They branch outward like the trunks and limbs of trees. That is a deliberate choice. The bridge borrows the form of the thing it was named for, and the steel spreads out the way a canopy does.

The bridge has more than one level. Paths split, climb, and drop, and you can choose your route across. Some routes are wide and open to the sky. Others pass beneath upper decks, so you walk through shade and then step back into light. Araghian has said she wanted people to stop and stay on the bridge, not just pass over it. The many paths make that easy. There is no single correct way to cross.

Why does that matter? Many footbridges are designed only for speed. You climb, you cross, you descend. Here, the idea was the opposite. The benches, the corners, and the changing levels invite you to slow down. On an evening walk, you will see families with children, groups of students, and couples standing close at the rail. In a large and busy capital, a calm public space like this has real value.

The second surprise is international. In 2016, Tabiat Bridge received the Aga Khan Award for Architecture. This is a respected prize that honours buildings serving communities in societies with a large Muslim population. The award recognised not only the engineering, but the way the bridge created a shared space for ordinary people. For a pedestrian bridge over a highway, that is unusual recognition.

Let your eyes travel north. The Alborz mountains rise behind the city, and in winter their peaks often carry snow. On a clear morning, the ridges look close enough to touch. On hazier days, they fade into a pale blue outline. Turn south, and the city spreads out below you, with towers and rooftops stretching into the distance. The bridge sits on a slope, so it works as a viewing platform in both directions.

The parks at each end are worth your time. Ab-o-Atash Park, the water and fire park, has fountains and features built around those two elements. On warm evenings, children run between the jets of water. Taleghani Park is greener and quieter, with trees and paths that climb the hillside. Walking from one park to the other across the bridge is the best way to understand why the bridge exists at all.

The lower level of the bridge holds spaces for cafés and restaurants. This adds another layer of life. People sit and eat with traffic moving below them, sheltered by the steel and wood above. In the evening, the smell of tea and food mixes with the cool mountain air. It is a good place to rest before you continue your walk, and a good place to watch the city.

The bridge changes completely after dark. Lights trace the lines of the structure and make the tree-like supports stand out against the night. Many residents come at this hour, when the heat of the day has passed. The expressway below becomes a stream of headlights and red tail lights. From the upper deck, that moving light looks almost calm, and the noise feels further away than it really is.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the railing. Feel the wood under your hands, worn smooth by many visitors. Listen to the traffic below and the voices around you. Look at the branching steel holding you above the road. This bridge was built to connect two parks, but it also connects people, who come here simply to walk, to talk, and to look at their city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come on a clear morning for the best mountain views.
Return after dark to see the bridge lit up.
Walk between both parks to understand the bridge's purpose.
Try different levels and paths on separate crossings.
Rest at a lower-level café before heading back.
