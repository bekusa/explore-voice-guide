The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the high walls, Tehran's traffic hums and honks without pause. In here, the loudest sound is often water, sliding along a long, shallow pool between plane trees and flower beds. Then lift your eyes. Every wall around this garden is covered in tiles. Yellow, rose pink, deep blue, leaf green. Painted flowers, birds, hunters on horseback, even European-style buildings. You are standing inside Golestan Palace, the old royal heart of Iran's capital.

The name Golestan means something like "the rose garden" or "the place of flowers" in Persian. The garden you see is only a fragment of what once stood here. This site began as a walled citadel, called the Arg, built in the Safavid era. The Safavids were the dynasty that ruled Iran from the early sixteenth century. At that time, Tehran was a modest town, far from the great Safavid capitals of Qazvin and Isfahan.

Everything changed in the late eighteenth century. Agha Mohammad Khan, founder of the Qajar dynasty, chose Tehran as his capital. This citadel became the seat of royal power. For more than a century, the Qajar shahs lived here, held court here, and received foreign ambassadors here. Each ruler added halls, terraces, and gardens. The complex grew into a small city of its own, with harems, offices, stables, and treasuries behind its walls.

One of the oldest surviving pieces is easy to miss. Look for a small, intimate building known as the Karim Khani Nook. It dates back to Karim Khan Zand, a ruler from the Zand dynasty who held power just before the Qajars. Inside is a little marble basin, once fed by running water. Locals say the later shah Naser al-Din liked to sit here and smoke his water pipe in the quiet. It is a private corner, not a ceremonial one.

Now find the Marble Throne terrace. It is a wide, open hall facing the garden, with tall twisted columns and a mirrored back wall. At its centre stands a throne carved from yellow marble. Small carved figures support its platform. This throne was made for Fath Ali Shah, the second Qajar king, in the early nineteenth century. In 1926, Reza Shah, founder of the Pahlavi dynasty, was crowned on this same throne. The stone has seen two very different eras of Iranian kingship.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look closely at the mirrorwork above and around the throne. Thousands of small pieces of mirror are set into the ceilings and walls in geometric patterns. This craft is called ayeneh-kari, which simply means mirror work. Legend has it that it began when large mirrors broke on the long journey from Europe, and Persian craftsmen used the pieces instead. Whether or not that story is true, the result catches every movement of light.

The Qajar ruler who shaped this palace most was Naser al-Din Shah. He reigned for almost fifty years in the second half of the nineteenth century. He travelled to Europe several times, and he kept diaries of what he saw. He returned with new ideas about architecture, gardens, and the image of a modern king. You can see that influence across the complex. Persian tilework meets European windows, clocks, and chandeliers.

The clearest example rises above you: the Shams-ol-Emareh, the Edifice of the Sun. It is a tall building with two slender towers and a clock set into its upper facade. When it was built under Naser al-Din Shah, it was one of the tallest structures in Tehran. The idea was simple. The shah wanted a view over his city, much as he had seen in European capitals. Notice how the arches stack in layers, like a wedding cake of tiles.

Inside the halls, the palace changes mood. Gilded ceilings, crystal chandeliers, and painted panels fill the rooms. The Hall of Mirrors is the most dazzling, its walls covered almost entirely in reflective glass. Other halls hold European porcelain, clocks, and gifts sent by foreign rulers. Mixed among them are Persian carpets, lacquer boxes, and portraits of Qajar kings. These shahs are often shown with very long black beards, jewelled belts, and narrow waists.

A second surprise: Naser al-Din Shah was one of the earliest royal photographers in the world. He was fascinated by the camera and took many pictures himself, including portraits of his court and his household. The palace keeps a large historic photograph collection from this era. These images are now a key record of nineteenth-century Iran. When you look at the painted portraits here, remember that the same king was also standing behind a lens.

Not every story here ends well. Much of the original citadel no longer exists. In the twentieth century, under the Pahlavi dynasty, large parts of the complex were demolished to make room for new government buildings and streets. What survives is the core around this garden. Still, the palace kept its symbolic role. The last shah, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi, held his coronation here in 1967. After that, the complex became mainly a museum.

In 2013, UNESCO added Golestan Palace to the World Heritage List. The listing recognises it as a key example of Qajar art and architecture. It shows how Persian craft traditions absorbed European ideas in the nineteenth century. Experts point to the tilework, the mirror halls, and the garden layout as the most important elements. The tiles here are often seven-colour tiles, called haft-rang, painted before firing. This method allowed larger pictures across many tiles.

The palace also sits at the edge of old Tehran. Just to the south lies the Grand Bazaar, with its covered lanes, spice sellers, and carpet merchants. In Qajar times, the court and the market were close neighbours. Merchants, officials, and messengers moved between them every day. When you leave, you step from royal calm straight back into the noise of the city. That contrast was part of life here for centuries.

Before you go, take one last slow walk along the pool. Watch how the tiled walls reflect in the water, broken by the wind. Notice the small details: a painted bird, a crack in an old tile, a pigeon resting on a column. Each piece was set by hand. Listen again for the water. Then, when you are ready, follow the sound of the street back into Tehran.
