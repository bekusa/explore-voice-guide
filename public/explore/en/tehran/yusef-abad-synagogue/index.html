The guide

The story

Listen first. The traffic of central Tehran hums a few streets away, and motorbikes weave between plane trees. Then a gate closes behind you, and the sound drops. Inside, you may hear something very different: soft Hebrew prayer, a murmured response, the shuffle of a prayer book being opened. This is the Yusef Abad Synagogue, and that change in sound is the first thing worth noticing here.

You are standing in one of the busiest Jewish houses of worship in Iran. Many visitors arrive surprised that such a place exists at all. It does, and it is not a museum. Families pray here, children learn here, and people meet friends after services. Everything you see is in daily use. That is the quiet surprise of this building... it is not a relic of the past but a working part of the present city.

To understand why it matters, step back for a moment, much further than this building. Jews have lived on the Iranian plateau for roughly two and a half thousand years. Their story here reaches back to the Babylonian exile, when Cyrus the Great, the Persian king, allowed exiled Jews to return to Jerusalem. Many chose to stay in Persian lands instead. Their descendants became one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities anywhere in the world.

Over the centuries, those communities grew in cities such as Hamadan, Isfahan, Shiraz and Kashan. In Hamadan, a shrine long associated with Esther and Mordecai, the heroes of the biblical Book of Esther, still draws pilgrims. Persian Jews developed their own traditions, their own melodies for prayer, and even their own dialects. They spoke Persian at home and in the market, and they kept Hebrew for prayer and study. You may hear both languages around you today.

Tehran came later to this story. It only became the capital at the end of the eighteenth century. As the city grew through the twentieth century, Jewish families moved here from older provincial towns. They looked for work, schools and new opportunities. Some settled first in the old Jewish quarter in the south of the city. Later, many families moved north into newer residential districts, and Yusef Abad was one of them.

So the neighbourhood around you tells part of the story. Yusef Abad grew in the twentieth century as a district of apartment blocks, shops and tree-lined streets. It is a middle-class area, practical rather than grand. The synagogue belongs to that modern city. It does not have the ancient stones of a medieval shrine. Instead, it carries the shape of a community that adapted, moved and rebuilt itself in a changing capital.

Now look at the space itself. A synagogue is built around a few central elements, and you can find them here. There is the ark, a cabinet that holds the Torah scrolls, the handwritten Hebrew scriptures. There is a raised platform from which the scrolls are read aloud. And there is usually a lamp that stays lit, a reminder of the lamp that once burned in the ancient Temple in Jerusalem.

Notice the direction of prayer. In synagogues around the world, worshippers face toward Jerusalem. From Tehran, that means facing roughly west. The ark is placed on that side of the hall so that the whole room turns in one direction during prayer. It is a simple detail, but it connects this room in Tehran to Jewish communities in Morocco, Ukraine or New York, all of them turned toward the same city.

Here is a second detail many visitors miss. The life of this place follows the Jewish calendar, not the Iranian working week. The Jewish Sabbath runs from Friday evening to Saturday evening. In Iran, Friday is the main weekly day of rest, and Saturday is an ordinary working day. So Jewish families here balance two rhythms at once, one set by the state and one set by their faith.

The community also has a place in national public life. Under Iran's constitution, Jews are recognised as a religious minority, and they elect their own representative to the national parliament. The community is much smaller than it was in the middle of the twentieth century, because many families emigrated in the decades after 1979. Even so, it remains one of the largest Jewish communities in the Middle East outside Israel.

If you visit during a service or a holiday, the building changes character. On ordinary days it can feel calm and almost empty. On festivals, the hall fills with families, and children move between the rows. At Passover, the spring festival of freedom, and at Purim, which celebrates the story of Esther, the mood becomes lively. Purim feels especially close to home here, because the Book of Esther is set in the Persian royal court.

A few words on being a good guest. This is an active place of worship, so dress modestly and keep your voice low. Expect security checks at the entrance, and carry identification. Ask before you take any photograph, especially of people at prayer. Men may be asked to cover their heads. If you are unsure about anything, simply ask. People here are often glad to explain their traditions to a respectful visitor.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Notice the light in the hall, the worn edges of the prayer books, the Hebrew letters you may not be able to read. Then step back out into Yusef Abad. Listen as the traffic returns, the shopkeepers call out, and the plane trees move in the wind. You have just walked between two worlds that share the same street, and the same long history.
