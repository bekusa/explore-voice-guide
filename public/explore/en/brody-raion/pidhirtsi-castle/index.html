The guide

The story

Gravel crunches underfoot as you climb the slope toward the gate. Grass covers the old earthen ramparts on either side, and the wind comes across the open fields of Lviv region. Ahead, a long pale façade rises on its hill. It looks less like a fortress than a country palace. That gap between the defensive walls and the elegant house behind them explains this whole place.

You are standing at Pidhirtsi Castle, in the countryside near Brody. It was built in the first half of the seventeenth century, roughly between the 1630s and 1640. This was a dangerous frontier then. The lands of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth bordered the steppe, and raids by Tatar horsemen were a real and regular threat. A noble who wanted to live in comfort here also needed walls, ditches and cannon.

The man who ordered it was Stanisław Koniecpolski, the Grand Crown Hetman. The hetman was the commander of the army. He spent his career fighting on these southern and eastern borders, so he understood the risks better than almost anyone. The design is often attributed to an Italian military engineer, Andrea dell'Aqua. Some historians also connect it with the French engineer Guillaume Le Vasseur de Beauplan. Both men worked in the region at the time.

Architects call this type of building a palazzo in fortezza. That means a palace inside a fortress. Look at the plan around you. A residential block sits at the back of a courtyard. Earthen ramparts with angled bastions surround it. A bastion is a pointed projection in a wall. It lets defenders fire along the walls and leaves no blind spots for attackers. Here the bastions protected a house built for receptions, music and art.

Now turn and look down the slope. In front of the palace, terraces step down toward the fields. This was once a formal garden in the Italian style. It had geometric beds, paths and a clear axis running from the house into the landscape. Much of the original planting is gone today. But the shape of the terraces still shows the idea. The owner wanted guests to see order and power stretching out from his windows.

Here is the first surprise. This palace once belonged to a king. Later in the seventeenth century the estate passed to Jan the Third Sobieski. He was the king of Poland famous for helping to break the Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1683. So the rooms behind this façade connect to one of the major military stories of early modern Europe. Sobieski had family ties to the Koniecpolskis, and the property moved within this circle of powerful families.

In the eighteenth century the estate came to the Rzewuski family. Wacław Rzewuski, a magnate and writer, changed its character. He made it less a military post and more an aristocratic residence. He filled the interiors with paintings, furniture and decoration. Under the Rzewuskis, Pidhirtsi became known as a place of collections. Visitors came for its galleries as much as for its views.

As you walk around the grounds, look for the large domed church nearby. This is the Church of Saint Joseph. It was built in the eighteenth century under Rzewuski patronage. It has a classical portico with columns at the front. Statues once stood along its roofline. The church and the palace were planned to work together, as one ensemble on the hill. Together they formed the centre of the estate.

The second surprise is a ghost story. Please take it as folklore, not history. Locals tell the legend of a White Lady who walks the halls at night. In one popular version, she is a young wife of a Rzewuski owner. Her jealous husband is said to have walled her up inside the palace. There is no historical evidence for this. But the story has been repeated for generations, and many guides still tell it with a smile.

In the nineteenth century the estate passed to the Sanguszko princes. The twentieth century then brought war, again and again. The collections were moved, scattered and partly lost during the world wars. After the Second World War, under Soviet rule, the building became a tuberculosis sanatorium. In 1956 a serious fire swept through the palace. It burned for a long time and destroyed much of the remaining interior decoration.

So what you see now is a building in recovery. Since the late twentieth century, the castle has been in the care of the Lviv National Art Gallery. Restoration has been slow and partial. Some rooms are repaired. Others still show bare brick and empty walls. This is part of what makes a visit here honest. You see a grand residence and also the damage that the last century did to it.

The castle has also had a second life on screen. Its gate, courtyard and façade have appeared in a number of historical films. The best known is the Soviet adaptation of The Three Musketeers from the 1970s. The scenery here could easily stand in for a French château. It is a good reminder of how European this frontier palace was in its taste and design.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the terraces for a moment. Look back at the long façade and the line of windows. Then look out at the flat, open fields. Four centuries ago, a lookout here would have scanned that same horizon for riders. Today you will probably hear only birds, wind and perhaps a car on the country road. As you walk back down the slope, notice the ramparts again. Grass covers them now — but they still hold the shape of a frontier that once needed them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full rampart circuit to understand the bastion layout.
Visit the nearby Church of Saint Joseph as part of the ensemble.
Wear sturdy shoes for grass slopes and uneven gravel paths.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the façade.
Combine the trip with Olesko and Zolochiv castles nearby.
