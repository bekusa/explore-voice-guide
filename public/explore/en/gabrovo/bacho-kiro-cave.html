The guide

The story

Cool air reaches you before the darkness does. At the mouth of the cave, the warmth of the Dryanovo valley simply stops. The air turns damp and still, and it carries a faint mineral smell, like wet stone after rain. Underfoot, the path is slick in places. Somewhere ahead, water drips slowly and steadily into a pool you cannot yet see.

You are standing at Bacho Kiro Cave, in the green limestone country of Gabrovo Province, close to the town of Dryanovo in central Bulgaria. Outside, the land falls into a narrow gorge. Forest climbs the cliffs on both sides. Close by stands the Dryanovo Monastery, and the cave and the monastery share both this valley and its story. Before we go deeper, it helps to know whose name the cave carries.

Bacho Kiro was a teacher, a writer and a patriot from this region in the nineteenth century. In Bulgarian, "Bacho" is a respectful word for an older man, close to "uncle". In 1876, Bulgarians rose against Ottoman rule in what history calls the April Uprising. Rebels took shelter at the Dryanovo Monastery, and Ottoman forces besieged it for several days. Bacho Kiro was among the fighters. He was captured and later executed. Today the cave carries his name as a memorial to that sacrifice.

The cave itself is far older than any human story. The river and the underground water slowly dissolved the soft limestone over a very long time, and they carved a branching system of passages and halls. Cavers have mapped several kilometres of tunnels on more than one level. Visitors walk only a part of this network, on a lit route with steps and handrails. The deeper sections stay dark and quiet, and only specialists enter them.

As your eyes adjust, look at the walls. The stone is not one colour. It moves from cream to rust to a dark, wet grey, depending on the minerals in the water that ran across it. Over thousands of years, dripping water left calcite behind. The calcite grew into stalactites that hang from the ceiling and stalagmites that rise from the floor. Where the two meet, they form columns. Guides often point out shapes that locals compare to figures and animals, so let your imagination help you.

Here is the first surprise. This cave is one of the most important archaeological sites in Europe. In 1938, the British archaeologist Dorothy Garrod excavated here with Bulgarian colleagues. She was one of the great prehistorians of her generation. Later, in the 1970s, a joint Polish and Bulgarian team returned. They studied stone tools so distinctive that archaeologists gave them their own name — the Bachokirian.

The biggest discovery came more recently. In 2020, an international team published results in the scientific journal Nature. They had found fragments of human bone and teeth in the cave sediments. Genetic analysis and radiocarbon dating showed that the bones belonged to our own species, Homo sapiens. These people lived here roughly forty-five thousand years ago. That makes them some of the earliest modern humans known anywhere in Europe.

Pause on that for a moment. Neanderthals still lived across the continent when these people sheltered in this valley. The researchers also found pendants made from cave bear teeth, along with stone blades and the bones of hunted animals. Some of the people here carried traces of Neanderthal ancestry in their DNA, from mixing a few generations earlier. In other words, the meeting of two human species is written into the bones found in this ground.

Now listen. Away from the voices of other visitors, the cave is almost silent. You hear the drip of water, your own footsteps and the soft echo of your breath. The temperature stays cool and steady through the whole year. In summer, a light jacket feels welcome after a few minutes. In winter, the cave can feel almost mild compared to the frozen valley outside.

You share this cave with other residents. Bats roost in its quieter corners. They are protected, and they play an important role in the forest ecology outside, because they hunt insects over the river at night. If you notice small dark shapes high on the ceiling, do not shine a bright light on them. In the dim zones near the entrance, the air also carries the faint, earthy smell of a living cave.

The cave has welcomed visitors for a long time. Its tourist route was first prepared in the late 1930s, which makes it one of the earliest caves in Bulgaria opened to the public. Since then, generations of Bulgarian school groups have walked these same halls. For many families in the region, a trip here is a childhood memory, often paired with a visit to the monastery and a picnic beside the river.

When you step outside again, take a few minutes in the gorge. The Dryanovo Monastery stands only a short walk away, with its white walls and its church. A small ossuary there holds the remains of rebels from 1876. Trails climb from the valley floor along the cliffs. From above, you can see how the river cut through the rock, and you can understand how the cave formed under your feet.

So, as you leave, notice the change once more. The cool, still air gives way to warmth, birdsong and the sound of running water. Behind you, the darkness holds forty-five thousand years of human life, a revolutionary's name and stone that is still slowly growing, one drop at a time. Look back at the entrance before you walk on. Few doorways in Europe open onto so deep a past.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a light jacket because the cave stays cool year-round.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp, slippery paths.
Combine your visit with the nearby Dryanovo Monastery.
Walk a gorge trail afterward for views over the valley.
Avoid shining bright lights at roosting bats.
