The guide

The story

Listen first. The ferry engine drops to a low hum, and gulls call over the grey-green water of Cork Harbour. Behind you, the painted houses of Cobh climb the hill in rows of pink, blue and yellow. Ahead, a low green island rises from the sea, and its top is cut flat by sloping grass walls. This is Spike Island. From the water it looks quiet, almost gentle. That impression does not last long once you step ashore.

The island sits near the mouth of one of the largest natural harbours in the world. That position explains almost everything that happened here. Any ship entering Cork Harbour had to pass close by. So for centuries, whoever held Spike Island could watch the harbour and defend it. Soldiers, monks, prisoners and guards all lived on this small piece of land. Each group left something behind, and you can still see much of it today.

The oldest story is a religious one. Tradition links the island to an early Christian monastery, said to have been founded by Saint Mochuda, a seventh-century Irish monk. Very little physical trace of that early settlement survives above ground. So treat it as the island's first chapter, half history and half memory. Even so, it is worth picturing. Long before cannons, a small community may have prayed here, with the same wind coming off the same water.

Now look at the walls around you. The great fort that crowns the island was built by the British in the early nineteenth century. It follows the star-shaped design used across Europe at the time. That shape has a purpose. The pointed bastions, which are the angled corners, let defenders fire along the walls from the side. No attacker could hide at the foot of the defences. The fort was first named Fort Westmoreland, after a British official.

Here is the first surprise. Much of this fort was finished by prisoners. In 1847, during the Great Famine, the island became a convict depot. Hunger and poverty drove many people to petty crime in those years. Some were convicted for stealing food. Thousands of men passed through Spike Island. Many of them broke stone, dug the deep ditch, and shaped the ramparts you are walking on now. The walls that held them were, in part, built by their own hands.

Life inside was hard. Prisoners slept in crowded rooms, and disease spread easily. Many were held here before transportation, which meant being shipped to the Australian penal colonies, often for life. Others never left the island at all. There is a burial ground on Spike Island, and many of the graves are unmarked. If you find it on your walk, take a moment there. It is one of the quietest places on the island.

One famous prisoner gave the fort its later name. John Mitchel was a writer and political activist in the Young Ireland movement, a nationalist group of the 1840s. After his conviction in 1848, he was held on Spike Island before transportation. Later, after Irish independence, the fort was renamed Fort Mitchel in his honour. A fortress built to hold Irish rebels now carries the name of one of them.

The island's story turns again in the twentieth century. During the Irish War of Independence, in the early 1920s, the British held republican prisoners here. Some of them managed to escape, which became part of local legend. Then came the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921. Under that agreement, Britain kept control of three Irish harbours, known as the Treaty Ports. Spike Island was one of them. British soldiers stayed here even after the new Irish state was born.

That changed in 1938. Britain handed the Treaty Ports back to Ireland, and the Irish flag was raised over Spike Island. For many people in Cobh, it was an emotional day. The last foreign garrison was leaving Irish soil. The fort then became a base for the Irish Defence Forces. Soldiers trained here, kept the guns ready, and watched the harbour through the uncertain years of the Second World War.

The second surprise comes much later. In 1985, Spike Island became a prison again. Within months, a serious riot broke out, and parts of the buildings were badly damaged by fire. The prison continued to operate until 2004. So the cells you can visit are not only Victorian relics. Some of them held prisoners within living memory. People in Cobh still remember the boats carrying guards to work each morning.

After the prison closed, the island opened to visitors as a heritage site. Today you can walk through cell blocks, punishment cells and soldiers' quarters. Some rooms are bare, with peeling paint and heavy iron doors. Others hold exhibitions about the people who lived here. Notice how small the solitary cells are. Stand inside one for a moment, and imagine the door closing. The silence feels very different from the open sky outside.

When you are ready, climb up onto the ramparts. From the top, the whole harbour opens around you. To the north, Cobh rises above the water, with the tall spire of its cathedral above the rooftops. Nearby is Haulbowline Island, home to the Irish naval service. Large ships still move slowly through the channel below. Cobh itself was once called Queenstown, and it was the last port of call for the Titanic in 1912.

Take one last look around before you walk back to the pier. The grass on the ramparts moves in the wind. Gulls circle over the ditch that prisoners dug by hand. Monks, soldiers, convicts and guards all stood where you are standing. As the ferry pulls away, watch the island grow smaller. From the water, it looks quiet and gentle again. Now you know what those green walls hold.

Before you go

Practical tips

Reach the island by ferry from Cobh's waterfront.
Book the ferry crossing in advance during summer.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed ramparts.
Wear sturdy shoes for grass slopes and uneven paths.
Allow time in Cobh before or after your visit.
