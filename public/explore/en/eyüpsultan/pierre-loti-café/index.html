The guide

The story

The air up here tastes different — thinner, sharper, laced with wood smoke and the faint salt of the Golden Horn far below. You are standing at one of Istanbul's highest perches, a hilltop café where the city spreads out like a living map, minarets and domes and the glittering water stitching Europe and Asia together. This is Pierre Loti, named for a French naval officer who fell hard for Istanbul in the eighteen-eighties and never quite recovered.

Louis Marie-Julien Viaud — that was his real name — served in the Ottoman navy and wrote novels under the pen name Pierre Loti. He spent long afternoons at a café somewhere near this spot, watching the city, nursing his melancholy, penning stories soaked in longing. The café you see today is not the original timber pavilion where Loti sat, but it occupies the same ridge in Eyüp, the same commanding view, the same role in the neighbourhood's rhythm. Locals have been coming to this hilltop for tea and conversation since Ottoman times, long before Loti arrived and long after he left.

The approach matters. Most visitors ride the cable car — a short, steep climb that swings you over the cemetery below, rows of Ottoman gravestones tilting under plane trees. Walking up is slower, a winding path through one of Istanbul's oldest burial grounds, where sultans and poets and ordinary families rest under carved marble. The gravestones lean at odd angles, roots nudging them askew over centuries. By the time you reach the top, you have earned the view.

Inside, the café feels like a terrace suspended in air. Low tables, cushioned benches, the clink of tea glasses, the hiss of a samovar. Waiters move through the crowd with trays of Turkish tea in tulip-shaped glasses, apple tea for tourists, occasionally a plate of simit or baklava. The décor leans into nostalgia — old photographs of Eyüp, framed calligraphy, brass lanterns — but the real attraction is the panorama. The Golden Horn curves below, ferries stitching back and forth, the old city rising on the opposite shore with Süleymaniye Mosque anchoring the skyline.

Loti's novels are mostly forgotten now outside France, but his name stuck to this place. He wrote about Istanbul with the fevered romanticism of an outsider in love, describing the city as a dream he could never fully enter. His most famous Turkish novel, Aziyade, tells the story of a doomed affair with a woman in a harem — whether it was fiction or memoir, no one knows for sure. The book scandalized Paris and charmed Istanbul, cementing Loti's reputation as a man who lived between worlds.

The café draws a mixed crowd. Older men come in the mornings, settling into corners with newspapers and backgammon boards. Couples arrive in the afternoons, leaning into the view, phones out. Tourists cluster near the railing at sunset, when the light turns the Bosphorus to hammered gold and the call to prayer drifts up from the mosques below. It is not a quiet place, but it is not frantic either — more a steady hum, a place where the city pauses to look at itself.

Eyüp itself is one of Istanbul's holiest neighbourhoods, built around the tomb of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, a companion of the Prophet Muhammad. Pilgrims have been coming here for centuries, and the district retains a quieter, more conservative character than the tourist centres across the water. The café sits just above the tomb complex, so the hilltop feels like a threshold — sacred ground below, secular vista above, the city holding both at once.

Come in the late afternoon if you can. The morning light is clean and sharp, good for photography, but the real magic happens as the sun drops and the city softens. The ferries trace white lines across the Golden Horn, the domes catch the last rays, the air cools and the tea tastes better. You will hear a dozen languages around you, see locals and visitors doing the same thing Loti did more than a century ago — sitting still, looking out, trying to hold the city in their gaze for just a moment longer.

When you leave, walk back down through the cemetery if you have time. The gravestones glow in the dusk, and the path feels older, quieter, like stepping back through layers of the city's long memory. The café will still be there when you are gone, the view unchanged, the tea still steaming, the city still unfolding below.
