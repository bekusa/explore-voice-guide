The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water laps against stone canal walls. A bicycle bell rings somewhere behind you. The air carries salt from the bay, and the brick under your feet has that slightly uneven feel of an old Dutch street. Look up, and the gabled rooftops stand in neat rows against the sky. Then you remember where you are. This is not Amsterdam. This is the western edge of Kyushu, in Japan, a short ride from the city of Sasebo.

Welcome to Huis Ten Bosch. The name is Dutch, and it means house in the woods. It comes from a real royal palace in The Hague, one of the official residences of the Dutch royal family. Here, on the shore of Omura Bay, that name was borrowed for something far larger than a single house. It is an entire Dutch town, built from the ground up, with canals, windmills, squares, hotels and a palace of its own.

Why the Netherlands, and why here? The answer lies about an hour's drive to the south, in Nagasaki. During the Edo period, when Japan's rulers sealed the country off from most of the world, the Dutch were the only Europeans allowed to trade. They lived on Dejima, a small artificial island in Nagasaki harbour. For roughly two centuries, much of what Japan learned about Western science, medicine and books came through that one narrow gate. This region remembers the Dutch with real affection.

The resort opened in 1992. It grew out of an earlier, smaller Dutch theme park in the same prefecture, and its founder dreamed bigger the second time. He did not want a painted film set. He wanted a working town, with real brick, real water, and buildings solid enough to last. The land itself had been a stretch of reclaimed coast, and the canals were dug and planted to bring it back to life.

Walk along any canal and look closely at the facades. Each house is a little different. Some have stepped gables, rising in small stairs to a point. Others have bell-shaped tops or simple sloping roofs. This variety is the whole idea. Real Dutch streets grew house by house over hundreds of years, and the designers here tried to copy that patient, uneven rhythm rather than repeat one shape down the line.

Now find the tall tower. It rises above the rooftops and is modelled on the Domtoren, the great medieval church tower of Utrecht. In the Dutch city, that tower has stood for centuries as a landmark visible across the flat land. Here it does the same job. It helps you find your way, and from its upper level you can see the whole town laid out below, with the canals curving toward the water of the bay.

The palace is the heart of the story. Its exterior follows the Huis ten Bosch Palace in The Hague, and it was built with the permission of the Dutch royal family. That detail surprises many visitors. This is not an unofficial copy. Behind it lies a formal garden of clipped hedges and geometric beds, laid out in the Baroque style of the seventeenth century, when European gardens were designed as ordered patterns meant to be seen from above.

The resort's road has not always been smooth. Building a whole town is expensive, and visitor numbers did not always match the dream. In 2003, the company behind it went bankrupt. For several years, its future looked uncertain. Then, around 2010, the Japanese travel company H.I.S. took over and began to change its course, adding events, shows and seasonal festivals that gave people new reasons to return again and again.

One of those changes made headlines around the world. In 2015, a hotel opened here staffed largely by robots. It is called Henn na Hotel, which can be translated as the strange hotel. Guests were greeted at the front desk by robotic receptionists, including, at one point, a robot dinosaur. Guinness World Records recognised it as the first hotel of its kind. Over time some robots were retired, but the idea stayed: a Dutch town hiding a test lab for the future.

The seasons shape this place more than most visitors expect. In spring, the tulips arrive, filling flower beds in solid stripes of red, yellow and purple beneath the windmills. Summer brings long evenings on the canals. In autumn the light turns softer. And in winter, the whole town is wrapped in millions of small lights, an event the resort calls the Kingdom of Light, when the brick streets glow gold after dark.

If you want a quieter moment, step onto one of the canal boats. From the water, the town looks different. You see the arched bridges from underneath, the reflections of gables in the still surface, the moored boats and the ducks that ignore all of them. The boats move slowly, and the low engine hum mixes with the creak of rope and the voices of people on the banks above.

In more recent years, ownership changed hands again. In 2022, the resort was sold to PAG, an investment firm based in Hong Kong. Plans for new attractions and upgrades have followed. So the town you are walking through is still changing. Some corners feel like a calm European village, while others are full of rides, music and screens. Both sides are part of what Huis Ten Bosch has become over three decades.

Take a moment to notice the smaller things. The cheese shops with their round yellow wheels. The smell of waffles or fried potatoes drifting from a stall. The sound of a barrel organ playing in a square. Many staff greet you in Japanese, then in another language, and the shop signs mix Dutch words with Japanese script. These overlaps are the real character of the place: two cultures, joined by four hundred years of memory.

Before you move on, stand still by the nearest canal. Watch the water carry the reflection of a gable, a cloud, perhaps a windmill turning slowly. Somewhere beyond the brick and the bridges lies the open bay, and beyond that, the old harbour of Nagasaki, where this whole friendship began. As you walk away, listen for the bells and the lapping water. They will follow you for a few more steps.
