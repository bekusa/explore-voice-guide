The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere nearby, hooves click softly on the path, and a deer lowers its head to nose at the grass. The air smells of pine and damp gravel. In front of you stands a pale stone building with tall windows and classical columns. It looks almost French, here in the heart of Japan's oldest capital. This is the Nara National Museum, and it holds some of the most important Buddhist art in the country.

That Western facade is your first surprise. The original building was designed by Katayama Tokuma, an architect who later worked on palaces for the imperial family. The museum opened in 1895, when Japan was eagerly adopting European styles of building. So a temple city of wooden halls and curved tiled roofs received a gallery of carved stone and symmetry. Today that first building is protected as an Important Cultural Property of Japan.

To understand why a museum like this belongs in Nara, you need one date. In 710, the imperial court moved its capital here, to a city called Heijō-kyō. For most of the eighth century, Nara was the political and religious centre of Japan. Buddhism had arrived from the Asian mainland some time earlier, and the court supported it with enormous energy. Temples rose across the city. Sculptors, painters, and monks gathered here, and their work filled the halls around you.

Many of those temples still stand within walking distance. Tōdai-ji, with its giant bronze Buddha, sits just to the north. Kōfuku-ji and its pagoda rise to the west. Kasuga Taisha, a Shinto shrine lined with stone lanterns, lies up the hill to the east. The museum is surrounded by the very places that produced much of its collection. Some objects travelled only a few hundred metres to reach these galleries.

The museum's great specialty is Buddhist sculpture. Inside the old building, statues stand in calm rows under soft light. You will see figures of the Buddha seated in meditation, gentle bodhisattvas, and fierce guardian kings with clenched fists and bulging eyes. A bodhisattva, in Buddhist belief, is an enlightened being who delays final peace in order to help others. Notice how their faces stay serene while the guardians seem ready to step off their pedestals.

Walk slowly, and you can follow a long story in wood, bronze, and lacquer. The earliest pieces come from the Asuka period, the seventh century, when Japanese sculpture still followed models from Korea and China closely. Later figures, from the Kamakura period of the twelfth to fourteenth centuries, feel much more alive. Their robes fold heavily. Their eyes sometimes shine with inlaid crystal. Look closely and the change in style is clear, even to a beginner.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. A large number of the statues are not owned by the museum at all. Temples and shrines around Nara lend them for safekeeping and display. In a sense, the museum is a shared house for sacred objects. Some pieces remain objects of worship, even inside a gallery. You may notice a quiet visitor who stops, places their palms together, and bows before moving on.

The museum grew in the twentieth century. Newer wings were added in the 1970s, built in a modern style that sits lower and more quietly beside the original hall. These spaces host changing exhibitions of paintings, calligraphy, sutras, and archaeological finds. A sutra is a Buddhist scripture, and some here were copied by hand with remarkable care, line after even line in black or gold ink. An underground passage links the old and new buildings.

Now, the second surprise. Every autumn, the museum hosts an exhibition that draws long queues of visitors from across Japan. It shows treasures from the Shōsōin, an ancient wooden storehouse at Tōdai-ji. After the death of Emperor Shōmu in the eighth century, his widow, Empress Kōmyō, donated his personal treasures to the temple. Many of those objects have survived for well over a thousand years.

The Shōsōin collection includes musical instruments, mirrors, textiles, glassware, and game boards. Some items came along the trade routes of the Silk Road, from Persia, Central Asia, and Tang dynasty China. Imagine an emperor in eighth-century Nara holding a cup made far away in western Asia. Only a selection is shown each year, and the pieces rotate. A visitor who comes every autumn can see different treasures each time.

In early spring, the museum often turns its attention to Omizutori, a water-drawing ceremony held at Tōdai-ji. Monks perform rituals at night, and burning torches swing along the balcony of a hillside hall. The museum displays objects connected with this ancient rite. It is a good example of how the collection connects to living practice, and not only to the distant past. The rituals continue just up the road.

When you step back outside, take a moment in the grounds. The lawns are open, and the deer move freely between the trees and the buildings. They are considered sacred messengers in local tradition, and they are protected. Many have learned to bow for the deer crackers sold nearby. Keep your bag closed and your paper tickets in your pocket. The deer are curious, and they will eat almost anything.

Look once more at the old hall. Its stone is pale and calm, and its windows reflect the trees of the park. Behind those walls, figures carved more than a thousand years ago keep their quiet watch. As you walk on toward the temples, listen again for the soft click of hooves on gravel. You are walking the same ground where many of these treasures were first made, and first prayed before.

Practical tips

Keep paper tickets and maps away from curious deer.

Allow time to walk slowly through the sculpture galleries.
