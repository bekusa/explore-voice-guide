The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the wind is often the loudest thing you will hear. It moves across low grass and grey lichen, over pale granite and still, cold water. There are almost no trees to catch it. The ground underfoot is springy in places, soft with moss, and then suddenly hard with bare rock. The sky feels very wide. Welcome to Hardangervidda, the great mountain plateau that rises above Eidfjord and stretches far to the east and south.

Hardangervidda is often described as the largest mountain plateau in Europe. The whole plateau covers thousands of square kilometres. The national park protects a large part of it, and was established in 1981, which makes it the largest national park in mainland Norway. Several municipalities share this land, and Eidfjord is one of the main western gateways. From the fjord below, the road climbs steeply. In less than an hour, you leave salt water and fruit orchards behind and arrive in a landscape that looks almost Arctic.

That contrast is the first surprise for many visitors. Down in Eidfjord, the air can feel mild and green. Up here, at more than a thousand metres above the sea, the climate is much harsher. Snow can stay on the ground well into summer. Small lakes hold ice when the valleys below are already warm. Plants and animals that normally live much farther north find a home on this plateau. In a sense, Hardangervidda is a piece of the far north, lifted up into southern Norway.

Look at the shape of the land around you. It is not jagged like the Alps. Instead, it rolls gently, with wide basins, rounded hills and countless lakes and streams. This is very old ground. Over a very long time, erosion wore the ancient mountains down into a broad, uneven surface. Then the ice ages came. Huge glaciers covered the plateau, scraped the rock, and left behind boulders, gravel ridges and hollows. When the ice melted, water filled those hollows. That is why you see so many lakes, scattered across the plateau like broken glass.

On a clear day, you may notice a few higher shapes on the horizon. To the north is Hardangerjøkulen, a glacier that sits like a white cap on the edge of the plateau. Some people also look for Hårteigen, a steep, flat-topped block of rock that rises sharply above the surrounding land. It is easy to recognise because nothing else looks quite like it. These landmarks have helped travellers find their way for generations, long before maps and phones.

Now, think about the animals that live here. Hardangervidda is home to the largest herd of wild reindeer in Europe. These are not the tame reindeer that herders keep in the far north. They are wild animals, and they are shy. They move across the plateau with the seasons, searching for lichen in winter and fresh grass in summer. If you see a herd, stay well back and keep quiet. Disturbing them uses up energy they need to survive the long winter.

People followed those reindeer for thousands of years. Archaeologists have found traces of Stone Age hunting camps on the plateau, often near lakes and along migration routes. Hunters also built stone walls to guide reindeer, and dug pits to trap them. Some of these old structures still survive, low and covered with lichen, and they are easy to miss. In a landscape that looks untouched, the human story is actually very long. It is simply written in quiet stone, close to the ground.

The plateau also has a dramatic chapter from the Second World War. During the German occupation of Norway, a small group of Norwegian commandos hid on Hardangervidda through the brutal winter of 1942 and 1943. They survived in hard conditions, partly by hunting reindeer. Their goal was the heavy water plant at Vemork, on the southeastern side of the plateau. That plant was linked to German nuclear research. The sabotage mission later became one of the most famous resistance stories in Norway.

Here is a lighter surprise. The northern edge of the plateau, near the glacier and the mountain station at Finse, was used as a filming location for the ice planet Hoth in The Empire Strikes Back. The film crew needed a frozen world, and this region gave them one. The famous Bergen Railway also runs along the northern edge, crossing high and exposed ground on its way between Oslo and Bergen. Even today, winter storms can make travel here difficult.

If you come from the Eidfjord side, you will likely pass through Måbødalen, a deep and narrow valley with steep rock walls. The road twists through tunnels and tight bends as it climbs. Near the top is Vøringsfossen, one of the best-known waterfalls in Norway. Its water drops into a dark gorge, and on many days, you can feel the cold spray in the air. It is a striking doorway between the sheltered fjord and the open plateau above.

Many visitors also stop at the Hardangervidda Nature Centre in Eidfjord. It is a good place to learn about the plateau before you go walking. Inside, exhibits explain the geology, the wildlife, the weather and the long history of people on the plateau. It helps you read the land when you are back outside. A small stone or a patch of lichen becomes much more interesting when you know what it tells you about this place.

If you plan to walk, respect the weather. On Hardangervidda, conditions can change very quickly. A calm, sunny morning can turn into wind, fog and cold rain within an hour. Paths are often marked with stones or cairns, sometimes with a red letter T painted on rock. That T marks routes linked to the Norwegian Trekking Association, which runs a network of cabins across the plateau. Bring warm layers, a map, food and water, even for a short walk.

Before you leave, take one more slow look around you. Notice the colours. There is grey rock, rust-brown heather, pale green lichen, and dark blue water. Listen again to the wind, and perhaps to running water somewhere close by. Feel how cool the air is on your face. Hardangervidda does not demand your attention with one single sight. It asks you to slow down. And as you walk back toward the road, or down toward the fjord, you may find that the quiet of the plateau stays with you for a while.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack warm, waterproof layers even on sunny summer days.
Visit the Nature Centre in Eidfjord before hiking.
Follow routes marked with cairns and red T signs.
Keep well back and quiet if you spot reindeer.
Carry a paper map; phone signal can be patchy.
