The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Underneath the voices and the footsteps, there is a soft, steady sound of moving water. Warm air rises from a wide green pool, and on a cool morning it curls upward in pale threads of steam. The stone under your feet is worn smooth by many visitors. There is a faint mineral smell here, a little like warm metal. You are standing at the heart of Bath, in a place where hot water has come out of the ground for thousands of years.

That water is the reason any of this exists. Bath sits on the only natural hot springs of this kind in Britain. Rain falls on the hills to the south, sinks deep into the earth, and is heated far below the surface. Then it pushes back up through cracks in the limestone. Geologists believe the water rising here today fell as rain thousands of years ago. It reaches the surface at roughly forty-six degrees Celsius — hot enough to steam on a winter day.

Long before the Romans arrived, local people treated this spring as sacred. The Celtic tribes of the area linked it to a goddess called Sulis. There is also a well-loved local legend. It says a prince named Bladud caught leprosy and was sent away from court. He worked as a swineherd, saw his pigs roll in the warm mud, and was cured when he followed them. It is a folk tale, not history — but Bath has told it for centuries.

The Romans came to Britain in the first century, and they understood the value of this place immediately. They built a temple and a bathing complex around the spring. They named the town Aquae Sulis, which means the waters of Sulis. In a clever move, they did not replace the local goddess. They joined her with their own goddess Minerva, the Roman goddess of wisdom. The result was a shared deity, Sulis Minerva, honoured by Britons and Romans together.

Now look at the large pool in front of you. This is the Great Bath, the centrepiece of the complex. Its floor is still lined with sheets of lead that the Romans laid down nearly two thousand years ago. The steps around the edge led bathers gently into the warm water. In Roman times, this hall had a high roof over it. The roof is long gone, and sunlight now reaches the water. That light feeds algae, and the algae give the pool its deep green colour.

Here is something many visitors miss. The figures standing along the terrace above the pool look Roman, but they are not. The statues of emperors and governors, and the balustrade itself, were added in the Victorian era, in the late nineteenth century. Everything below that level is the genuine Roman work. So when you look up from the water, you are seeing two eras at once. The ancient town sits several metres below the modern street.

For a Roman visitor, the Great Bath was only one part of the experience. Around it were rooms of different temperatures. There were warm rooms, hot rooms heated by air flowing under the floors, and cold plunge pools to close the routine. You can still see the low stacks of tiles that held up those heated floors. A trip to the baths was social as well as practical. People came to wash, to talk, to do business, and to rest.

The spring itself was holy, and that changed how people behaved here. Visitors threw offerings into the Sacred Spring to ask the goddess for help. Archaeologists have recovered thousands of Roman coins from the water. They also found something far more personal — small sheets of lead and pewter scratched with messages. These are known as curse tablets. Around 130 of them have been recovered, and they give us the voices of ordinary people in Roman Britain.

The curse tablets are wonderfully human. Most of them ask the goddess to punish a thief. Someone has stolen a cloak, a pair of gloves, or a few coins, and the writer wants revenge. Some tablets list possible suspects by name. Some ask for the culprit to lose sleep or health until the item is returned. Many were written backwards or in a kind of code. Reading them today, you sense the frustration of someone who lost a favourite cloak at the baths.

In the museum, look for the gilded bronze head of Sulis Minerva. It was found by workmen digging a sewer trench in 1727, long before anyone understood how much lay under the city. Look also for the great carved head from the temple pediment, the triangular stone panel above its entrance. In classical myth, the Gorgon was a female monster. But this face has a beard and wild hair, and it looks distinctly male. Scholars still discuss what it was meant to show.

After Roman rule in Britain ended in the fifth century, the complex slowly fell into ruin. Mud and rubble covered the old buildings, and the town grew on top. The hot water kept flowing, and people still bathed in later baths built nearby. In the eighteenth century, Bath became a fashionable spa for wealthy society. The elegant Pump Room next door comes from that era. Only in the Victorian period did serious excavation uncover the Roman remains you see today.

One practical note. The water in these pools is not treated, so visitors are not allowed to touch it or bathe in it. If you want to try the famous spa water, you can taste it at a fountain in the Pump Room. Many people find the flavour surprising — warm, and strongly mineral. If you want to bathe in Bath's hot water, a modern spa elsewhere in the city uses the same springs. The ancient pools here are for looking, not swimming.

As you finish, stand by the edge of the Great Bath once more. Watch the water move, the light on the green surface, and the steam lifting into the air. Above you rises the tower of Bath Abbey, built many centuries after the Romans left. Around you are Roman stones, Victorian statues, and Georgian streets, all stacked on the same spot. And beneath them all, the spring keeps rising, as warm today as it was two thousand years ago.
