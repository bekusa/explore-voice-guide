The guide

The story

Put your hand on the wall beside you. The stone is rough, porous and rust-red, almost like a dried sponge. This is laterite, the iron-rich rock of the Deccan plateau. It is soft when first cut and it hardens in the open air. Nearly everything around you was carved out of this ground and then built back up from it. Bidar Fort grew out of the plateau it stands on, and you can feel that the moment you touch it.

You are standing in the old heart of Bidar, a city in the northeastern corner of Karnataka in southern India. Today it feels like a quiet district town. For a long stretch of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, though, this hilltop was a royal capital. Ambassadors, scholars, poets and soldiers passed through these gates. Merchants arrived from Persia and Central Asia. The fort was the seat of power for much of the Deccan, the high plateau that spans the middle of India.

The story begins with the Bahmani Sultanate, a Muslim kingdom that ruled large parts of the Deccan. Its first capital was at Gulbarga, to the southwest. In the 1420s, Sultan Ahmad Shah Wali Bahmani moved the capital here to Bidar. Historians usually point to the high plateau, the cooler air and the strong natural defences as his reasons. Much of the fortress you see grew from that decision, and later rulers kept adding to it.

Look at how the defences are layered. Instead of a single wall, the builders combined ramparts, bastions and a series of moats. Those moats were cut straight into the laterite rock. In places there are three parallel ditches, one behind another. Imagine an attacking army reaching the first ditch, then finding two more behind it. The fort used the geology of the plateau as a weapon. The same rock that gave the builders their stone also gave them their trenches.

As you walk in, you pass through a sequence of gateways rather than one grand entrance. One of them, often called the Sharza Darwaza, carries carved figures of tigers or lions on its facade. The approach bends and turns. That was deliberate. A winding route slowed down attackers and exposed them to defenders on the walls above. Notice how your own pace slows as you move through. The architecture still controls how people enter, centuries after the last siege.

Here is something many visitors miss. Beneath the plateau runs an ancient water system called a karez. It is a technique that came from Persia, where it is known as a qanat. Builders dug a chain of vertical shafts and joined them with a tunnel underground. Groundwater then flowed gently downhill by gravity, with no pumps at all. In a dry region, that hidden water made a large city possible. Some of these shafts can still be seen around Bidar today.

Inside the walls, one of the oldest buildings is the Solah Khamba Mosque. The name means 'sixteen pillars', after the row of columns along its front. It dates from the early Bahmani period and is counted among the earliest Islamic buildings in Karnataka. The style is plain and solid, with a heavy dome. Stand in front of the arches for a moment. The rhythm of the columns creates a steady beat of light and shadow across the floor.

Now for the colour. The Rangin Mahal, which means 'coloured palace', is the jewel of the fort's interiors. It is associated mainly with the Barid Shahi rulers, a dynasty that held Bidar after Bahmani power faded. Inside, look for glazed tiles and wooden carving. Above all, look for the panels of black stone inlaid with mother-of-pearl. The shell catches the light and shimmers against the dark background. It is delicate work, and you do not expect it inside such a tough military fortress.

That inlay connects to a craft that still carries this city's name. Bidri ware is a metalwork tradition from Bidar. Artisans cast objects in a dark alloy based mostly on zinc. Then they inlay patterns of silver into the surface. Finally, the metal is blackened with a treatment that traditionally uses soil from the local area. Some say the soil from inside this fort works best. Treat that as local lore, but the craft itself is real and still practised in the town.

The fort also holds the remains of other palaces and halls. Their names include the Gagan Mahal, the Tarkash Mahal and the Takht Mahal, which means 'throne palace'. Many are now roofless shells. Arches open to the sky and grass grows where courtiers once stood. The ruins are still rewarding. Without roofs, you can read the plan of each building more clearly. You can see where rooms met, where light came in, and how the spaces linked together.

Power changed hands here more than once. After the Bahmanis, the Barid Shahi dynasty ruled from Bidar. In 1656, the Mughal army under the prince Aurangzeb, who later became emperor, captured the city. Later still, Bidar came under the Nizams of Hyderabad. Each new ruler repaired, rebuilt or neglected different parts of the fort. So the walls around you carry layers from several centuries and several courts, one on top of another.

If you have time after the fort, the town has more to offer. The madrasa of Mahmud Gawan, a fifteenth-century college founded by a powerful Bahmani minister, stands in the old city. Part of it has collapsed, but traces of coloured tilework survive on its surface. Outside town, at Ashtur, the domed tombs of Bahmani sultans rise from the open fields. Together with the fort, they show how wide and ambitious this capital once was.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot on the ramparts and look out. The plateau drops away and the land spreads wide below you. You can see why a sultan chose this height. Listen for a moment. Birds call in the ruins, and wind moves through the old arches. As you walk back out through the turning gates, notice the red stone under your feet. It is the same rock the fort was cut from, and the same ground the city still stands on.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven laterite paths and steps.
Visit early morning to explore the ruins before the heat.
Carry water since shade is limited across the open grounds.
Pair the fort with Mahmud Gawan's madrasa in town.
Ask local shops to show you how Bidri inlay is made.
