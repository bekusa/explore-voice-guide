The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the canopy, the air feels thick and green. Water drips from leaves long after the last rain, and somewhere a bird calls from a branch you cannot see. The path under your feet is often damp, and the smell is of wet soil, moss and old bark. This is Kebun Raya Bogor, the Bogor Botanical Gardens, and it is one of the oldest botanical gardens in Asia.

The gardens were founded in 1817, while Java was under Dutch colonial rule. The man behind the project was Caspar Georg Carl Reinwardt, a German-born botanist working for the Dutch government. His idea was practical as well as scientific. He wanted a place to collect, study and grow the plants of the region. He also wanted to test which crops could grow well in these islands. Two centuries later, that mix of science and usefulness still shapes the place.

The city around you also had a different name then. The Dutch called it Buitenzorg, which means roughly "without worry" or "carefree." It was a cool retreat from the heat of Batavia, the colonial capital that is now Jakarta. Look across the lawns toward the large white building with columns. That is the Bogor Palace, once the residence of Dutch governors-general. Today it is one of the official palaces of the President of Indonesia. The gardens began, in a sense, as the palace's backyard.

Near the palace side, watch the grass beyond the fence. Herds of spotted deer often graze there, quiet and unbothered by visitors. They are a small surprise for many people, who come for plants and find deer instead. The view of the white palace, the green lawn and the grazing herd is one of the classic pictures of Bogor. Nearby, ponds hold lotus and water lilies, and their broad leaves float flat on the still water.

Now think about scale. The gardens cover a large area, close to ninety hectares, in the middle of a busy city. Thousands of plant species grow here, from towering trees to small ferns. The collection is arranged with science in mind. Many plants carry labels with their Latin names, and plants from the same family often grow near each other. So a walk here is also a walk through a living catalogue of tropical plant life.

Here is the first surprise. Some of the most important history in this garden is not in a museum. It is in the soil. In 1848, a few young oil palms arrived here from West Africa, by way of Europe. They were planted as an experiment. Their descendants helped start the oil palm plantations that now cover huge parts of Indonesia and Malaysia. A single small planting in this garden is linked to one of the largest agricultural industries in the region, with all of its economic weight and environmental controversy.

The gardens also became a centre of serious science. In the late nineteenth century, laboratories here welcomed researchers from Europe and beyond. Botanists came to study tropical plants in the field, not as dried specimens in a cold northern museum. The garden's great collection of pressed plants, known as the Herbarium Bogoriense, grew from this work. Over time it became one of the most important plant archives in Southeast Asia. Much of that scientific work has since moved to newer facilities outside the old grounds.

Now find your way, if you can, to the small classical monument with columns and a dome. This is the memorial to Olivia Mariamne Raffles, the first wife of Thomas Stamford Raffles. Raffles governed Java during a short period of British rule in the early nineteenth century, before he went on to found modern Singapore. Olivia died in Java in 1814, and Raffles had this memorial built in her honour. Inside, a plaque carries a poem written for her. It is a quiet, personal corner in a garden made for science.

The second surprise is the strangest plant in the collection. Bogor is linked to two giants of the plant world. One is Rafflesia arnoldii, which has the largest single flower known. The other is the titan arum, Amorphophallus titanum, a towering flower that smells of rotting meat when it blooms. Locals often call it the corpse flower. Both are rare and bloom only for short periods, so you may not see one in flower. When one does bloom, crowds gather, holding their noses and taking photographs.

As you walk, notice the tall trees along the main avenues. Their trunks are straight and pale, and some rise high above the path. Many large trees here have buttress roots, thick wooden fins that spread out from the base. These roots help the trees stand firm in soft, wet tropical soil. Children often like to stand between the fins as if inside a wooden room. Look up as well. Climbing plants, ferns and orchids grow high on the branches, using the trees as a ladder toward the light.

Water moves through the garden too. The Ciliwung River runs along the edge of the grounds, brown and fast after heavy rain. Bogor is often called the City of Rain, and with good reason. Afternoon storms are common here, and they can arrive quickly. When the rain comes, the garden changes. The noise of water on leaves becomes a steady roar, and the colours of the foliage turn darker and richer. Then the sun returns, and steam rises gently from the paths.

The garden is not only for scientists and tourists. On weekends, local families spread mats on the lawns and share food. Students sit in groups under the trees. Couples walk slowly along the shaded paths. School groups move in long lines behind their teachers, wearing matching uniforms. For many people in Bogor and Jakarta, this is simply their park, a green place to breathe away from traffic. The garden carries two centuries of colonial and scientific history, but it also belongs to ordinary Sunday afternoons.

Before you leave, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Look at one tree closely, at the bark, the moss and the small insects moving across it. That tree may be older than anyone you know. Listen again to the drip of water and the distant hum of the city beyond the walls. As you walk back toward the gate, notice how the air grows warmer and the noise grows louder, step by step... and how much of the green you carry out with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to walk before heat and afternoon rain.
Carry a compact umbrella; Bogor storms arrive fast.
Wear shoes with grip for damp, mossy paths.
Ask staff whether a corpse flower is blooming.
Visit on a weekday for quieter shaded avenues.
