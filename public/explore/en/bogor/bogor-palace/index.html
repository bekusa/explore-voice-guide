The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the iron fence, a deer snorts and shakes its head. Traffic hums along the road behind you, motorbikes and green minibuses weaving past. The air is heavy and damp, and it smells of cut grass and wet earth. In front of you, a long white building sits low on a wide lawn, its columns pale against the deep green of old trees. This is Bogor Palace, known in Indonesian as Istana Bogor, and it has been a seat of power for more than two and a half centuries.

Start with the name of the town itself. Under Dutch colonial rule, this place was called Buitenzorg. In Dutch, that means something close to without worries, or free from care. The name tells you what the colonial elite wanted from it. Batavia, the old name for Jakarta, sat on the hot, humid coast, where disease was a constant fear. Up here, in the cooler foothills below the volcanoes, officials could rest, breathe easier, and escape the port city for a while.

The first building on this site went up in the mid-eighteenth century. It was ordered by Gustaaf Willem van Imhoff, a Governor-General of the Dutch East India Company, as a country retreat. Over the following decades, later governors expanded and remodelled it. Then, in 1834, a strong earthquake badly damaged the structure. The rebuilding that followed changed its shape. The new palace was designed with a single main storey, a sensible choice in a land where the ground can move without warning.

Now look at the building itself. The style is European and classical, with a central block, a columned portico, and long symmetrical wings stretching out to each side. The white walls and the triangular pediment over the entrance follow the language of Greek and Roman temples, which European architects borrowed widely in that era. But notice how the building sits. It is wide rather than tall, with deep shade under its roofs. It is a European idea, adapted to a tropical climate and a restless earth.

Here is a story many visitors never hear. Between 1811 and 1816, during the Napoleonic Wars, the British briefly took control of Java. The man in charge was Thomas Stamford Raffles, who later founded modern Singapore. He used this palace as a residence. His first wife, Olivia Mariamne, died here in 1814. A small memorial to her still stands in the botanical gardens next door. So this quiet Dutch retreat is also part of a British story, one that is easy to forget.

Now, the deer. You have probably already seen them, grazing on the lawn or resting under the trees. These spotted deer are not native to Java. They were brought in during the colonial period, and the herd has lived on the palace grounds for generations. Today there are many hundreds of them. Watch how calm they are. They have grown up with traffic, crowds, and cameras, and they barely lift their heads. For many local families, the deer are the main reason to stop here.

Along the fence, you will often find small vendors selling bundles of carrots or long beans. Local people buy them and push them through the bars to the deer. Children squeal when a deer comes close and takes a carrot from their fingers. It is one of the simplest pleasures in Bogor, and it costs very little. On weekends, the pavement here fills with families, couples, and groups of students. It is a city ritual as much as a tourist stop.

Behind and around the palace lies the Bogor Botanical Gardens, known locally as Kebun Raya Bogor. The gardens were formally founded in 1817, during the early years after Dutch rule returned. They grew into one of the most important centres for tropical plant research in the world. Seeds and specimens passed through here on their way across the colonial empire. The palace and the gardens were planned to work together. The building looks out over landscape, and the landscape frames the building.

In 1945, Indonesia declared its independence, and after years of struggle, the colonial era ended. The palace changed hands with history. It became one of several presidential palaces across the country. The first president, Sukarno, was especially fond of it and spent a great deal of time here. He was a passionate collector of art, and the palace still holds a large collection of paintings and sculptures gathered during his years in office. Many of these works are by Indonesian artists.

This is still a working state building, not a museum frozen in time. Presidents have received foreign leaders here and held cabinet meetings in its rooms. In 1994, leaders of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, known as APEC, met in Bogor. The agreement they reached became known as the Bogor Declaration, a commitment to freer trade across the region. More recently, President Joko Widodo chose to live and work here during his time in office, which brought new attention to the palace.

Because it is an active presidential palace, entry is restricted. You cannot simply walk in through the gates. Guided visits to the interior are sometimes possible, but they usually need to be arranged in advance, and rules change often. There are also occasional open days when the public is invited inside. For most visitors, though, the experience happens right here, at the fence, looking across the lawn. And honestly, this view is a fine way to understand the place.

One more thing about this town. Bogor is often called the City of Rain. Afternoon storms are common here, and they can arrive quickly. The clouds build over the mountains, the light turns grey and soft, and then the rain comes down hard on the leaves. That rain is part of why the gardens are so green, and why the colonial officials loved this cooler air. If you get caught in a shower, take shelter and watch. The deer usually do not seem to mind.

Before you leave, take one last look across the lawn. Follow the line of white columns. Notice how the trees stand taller than the roof, and how the deer move slowly between them. A Dutch governor, a British administrator, and the presidents of a free Indonesia have all looked out from those windows onto this same green space. Now it is your turn to look back. As you walk on, listen for the rain on the leaves... and the quiet sound of grazing deer.
