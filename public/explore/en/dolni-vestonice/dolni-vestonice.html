The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves through the vineyards on the slopes, and somewhere below, water lies flat and silver in the great reservoirs. Above you, pale limestone cliffs rise from the green. The soil underfoot is fine and yellowish, almost like flour when it is dry. That soil matters more than anything else you can see here. It is called loess, a dust carried by wind during the Ice Age, and it has kept some of the oldest human secrets in Europe.

This is Dolní Věstonice, a small wine village in South Moravia, in the southeast of the Czech Republic. It sits at the foot of the Pavlov Hills, also known as Pálava. Today it looks quiet and ordinary. There are cellars, a church, cyclists, and rows of vines. But beneath these fields, archaeologists found the traces of a camp where people lived roughly twenty-five to thirty thousand years ago, during the coldest stretch of the last Ice Age.

Imagine the view without the vineyards or the reservoirs. The landscape was open, cold steppe, closer to tundra than to forest. Herds of mammoth, reindeer, and horse moved through the valley below. The Pavlov Hills formed a natural landmark, and the river corridor made a route for animals and people alike. The hunters who camped here chose the spot well. From these slopes they could watch the valley and follow the herds.

Archaeologists link these people to a culture called the Gravettian. The name simply comes from a site in France where similar tools were first described. Gravettian groups lived across much of Europe, and they are known for carved figures, personal ornaments, and careful burials. At this site, the people were often called mammoth hunters. Large quantities of mammoth bone were found in the settlement layers, used as food, as fuel, and as building material.

Now for the discovery that made this village known to scientists across the world. In 1925, during excavations led by the Moravian archaeologist Karel Absolon, workers uncovered a small female figure. It was found broken in two pieces among ash and charcoal. The figure has heavy breasts, wide hips, and a simple head with slits for eyes. Today it is called the Venus of Dolní Věstonice.

Here is the first surprise. The Venus is not carved from stone or ivory, like most Ice Age figures. It is made of fired clay. People here mixed local loess with water, shaped it, and baked it in heat. That makes it one of the oldest known ceramic objects in the world. It was made many thousands of years before anyone used clay to make pots for cooking or storage.

And the clay figures were not unique. Excavators found many fragments of baked clay animals and figures at the site, together with traces of hearths that may have been used like simple kilns. Many of the fragments are cracked in a way that suggests they were placed in the fire while still wet. Some researchers think this was deliberate. The bursting and cracking may have been part of a ritual, though nobody can say for sure what it meant.

The second surprise is very human. When scientists studied the Venus closely, they found a fingerprint pressed into the clay before it was fired. Studies have suggested the print may belong to a young person, perhaps a child. We cannot know whether that person made the figure or simply handled it. But somewhere in this valley, many thousands of years ago, a small hand touched wet clay, and the mark is still there.

The site also gave archaeologists remarkable burials. One grave held a woman laid in the ground with red ochre, a mineral pigment, and with stone tools and animal remains. Her skeleton showed signs of an old injury to the face. Separately, a small ivory head was found at the site. Its face appears slightly uneven on one side. Some researchers have suggested it may portray the same woman, which would make it one of the earliest known portraits of a real person.

In 1986, another burial was found here that still puzzles experts. Three young people were placed together in a single grave. Their heads were covered with red ochre, and they were surrounded by ornaments made of teeth and ivory. Their positions in the grave appear deliberate. Many explanations have been proposed, but the true story behind this triple burial remains unknown. It is an honest mystery, and that is part of its power.

If you want to see these finds, a few practical notes help. The original Venus belongs to the Moravian Museum in Brno and is only rarely shown to the public. In the village, a small local museum tells the story of the excavations with replicas and displays. A short drive away, in the neighbouring village of Pavlov, the Archeopark Pavlov presents finds from this whole area in a modern building set partly into the hillside.

The landscape itself is worth your time too. The Pavlov Hills are a protected area, with limestone cliffs, dry grasslands, and rare plants. On one of the ridges, you can see the ruins of a medieval castle called Děvičky. Walking trails climb from the villages to the ridge, and from the top you can look across the reservoirs and the plain. This is the same valley the Ice Age hunters watched, only greener and full of water.

Before you leave, look down at the ground again. That pale, soft soil holds the story of this place. The wind laid it down layer by layer, and those layers covered the hearths, the bones, and the small clay figures. Now the same soil feeds the vines that make the local wine. As you walk away, notice the cliffs, the quiet water, and the open sky. People stood here long before history was written, and they looked at these same hills.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the village museum to see replicas and excavation displays.
Pair your visit with Archeopark Pavlov in the neighbouring village.
Wear sturdy shoes for trails up into the Pavlov Hills.
Ask at the Moravian Museum in Brno about the original Venus.
Combine the walk with a stop at a local wine cellar.
