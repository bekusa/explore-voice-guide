The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the stone. It is pale, almost cream in soft light. Some blocks look clean and new, and others are darker and scarred. That patchwork is the first thing to notice here, before any date or name. You are looking at a building that was lost once and then carefully put back together. The roofs of central Banja Luka spread around you, and the minaret rises straight above the courtyard.

Locals simply call this place Ferhadija. The name comes from the man who paid for it, Ferhad Pasha Sokolović. He was an Ottoman commander and governor in Bosnia in the late sixteenth century. The Ottoman Empire, ruled from Istanbul, controlled much of the Balkans for centuries, and its governors often shaped whole towns. Ferhad Pasha did exactly that. The mosque was completed in 1579, and it became the centre of the town he was building around it.

There is a story about where the money came from, and it is told often. According to the popular account, Ferhad Pasha defeated an Austrian army on the frontier in the 1570s. He captured a young nobleman and released him only after a large ransom was paid. That ransom, the story goes, helped fund this mosque and other buildings nearby. Historians treat the battle as real, but the exact link between ransom and stone is best heard as tradition.

Ferhad Pasha did not build only a prayer hall. He built a town core. Around his mosque grew shops, workshops, a market, and roads leading down toward the river Vrbas. This kind of complex was common in the Ottoman world. A mosque stood at the heart, and charitable buildings supported daily life around it. So when you stand here, you stand at the point where much of old Banja Luka first took shape.

Now look at the building itself. It follows the classical Ottoman style of its age. A single large dome covers the main prayer hall. In front, a covered porch rests on slender columns, topped by a row of smaller domes. The minaret is tall, thin, and pencil-shaped, with a balcony where the call to prayer was once sung by voice alone. The proportions are calm and balanced, with nothing heavy or crowded.

In the courtyard you will find a fountain for ritual washing. Before prayer, Muslims wash their hands, face, and feet. Listen for the water if it is running. It is a small, steady sound against the traffic of the city. Near the mosque stand small domed tombs, called turbe in the local language. These mark the graves of important people connected with the mosque. Their shape repeats the great dome on a tiny scale.

For more than four centuries, this mosque stood through wars, earthquakes, and changes of empire. The Ottomans left, and Austria-Hungary arrived. Then came the Kingdom of Yugoslavia, the Second World War, and socialist Yugoslavia. Through all of this, Ferhadija remained. It was valued well beyond the Muslim community. Many people saw it as one of the finest Ottoman buildings in the whole Balkan region.

Then came the war of the 1990s. On 7 May 1993, during the Bosnian war, the mosque was blown up with explosives. It was not damaged in a battle. It was destroyed on purpose, at night, by Bosnian Serb forces. The other mosques of Banja Luka were also destroyed in that same period. The rubble was taken away and dumped outside the city. Where the mosque had stood, there was only an empty space.

Here is something many visitors do not know. After the war, people went looking for the lost stones. Fragments of the original building were recovered from the dump sites. Experts sorted, measured, and catalogued them. Where possible, the old pieces were placed back into the new walls. That is why the stone around you looks uneven in colour. The darker, rougher blocks are often survivors of the original building from the sixteenth century.

The road back was not easy. In 2001, a ceremony was held to lay the foundation stone for reconstruction. It was met by a violent crowd, and people were injured. One man later died from his wounds. Work stopped and started for years. Specialists studied old photographs, drawings, and the recovered fragments. They wanted the new building to match the old one as closely as possible, using traditional methods wherever they could.

The mosque finally reopened in 2016. The date chosen was 7 May, exactly twenty-three years after its destruction. That choice was deliberate. It turned a day of loss into a day of return. Bosnia and Herzegovina has declared Ferhadija a national monument. Today it stands as a working mosque again, and as a lesson in how a community can rebuild what was taken from it.

If you go inside, the space opens upward under the dome. Light falls through windows set in rows. The walls carry painted decoration in soft colours and flowing patterns. A niche in one wall points toward Mecca, the direction of prayer. Beside it rises a tall stair-like pulpit for the Friday sermon. Notice how quiet it becomes, even a few steps from the street outside.

Before you leave, step back into the courtyard and look once more at the walls. Find a dark, worn stone set beside a bright new one. One has stood here since the sixteenth century. The other was cut only a few years ago. Together they hold up the same building. As you walk back into the streets of Banja Luka, listen for the call to prayer, rising again from this minaret.
