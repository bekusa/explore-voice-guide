The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest reaches you as sound. There is a steady drip of water from leaves high above. There is the dry rattle of insects and a sudden call from a bird you cannot find. Under your boots the ground is soft and dark, layered with fallen leaves that never quite dry out. The air is warm and heavy, and it smells of wet earth and green growth. This is Darién National Park, at the far eastern edge of Panama.

The park covers a huge territory, more than half a million hectares. That makes it one of the largest protected areas in Central America. It runs along much of Panama's border with Colombia. Within its limits are lowland rainforest, river valleys, mangroves, beaches, and steep, cloud-covered ridges. Panama created the park in 1980. Just one year later, in 1981, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage list. It was later recognised as a biosphere reserve as well.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The road ends here. The Pan-American Highway runs for thousands of kilometres through the Americas, from Alaska toward the far south of the continent. But it stops in this region of Panama and starts again in Colombia. The missing section is called the Darién Gap. No highway has ever been built through it. The swamps, rivers, and mountains are one reason. Concern for the forest and the people who live here is another.

That absence of a road shapes everything around you. In much of the Darién, rivers are the real highways. The Tuira and the Chucunaque are the great rivers of the province. People travel along them in long, narrow wooden boats called piraguas, often with an outboard motor at the back. Goods, children, and news all move by water. If you came in by boat, you travelled the way people here have travelled for generations.

The forest is not empty of people. Indigenous communities have lived in the Darién for a very long time. The Emberá and the Wounaan live mainly along the rivers, in houses raised on stilts with thatched roofs. The Guna also have communities in the wider region. Each group has its own language and traditions. The Emberá and Wounaan are known for fine woven baskets and for carvings in cocobolo wood and in tagua, a hard palm seed sometimes called vegetable ivory.

Now look up into the canopy. This forest is a meeting point of two continents. Panama is a land bridge between North and South America. Over a very long time, animals crossed it in both directions. So in the Darién, species from the north and the south live side by side. Scientists value the park for exactly this reason. It holds a great variety of plants, birds, mammals, reptiles, and amphibians in one connected landscape.

The animal that many people hope to see is the harpy eagle. It is the national bird of Panama. It is one of the most powerful eagles in the world, with thick legs and large talons. It hunts sloths and monkeys in the treetops. A grey crest of feathers stands up on its head like a crown. Sightings are never guaranteed. But the Darién is one of the places where this bird still finds the large, unbroken forest that it needs.

Other animals stay out of sight most of the time. Jaguars live here, and so do pumas and ocelots. Baird's tapir, the largest land mammal of Central America, moves quietly through the undergrowth near water. There are giant anteaters, peccaries, and several kinds of monkeys. You are more likely to find signs than animals. Look for a pressed track in river mud, a broken path through low plants, or a branch shaking after something has jumped away.

The Darién also has a long colonial story. In the early sixteenth century, Spanish explorers came to this region. In 1513, Vasco Núñez de Balboa crossed the isthmus through the Darién area. He became the first European on record to see the Pacific Ocean from the Americas. Later, the Spanish worked gold mines in the mountains here. The old mining area of Cana, deep in the southern part of the park, was one of the most important. Today, forest has grown back over much of it.

A second surprise comes from far away, from Scotland. In the late seventeenth century, Scotland tried to build a colony on the Caribbean coast of the Darién region. It was called the Darien scheme. Disease, hunger, and conflict with Spain destroyed it within a few years. The failure cost Scotland a huge share of its wealth. Many historians see it as one of the pressures that led toward union with England. That colony was on the northern coast, not inside this park, but it carries the same name.

In recent years, the Darién Gap has also become known for something painful. Many migrants from many countries have tried to cross this jungle on foot, heading north. The journey is long and dangerous, with rivers, mud, heat, and criminal groups. It is a reminder that this forest is not only a place of beauty. For some people, it is a place of real hardship and risk. Remote areas of the region can be unsafe, which is one reason visits here are organised with care.

For visitors, the park asks for patience and planning. Travel usually depends on permits, local guides, boats, and the weather. Ranger stations and community-run lodges give access to trails in some areas. Areas such as Cerro Pirre, a mountain ridge in the park, are known among birdwatchers. In the higher forest, mist often hangs in the trees in the morning. Rain can arrive quickly, then stop, and leave the whole forest dripping and shining.

So take a moment where you stand. Notice the colour of the light as it filters down through many layers of leaves. Notice the roots that spread across the ground, and the vines that climb toward the sun. Listen again for that drip of water and those distant calls. As you walk on, step slowly, and keep your eyes on the edges of the trail. In a forest this large and this quiet, the smallest movement may be the moment you remember.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange your visit through an authorised guide or operator.
Check current permit requirements before travelling to the park.
Pack quick-drying clothes and a reliable rain layer.
Bring binoculars for spotting birds high in the canopy.
Start walks early when birds and animals are most active.
