The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes off the prairie and moves across open ground where two rivers meet. Under your feet the paths are smooth and wide. Above you, glass catches the sky and shifts colour with every passing cloud. On a bright day the building looks almost silver. On a grey day it turns pale and soft, like weather itself has taken a shape. Before any history, this is what the place gives you: a sense of something rising out of the flat land.

You are standing at The Forks, where the Red River and the Assiniboine River join. For thousands of years, Indigenous peoples gathered at this spot to trade, to meet, and to settle disputes. Later came fur traders, settlers, and the railway. So the location was not chosen by accident. A museum about human dignity sits on ground that has long been a meeting place for different peoples, and not always a peaceful one.

The Canadian Museum for Human Rights opened in 2014. It is a national museum, and that matters here. Most of Canada's national museums sit in or near Ottawa, the capital. This one was built on the prairies, in the middle of the country. Many Winnipeg residents speak about that choice with real pride. It placed a national conversation in a city that visitors often only pass through.

The idea came largely from Israel Asper, a Winnipeg media businessman. He wanted a place where young Canadians could learn about human rights. He died in 2003, before construction began, but his family and his foundation carried the project forward. Governments and many private donors joined them. The tall glass spire you can see is named the Israel Asper Tower of Hope, in his memory.

The architect was Antoine Predock, an American known for buildings that respond to landscape. Look at the shape for a moment. There is no simple box here. Stone forms at the base look heavy and rooted, almost like the earth folding upward. Above them, a wrap of glass curves around the structure like a cloud or a pair of folded wings. Predock spoke of roots, of mountains, and of a dove. You do not need to see all of those. Just notice how the building moves from heavy to light as your eye travels up.

Here is something many visitors miss before they even enter. Before the museum was built, archaeologists carried out a major excavation on this site. They recovered a large number of objects from earlier peoples who camped and lived at the river junction. So the ground under the building is not empty. It holds a long human record, which feels fitting for a museum about people and their rights.

Inside, the journey is designed with care. You begin low, in a darker and more enclosed space. As you move through the galleries, you climb gradually upward toward more light. The idea is simple and you will feel it in your body. The stories near the start are often hard. The path ahead keeps opening toward the sky.

The ramps are the part people remember most. They are clad in alabaster, a soft, translucent stone, and they are lit from within. The stone glows a warm white and gold. The ramps cross back and forth through a vast open space, linking the galleries. Walk slowly here. Touch the edge of the stone if you like, and notice how cool it feels compared with how warm it looks.

Now a second surprise. This is a museum of ideas more than a museum of objects. Many museums are built around a famous collection. This one is built around stories, testimony, and questions. You will find personal accounts, films, and interactive displays that ask you what you think. Galleries look at Indigenous perspectives, at Canadian struggles for rights, at the Holocaust, and at other genocides around the world. Some rooms are quiet and heavy. Give yourself permission to sit down.

The museum has also faced hard questions of its own. People have debated which histories receive the most space, and how difficult chapters of Canada's past are presented. Those debates have been public and sometimes painful. In a way, they show the subject of the building at work. Human rights are not a finished story. They are argued about, in the open, by real people.

Somewhere along your route you will reach a garden space, a calm area of greenery and stone set inside the building. It is a good place to rest between galleries. Listen to how the sound changes there. The noise of voices softens. After the weight of some exhibits, this pause matters. The designers clearly understood that visitors need space to breathe.

If you continue to the top, the Tower of Hope opens a wide view over Winnipeg. You can see the rivers bending together, the rooftops of downtown, and the flat land running toward the horizon. In winter the rivers may be frozen white. In summer they run brown and slow. From up here the city feels small and the sky feels very large, which is a common feeling on the prairies.

When you step back outside, take one more look at the building from a distance. Walk a little way toward the rivers and turn around. Watch how the glass catches the light at that exact moment, because it will never look quite the same twice. Then notice the people around you, the walkers, the families, the cyclists on the paths. This was a meeting place long before the museum. It still is. And perhaps that is the quietest lesson of all: rights begin with how people share the same ground.
