The guide

The story

Listen first. Gulls call over the harbour, and wind comes in off the water with a smell of salt and wet stone. Under your feet the ground is uneven, cobbles worn smooth by centuries of boots. Grey walls rise around you, thick and plain, built more for strength than for show. This is Bergenhus Fortress, at the mouth of Bergen's old harbour. It is one of the oldest and best-preserved fortifications in Norway.

Before it was a fortress, this headland had a simpler name. Locals called it Holmen, which means something like the islet or the small rise. In the Middle Ages, Norway's kings chose this spot for their royal residence. From here they could watch every ship that sailed into the bay called Vågen. That bay was the commercial heart of the city. Whoever held this point could see trade, threats, and visitors long before they reached the quay.

It helps to remember how important Bergen once was. In the thirteenth century, this was effectively the capital of Norway. It was the largest town in the country and its main trading port. Kings were crowned here. Royal weddings took place here. Laws were debated within these walls. Oslo only took over the leading role later. So when you stand on this headland, you are standing at what was once the political centre of a medieval kingdom.

The great stone building in front of you is Haakon's Hall. King Haakon the Fourth, Haakon Haakonsson, built it in the middle of the thirteenth century. It was a ceremonial hall, made for banquets and grand state occasions. Tradition links its completion to the wedding of his son Magnus in the early 1260s. Look at the tall, narrow windows and the steep gable. This is Gothic architecture — the style of pointed arches and soaring height that spread across medieval Europe. In Norway, very few secular buildings of this age survive.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The hall you see is not entirely the original. On 20 April 1944, during the German occupation, a Dutch cargo ship called the Voorbode exploded at the quay below. It was loaded with explosives. The blast shook the whole city. It killed many people, damaged large parts of Bergen, and set the roof of Haakon's Hall on fire. The interior burned out. What you see today is the result of careful restoration after the war.

That restoration took many years. Restorers kept the surviving medieval stonework and rebuilt the roof and interior. Today the hall is used again for formal events, much as its builder intended. Concerts, receptions, and official royal occasions still take place under its rafters. So the building has returned to its first purpose. It is not a museum piece frozen in one moment. It is a working ceremonial hall with a scarred and repaired past.

Now turn toward the squat, heavy tower closer to the harbour. This is the Rosenkrantz Tower. It carries the name of Erik Rosenkrantz, a governor of Bergenhus in the sixteenth century. At that time, Norway was ruled from Copenhagen as part of a union with Denmark. Rosenkrantz rebuilt and enlarged an older medieval tower here. He turned it into a combination of fortified residence and gun tower. The result looks less elegant than Haakon's Hall, and that was the point.

Here is the second surprise. Inside the Rosenkrantz Tower, the older medieval core still survives. Parts of it go back to the time of King Magnus, Haakon's son, in the thirteenth century. So one building holds two eras stacked together. If you go inside, you climb narrow spiral stairs and pass through cramped rooms with thick walls. Near the top, the windows look out over the harbour and the old wharf. Like the hall, the tower was damaged in 1944 and restored afterwards.

The fortress has seen real fighting. In 1665, an English fleet attacked Dutch merchant ships sheltering in Bergen's harbour. This became known as the Battle of Vågen. Guns from the fortress and from positions around the bay fired on the English ships. The attack failed, and the English withdrew. It is a reminder that this was never only a royal stage. It was a defensive position, built to protect a city whose wealth floated in the bay.

The twentieth century brought a darker chapter. During the Second World War, German forces used Bergenhus as a base. The fortress museum on the grounds tells this part of the story. It covers the occupation and the Norwegian resistance, and also the city's links to the wider war at sea. It is a quieter visit than the medieval buildings, but it fills in what happened here within living memory. Parts of the fortress area still have a military role today.

As you walk the grounds, look down at the grass. In places, low stone outlines mark the foundations of buildings that no longer stand. One of them was a great medieval church, which stood near the royal buildings. For a time it was the cathedral of Bergen. It once held the relics of Saint Sunniva, a local saint linked to an Irish princess in Norwegian legend. Only the outline in the ground remains today, but it shows how large this royal complex once was.

The open lawns between the walls have a modern life too. In summer, the fortress grounds become a stage for concerts and festivals. Big crowds gather where kings once held court. On an ordinary day, though, the space is calm. Office workers cut through on their lunch break. Families sit on the walls. Tourists drift between the hall and the tower. This mix of everyday life and deep history is part of the character of Bergen.

Take a moment before you leave. Look past the walls toward the harbour and the colourful wooden houses of Bryggen along the waterfront. That was the old trading wharf, where merchants of the Hanseatic League worked for centuries. The Hanseatic League was a powerful network of north German trading towns. From this headland, kings and governors watched that busy world of fish, grain, and money. Notice the boats in the bay now, the mountains rising behind the city, and the soft northern light. The view has changed, but the reason for this fortress is still right in front of you.
