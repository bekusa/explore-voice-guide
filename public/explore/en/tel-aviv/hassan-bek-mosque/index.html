The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the sea rolls onto the sand. In front of you, traffic hums along a wide coastal road. Between the two stands a pale stone mosque with a slender minaret, quiet and a little apart from everything around it. The light here is strong and salty, and the limestone takes it in, turning almost white at midday and honey-coloured in the late afternoon.

This is the Hassan Bek Mosque. It sits on the seam where Tel Aviv meets Jaffa, and that seam is the key to its story. Today the building looks almost lonely, surrounded by open lawns, hotels and a seafront promenade. But it was not built to stand alone. It was built to anchor a whole neighbourhood, and that neighbourhood has almost completely disappeared.

The mosque dates from 1916, during the final years of Ottoman rule. The Ottoman Empire, governed from Istanbul, controlled this coast for about four centuries. At the time, the area was part of Manshiya, a crowded Arab district that stretched north from old Jaffa along the shore. The mosque took its name from its patron, Hassan Bek, the Ottoman military governor of Jaffa during the First World War.

Hassan Bek is a debated figure. Accounts from the period describe him as harsh and ambitious, a man who pushed large building projects through quickly. Some stories say he used forced labour, and that stone was taken from other buildings in the area. These accounts are widely repeated, but treat the details with some care. What is clear is that he wanted a strong Muslim landmark on the northern edge of Jaffa.

Why here? Many historians link the location to the growth of Tel Aviv itself. The new Jewish city, founded just a few years earlier, was spreading quickly along the sand dunes. A popular interpretation holds that the mosque was meant to mark a boundary, a clear statement of where Jaffa ended. Whether that was the governor's main intention, or simply a later reading, is still discussed today.

Now look at the building itself. The style draws on Ottoman and local Arab traditions. Notice the arched windows, the clean lines of the prayer hall, and the tall minaret, the tower from which the call to prayer is given. The stone is local limestone. Over a century of sea air has softened its edges and left small marks of weathering on the surfaces.

The minaret you see is not exactly the original. The first tower was damaged over time, and it was later rebuilt, in the decades after the state of Israel was founded. So when you look up, you are looking at a reconstruction that follows the earlier form. It is a small detail, but it says something about this place — it has been repaired many times, each repair carrying its own moment in history.

The hardest chapter came in 1948. During the war that year, fighting broke out between Jaffa and Tel Aviv. The tall minaret overlooked the streets to the north, and it was used as a position by Arab fighters firing toward the Jewish neighbourhoods. For many Tel Aviv residents, the mosque became a symbol of that danger. For many Palestinians, the events of that year remain a story of loss and displacement.

After the war, most of the Arab population of Manshiya was gone. In the decades that followed, the old houses and streets were cleared. Here is something many visitors miss. The wide green lawns and open space around you were once dense rows of homes, shops and alleys. The mosque is one of the very few buildings left from that neighbourhood. You are standing in the empty outline of a district.

If you walk a short way along the shore toward Jaffa, you will find a small museum inside a stone building set into a modern glass structure. It tells the story of the 1948 battle from the perspective of the Jewish fighters. Together, the mosque and that museum show how one stretch of coast holds very different memories, depending on who is telling the story.

The mosque has also faced conflicts over its future. At different times, there were plans to change its use or develop the land around it. Members of the local Muslim community, many from Jaffa, pushed back and worked to keep it as a place of worship. It has also suffered damage during periods of tension. Even so, it remains an active mosque, and prayers are still held inside.

That is the second surprise. This is not a museum or a ruin. It is a working mosque, used by the Muslim community of Jaffa and beyond. If you hope to enter, dress modestly, cover your shoulders and knees, and remove your shoes at the door. Avoid prayer times, especially on Friday at midday, which is the main weekly prayer. Ask quietly at the entrance if visitors are welcome.

From the street, try stepping back toward the seafront promenade. From there you can frame the minaret against the hotel towers of modern Tel Aviv. The contrast is sharp: a small Ottoman tower in front of glass and concrete. In the late afternoon, when the sun drops toward the sea, the stone warms in colour and the shadows of the arches become long and clear.

Before you move on, take one more look. Listen to the waves, the traffic and perhaps the call to prayer. Picture the narrow streets that once surrounded this building on every side. Then, as you walk away along the shore, notice how open the ground feels. That openness is part of the story too.
