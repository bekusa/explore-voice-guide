The guide

The story

Run your hand along almost any wall here, and you will feel it. Rough plaster, warm from the sun, softened by sea air and decades of repainting. Above you, a balcony curves around a corner like the deck of a ship. Below, a ficus tree pushes its roots through the pavement. Somewhere nearby, a scooter hums past, and a café spoon taps against a glass. This is the White City, and you are standing inside it right now.

The White City is not one building. It is a whole district, spread through the heart of Tel Aviv. It holds thousands of buildings in a style the world calls modernist, or International Style. Most of them went up in the 1930s, with more following into the 1940s and 1950s. In 2003, UNESCO added the White City to its World Heritage list. The reason was simple. Nowhere else on earth has so many buildings of this kind, gathered in one place, still lived in every day.

To understand why it looks this way, start with the street plan beneath your feet. In the 1920s, the young city asked a Scottish planner named Patrick Geddes to design its growth. Geddes thought of a city as a living organism. He drew long main roads running toward the sea, and quieter residential streets between them. He also planned small public gardens tucked into the blocks. That is why this neighbourhood feels green and walkable. The breeze from the Mediterranean was meant to travel down these streets.

Then came the architects. Many of them had trained in Europe — in Germany, in France, in Belgium, in Italy. Some had studied at the Bauhaus, the famous German school of art and design that taught clean lines, honest materials, and function before decoration. When the Nazis rose to power in 1933, the school closed. Jewish architects and their families left Europe, and many of them arrived here. They brought new ideas with them... and a very new city ready to receive them.

You will often hear people call this a Bauhaus city. That is partly true, and partly a nickname. Only a small number of the architects actually studied at the Bauhaus. Others followed the Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier, or the German modernist Erich Mendelsohn. What united them was a shared belief. A building should serve the people inside it. It should be light, practical, and affordable. It should not pretend to be a palace.

But Europe and the Levant are very different places. A design that works in grey Berlin can turn into an oven under a Mediterranean sun. So the architects changed the rules. Look at the windows around you. Many are smaller than you might expect for modernist buildings. Deep balconies cast shadow onto the rooms behind them. Overhanging ledges shade the glass. The white and pale walls reflect the heat. This is European modernism, adapted to a hot coastal climate — and that adaptation is a big part of what UNESCO recognised.

Now look up at a stairwell. On many buildings you will see a tall, narrow strip of windows, running vertically up the facade. Locals call these thermometer windows, because they look like the glass tube of a thermometer. They let light into the staircase while keeping out the worst of the heat. Once you notice one, you will start seeing them on almost every block. It is a small, practical detail, and it gives the whole district a kind of rhythm.

Here is something most visitors miss. Many buildings stand on slim columns, with an open space at ground level. These columns are called pilotis, a word borrowed from Le Corbusier. The open ground floor lets air move under the building and gives shade to anyone passing. One of the earliest examples in the city is Engel House on Rothschild Boulevard, designed by the architect Ze'ev Rechter. Today, many of those open spaces have been closed in for shops or flats — so the ones that remain are worth a second look.

Rothschild Boulevard is a good spine for your walk. A long green strip runs down its centre, shaded by trees, lined with benches, kiosks, and bicycle lanes. On either side, you can see modernist facades with rounded corners and ribbon windows — long horizontal bands of glass that wrap across the front of a building. In the early morning, joggers and dog walkers fill the path. By evening, the kiosks are busy and conversations spill out from the cafés.

Further north, Dizengoff Square is another anchor of the White City. In the 1930s, it was laid out as a round plaza, surrounded by curved buildings that follow its circle. The square has been rebuilt more than once over the decades, and its look has changed. Still, if you stand at its edge and turn slowly, you can see how the facades bend together around the space. It is a lesson in how buildings can shape a public room without any walls.

A surprise for many visitors: the White City is not always white. Look closely and you will find cream, pale yellow, soft grey, and stains of age. Some buildings are freshly restored, their plaster smooth and bright. Their neighbours may be crumbling, with air-conditioning units bolted onto balconies and cables looping across the walls. Restoration here is slow and complicated, because nearly every building is a private home. People live, cook, and hang laundry behind these historic facades.

That living quality is the real point of this place. It was never built as a museum. It was built as housing for a fast-growing city of newcomers — people starting again, often with very little. The flat roofs once served as shared spaces for drying clothes and catching the evening breeze. The balconies were outdoor rooms. If you want to go deeper, the city has visitor centres dedicated to this heritage, where you can learn how the buildings are studied and repaired.

As you walk, try a simple habit. At each corner, pause and look at the building on the corner itself. Architects here loved corners. They rounded them, wrapped them with balconies, or cut them back to open the view. Then look down the side street. Notice the small gardens, the bougainvillea climbing a fence, the cats sleeping on low walls. Geddes planned for green spaces, and the city still carries that plan.

So stand still for a moment. Feel the plaster again, or the shade of a balcony overhead. Listen to the traffic, the birds in the ficus trees, someone practising piano behind an open window. These buildings were designed with an idea about how people should live — in light, in air, close to their neighbours. Almost a century later, people are still living that idea. And as you walk on, every white wall you pass is part of the same story.
