The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere among the pine trees, a peacock calls, a sharp, startling cry that seems far too loud for such a calm garden. Underfoot, the paths change from gravel to stone to packed earth. Sunlight falls through the branches onto low pavilions scattered across the grounds. This is the Eretz Israel Museum, in the northern Tel Aviv neighbourhood of Ramat Aviv, and it feels less like one building and more like a small village of ideas.

The name itself is worth a pause. Eretz Israel is Hebrew for the Land of Israel. That idea shapes the whole collection. The museum does not tell one story. It gathers many threads: archaeology, glass, coins, copper, crafts, folklore and everyday life. Each thread has its own pavilion, and you walk between them in the open air. So the experience is a kind of slow wander. You move from room to garden to room again, and the Mediterranean light follows you.

The museum opened in the 1950s, in the early years of the young state. Its founders wanted a place where people could see the material culture of this land across thousands of years. Over time, the campus grew outward, pavilion by pavilion. That gradual growth explains the unusual layout. There is no grand entrance hall and no single route. Instead, you choose your own path, which means two visitors can leave with very different memories of the same morning.

Now for the first surprise. The museum sits on top of a real archaeological site. It is called Tel Qasile. A tel is a mound formed by layer upon layer of ancient settlement, each town built on the ruins of the one before. Excavations here began around 1948, and they are often described as the first major dig carried out by the new State of Israel. So the museum did not simply collect the past. It was built beside the past, which was already in the ground.

What did the archaeologists find? Tel Qasile was a port town near the Yarkon River, settled in the Iron Age. Its most famous discovery is a series of Philistine temples, built one above another over time. The Philistines were a seafaring people who settled along this coast roughly three thousand years ago. They appear often in the Bible, usually as rivals. Here, though, you can see where they actually worshipped, and that turns a name from old stories into something solid you can walk around.

Take a moment at the site itself. The remains are low walls of stone and mud brick, outlines more than buildings. You need a little imagination. Picture a small sanctuary with a raised platform, offerings of pottery, and a town of houses crowding around it. Picture the river nearby, with boats carrying goods to and from the sea. The quiet today is a strong contrast. Apart from the birds and the distant traffic, this place is still.

Back among the pavilions, the Glass Pavilion is a favourite for many visitors. Glass has a long history in this region. The coast had sand, and skilled workers here and in nearby lands helped develop glassmaking in the ancient world. In the display cases you can see small bottles, bowls and vessels in pale greens, blues and ambers. Many have a soft, rainbow shimmer on the surface. That shimmer is not paint. It comes from the slow chemical weathering of glass buried in the earth for centuries.

Next, consider the coins. The museum holds a numismatic collection, which simply means a collection of coins. Coins are small, but they carry a great deal of information. Each one shows who ruled, what symbols mattered, and how people traded. In one case you might see the profile of a Roman emperor. In another, you might see a palm tree, a grape cluster or a temple facade. Lean close. The details are tiny, and the craftsmanship on some pieces is remarkably fine.

Here is the second surprise. One pavilion is dedicated to copper, and to the ancient mining of it in the southern desert, in the Timna area near Eilat. Copper mining there goes back thousands of years. People dug shafts, smelted ore in small furnaces, and carried metal across long distances. It is easy to think of this land only in terms of farming and faith. This pavilion reminds you that it was also a place of industry, heat, smoke and hard physical labour.

That theme of work continues in the crafts displays. The museum shows traditional tools and methods: pressing olives for oil, grinding grain into flour, weaving, pottery and metalwork. Olive oil was central to life here for millennia. It was food, fuel for lamps, medicine and an offering in temples. When you see a heavy stone press, think about the effort behind every jar of oil. These are not abstract artefacts. They were the daily machinery of ordinary families.

Other spaces turn to folklore and Jewish ethnography. Here you may find clothing, jewellery, ritual objects and household items from communities across many countries. Together they show how people carried traditions with them as they moved and settled. The museum also hosts changing exhibitions, often on photography, design or local history. Because these rotate, it is worth checking what is on before you visit. You may find something completely new beside the permanent collections.

As you walk, notice the grounds themselves. The gardens are part of the experience, not just the space between buildings. Ancient stone pieces stand among the plants. Benches sit in the shade. And the peacocks move freely, sometimes spreading their tails on the lawn, sometimes perching on a low wall. Children often follow them. Adults pretend not to, and then follow them too. On a hot afternoon, these shaded paths are a good place to slow down.

The museum also sits close to the Yarkon River and its large park, and the campus of Tel Aviv University is nearby. So this part of the city has a different rhythm from the beaches and the busy streets of the centre. It is greener, quieter and more spread out. If you have time, you can combine a visit here with a walk in the park, following the same river that once served the port town of Tel Qasile.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Look at the low pavilions, the pines and the stones from the ancient town. Listen again for that peacock. Around you are layers: a modern city, a museum from the 1950s, and beneath them a settlement from about three thousand years ago. As you walk back toward the gate, notice the ground under your feet. Somewhere below, the past is still there, quietly holding up the present.
