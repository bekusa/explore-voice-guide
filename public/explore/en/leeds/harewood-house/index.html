The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel shifts under your feet, rooks call from the old trees, and a soft wind moves across open parkland. Ahead, a long pale front of honey-coloured stone stands above the green slopes. This is Harewood House, a few miles west of Wetherby, between Leeds and Harrogate. It is a grand country house. It is also a place with a difficult story, and both belong to this walk.

The house was built in the second half of the eighteenth century for Edwin Lascelles. Work began around 1759 and continued through the following decade. Lascelles wanted a house that showed his family's new place among the English landed elite. He gathered some of the most celebrated designers of Georgian Britain to make it happen. Georgian here simply means the age of the four King Georges, roughly the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.

The exterior was designed by John Carr, a Yorkshire architect who built many houses across the north of England. Carr's design is calm and balanced, with a strong central block and long wings on either side. For the rooms inside, Lascelles turned to Robert Adam, a Scottish architect famous for delicate ceilings and refined classical detail. Adam's style drew on ancient Rome and Greece, but made it lighter and more decorative.

Now the surprise that many visitors miss. Much of the furniture was made by the workshop of Thomas Chippendale. His name is known around the world for elegant Georgian furniture. Chippendale was born in Otley, a small market town only a short drive from here. So the great cabinetmaker came home to Yorkshire for this commission. The Harewood work is often counted among the largest he ever undertook.

The money behind all this needs to be said plainly. The Lascelles family made much of their fortune through the West Indies, especially Barbados. That wealth came from sugar plantations, trade, and moneylending, and it depended on the labour of enslaved Africans. Today the house does not hide this. The Harewood House Trust has worked with artists, historians, and Caribbean communities to tell this part of the story openly.

Think about that as you look at the fine plaster, the gilding, and the polished wood. The beauty is real. So is the cost behind it. Many English country houses carry links to slavery, but not all of them say so. At Harewood, you may find exhibitions and artworks that ask visitors to hold both truths at once. It is a thoughtful, sometimes uncomfortable, and very honest kind of visit.

Turn now toward the landscape. The park was shaped by Lancelot Brown, better known as Capability Brown. He earned the nickname because he often told clients their land had good capability for improvement. Brown did not build formal hedges and straight paths. He created rolling grass, scattered trees, and a long curving lake. The aim was a landscape that looked natural, even though it was carefully planned.

When you walk the grounds, notice how the views open and close. A clump of trees hides the lake, then a turn of the path shows it again. This was deliberate. Brown designed the park like a series of pictures, revealed one after another as you move. On a misty morning, the lake and the far woods soften into grey and green layers, and the effect is quiet and still.

In the nineteenth century, the house changed. The architect Charles Barry, who also worked on the Houses of Parliament in London, remodelled parts of the building in the 1840s. He added a formal terrace on the south side, with patterned beds and fountains. So here you can see two ideas of beauty side by side. There is Barry's ordered terrace near the house, and Brown's open, flowing park beyond it.

The twentieth century brought a royal connection. Princess Mary, the only daughter of King George the Fifth, married Viscount Lascelles in 1922. He later became the sixth Earl of Harewood. The Princess Royal, as she was known, made Harewood her home for many years. Visitors can see rooms and objects linked to her life here. Her presence gave this Yorkshire house a quiet place in modern royal history.

Inside, the house also holds a strong collection of art. There are family portraits, fine porcelain, and paintings gathered over generations. The young artist J. M. W. Turner came here and painted views of the house and its castle ruins. Those early works show the estate through the eyes of a painter who later became one of the most important artists in British history. Look out for changing displays, too, because the house often brings in contemporary art.

Below stairs, the story shifts again. The kitchens and service areas show the working side of a great house. Here cooks, maids, and footmen kept daily life running for the family upstairs. These spaces are plainer, with stone floors and practical fittings. They are a useful reminder that a house like this was a workplace for many people. The quiet rooms upstairs depended on the busy rooms below.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look back at the long stone front. Notice how the light changes its colour, from soft cream in the morning to warmer gold late in the day. Listen again for the rooks and the wind in the trees. As you walk away across the park, you carry the whole story with you — the craft, the landscape, the family, and the people whose labour paid for it all.
