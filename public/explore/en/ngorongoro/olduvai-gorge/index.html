The guide

The story

Stand at the rim and feel the wind first. It comes across the plains warm and dry, carrying fine dust that settles on your lips and the backs of your hands. Below you, the ground falls away into a long, ragged ravine. You can see bands of colour in its walls: pale cream, rust red, grey, and soft brown, stacked one on top of another like the pages of a closed book. That book is what brought scientists here, and it is why this quiet stretch of northern Tanzania matters to anyone curious about where people come from.

This is Olduvai Gorge. It sits in the Ngorongoro District, between the highlands around Ngorongoro Crater and the open grasslands of the Serengeti. It belongs to the eastern branch of the Great Rift Valley. That is the long system of faults where the African continent is slowly pulling apart. The gorge runs for dozens of kilometres, and in places it drops close to a hundred metres. Seasonal water has cut into these slopes for a very long time. Each rainy season, that water exposes a little more of what lies inside.

Here is the first surprise. Many people pronounce the name Olduvai, but that spelling came from a mishearing. The Maasai word is closer to Oldupai, and you will see that spelling on newer signs. It refers to a wild sisal plant that grows on these slopes. The plant has stiff, spiky green leaves. The Maasai have long used its fibres for rope and its leaves as a natural bandage. Look around the rim and you will probably spot clumps of it. The whole place is named after this plant.

Now look again at those coloured bands. Geologists divide them into a series of numbered beds. The oldest, called Bed One, lies near the bottom. The younger layers sit above it. Between them are layers of volcanic ash from nearby volcanoes that erupted again and again. Long ago, this was not a dry gorge at all. A shallow lake sat here, with marshes, streams, and grazing animals along its edges. Early human ancestors came to that water too.

The ash layers are the key to this place. Volcanic ash contains minerals that can be dated with scientific methods, because certain elements inside them decay at a steady rate. Olduvai was one of the first places where this technique was used to date the remains of human ancestors. The results were a shock. They showed that human history in Africa went back far further than most scientists had believed in the mid-twentieth century. Bed One is roughly two million years old.

The first scientific interest came early in the twentieth century, when a German team collected fossils here. Then came the people most closely linked with the gorge: Louis and Mary Leakey. Louis was a Kenyan-born archaeologist, and Mary was a British archaeologist with sharp skills in excavation and drawing. Starting in the 1930s, they returned season after season. They worked in heat and dust with simple equipment. For decades they found many stone tools and animal bones, but they found few remains of the toolmakers themselves.

That changed in 1959. Mary Leakey was walking an exposed slope when she noticed fragments of bone and teeth. They belonged to a skull. Her team pieced it together from hundreds of fragments. The skull had a heavy jaw, very large flat molars, and a bony ridge along the top of the head for strong chewing muscles. Newspapers called it Nutcracker Man. Scientists now place it in a group called Paranthropus boisei. Dating from the ash showed it was about 1.75 million years old, and the find made Olduvai famous around the world.

The second surprise came soon after. In the early 1960s, the team found other fossils nearby with smaller teeth and a larger braincase. In 1964 these remains were given a new name: Homo habilis, which means handy man. The name reflected an idea that this species may have made the stone tools found here. So, at roughly the same time and in the same landscape, at least two different kinds of early human relatives may have shared this lake shore. Scientists still debate exactly how they lived alongside each other.

Those tools gave their name to a whole period of prehistory. Archaeologists call the oldest widespread stone tool tradition the Oldowan, after this gorge. An Oldowan tool is simple to look at. It is a rounded stone with a few flakes knocked off one side to make a sharp edge. Do not let that simplicity fool you. Making one takes planning, a good choice of rock, and controlled strikes. Cut marks on animal bones from these layers suggest that early humans used such tools to remove meat and break bones for marrow.

Higher up the walls, in the younger beds, the tools change. Large teardrop-shaped hand axes appear, worked carefully on both sides. Archaeologists link these with a later tradition called the Acheulean, named after a site in France. Because the layers stay in order, you can follow a story of slow change from bottom to top. Simple flaked stones give way to more carefully shaped tools. Few places on Earth show this sequence so clearly in a single landscape.

On the rim there is a small museum and a covered viewpoint. Guides often give short talks there while you look down into the main gorge. Inside the museum you will find casts of key fossils, examples of stone tools, and displays about the Leakeys and the teams who worked with them. Many of those team members were Tanzanian and Kenyan field workers, and their trained eyes found many of the important pieces. The original fossils are kept in national collections, so what you see here are careful replicas.

The gorge is also part of a wider story. To the south lies Laetoli, a separate site where Mary Leakey's team later found footprints preserved in volcanic ash. They are even older than the fossils from Olduvai. Not far from here is a dune of black volcanic ash that slowly moves across the plain with the wind. Many visitors combine these places in one day. Maasai herders still move cattle and goats through this country, as their families have done for generations. You may hear goat bells drifting up from the slopes.

Before you leave, stand still at the rim for a moment. Watch the light move across the bands of rock as clouds pass overhead. Notice the acacia trees, the sisal plants, and the dust on your shoes. Those small, flaked stones were once held in hands not so different from your own, at the edge of a lake that is long gone. As you walk back toward the road, look down at the ground under your feet. Somewhere beneath it, the next layer is still waiting for the rain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the morning before heat and haze build up.
Bring water, a hat, and strong sun protection.
Join a guide's talk at the rim viewpoint.
Combine your visit with the nearby shifting black ash dune.
Allow extra time if you travel between Ngorongoro and Serengeti.
