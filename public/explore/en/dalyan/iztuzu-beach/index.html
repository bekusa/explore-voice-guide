The story

Kick off your shoes and feel the sand. It is fine and pale gold, packed firm near the water line. The beach runs away from you in a long, bright ribbon until it fades into sea haze. On one side, the Mediterranean arrives in slow, even waves. On the other, only a few hundred metres away, a green river delta spreads flat and quiet, full of reeds and birdsong. Very few beaches put you between fresh water and salt water at the same time. This one does.

İztuzu is a sandbar. It stretches about four and a half kilometres, sealing the delta of the Dalyan river off from the open sea. The river winds down from Köyceğiz lake through a maze of channels lined with tall reeds. Most visitors arrive by small boat, threading through that maze. Captains steer by memory, because the walls of reeds all look the same. When the boat finally slips out of the channels and the beach opens ahead, the whole boat usually goes quiet.

This is also one of the most important turtle beaches in the Mediterranean. Loggerhead sea turtles nest here every summer. Scientists call them Caretta caretta, and along this coast, locals use the same name. Female loggerheads haul themselves ashore at night, dig a pit with their rear flippers, and lay dozens of soft eggs. Then they cover the nest, drag themselves back to the water, and vanish. Many of them hatched on this exact beach decades ago, and returned by instinct to the sand where they began.

About two months later, the hatchlings dig their way out, usually after dark. Each one is small enough to sit in your palm. They race for the brightest horizon, which on a natural beach means the sea. Artificial light confuses them, and confused hatchlings die. That is why İztuzu closes at night during the nesting season, and why the buildings here stay low and dark. The rules are strict on purpose. The turtles were here first.

The beach almost lost all of this. In the 1980s, developers planned a large hotel complex on the sandbar. Conservationists in Turkey and abroad fought the project. The loudest voice belonged to June Haimoff, an Englishwoman who had settled here in a simple beach hut. Locals called her Kaptan June — Captain June. She wrote letters, recruited scientists and journalists, and refused to give up. The campaign drew international attention, and the pressure worked.

In 1988, Turkey declared the Köyceğiz and Dalyan region a Special Environmental Protection Area. The hotel was never built. İztuzu stayed a beach of sand, reeds, and turtles rather than concrete. Conservation groups have praised it many times since, and it is often listed among the best-protected beaches in Europe. Haimoff spent the rest of her long life beside the water she helped save, and her old hut has been preserved as a small monument to the campaign.

Walk toward the far end of the beach, near the road entrance, and you reach a sea turtle rescue centre. Vets and volunteers treat turtles injured by boat propellers, fishing hooks, and swallowed plastic. Recovering animals rest in shaded tanks, and visitors are welcome to look. Healthy turtles go back to the sea, and some carry satellite tags so researchers can follow their journeys across the Mediterranean. A small donation helps keep the tanks running.

As you walk, watch the sand itself. Wooden stakes and small cage-like frames mark active nests, each one holding eggs a hand's depth below the surface. Give them space. Sun umbrellas are allowed only in marked zones set back from the water, because a pole driven into the wrong spot can crush a clutch of eggs. Tracks in the morning sand sometimes show where a female came ashore overnight — two ridged lines, like the print of a tractor tyre.

Behind the beach, the delta carries on its own busy life. Herons stand frozen in the shallows. Kingfishers flash between the reeds. Blue crabs scuttle across the channel beds, and mullet ripple the surface. The mix of fresh and salt water makes this one of the richest wetlands on the Turkish coast. If you ride a boat back toward Dalyan in the late afternoon, the light turns the reeds gold and the birds come out to feed.

The town of Dalyan takes its name from fishing weirs — fence-like traps that catch fish moving between the lake and the sea. People have fished this water for thousands of years. High on the cliffs above the river, tombs carved into the rock face look down on the passing boats. They belonged to Kaunos, an ancient city whose ruins still stand nearby. The carved facades resemble small temples, complete with columns and pediments cut straight into the stone.

You can also reach İztuzu by road, on a winding drive through pine forest from Dalyan. The two ends of the beach feel different. The road end has the rescue centre and a busier stretch of sunbeds. The river end feels wilder, with dunes, driftwood, and the channel mouth. In between lie long stretches where you can walk for ten minutes and meet nobody. Bring water, because shade is scarce along the middle of the sandbar.

The swimming is easy and open. The seabed slopes gently, the water is clear, and the waves are usually soft. In early summer you may share the sea with more life than you expect, because the adult turtles feed and wait in the bay just offshore. Seeing one surface for a breath — dark shell, blunt head, a slow blink — is a moment people remember for years.

Before you leave, stand at the water line and look down the full length of the sandbar. Almost nothing here is built, and that absence is the point. This beach looks the way it does because people argued, wrote letters, and stubbornly cared. Under your feet, somewhere in the warm sand, the next generation of loggerheads may already be waiting for dark, ready to dig their way out and run for the waves.

Practical tips

Arrive by boat from Dalyan for the reed-channel approach
Check closing hours — the beach shuts at night in nesting season
Plant umbrellas only in marked zones away from the waterline
Carry water — shade and kiosks are scarce mid-beach
