The guide

The story

A boat engine putters somewhere in the reeds, and the reeds themselves — taller than a person — hiss against each other in the breeze. The river slides past, green and slow. A fish jumps, slaps down, and is gone. Then you look up, and the cliff across the water stops you completely. Carved into the rock face, high above the river, stand the facades of temples — columns, doorways, triangular pediments — as if buildings were trying to step out of the mountain.

Start with the town's name, because it explains the landscape. Dalyan means fishing weir in Turkish — a barrier for trapping fish. The town sits on a winding channel that connects Köyceğiz Lake, inland behind you, with the Mediterranean Sea. Between the two, the river splits into a maze of channels through the reed beds. Fish move up and down with the seasons, and weirs have harvested them here for generations. Boats, not cars, are how this place thinks.

Now those carved facades. They are tombs, cut in the fourth century BC for the rulers and leading families of Kaunos, the ancient city nearby. The grandest are shaped like Greek temple fronts, with two columns between pilasters and a carved triangular pediment above. Inside each is a small chamber with stone benches for the dead. Carving them high on the cliff put the dead above the living world, facing the river — visible to everyone, reachable by almost no one.

Look along the row until you find the tomb that was never finished. One facade simply stops partway — the upper portion carved in detail, the lower part still rough rock. It is a lesson frozen mid-sentence. The carvers worked from the top down, cutting away the cliff face as they descended, so they never needed scaffolding from below. Something interrupted the work — history does not record what — and the stone has been waiting ever since.

The people who commissioned these tombs were Carians, and their city, Kaunos, sat on the border with Lycia, whose rock tombs these strongly resemble. The Kaunians struck outsiders as unusual even in antiquity. The Greek historian Herodotus wrote about their distinctive customs — he claimed they gathered to drink in large companies of men, women, and children together, which scandalised and fascinated his readers. A border town with its own way of doing things, in other words. Some things persist.

You can visit Kaunos itself on the far bank. A small rowing-boat ferry has crossed the river here for as long as anyone remembers, and the short ride is part of the charm. On the site you will find a theatre carved against the hillside, Roman baths, temple terraces, and a fountain house by the old harbour agora. The city climbs toward an acropolis rock with sweeping views over the channels. Give it a couple of unhurried hours.

Standing in the ruins, you will notice something odd: the sea is missing. Kaunos was a port, yet the coast now lies kilometres away. The river did that. Century after century, it carried silt down from the mountains and pushed the shoreline seaward, strangling the harbour into a marsh. Trade faded as the water retreated. The pattern is common on this coast, but few places show it as clearly — a working port slowly beached by its own river.

Follow the channels the other way and they open onto İztuzu Beach, a slender sandbar stretching over four kilometres between the river delta and the open sea. Fresh water on one side, salt on the other, fine sand in between. It is one of the Mediterranean's important nesting beaches for loggerhead sea turtles, known scientifically as Caretta caretta. On summer nights, females haul themselves up this sand to lay eggs, as their ancestors have done for a very long time.

In the late 1980s, that beach nearly became a resort. A large hotel development was planned right on the nesting sand. A British-born woman named June Haimoff — locals called her Kaptan June — had settled here in a beach hut, and she led an international campaign against the project, drawing conservationists and headlines to Dalyan. The development was stopped, and in 1988 the area received formal protection. The turtles kept their beach because people argued loudly enough.

The protection has teeth, and visitors are part of it. In nesting season, the beach closes at night so females and hatchlings are not disturbed. By day, stay off the marked zones and leave the wire cages you may see in place — they shield nests from predators. At the far end of the beach, a rehabilitation centre treats injured sea turtles, and you can visit. If you meet a turtle in the water, admire it without chasing or feeding.

The river itself runs like a slow economy. Blue crabs sidle through the shallows, mullet school in the channels, and the fishing cooperative still works the weirs near the river mouth. Boats shuttle between town, tombs, beach, and lake all day. On the lake side, thermal springs and mud baths at Sultaniye draw bathers who coat themselves grey and bake in the sun — locals credit the mud with all manner of benefits, a belief best enjoyed as tradition and a very good photograph.

At some point today, find yourself back on the water with the engine cut, drifting. The reeds hiss. The river slides. The tombs look down as they have for some twenty-four centuries, and somewhere beyond the sandbar, a turtle is circling in the swell, waiting for dark. Rulers, historians, campaigners, fishermen — everyone here worked around this river, never against it. For a few quiet minutes, you are doing exactly the same.
