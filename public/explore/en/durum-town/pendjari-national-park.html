The guide

The story

Listen first. Before the engine settles, before anyone speaks, there is a dry, papery rustle of tall grass moving in the heat. A hornbill calls somewhere in the trees. The air smells of dust, warm leaves and, faintly, smoke from distant grass fires. The ground under your feet is red laterite, hard as fired clay, and it holds the day's heat long after the sun drops. This is Pendjari, in the far northwest of Benin, and it is one of the wildest places left in West Africa.

The park takes its name from the Pendjari River. The river curves along much of the northern and western edge of the park, and for a long stretch it marks the border with Burkina Faso. Follow its course on a map and you will see a long, looping arc. That arc matters more than any road here. In the dry months, when the savanna turns gold and brittle, the river and its pools become the centre of life. Almost every animal in the park eventually comes back to the water.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. Pendjari is not an island. It is one part of a much larger protected landscape that crosses three countries — Benin, Burkina Faso and Niger. Together these parks form what is called the W-Arly-Pendjari complex. In 2017, UNESCO extended an existing World Heritage listing to cover this whole cross-border system. Pendjari had already been named a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve back in the 1980s. Animals do not see the borders. A herd grazing here today may be in another country next week.

The landscape itself is Sudanian savanna — a broad belt of grassland and scattered trees that runs across Africa south of the Sahara. Look around and you will notice the shapes. There are tall fan palms with straight grey trunks. There are wide, swollen baobabs. There are thorny acacias and low, twisted shrubs. Along the river, the scene changes. A narrow band of gallery forest grows there — taller, greener, shadier trees that drink from the riverbank. Those strips of forest are where you often find monkeys, birds and resting antelope.

To the south and east rises the Atakora range, a long line of low rocky hills. From some viewpoints you can see the escarpment as a dark wall on the horizon. It shapes the weather, the rivers and the people who live in its shadow. The hills give the park a frame. On a clear morning the light catches the ridges first, and the plain below stays blue and cool for a few minutes longer.

Now, the animals. Pendjari is one of the most important refuges for wildlife in the whole of West Africa. Elephants move through the park, sometimes in large family groups. Buffalo graze in dark herds. Hippos crowd the deeper river pools, and crocodiles lie still on the sandbanks. You may see warthogs trotting with their tails held straight up, like small flags. Baboons patrol the roadsides with an air of ownership.

The antelope are a quiet highlight. The kob is common here — a reddish, elegant antelope that often stands in groups near water. Look also for the hartebeest, with its long face and oddly bent horns. And watch for the roan antelope, one of the largest antelopes in Africa, with a grey-brown coat and a black-and-white face that looks almost painted. Many visitors come for the big cats and leave talking about the roan.

Then there are the predators. Pendjari and its neighbouring parks hold one of the last strongholds of the West African lion. These lions are a distinct and threatened population, and very few remain in the region. The park is also one of the rare places where the Northwest African cheetah may still survive, though it is extremely hard to see. Leopards, hyenas and other smaller hunters live here too. Seeing a lion is never guaranteed. That uncertainty is part of what makes a sighting here feel earned.

The park has a difficult recent chapter, and it is worth knowing. Since 2017, the conservation organisation African Parks has managed Pendjari in partnership with the government of Benin. Their work has focused on rangers, anti-poaching patrols and support for nearby communities. In recent years, armed groups active across the wider Sahel region have also reached this border area, and rangers have lost their lives here. Security conditions can change quickly. Your guide and the park authorities will know the current situation far better than any recording can.

The human story around the park is just as layered. The villages near the Atakora hills belong to several peoples, including the Batammariba, also known as the Somba. They are known for their tata houses — fortified homes of clay with small round towers, built almost like miniature castles. Families traditionally lived, stored grain and kept animals within these walls. If your route passes near the hills, you may see them standing in the fields, their earth walls the same colour as the road.

On the edge of the hills near the park, there is also the Tanougou waterfall. Water drops over smooth rock into a deep pool, and in the wetter months the falls run strongly. In the dry season the flow becomes thinner and calmer. Many travellers stop here for a swim after hours of dust. It is a reminder that this landscape is not only savanna. It also has cool rock, shade and moving water.

A few words on rhythm. The dry season, roughly from the end of the year into spring, is usually the best time for spotting animals. The grass is lower, and wildlife gathers at the waterholes. Early mornings and late afternoons bring the most movement. In the middle of the day, most animals rest in the shade, and so should you. Keep your voice low on game drives. Here, patience works better than speed.

The birds deserve their own attention. Pendjari is home to hundreds of bird species, from tiny, bright bee-eaters to large birds of prey circling on rising heat. Ground hornbills walk through the grass like slow, dark figures. Near the water, look for herons, storks and kingfishers. Even when the larger animals hide, the birds keep the park full of sound and colour.

So pause for a moment. Look at the line where the grass meets the trees. Notice how the colours change with the light — gold, then grey, then rust. Listen again for that dry rustle and the calls from the trees. When you leave, the red dust will stay on your shoes and clothes for days. Let it stay a while. It comes from one of the last great wild places in West Africa.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan game drives for early morning or late afternoon.
Visit in the dry season for better waterhole sightings.
Check current security advice with park authorities before travelling.
Bring binoculars for distant antelope and birds of prey.
Carry a scarf to keep red dust off your face.
