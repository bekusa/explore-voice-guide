The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, below the slopes of Mount Song, the city traffic is far away. You may hear wind in the pine and cypress trees, a few birds, and the crunch of gravel under your shoes. Then the pagoda comes into view. It is a tall, pale column of brick that curves gently inward as it rises, like the outline of a bullet or an unopened bud. Take a moment before you walk closer. You are looking at something people built roughly fifteen hundred years ago.

This is the Songyue Pagoda. It stands in the countryside around Dengfeng, a small city southwest of Zhengzhou, at the foot of the Song mountains. Most scholars date it to the early sixth century, during the Northern Wei dynasty. The Northern Wei ruled northern China at that time and supported Buddhism with great energy. The usual date is around the year 523. That makes this the oldest surviving brick pagoda in China. Brick pagodas came before it, but none of them still stands. This one has survived wars, earthquakes, and centuries of weather.

Now look at the shape of the base, and count its sides. Here is the first surprise. Most Chinese pagodas are square or eight-sided. This one has twelve sides. Historians know of no other surviving pagoda in China with this twelve-sided plan. Nobody can say for certain why the builders chose it. Perhaps it was an experiment from an age when the pagoda was still a fairly new form in China. Perhaps it carried a meaning that has since been lost. Either way, you are standing in front of a true one of a kind.

It also helps to know where the idea of a pagoda came from. Buddhism reached China from India and Central Asia. With it came the stupa, a domed mound that held sacred relics. Chinese builders slowly turned that mound into a tall tower and added elements from their own wooden architecture. The Songyue Pagoda sits near the start of that long change. Its rounded, swelling outline still carries something of the Indian stupa. Its stacked rooflines point toward the Chinese towers that came later.

So let your eyes travel upward. Above the tall lower section, the tower rises in fifteen tiers of eaves. They are packed tightly together, one directly on top of the next. Architects call this style a dense-eave pagoda. The upper storeys are not real floors that you could walk through. They act more like a rhythm, a series of thin brick ledges that step slowly inward. As the tiers shrink, the whole outline bends into that smooth curve. The tower reaches a height of nearly forty metres.

The curve is worth a second look, because it is very hard to make in brick. Each layer had to be set slightly inward from the one below, and the change had to stay even all the way to the top. If the masons had made mistakes, the profile would look lumpy or crooked. Instead, the line is clean and calm. Here is the second surprise. Sources describe the bricks as bound with clay mortar rather than lime. Even so, the tower has held its form for about fifteen centuries.

Now come closer to the lower body of the pagoda. At the corners you will see brick columns. Look at their bases and tops for lotus shapes. The lotus is a central Buddhist symbol. It grows from mud and opens clean, like the mind moving toward enlightenment. Between the columns there are niches. Some of them take the form of tiny one-storey pagodas, small buildings inside a building. You may also find worn reliefs of lions. In Buddhist art, lions often guard sacred places. Centuries of weather have softened these carvings, so stand close and give your eyes time.

The pagoda did not always stand alone in a quiet field. It belonged to a larger complex called Songyue Temple. Records connect the site to the Northern Wei court. It began as a mountain retreat for the imperial family. Later it became a Buddhist monastery, and in its great days it held many halls and many monks. Almost all of those buildings are gone now. The tower is the main survivor. The few structures you see around it are much later than the pagoda itself.

Think about why a court and a monastery chose this spot. Mount Song is the central peak of China's Five Sacred Mountains. For thousands of years, emperors, Daoists, Buddhists, and Confucian scholars treated these mountains as places where heaven and earth meet. Ancient Chinese thinkers even placed the centre of the world in this region. That belief is the reason for the name of the UNESCO World Heritage listing that now includes this pagoda. The listing groups it with other old monuments around Dengfeng under the title of the centre of heaven and earth.

Some of those neighbours are famous. Shaolin Temple, known around the world for its martial arts tradition, is not far away in the same mountains. There is also the Songyang Academy, an old centre of Confucian learning. Many visitors rush to Shaolin and skip this quieter site. That is a shame, but it is also lucky for you. Here you can often stand with the tower almost to yourself. You can read its shape slowly, without crowds in front of you.

The pagoda is a monument to look at, not a tower to climb. Inside, the structure is hollow, like a tall chimney. In the old temple, the tower likely served as a focus for devotion. Monks and pilgrims would walk around a pagoda in a slow circle as a form of prayer. That practice is old and widespread in Buddhism. You can follow the same path today. Walk once around the base, and watch how the twelve faces turn past you, one after another.

As you walk, notice how the light changes on the brick. In the early morning or late afternoon, sunlight falls low across the surface. Each brick ledge then casts a thin shadow, and the fifteen tiers stand out like lines on a page. At midday the tower looks flatter and paler. On grey days the mountains behind it soften into haze, and the pagoda becomes a dark, quiet shape against the sky. Every visit can look a little different.

Before you leave, step back to where you first saw it. Look again at that soft, rising curve. Fifteen centuries ago, people pressed clay, fired bricks, and set them one by one, aiming for something higher than themselves. Their temple has mostly gone, and their names are lost. But their tower is still here, in the wind below Mount Song. As you walk away, turn around once more. Watch the pagoda grow smaller against the mountains, still keeping its patient shape.
