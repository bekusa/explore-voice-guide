The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the beech and pine trees around you, and the gravel crunches under your feet. Somewhere below, a car passes on the road to Mödling, and then the forest is quiet again. Ahead of you, pale grey stone rises straight out of a ridge of limestone, as if the rock had grown a castle. This is Liechtenstein Castle. It sits on the eastern edge of the Vienna Woods, just above the small town of Maria Enzersdorf.

Before we go any further, here is the first surprise. Most people know Liechtenstein as a tiny country between Switzerland and Austria. But that country takes its name from a family, and the family takes its name from this castle. The word means something like 'light stone'. Look at the rock under the walls and you can see why. In the right light, the limestone glows almost white against the dark green of the trees.

The story begins in the first half of the twelfth century. A nobleman named Hugo built a fortress on this outcrop, and he began to call himself 'of Liechtenstein'. At that time this land was a frontier. It was the eastern march of the Holy Roman Empire, a border zone that faced the plains of Hungary. A castle on a high rock gave its lord a view across the approaches to Vienna, and a safe place to retreat.

The oldest part of the castle that you can still recognise is the chapel. It is Romanesque, which means it belongs to the style of building that came before the Gothic. Romanesque buildings use thick walls, small windows and round arches, not pointed ones. When you look for the chapel, look for those rounded forms. They are among the earliest surviving pieces of this site, and they connect you directly to Hugo's generation.

Here is the second surprise. The Liechtenstein family did not keep their namesake castle for long. Over the following centuries, the property passed out of their hands. The family grew powerful elsewhere, in Moravia and in the service of the Habsburg emperors. They gathered great estates, and much later they bought the territory that became the Principality of Liechtenstein. For a long time, the castle that gave them their name belonged to other people.

Those centuries were hard on the walls. During the Ottoman sieges of Vienna, in 1529 and again in 1683, raiders swept through the countryside around the city. Castles like this one were burned and damaged. By the eighteenth century, much of the fortress stood as a ruin on its rock. Trees grew in the courtyards, and travellers saw broken walls rather than a working stronghold.

Then, in the early nineteenth century, the family came back. In 1807, Prince Johann the First of Liechtenstein acquired the castle and the land around it. He was a soldier and a man of the Romantic age, and he loved landscapes with ruins and towers. Across these hills he also shaped parks and built decorative structures. As you walk the surrounding trails, you may notice other follies and lookout points that belong to the same romantic vision.

The castle you see today is largely the result of a later rebuilding, in the late nineteenth century. Architects and craftsmen restored the ruin and added new parts in a historic style. This was a common idea in that period. People wanted castles to look the way they imagined the Middle Ages, with towers, battlements and steep roofs. So when you look at the walls, you are reading two stories at once — real medieval stone, and a nineteenth-century dream of the medieval.

Try to spot the difference as you walk around. The lower masonry tends to be rougher and more irregular, with stones of many sizes fitted into the rock. Higher up, some of the work is cleaner and more regular. The joints are neater and the shapes are sharper. Neither part is fake. Both are honest records of the people who built them, eight centuries apart in some places.

Notice also how closely the castle hugs the rock. The builders did not flatten the summit first. They followed its edges and let the stone become part of the defence. In places it is hard to say where the natural cliff ends and the wall begins. This is one of the most satisfying details of the site. Walk slowly along the base and follow that line with your eyes.

The castle still belongs to the princely family of Liechtenstein. It is not a royal residence today, but it remains a family monument, a kind of ancestral address. The interior is usually seen on a guided visit, and the courtyards are often used for events. If you go inside, look for the small scale of the rooms. Medieval castles were cramped, cold and practical, even after a Romantic restoration.

After the castle, the forest is worth your time. Paths lead out from here into the Vienna Woods, a long chain of wooded hills that wraps around the west and south of the Austrian capital. For centuries, Viennese families came out here to walk, picnic and breathe cleaner air. On weekends you will still meet hikers with sticks and dogs, heading for the next viewpoint or a country inn.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look up at the walls. Feel the cool air that comes off the stone, even on a warm day. This rock carried a family name across Europe, to a country that most people never connect with this quiet hillside. As you walk back down through the trees, listen again for the wind in the beeches. It is the same sound Hugo heard when he first called this place Liechtenstein.
