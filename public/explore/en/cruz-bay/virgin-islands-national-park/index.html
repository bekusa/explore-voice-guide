The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the warm wind there is a dry rattle of palm fronds, and somewhere below you the soft hiss of water moving over sand. The air smells of salt and sun-warmed leaves. The light here has a weight to it, bright and white on the rocks and green in the hills. This is Virgin Islands National Park. Most of the island of Saint John lies inside its borders, and from the edge of Cruz Bay the park begins almost at your feet.

It helps to know the shape of the place. Saint John is small, steep, and rugged. Ridges climb sharply from the shore, and the coast breaks into dozens of bays, each one curved like a cupped hand. The national park covers roughly three-fifths of the island's land. It also protects a large area of the surrounding sea. So when you look at those green slopes and that clear water, you are looking at one protected landscape, from the hilltops down to the reef.

The park exists because of one unusual gift. In the 1950s, the conservationist and philanthropist Laurance Rockefeller bought up large parts of Saint John. He then donated that land to the United States government. The park was established in 1956. A few years later, in the early 1960s, its boundaries grew to include offshore waters. Few national parks began this way, with a single private buyer assembling an island's wild heart and then giving it away.

But the quiet you hear today hides a hard past. In the eighteenth century, Saint John was a Danish colony. Planters cleared these steep hills for sugar cane and cotton. Enslaved Africans did that work, cutting terraces into slopes that seem impossible to farm. Today the forest has returned, and it is easy to imagine these hills were always wild. They were not. Almost every green ridge you see was once stripped bare for the plantations.

In 1733, enslaved people on Saint John rose up against the Danish planters. The revolt was one of the earliest large slave rebellions in the Americas. Rebels held much of the island for months before colonial forces crushed the uprising. The story is still remembered on the island today. Danish rule continued until 1917, when the United States bought these islands from Denmark. That is why many place names here still carry Danish and Dutch echoes.

The clearest window into that era is the ruin of the Annaberg sugar plantation on the north shore. Here you can walk among stone walls made from coral, local rock, and imported brick. A round windmill tower still stands on the hill. Once, wind turned its sails to crush cane. Nearby are the remains of the boiling house, where cane juice became sugar and molasses. Stand in the shade of those walls and think of the heat of the fires inside.

The island's human story goes back much further. Long before Europeans arrived, Indigenous peoples of the Caribbean lived on Saint John. Along the Reef Bay Trail, carvings are cut into the rock beside a small freshwater pool. These petroglyphs are usually linked to the Taíno, the Indigenous people who lived across much of the Caribbean. On a still day, the carvings reflect in the water below. Archaeologists have also studied an Indigenous site at Cinnamon Bay, close to the beach.

Now turn toward the sea, because half of this park is underwater. The bays shelter coral reefs, seagrass meadows, and rocky shallows. At Trunk Bay, the park created an underwater snorkel trail with markers that teach you about the reef as you swim. In the seagrass you may meet green sea turtles grazing slowly, ignoring the people above them. Parrotfish nibble at coral, and much of the pale sand on these beaches passes through their bodies.

Here is something many visitors never realise. On land, the only native mammals of these islands are bats. Every other mammal you see came with people. The deer, the goats, the donkeys that wander the roads, and the mongoose darting through the brush were all introduced. The mongoose was brought to the Caribbean to control rats in the cane fields. It ate birds' eggs and reptiles instead, and it is still a problem today.

The forest itself changes as you move around the island. On the wetter north side, the hills hold taller, shadier woodland. On the drier south and east, you find cactus, thorny scrub, and low trees bent by the wind. In one short drive you can pass from damp green shade into country that feels almost like desert. Watch for termite nests on tree trunks, dark and lumpy, and for lizards sunning on warm stones.

This landscape has also faced storms. In 2017, two powerful hurricanes, Irma and Maria, struck the Virgin Islands within weeks of each other. They tore away forest canopy, damaged trails and buildings, and battered the reefs. Recovery has taken years of patient work by park staff, volunteers, and island residents. If you notice bare trunks among new growth, or rebuilt boardwalks, you are seeing that recovery in progress.

A good way to begin is the park visitor centre near the ferry dock in Cruz Bay. Rangers can tell you which trails are open and which beaches are calm today. From town, the North Shore Road winds along the coast toward the bays. The curves are tight and steep, and traffic drives on the left. Take it slowly. Every bend opens a new view of turquoise water and small cays offshore.

Before you go, look around you once more. Notice the colour of the water where it meets the sand, pale green, then blue, then deep navy over the reef. Notice the old stones in the hills, if you can find them through the trees. And notice the quiet. This island was cleared, worked, fought over, and then given back to the forest and the sea. What you are standing in now is that second chance.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the visitor centre near the Cruz Bay ferry dock.
Use reef-safe mineral sunscreen to protect the coral.
Never stand on or touch coral while snorkelling.
Carry plenty of water on hot, exposed trails.
Drive the North Shore Road slowly and keep left.
