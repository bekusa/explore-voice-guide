The guide

The story

Stand at the railing and listen. At first there is only wind in the beech trees behind you. Then, if you lean slightly forward, you notice something else — cool, damp air rising from below, with a faint smell of wet stone and moss. It is a little like opening a cellar door on a warm afternoon. Below the metal walkway, pale limestone walls drop away almost straight down, into green shadow and, far at the bottom, still water.

This is Macocha, a collapse sinkhole in the heart of the Moravian Karst, close to the village of Vilémovice in the Czech Republic. Its depth is usually given as around 138 metres. That makes it one of the deepest openings of its kind in Central Europe. A karst landscape, if the word is new to you, is a region of soluble rock, usually limestone, where water has dissolved caves, tunnels and hollows beneath the surface.

How did a hole this size appear? Not through a single dramatic event that anyone saw. Over a very long time, water moved through cracks in the limestone and slowly widened a large underground cave. The roof of that cave grew thinner and weaker. Eventually it gave way and fell into the space below. What you see now is the open sky above a former cavern, with the rubble of that old ceiling buried somewhere under the floor.

The rock itself is very old. The limestone of the Moravian Karst formed from the remains of sea creatures, in a warm shallow sea that covered this region hundreds of millions of years ago. So when you look at these grey and cream walls, you are looking at an ancient seabed, lifted up and cut open. Streaks of darker colour on the cliffs come from water, minerals and the slow growth of moss and lichen.

Now, the name. Macocha means stepmother in Czech, and it comes from a story that locals still tell. Legend has it that a widowed father remarried, and his new wife wanted her own child to inherit everything. One day she led her stepson to the edge of the abyss and pushed him over. According to the tale, the boy did not fall all the way. A branch caught him, and his cries were heard.

The legend continues. Villagers came, pulled the boy back to safety, and learned what had happened. In the version most people know, they threw the stepmother into the gorge as punishment. It is folklore, not documented history, and there is no record that proves it. But the story stuck so firmly that it gave the place its name. Many visitors look at the cliff edge a little differently after hearing it.

For centuries, the bottom of this gorge was a mystery. People could see water and stones, but reaching them was dangerous. In the early eighteenth century, a monk was lowered down on a rope to explore and report on what lay below. Later descents followed, each one adding a little more knowledge. The real breakthrough came in the early twentieth century, when the explorer and scientist Karel Absolon led work that connected Macocha with the caves along the Punkva River.

The Punkva is the hidden river of this landscape. On the surface, streams in the Moravian Karst often disappear into the ground, swallowed by sinkholes. Underground, the water finds its own routes through the rock. The Punkva passes through the bottom of Macocha and then continues through cave passages before it comes out into daylight further down the valley. Those two small lakes you can see at the bottom are part of that same underground system.

Here is something many visitors miss. The gorge has its own small climate. Cold air is heavier than warm air, so it sinks and collects at the bottom and stays there. The result is a kind of upside-down world. Down at the floor, botanists have recorded plants that normally grow in cooler, higher places, while the warmer rim supports plants that prefer gentler conditions. The deeper you look, the colder the world becomes.

There are two main viewing platforms on the rim. The upper bridge sits higher and gives you the widest view across the opening and down the walls. The lower bridge pushes out further over the drop, so you look more directly into the depths. Many people visit one and skip the other. It is worth walking between them. The short path through the forest shows you how suddenly the ground simply ends.

A second surprise: you can also see Macocha from the bottom. The guided tour of the Punkva Caves leads visitors through decorated underground halls, with stalactites hanging from the ceiling and stalagmites rising from the floor. The route reaches the floor of the gorge, so you can stand where the legend says the boy almost fell, and look up at a small circle of sky. Part of the tour continues by boat along the underground river.

The area around the gorge is protected as part of the Moravian Karst landscape area. That is why the paths are marked and the railings are firm, and why you should stay on the official trails. The cliff edges can be loose, and the plants on the rim are fragile. Around you, the forest is mostly beech and other broadleaf trees. In spring it is bright green, and in autumn the leaves turn copper and gold.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute at the railing. Notice the change in temperature on your face as the cool air comes up from below. Look at the far wall and try to follow one crack from the top to the water. Listen for birds circling inside the walls, and for the drip of water somewhere you cannot see. When you walk back through the trees, remember that the river is still moving under your feet — slowly, quietly, shaping the next cave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit both the upper and lower viewing bridges.
Book Punkva Caves tickets ahead in busy summer months.
Bring a warm layer for the cool cave tour.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp paths.
Stay on marked trails near the cliff edges.
