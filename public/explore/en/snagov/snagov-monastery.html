The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against reeds, a bell sounds somewhere ahead, and wooden planks shift slightly under your feet. Snagov Monastery stands on a small island in a long, quiet lake north of Bucharest. You reach it by crossing the water, and that short crossing matters. The world of traffic and roadside cafés falls away behind you. In front of you, through the trees, rises a compact church of warm red brick.

The island setting is no accident. For centuries, monks in Wallachia, the historic southern region of Romania, looked for places set apart from the world. A lake island offered silence and a natural wall of water. It also offered safety. In uncertain times, a monastery that could only be reached by boat was much harder to raid, and its treasures and books were easier to protect.

The monastic community here is old. Tradition places its beginnings in the fourteenth century, when Wallachia was still a young principality. Over the following generations, princes gave land and gifts, and the monastery grew in importance. Very little of that earliest complex survives. Wars, fires and the slow work of the lake's damp air have taken most of it. What you see today belongs mainly to later rebuilding.

The church in front of you dates largely from the early sixteenth century, the time of Prince Neagoe Basarab. He was one of the great church builders of Wallachia, a ruler who put his faith and his wealth into stone and brick. Look at the outline. The building is not tall, but it is solid and balanced, with towers rising above the roof. This is a typical Wallachian form, shaped by Byzantine tradition and adapted to local materials.

Notice the brick itself. Builders in this region often used brick as decoration, not only as structure. Rows of brick are set in patterns, and the surfaces catch the light differently through the day. In the morning, the walls look almost pink. In the late afternoon, they turn deeper and redder. The plain, honest material gives the church a calm, grounded feeling.

Now to the name that brings most visitors across the lake... Vlad the Third, the Wallachian prince known as Vlad the Impaler. Abroad, he is better known through the novel that borrowed his family name, Dracula. Vlad ruled Wallachia in the fifteenth century, in a brutal age of war with the Ottoman Empire. He was killed in battle, and the place of his burial was never clearly recorded.

According to a long local tradition, the monks of Snagov received his body and buried him here, near the altar. Inside the church, a stone slab marks the spot that tradition links to him, and it often carries flowers or a portrait. It is important to be honest with you. This is a tradition, not a proven fact. Historians still debate where Vlad truly lies, and some point to another monastery, at Comana, which he is said to have founded.

In the 1930s, archaeologists excavated beneath the floor here, hoping to settle the question. The accounts of what they found are confusing and sometimes contradictory, and no remains were clearly identified as Vlad's. So the mystery stays open. Perhaps that is why people keep coming. Standing beside the slab, you are standing at the meeting point of history, rumour and literature — and no one can tell you with certainty where one ends and the next begins.

Here is a story many visitors miss, and it has a surprising link to Georgia. At the end of the seventeenth century, a monk named Antim, born in Georgia, led this monastery. He is remembered in Romania as Antim Ivireanu, which means Antim the Iberian, an old name for Georgia. He had been taken from his homeland as a young man, and he later reached Wallachia as a skilled craftsman and scholar.

At Snagov, Antim ran a printing press. From this small island, books went out in several languages, including Romanian, Greek and Arabic. Some of those books served Orthodox Christians far beyond Wallachia, across the Middle East. Antim later became Metropolitan, the head of the church in Wallachia, and he did much to bring the Romanian language into church services. So this quiet island was once a working centre of print and learning.

Step inside, if the church is open, and let your eyes adjust to the dim light. The walls carry old paintings of saints, prophets and scenes from the life of Christ. Time and smoke have darkened many of them, but faces and robes still appear when you look closely. The air often smells of candle wax and old wood. Speak softly. This is still a living place of prayer, not only a monument.

Outside again, walk slowly around the church. The bell tower, the trees and the edge of the water frame the building from different angles. From some points, you can see the lake through the branches, flat and grey or bright and blue, depending on the sky. The island is small, so you can take your time. Nothing here asks you to hurry.

Before you cross back, pause for a moment at the water's edge. Behind you is a church built by a prince, rebuilt by monks, and filled with the work of a Georgian printer. Somewhere near its altar, a famous and frightening ruler may or may not rest. In front of you, the lake moves quietly, as it has through all of it. Listen once more to the reeds... and then begin your walk back to the shore.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross to the island on foot and take it slowly.
Dress modestly; this is an active Orthodox monastery.
Bring insect repellent for summer evenings by the lake.
Visit late afternoon to see the brick turn deep red.
Keep voices low inside during services and prayers.
