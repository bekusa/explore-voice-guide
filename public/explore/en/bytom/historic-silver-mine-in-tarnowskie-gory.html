The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the air. It is cool and damp, and it smells of wet stone. Somewhere ahead, water drips onto rock in a slow, steady rhythm. Your helmet feels strange at first, and then it feels necessary. The ceiling drops low in places, and the walls are rough and pale under the lamps. You are now underground, inside a mine that people have worked, flooded, drained, and explored for centuries.

You are in Tarnowskie Góry, a small town a short drive north of Bytom in Upper Silesia, in southern Poland. Most people link this region with coal, with tall pit towers and heavy industry. But long before coal ruled here, the ground around this town was valued for something else. Under the fields lay ore that held lead and silver. This mine is the place where that older story is easiest to see, touch, and hear.

The main ore here is galena. It is a heavy, grey, metallic mineral, and it is the most important ore of lead. Galena often carries small amounts of silver as well. In this area it sits inside dolomite, a hard, pale rock similar to limestone. Miners followed the ore through this rock by hand. Legend has it that the rush began when a local farmer turned up a shining lump of ore with his plough. Whether or not that happened, the tale is still told with pride.

What is clear is that mining shaped the town itself. In the sixteenth century, prospectors and miners arrived in large numbers. Settlements grew around the shafts, and a town took form above the workings. Mining law, trade, and the money from silver gave the place its early character. When you walk the old town later, remember that its streets grew up because of the tunnels beneath your feet right now.

Underground, the biggest enemy was never the rock. It was water. The deeper the miners went, the more water flowed into the workings. Pumps powered by people and horses could only do so much. Over time, flooding made many of the deeper ore bodies impossible to reach. The mines declined, and for a long period the work here slowed down. The ore was still there... but the water held it.

The revival came in the late eighteenth century, when this part of Silesia was under Prussian rule. The state invested in mining again, and engineers brought a new tool — the steam engine. Steam-powered pumps could lift water on a scale that muscle never could. This mine became one of the places where steam power was used early in continental Europe. Here is the first surprise for many visitors: the history of the industrial machine age runs through this quiet town.

The miners also cut long drainage tunnels, called adits. These are nearly level passages that carry water away by gravity. Over time, the people here built a whole system of shafts, tunnels, and pumps to control water underground. That system is the reason this site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2017. And here is the second surprise. Water pumped from these mines was later used to supply drinking water to towns in the region.

As you walk the galleries, look closely at the walls. In places you can see marks left by tools, short grooves and scars in the dolomite. Some passages are wide and tall, where ore bodies were removed. Others are narrow, and you may need to bend your head. Timber props and old equipment show how the work was done. Figures and displays along the route show miners at work, but the rock around them is real and very old.

For many visitors, the most memorable part comes next. Part of the route runs along a flooded tunnel, and you travel it by boat. The boats are simple and low. The guide moves the boat along through the dark, and the only sounds are the water and the voices of the group. The ceiling sits close above your head. It is a slow, calm ride, and it shows clearly how much water these miners had to manage.

Every mine has its stories, and Silesia has a famous one. Locals tell of the Skarbnik, a spirit who guards the treasures of the underground. In the tales, he rewards honest, hardworking miners and punishes the greedy or the careless. Some say he warns workers before a collapse. This is folklore, not history, but it carries something true. Underground work was dangerous, and miners needed both rules and hope to go back down each day.

Back on the surface, the mine grounds hold more to see. There is an outdoor collection of old steam engines and machines from the region's industrial past. Iron wheels, boilers, and pistons stand in the open air, and children often enjoy them as much as adults. The town also keeps its mining traditions alive. Each year, usually in September, it holds a festival called Gwarki, named after an old local word for miners, with parades and costumes.

If this visit leaves you curious, there is a second underground site nearby. It is called the Black Trout Adit, and it is part of the same old drainage system. There, too, visitors travel by boat through a long, dark tunnel cut to carry water away. The name comes from the dark trout that people say once lived in the stream near its outlet. It is a separate visit, so plan time for both if you can.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment wherever you are. Listen to the water, if you can still hear it. Feel the cold on your hands, and look once more at the pale rock. People cut these spaces by hand, by candlelight, for silver and lead. When you step back into daylight, notice the quiet fields and the ordinary streets of Tarnowskie Góry. Under them, the tunnels and the water are still there.
