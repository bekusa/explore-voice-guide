The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the water in a steady, warm push. Tires hum across concrete joints in a slow rhythm. Below, the lake moves brown-green and wide. Above, pale concrete towers rise in a line that runs toward the far shore. This is the General Rafael Urdaneta Bridge, and from where you stand it seems to go on almost forever.

That feeling is accurate. The bridge runs for roughly eight and a half kilometres across the narrow neck of Lake Maracaibo. That neck is the strait that links the lake to the Gulf of Venezuela and the Caribbean beyond. On this side is the city of Maracaibo. On the far side is the eastern shore of the lake. For most of the city's history, that short stretch of water was a real barrier.

Before the bridge, people crossed by ferry. Cars, trucks, and passengers waited at the docks, sometimes for a long time. For a city this important to Venezuela, that was a problem. Maracaibo sat at the heart of the country's oil industry, yet it had no fixed road link to the rest of the nation. The bridge changed that when it opened in 1962. Suddenly the city was joined to the national road network by solid ground.

The man behind the design was the Italian engineer Riccardo Morandi. He was known for his work with prestressed concrete. That is concrete squeezed by steel tendons under tension, so it can carry loads that ordinary concrete cannot. At a time when most long bridges used steel, Morandi built this crossing mostly from concrete. When it opened, it was among the longest prestressed concrete bridges in the world.

Look at the tallest section, near the middle. Here you will see the shape that makes this bridge easy to recognise. Each main tower looks like a tall concrete frame. Angled concrete stays reach down from the top of each tower to hold the road deck. It is an early form of the cable-stayed bridge, a design where straight cables run directly from towers to the deck. Here, though, Morandi wrapped his stays in concrete, which gives them their thick, solid look.

There are five of these large spans in the centre, each about 235 metres long. Why so tall and so wide just there? The answer sits on the water. Lake Maracaibo is a working lake. Oil tankers and cargo ships pass through the strait to reach terminals and fields inside the lake. The high central spans give those ships room to pass underneath. The rest of the bridge sits lower, on a long row of shorter supports.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The pattern you see from shore is not perfectly uniform. The road climbs gently toward the high central section and then falls away again. From a distance, the bridge looks a little like a long, low wave with a crest in the middle. That shape is pure function. It is the profile needed to lift the road over the shipping channel and bring it back down.

The bridge carries the name of General Rafael Urdaneta. He was born in Maracaibo and became one of the senior officers in Venezuela's wars of independence from Spain, in the early nineteenth century. He fought alongside Simón Bolívar, the leader who freed much of northern South America from Spanish rule. Naming the crossing after him tied a modern engineering project to a local hero. For people here, it is simply called the bridge, or el puente.

Now for a darker chapter. In 1964, only a couple of years after the opening, an oil tanker lost control in the strait at night. It struck the bridge and brought down several sections of the road deck. Vehicles fell into the water, and people lost their lives. The damaged spans were rebuilt. The accident remains part of the bridge's story and is a reminder of how close the shipping lane runs to these supports.

The second surprise reaches far beyond Venezuela. Morandi used a similar concrete-stay design for a bridge in Genoa, Italy, that opened later in the 1960s. In 2018, a section of that Genoa bridge collapsed, killing dozens of people. After that disaster, engineers around the world looked again at bridges of this type. So when you look at these towers, you are looking at a close relative of one of Europe's most discussed structures.

For daily life in Maracaibo, the bridge is less a monument and more a road. Families cross it to visit relatives on the eastern shore. Trucks move goods. Workers commute. Locals often treat it as a symbol of the city and of the state of Zulia, which surrounds the lake. You may see its outline on signs, on souvenirs, and in local artwork. In the city's imagination, it sits next to the lake itself.

The best way to appreciate the bridge is from the shore, not from the deck. It is a fast motorway, so it is not a place to walk or stop. Along the Maracaibo waterfront you can see its long line stretch across the horizon. In the early morning, haze often softens the far end. In the late afternoon, low sun lights the towers from the west and throws long shadows across the water.

Pay attention to the lake too. Small fishing boats sometimes cross near the supports. Birds gather on the water and on the concrete. The colour of the lake shifts with the light, from grey to green to gold. The heat here is real for much of the year, and the breeze off the water is one of the few things that softens it. It helps explain why so much of the city turns toward the shore.

So take one more look. Follow the line of concrete from the near bank to the high towers, and then on toward the faint far shore. Listen to the traffic hum and the wind across the water. For decades, this was a crossing made by boat and patience. Today it is a single long line of concrete. As you walk away, notice how often it stays in view, sitting low and quiet on the edge of the lake.

Practical tips

Do not stop or walk on the bridge itself.
