The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the wall beside you. The stone is rough, uneven, and warm if the sun has been on it. Beneath your feet, the walkway along the top of the ramparts is narrow. In most places there is no railing at all. On one side, you see terracotta roofs and white walls. On the other side, the green countryside of western Portugal rolls away towards the coast. Welcome to the Castle of Óbidos, and to the walled town that has grown up inside it.

Before we go further, a word of care. These walls were built for soldiers, not for tourists. The path is uneven, the drops are real, and the stone can be slippery after rain. Walk slowly, let people pass at the wider points, and keep a hand free. If heights make you uneasy, there is no shame in enjoying Óbidos from the streets below. The town is just as rewarding at ground level, and you will hear every part of this guide just the same.

People fortified this hilltop long before Portugal existed as a country. Its position made it valuable. It is a limestone ridge rising above farmland, with long views in every direction. In the centuries before Portugal was founded, Muslim rulers held much of this region, and they knew the value of this hill. The castle you see today is the result of many rebuildings, layer upon layer. Look closely at the stonework, and you will see different sizes of stone and different styles of joints, side by side.

The turning point came in 1148. That year, Afonso Henriques, the first King of Portugal, took Óbidos from its Moorish defenders. It was part of a long campaign to push the border of his new kingdom south. Later kings strengthened and extended the walls. They added towers and gates, and they made the keep stronger. What began as a frontier fortress slowly became something else. It became a protected town, where people lived, traded, prayed, and raised families inside a ring of stone.

Now, here is the story that gives Óbidos its nickname. In the thirteenth century, King Dinis married Isabel of Aragon. Tradition says the young queen loved this town, and the king gave it to her as a wedding gift. Isabel was later honoured as a saint, and she is still remembered across Portugal. After her, Óbidos was often given to Portuguese queens as part of their personal property. For this reason, many people still call it the town of the queens.

That royal connection did not end with Isabel. In the sixteenth century, a queen paid for an aqueduct to bring fresh water to the town. You can still see its arches stretching across the fields outside the walls. And here is a detail most visitors miss. In 1441, the young King Afonso the Fifth married his cousin Isabel in the Church of Santa Maria, inside these walls. The bride and groom were both still children. A royal wedding was a political event first, and a personal one later.

So how did a medieval fortress become a place where people sleep? In 1755, a great earthquake struck Portugal. It caused terrible damage in Lisbon, and it also cracked and damaged the castle here. For a long time, parts of the castle stood in poor condition. Then, in the twentieth century, the state restored the castle and opened it as a pousada. A pousada is a Portuguese state hotel, often set inside a historic building. Today, guests can sleep within the old royal residence itself.

Let's walk down into the town. The main gate is called the Porta da Vila. It is built with a bend, so attackers could not charge straight through it. Stop inside the passage and look up. The walls and the vaulted ceiling are covered with blue and white tiles. In Portugal, these painted ceramic tiles are called azulejos. Above you, there is also a small balcony that works as a chapel. Musicians often play in this passage, because the stone vault carries the sound so well.

From the gate, the main street runs through the town. It is called Rua Direita, which means Straight Street, although it is not very straight. The houses are whitewashed and trimmed with bands of bright blue or ochre yellow. Bougainvillea climbs the walls in deep pink and purple. Cobblestones press through the soles of your shoes. Shops sell ceramics, cork goods, and books. In the late afternoon, the smell of cherry liqueur and chocolate often drifts out of open doorways.

That cherry liqueur is called ginjinha. It is made from sour cherries, and it is a local favourite. In Óbidos, people often drink it from a small cup made of dark chocolate. You drink the liqueur, and then you eat the cup. It is a small ritual, and it is very much part of a visit here. If you do not drink alcohol, the chocolate alone is still worth trying. Many shops along the main street sell both.

Here is a second surprise. This small medieval town is a UNESCO City of Literature. It earned that title in 2015. Look around and you will find bookshops in unexpected places. One bookshop sits inside a former church. Another shares space with a fresh produce market. Each year, Óbidos also hosts a literary festival. So the town is not only a museum of stone. People still use it as a place to read, write, and talk about ideas.

Óbidos also changes with the seasons. In summer, the town holds a medieval market, with costumes, music, and food cooked over open fires. In spring, it has often hosted a chocolate festival. Around Christmas, a festive village takes over parts of the town. In 2007, the castle was chosen as one of the Seven Wonders of Portugal, in a national public vote. On a festival weekend, the narrow streets fill quickly. On a quiet weekday morning, you may have long stretches of wall almost to yourself.

Before you leave, return to a high point on the walls and look west. Today the land runs green towards the sea. But historians believe the coastline and the nearby Óbidos Lagoon once came much closer to this hill. Boats could reach closer to the town than they can today. Over centuries, silt filled the low ground, and the water moved back. The fortress once watched over water. Today it watches over fields, vineyards, and orchards.

Take one more moment before you go. Listen to the swallows above the towers, the footsteps on cobblestones, and the voices from the street below. Feel the wind that moves along the top of the wall. Soldiers stood on these walls, and queens once owned this town. Now ordinary travellers like you walk here. As you head back through the tiled gate, look up one last time at the blue and white walls. Then step out into the present day.

Practical tips
