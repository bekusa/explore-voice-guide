The guide

The story

The boat engine cuts out, and the first thing you notice is the quiet. Water slaps softly against the hull. Then you climb. The path rises through green forest on a limestone headland, and the air grows warm and damp. Near the top, a wide mouth opens in the rock. Cool air flows out of it, and it smells of earth and old stone. You are standing at the entrance to Tabon Cave.

This headland is called Lipuun Point, on the western coast of Palawan, near the town of Quezon. It is not a single cave. It is a whole complex of caves cut into the limestone, often counted at around two hundred. Only some of them have been explored by archaeologists. The main chamber, the one in front of you, is the one that changed how the Philippines understands its own past.

Here is the story. In 1962, an American anthropologist named Robert Fox led a team from the National Museum of the Philippines to this site. They began to dig through the cave floor, layer by layer. Among the finds were fragments of a human skull and a jawbone. These remains became known as Tabon Man. They are among the oldest human remains ever found in the Philippines.

How old are they? Early estimates placed the bones at around twenty thousand years. Later scientific dating pushed that figure much further back. Researchers now place the skull fragment at roughly 47,000 years old. Think about that for a moment. People stood in this same opening, looking out at the same sea, long before farming existed anywhere on Earth.

Now look at the shape of the cave around you. The ceiling is high, and the floor is broad and fairly level. Light reaches deep inside from the wide entrance. For early people, this was a good home. It was dry, sheltered from storms, and close to the sea and the forest. Excavations found stone tools and the remains of cooking fires in the deposits. These traces show that people returned here again and again over thousands of years.

Here is something many visitors miss. The name Tabon may not come from people at all. It comes from a bird. The Tabon scrubfowl is a ground-dwelling bird found on Palawan. Locals say these birds once nested in large numbers around the caves. The scrubfowl has an unusual habit. It does not sit on its eggs. Instead, it buries them in mounds of soil and rotting leaves, and the heat of the mound keeps the eggs warm.

The caves held more than everyday life. For a much later period, they also served as a place for the dead. Across the complex, archaeologists found burial jars made of clay. In this tradition, the bones of the dead were placed inside a jar after the body had decomposed. Archaeologists call this a secondary burial. The jars were then set in the caves, often with offerings such as beads, pottery, and ornaments.

The most famous of these jars came from Manunggul Cave, which is part of this same complex. It is called the Manunggul Jar. It dates to the late Neolithic period, nearly three thousand years ago. Its lid carries a small, beautiful sculpture. Two human figures sit in a boat. One sits at the back and steers. The other sits in front, with arms folded across the chest.

What does the boat mean? Many scholars read it as a journey of the soul. The figure in front may be the dead person, and the one at the back may be a guide, taking the soul across the water to the afterlife. Boats and sea travel run through the cultures of the islands. So the image makes sense here, on a headland above the open sea. The jar is now a national treasure of the Philippines. It once appeared on the country's thousand peso banknote.

The original jar is not in the cave today. It is kept at the National Museum in Manila. In fact, most of the important finds from Tabon left the site long ago for study and safekeeping. So what you see on the cave walls and floor is mostly bare rock. Look closely at the floor, though. In places you may see marks where the ground was dug and studied. The cave itself is the artefact now.

Back in the town of Quezon, near the jetty, there is a small site museum connected to the caves. It is worth visiting before or after the boat trip. The museum displays replicas and explanations of the finds, and it helps you picture the deep timeline of this place. Many people call Tabon the cradle of Philippine civilisation. That is a big phrase. Standing here, you can see why it stuck.

Take a moment to listen inside the cave. Sound behaves differently here. Your voice softens and travels. Somewhere you may hear water dripping, or the wings of bats or swiftlets moving near the ceiling. The light changes as you walk deeper. Near the entrance it is bright and green from the forest. Further in, it turns grey, then dark. Mind your footing on the uneven stone.

The caves are a protected site, and that matters. Every layer of soil here may hold information that science has not yet read. So stay on the marked paths. Do not touch or scratch the walls, and take nothing with you except photographs. The value of Tabon is not in treasure. It is in context, in the exact place where each bone, tool, and shard was found.

Before you leave, turn around and face the entrance. Look out through that wide opening toward the trees and the bright sea beyond. This is the view people saw from here for tens of thousands of years. The coastline has changed, and the world has changed completely. But the frame of rock around the view is the same. As you walk back down to the boat, listen to the birds in the forest. One of them may be the scrubfowl that gave this place its name.

Limestone formations along the path up Lipuun Point.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the site museum in Quezon before the boat ride.
Wear shoes with grip for the climb and damp stone.
Bring water; the walk up the headland is humid.
Bring a torch for the darker inner chambers.
Hire a local guide at the jetty for context.
