The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. It moves across the high grassland without much to stop it, bending the yellow grass in long waves. Underfoot, the ground is soft and uneven, dotted with small hollows. And then, scattered across the slope in front of you, are the jars. Hundreds of hollow stone vessels lean at odd angles, some upright, some tipped on their sides, some cracked open. They look as if a giant left a picnic here and never came back.

You are standing on the Xiangkhouang Plateau in northern Laos, near the town of Phonsavan. This is the Plain of Jars. The name sounds like one place, but it describes a whole landscape. Across this province, researchers have recorded jars at many dozens of separate sites, and thousands of jars in total. Some sit on hilltops, some on gentle ridges, some half lost in forest. The site you are likely walking now, often called Site 1, is the largest and easiest to reach.

Look closely at the nearest jar. Most of these vessels are carved from sandstone, and you can see the grain of the rock in the walls. Others were cut from granite, limestone or conglomerate, a rock made of pebbles cemented together. The smaller jars are roughly waist height. The largest stand taller than a person, around three metres high, and weigh many tonnes. Each one was shaped from a single block of stone, with the inside hollowed out by hand.

So who made them, and when? The honest answer is that no one knows for certain. The jars are generally linked to an Iron Age culture that lived in this region more than two thousand years ago. Recent scientific work suggests some jars may have been placed here even earlier, around three thousand years ago. Nobody has found written records from the people who carved them. Their name, their language and their beliefs are still a mystery.

What archaeologists have found points towards death and memory. In the 1930s, a French archaeologist named Madeleine Colani spent long seasons studying these sites. Near this field, in a limestone cave, she found burned human bones and ash. She believed the jars were part of a funeral practice. Later excavations around the jars have uncovered human remains, buried pottery and stone markers in the ground nearby. Today, most researchers agree that these jars were connected to the treatment of the dead.

One idea is that bodies were placed inside the jars for a time, to let them decompose. Later, the bones may have been removed, cleaned and buried or cremated nearby. This is still a theory, not a settled fact. But it helps explain why the jars sit where they do, often on high ground, often facing open views. Stand beside one and look out over the valley... it is not hard to imagine this as a place of ceremony.

Local people have their own explanation, and it is a much happier one. According to a popular legend, an ancient king named Khun Cheung won a great victory here. To celebrate, he ordered huge jars to be made for brewing rice wine for his army. In some versions of the tale, the wine flowed for days. Archaeologists do not support this story, but locals still tell it with a smile. It is a good reminder that every landscape carries more than one kind of truth.

Now look down at the ground again — at those round hollows in the grass. Many of them are bomb craters. During the Vietnam War era, from the mid 1960s to the early 1970s, the United States carried out a secret air campaign over Laos. Xiangkhouang Province was hit very hard. Laos is often described as the most heavily bombed country in the world per person. Some of the jars here were cracked or shattered by that bombing.

The danger did not end with the war. Many bombs, especially small cluster bomblets, failed to explode when they landed. They stayed in the soil for decades. Clearance teams, including the organisation known as MAG, have worked to make the main jar sites safe for visitors. As you walk, you may see small marker stones along the paths. These show the cleared areas. Stay on the marked paths and between the markers. Do not step off into long grass or pick anything up.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look around the larger jars for flat stone discs lying in the grass. Some researchers believe these were lids for the jars. A few discs have a raised rim or a small knob, and some show faint carved patterns. On at least one jar at this site, you can find a carved human figure on the outside wall. It is easy to walk straight past it. Take a slow lap around the biggest jars and look for it.

Another surprise is how far the stone travelled. The rock for many jars did not come from right where they stand. Archaeologists have identified quarry sites in the hills, some several kilometres away. That means people moved blocks weighing many tonnes across rough country, with no wheels or machines as we know them. How they did it is still debated. It shows a community that could organise a lot of labour and that cared deeply about this work.

In 2019, these megalithic jar sites were added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. That recognition brought more attention, more research and more protection. It also means you share this landscape with scientists, clearance workers and local families. The fields around the sites are still used for grazing and farming. You may hear cattle bells on the slopes or see children walking home along the dirt road. The jars are ancient, but they sit inside a living place.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot and let your eyes move from jar to jar. Notice how the light changes the stone. In the morning it can look grey and cool. In the late afternoon it turns warm and golden, and the shadows inside the jars grow deep. Run a hand along the rim of one jar, if it is allowed, and feel the rough texture. People shaped this edge thousands of years ago, for reasons we are still trying to understand.

As you walk back towards the path, listen to the wind in the grass again. Look at the craters, the jars and the farmland side by side. Three very different chapters of human history are lying here in the same field. Some of it is a mystery, some of it is painful, and some of it is simply daily life. Carry that quiet with you as you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stay strictly on marked paths cleared of unexploded bombs.
Visit in late afternoon for warmer light on the stone.
Bring sun protection; the plateau has very little shade.
Hire a local driver to reach Sites 2 and 3.
Visit the bomb clearance centre in Phonsavan for context.
