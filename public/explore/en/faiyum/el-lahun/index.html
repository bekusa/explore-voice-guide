The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. It is dry, pale, and gritty. Small stones shift under your shoes. Ahead of you rises a broad, worn mound of grey-brown mudbrick. It does not look like the clean triangles on postcards. It looks older and more human, like something made by hand, one brick at a time. That is exactly what it is.

This is El Lahun, on the eastern edge of the Faiyum, in Egypt. The structure in front of you is the pyramid of Senusret the Second. He was a pharaoh of the Twelfth Dynasty, part of the period historians call the Middle Kingdom. That was a time of recovery and order, after years of division. His reign falls in the nineteenth century before Christ. That makes this monument nearly four thousand years old.

Look at how the pyramid is built. Its builders did not cut a whole mountain of stone. Instead, they used a core of natural rock and a framework of stone walls. Then they filled the spaces with mudbrick. A smooth outer skin of fine white limestone once covered everything. In later centuries, people took that casing away for other buildings. What you see now is the inner body, exposed to the sun and wind.

Here is the first surprise. Most Egyptian pyramids have their entrance on the north side. The north pointed toward the stars that never set, which the Egyptians linked with eternal life. At El Lahun, the builders moved the entrance to the south, and hid it away from the obvious face. Many scholars think this was a defence against tomb robbers. If so, it did not work. The burial chamber was found already plundered in modern times.

Now think about the location. The Faiyum is a large, low basin west of the Nile. A branch of water, today called the Bahr Yusuf, flows into it near here. The name Lahun is often traced to an ancient Egyptian phrase meaning the mouth of the canal or lake. So this pharaoh chose a place at a gateway. The kings of this dynasty worked hard to develop the Faiyum, with farmland and water control. Senusret placed his tomb where the water entered the land.

The modern story of El Lahun belongs largely to the British archaeologist Flinders Petrie. He worked here at the end of the nineteenth century. Petrie was famous for patience and careful method. He recorded small things that earlier diggers often threw away, such as broken pottery and simple tools. Thanks to that habit, El Lahun gave the world far more than one empty tomb.

A short distance from the pyramid lies the site of an ancient town. Archaeologists usually call it Kahun. It was a planned settlement, laid out in a grid, built for the people connected with the pyramid and its temple. There were large houses for officials and rows of small houses for workers. Today you will see mostly low traces and sand. But this was once a living town, with kitchens, workshops, and children playing in narrow streets.

Here is the second surprise. In that town, Petrie's team found papyrus documents. They are among the oldest written records of daily life in Egypt. Some deal with accounts and letters. One is a medical text about women's health, often described as the oldest known gynaecological papyrus. Others include mathematics and even the care of animals. These pages show ordinary worries, ordinary work, and ordinary lists, from almost four thousand years ago.

The pyramid itself held one more secret. In the early twentieth century, excavators working with Petrie cleared a tomb shaft near the pyramid. It belonged to a royal woman, Princess Sithathoriunet. Robbers had taken her coffin goods long ago. But they missed a small recess filled with mud. Inside it lay her jewellery and cosmetic objects. There were gold and gemstone pieces, mirrors, and boxes. Today this treasure is divided mainly between museums in Cairo and New York.

One piece is especially admired. It is a chest ornament, called a pectoral, made of gold and coloured stones. Its design includes the name of Senusret the Second. Craftspeople set tiny pieces of stone into gold cells with great precision. When you stand here, it is worth imagining that delicate object resting for thousands of years in the dark, a few steps from this rough mudbrick mass.

Walk slowly around the base, if access allows. Notice the different surfaces. Some areas show stacked brick, some show stone. In places, you can see the lines of the rock core. Around the pyramid, there are also smaller tombs and the remains of other structures. Petrie found signs that trees were once planted near the monument. So this was perhaps not a bare desert scene, but a place with some green shade.

Listen to the setting too. Behind you, toward the east, are fields and villages. You may hear a dog, a motorbike, or a call to prayer drifting over the farmland. The Faiyum is still an agricultural region, fed by the same water route that drew the ancient kings. The desert begins almost at the edge of the green. You are standing on the line between them, as the builders did.

Before you leave, look once more at the pyramid. It has lost its white shell, its temple, and its treasure. What remains is the honest structure underneath, brick and stone and labour. Somewhere nearby, under the sand, are the streets of the people who built it. As you walk back across the gravel, notice how quiet it is, and how close the fields are. The gateway to the Faiyum is still here, and so are you.
