The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the green fields where the Faiyum meets the Nile valley, and listen. A pump coughs somewhere beyond the palms. A donkey cart rattles along a dirt track. The air smells of wet earth and clover. Under your feet, or somewhere very close, lies one of the great capitals of ancient Egypt. Nobody can point to it exactly... and that is the first strange truth about Itjtawy.

About four thousand years ago, a new royal family came to power in Egypt. Historians call it the Twelfth Dynasty, and its age is known as the Middle Kingdom, a period of recovery after decades of division. The first king of this line was Amenemhat the First. He did something bold. He left the southern city of Thebes, his family's home, and built a new capital in the north, near the entrance to the Faiyum.

The full name of that city was Amenemhat-itj-tawy. In the ancient Egyptian language, this means roughly "Amenemhat is the one who seizes the Two Lands." The Two Lands were Upper and Lower Egypt — the long southern valley and the wide northern delta. So the name was not poetry. It was a statement of power. The king announced, in the very name of his city, that he held the whole country together.

Why move here? The reasons were practical. This stretch of the river sits close to the point where the valley opens into the delta. From here, a ruler could watch the river traffic in both directions. He could keep an eye on the old northern capital, Memphis, which lay a short journey downstream. Many scholars also think the move broke the king away from rival families in the south. A fresh city meant a fresh court, loyal to him alone.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Archaeologists have never found the city itself. Its palaces, offices and houses were built mostly of mud brick, and mud brick melts back into the soil over the centuries. The Nile shifted its course. Farmers planted and replanted the rich flood land. The capital most likely lies beneath the cultivated fields, or beneath later villages, somewhere near the modern site of el-Lisht. What survives are the tombs.

At el-Lisht, on the desert edge, stand the remains of two royal pyramids. One belongs to Amenemhat the First. The other belongs to his son, Senusret the First. Today they look like low, rough hills of rubble and sand. They have lost their smooth outer casing of fine limestone. But their position tells a story. Kings were buried close to the capital they ruled from, so these pyramids are our best signpost to the vanished city.

Now for a second surprise. When Amenemhat the First built his pyramid, his workers reused blocks taken from older monuments. Some of these stones came from buildings of the Old Kingdom, the age of the great pyramid builders centuries earlier. Excavators found carved and decorated pieces placed inside the core of his pyramid. Nobody is fully sure why. Perhaps it saved effort. Perhaps the king wanted the power of famous ancestors built into his own tomb.

The founding king did not have a peaceful end. Ancient Egyptian literature preserves a text known as the Instruction of Amenemhat. In it, the dead king speaks to his son and describes an attack inside the palace at night. Many historians read this as a sign that Amenemhat the First was killed in a conspiracy. It is a rare and personal voice from the royal court, and the palace it describes was most likely here, at Itjtawy.

The same moment opens one of the most famous stories of ancient Egypt, the Tale of Sinuhe. In the tale, a courtier hears news of the old king's death. Fearing trouble, he flees Egypt and lives for many years abroad. In old age he longs to come home and be buried in his own land. The story was copied by scribes for centuries. It begins with the royal residence at Itjtawy, which shows how central the city was in Egyptian memory.

The move north also turned royal attention toward the Faiyum. This is a large, low basin west of the Nile, fed by a branch of the river that runs into a lake. Later kings of the dynasty invested heavily in this region. They built pyramids at el-Lahun and Hawara, on the edges of the basin. Work on water channels and farmland made the Faiyum one of the most productive corners of the country. Itjtawy was the doorway to all of it.

Itjtawy remained the seat of government for the rest of the Twelfth Dynasty, and kings of the following dynasty seem to have ruled from here too. For generations, officials, scribes, priests and craftsmen lived and worked in this place. Their names survive on statues and tombs across Egypt, often with titles linked to the royal residence. Their streets have gone, but their careers left a long paper trail in stone.

So what can you actually see? Not a city of columns and gates. Instead, you see the landscape that the Egyptians chose. The flat green strip of the floodplain. The sudden line where the fields stop and the pale desert begins. The low mounds of the pyramids on that line. Ancient Egyptians lived on the black, fertile soil and buried their dead on the dry, red sand. Here you can see that boundary with your own eyes.

Take a moment before you move on. Look across the fields toward the desert edge. Notice how the light changes from the deep green of the crops to the pale gold of the sand. Listen to the water in the irrigation ditches, still doing the work it did four thousand years ago. Somewhere under this quiet ground lies the city that once held the Two Lands together. As you walk away, every field around you may be resting on its streets.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the el-Lisht pyramids to orient yourself to the capital.
Come early morning to avoid strong desert heat.
Bring water, sun protection and sturdy closed shoes.
Check site access and permits with local authorities beforehand.
Pair the trip with pyramids at el-Lahun or Hawara.
