The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes. It crunches with every step, and the sound bounces back from long walls of pale stone. To one side, the ground drops away sharply. Far below, a green river moves quietly toward the north. The air up here often feels cooler than in the town. This is Burghausen Castle, and you are standing on a narrow ridge that has carried fortifications for many centuries. Take a breath and look along the walls. They keep going.

That is the first surprise. This is not a castle with one tall keep and a single courtyard. It is a long chain of buildings, gates, and yards, stretched along the spine of the hill. From end to end, the complex runs for just over a kilometre. It is often described as the longest castle complex in the world, and Guinness has listed it with that record. You do not see it all at once. You discover it, yard by yard, as you walk.

The river below is the Salzach. Its name comes from salt, and that matters here. For centuries, salt from the Alpine region travelled on this river, and the trade brought wealth to the town at its bank. Whoever held this ridge could watch the river, and could tax what moved on it. Today the far bank belongs to Austria. The water marks the border. So, from these walls, you are looking straight into another country.

The history of the castle goes back a long way. Burghausen appears in written records around the early eleventh century. In the twelfth century, the castle came into the hands of the Wittelsbach family, the dynasty that ruled Bavaria for hundreds of years afterwards. When Bavaria was divided among different branches of the family, Burghausen became one of the most important seats of the dukes of Lower Bavaria, whose main court was at Landshut.

The castle you see today owes much of its shape to the late fifteenth century. Duke George, remembered as George the Rich, expanded and strengthened it heavily. Europe was nervous in those years. The Ottoman Empire was advancing in the Balkans, and many rulers feared an attack. George turned this ridge into a powerful fortress, with stronger walls, towers, and defensive gates. He also used the castle as a secure place to keep his treasury.

There is a human story inside these walls too. In 1475, George married Hedwig, a Polish princess from the Jagiellonian royal family. Their wedding in Landshut was a huge celebration, and the town still re-enacts it in a famous festival. But Hedwig's later life was spent largely here, at Burghausen. The castle served as a residence for the duchesses, somewhat apart from the court. Hedwig died here in the early sixteenth century. When you walk the inner rooms, it helps to picture her daily life on this quiet ridge.

Now look at the way the complex is arranged. The main castle, the oldest and most important part, sits at the high southern end of the ridge. From there, a series of outer courtyards runs northward. Each one is separated by a moat, a gate, or a wall. An attacker would have had to break through one defence, then another, and then another. It is a simple idea, repeated many times. The result is this long, layered fortress.

As you walk through the outer courtyards, notice how quiet and lived-in they feel. These yards once held workshops, stables, storehouses, and the homes of officials and craftsmen. Some of the buildings are still occupied today. You may see flower boxes, bicycles, and ordinary front doors set into medieval walls. It is a strange and pleasant mix. A defensive structure built for war has become, in part, a residential street.

The castle also has two chapels, and both are worth a moment of your time. The inner chapel, dedicated to Saint Elisabeth, belongs to the main castle and is one of the older parts of the complex. The outer chapel, dedicated to Saint Hedwig, stands in one of the outer courtyards. It is a late Gothic building, the pointed and vertical style of the late Middle Ages. Look up at its vaulted ceiling when you step inside.

Along the walls you will also find a tower known as the Witches' Tower. Its name points to a darker chapter. Many castles of this period held prisons, and towers like this were used to confine people. Local tradition connects this one with the witch trials of the early modern era. Treat the name as a reminder rather than a precise record. It is a grim story, and it sits only a few steps from the peaceful chapels.

Inside the main castle, there are museum spaces you can visit. One part holds a branch gallery of the Bavarian State Painting Collections, with late Gothic paintings shown in rooms that suit them well. Another part presents the history of the castle and the town. Inside, the light is softer, and the stone keeps the rooms cool. After the open courtyards, the change in atmosphere is very noticeable.

Now walk to the edge and look west, away from the river. Below the castle, on the other side of the ridge, lies a small lake called the Wöhrsee. It sits in an old bend of the river, and it fills the valley between the hill and the newer parts of town. In summer, people swim there, right under the castle walls. On the river side, the old town sits low along the bank, with colourful facades and a long market square.

Take one last look along the ridge. Stone, grass, gates, and roofs run away from you in a long line. The river still moves below, carrying nothing more than water now, and maybe a few kayaks. The defences were built for an attack that never came here. What remains is a place for walking and looking. As you head back, listen again to the gravel, and notice how far you have actually come.

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for the long walk along the ridge.
Start at the main castle and walk northward through courtyards.

Walk down to the Wöhrsee for the best view of the walls.
Visit the old town square below after your castle walk.
