The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath the trees, a small river runs over stones. The air down here is cool and damp, and it smells of moss and wet leaves. Then the forest opens, and on a rock in the middle of the valley stands a cluster of towers, half-timbered walls and dark slate roofs. They seem to grow straight out of the stone. This is Eltz Castle, and you are looking at one of the very few medieval castles in Germany that was never destroyed.

You probably arrived the way most visitors do, from the car park near the village of Wierschem, up on the plateau. From there the path drops down through beech and oak forest. The castle stays hidden for most of the walk. That is the first surprise of this place. It does not stand proudly on a mountaintop like many Rhine castles. It sits low, in a deep side valley of the Moselle, almost tucked out of sight.

That position was not an accident. The rock spur below the castle is surrounded on three sides by the Elzbach, a small stream that winds through this valley. In the Middle Ages, an old trade route crossed the area, connecting the fertile Maifeld plateau with the Moselle. A castle here could watch that route and protect it. Its builders did not need a mountain. They needed water, steep rock and a road to control.

The castle first appears in written records in the year 1157. A document of the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick Barbarossa names a nobleman called Rudolf of Eltz as a witness. That is the earliest firm date we have. Here is the second surprise, and it is the one that stays with people. The same family still owns the castle today. Descendants of those early lords have held it for more than eight hundred years, across more than thirty generations.

The buildings in front of you look like one castle, but in fact they are several homes joined together. In the thirteenth century, the family split into three branches. They did not divide the land and move away. Instead, they stayed and shared the castle. Each branch built its own living quarters around the inner courtyard. Historians call this type of shared stronghold a Ganerbenburg, a castle of joint heirs, which several families own and run together.

That explains the strange, crowded shape of the place. Each branch needed more space, but the rock could not grow. So the families built upward. Some of the houses rise to eight storeys. Towers, oriels and small turrets press against each other. The oldest surviving part is a stone keep from the twelfth century. Later houses were added over the following centuries, into the sixteenth century. When you look at the castle, you are looking at roughly four hundred years of building on one rock.

Living together was not always easy, and the families had rules for shared spaces like the courtyard, the well and the chapel. At its busiest, the castle held a large household of family members, servants and guards. Imagine the courtyard then. Horses, smoke from kitchen fires, children, voices from several different households, all within the same walls. The narrow courtyard you will walk through was once the busy centre of three separate family worlds.

The castle did face real danger. In the fourteenth century, the lords of Eltz and other local knights opposed the growing power of Baldwin, the Archbishop of Trier. Around the 1330s, the archbishop built a small siege castle on the hill above, so his men could attack and block Eltz. Its ruins still stand on the slope, called Trutzeltz. The siege lasted about two years. In the end, the knights of Eltz accepted the archbishop's terms, and the castle itself survived.

That pattern continued for centuries. Wars passed through this region many times, including the destructive French campaigns of the late seventeenth century, which ruined many castles along the Rhine and Moselle. Eltz escaped. Careful diplomacy, family connections and its hidden position all helped. Because of this, the castle kept not only its walls but also its interiors. Furniture, paintings, weapons and household objects stayed here, in the rooms where the family used them.

Over time, one branch, the line known as Eltz-Kempenich, became the sole owner. In the nineteenth century the family carried out a major restoration, repairing the old houses carefully rather than rebuilding them in a modern style. Many castles of that era were rebuilt as romantic fantasies. Eltz was repaired as itself. That is why the half-timbering, the slate and the stone here feel consistent. The silhouette you see is very close to the one that grew here in the Middle Ages.

The interiors can be visited on a guided tour. Inside, you move through living rooms, bedrooms and halls with painted walls, carved wooden ceilings and tiled stoves. Separate from the tour, the treasury holds a collection of goldsmith work, silver, porcelain, jewellery and weapons gathered by the family over centuries. A third surprise for many visitors... for decades, a picture of this castle appeared on the German five hundred mark banknote. Millions of people carried Eltz in their wallets.

Before you leave, take a slow walk around the outside. From the bridge and the path below, you can see how the buildings follow the shape of the rock. Notice how the walls lean and step back, how small windows sit high above the stone. In autumn the forest around the castle turns gold and copper. In spring it is bright green. The castle changes with the light, from pale grey in the morning to warm tones in late afternoon.

Now stand still for a moment, wherever you are. Listen again to the Elzbach below. The water was here before the first stone was laid, and it gave this place its name. The same family name, the same river, the same rock. As you climb back up through the trees toward Wierschem, turn around once more. The towers will slowly disappear behind the leaves, just as they did for travellers on this road eight hundred years ago.
