The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in the courtyard and listen. The noise of the old town fades behind the curved stone gateway. Your footsteps sound on pale paving, and the walls around you glow a soft, warm cream in the light. Just behind you, the red sandstone of the cathedral rises dark and steep. Here, everything is calmer and more orderly. This is the Palais Rohan, and its calm order is the whole point of the building.

To understand why, it helps to know who lived here. For centuries, Strasbourg was ruled in religious matters by a prince-bishop. He was both a churchman and a powerful lord. In the eighteenth century, that office was held for generations by members of the Rohan family, one of the grandest noble houses in France. The palace was built for the first of them, Cardinal Armand-Gaston-Maximilien de Rohan, in the 1730s and early 1740s.

The timing matters. Strasbourg had become French in 1681, after a long history inside the Holy Roman Empire. The city spoke German, followed German customs, and much of its population was Protestant. A new French palace beside the cathedral sent a clear message. The Catholic Church was back in charge of the cathedral, and the French crown was here to stay. Every straight line of this building carries that message.

The design came from Robert de Cotte, the chief architect to the French king. He had trained in the circle that worked on Versailles. De Cotte did not spend much time in Strasbourg himself. He sent drawings from Paris, and a local builder named Joseph Massol supervised the work on site. So what you see is Parisian taste, built by Alsatian hands with stone from this region.

Look at how the buildings are arranged. The courtyard faces the cathedral side, closed off from the street by that grand curved entrance. The main living rooms face the other way, toward the river Ill. This plan has a French name, the hôtel entre cour et jardin, which means a mansion between a courtyard and a garden. Here, the river takes the place of the garden. It is a classic Paris layout, placed in the middle of a medieval German city.

Now walk around to the river side, if you have time. This is the façade most visitors miss, and it is the more elegant one. A long, calm front rises above the water, with tall windows and a central section that steps forward slightly. Tour boats slide past below. From the opposite bank, you can see the whole composition at once, with the cathedral spire behind it. It is one of the best views of how the city layers its centuries.

Back to the people. This palace was built to receive kings, and it did. Louis the Fifteenth stayed here in 1744. Then, in 1770, a fourteen-year-old Austrian princess spent a night here on her way to Paris. She was Marie Antoinette, travelling to marry the future Louis the Sixteenth. Strasbourg was her first real stop in France. Imagine the courtyard full of carriages, torches and curious crowds.

The Rohan family also left a famous scandal behind. The last prince-bishop to live here in style was Cardinal Louis de Rohan. In the 1780s, he was drawn into a fraud known as the Affair of the Diamond Necklace. A group of swindlers convinced him that Queen Marie Antoinette secretly wanted a very expensive necklace. The scheme collapsed, the cardinal was arrested, and the public blamed the queen. Many historians see the affair as one small crack in the monarchy before the Revolution.

The Revolution changed everything here. The prince-bishops lost their power and their palace. Later, under Napoleon, the building became an imperial residence, and Napoleon and Josephine stayed here. Over the following generations, the palace passed through several hands and uses. That kind of reuse is typical for great French buildings. Grand rooms rarely stay empty for long.

In the twentieth century, the palace suffered serious damage. During the Second World War, Allied bombing in 1944 struck Strasbourg, and the palace was badly hit. Much of what you see today is the result of careful restoration after the war. When you walk through the state rooms, remember that some of the elegance around you was rebuilt, piece by piece, by people who wanted the city's memory back.

Today the palace holds three museums under one roof. On the lower level is the Archaeological Museum, which covers the long story of the region from prehistory to the early Middle Ages. On the ground floor is the Museum of Decorative Arts, including the restored apartments of the cardinals. Upstairs is the Museum of Fine Arts, with paintings by European masters, including Italian and Flemish artists. You can choose one, or spend a whole afternoon on all three.

Here are two details many visitors walk past. In the decorative arts collection, look for Strasbourg faience. This is glazed earthenware made in the city in the eighteenth century, much of it by the Hannong family workshop. It is painted with bright flowers in pink, green and purple. The collection also displays mechanical pieces linked to the cathedral's earlier astronomical clock. One of them is a large iron rooster that once flapped its wings and crowed for the crowd.

In the cardinals' apartments, slow down. Notice the carved wood panels, the gilded details, and the way the rooms open one into another in a long line. This arrangement is called an enfilade, a row of rooms with doors aligned so you can see straight through them. It was designed to impress guests, who walked deeper and deeper toward the most important person in the house.

When you step back out into the courtyard, look up at the cathedral once more. On one side stands medieval stone, rough and soaring. On the other stands this palace, smooth and balanced. They were built centuries apart, by very different worlds. Yet here they share the same small square of the city. Listen for the bells, watch the light on the cream stone, and take that contrast with you as you walk on.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross the river Ill for the full view of the façade.
Allow at least a half day to see all three museums.
Pair your visit with the cathedral just steps away.
Take a river boat tour to see the palace from water.
