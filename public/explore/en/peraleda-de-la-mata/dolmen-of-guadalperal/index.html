The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves across cracked mud, and somewhere close the water of the reservoir laps at a shore that was not here last year. Under your feet the ground is pale and split into plates, like old pottery. Ahead of you, a ring of grey granite stones stands up out of this strange landscape. Some lean. Some stand straight. All of them have spent most of the last sixty years underwater.

This is the Dolmen of Guadalperal, near the village of Peraleda de la Mata, in the province of Cáceres, in western Spain. A dolmen is a type of prehistoric stone monument. People raised large upright slabs, often covered them with a roof stone and earth, and used the chamber inside for burials and ritual. Spanish newspapers sometimes call this place the Spanish Stonehenge. The comparison is loose, but it helps people picture what they are about to see.

Archaeologists generally date the monument to the Neolithic and Chalcolithic periods, the late Stone Age into the early Copper Age. That puts its construction roughly five thousand years ago, and possibly earlier. Estimates vary, so treat any single date with care. What is certain is this: the stones were standing here for thousands of years before the Romans arrived in the region, and long before anyone wrote down the name of the river nearby.

That river is the Tagus, the longest river on the Iberian Peninsula. It runs west from central Spain, through Extremadura, and on across Portugal to the Atlantic at Lisbon. In prehistoric times this site stood on dry land near the riverbank. The builders chose a spot close to water, with open views across the valley. Today, that same water is the reason the monument is so hard to see.

In the 1920s, a German archaeologist and priest named Hugo Obermaier excavated the dolmen. He was one of the leading prehistorians working in Spain at the time. His team recorded the layout, cleared the chamber, and studied what remained of the burial deposits. His findings were published only years later, and for a long time few people outside academic circles knew much about this place.

Then, in the early 1960s, the Spanish state completed the Valdecañas dam on the Tagus. This was part of a wide programme of dams and reservoirs built under the dictatorship of Francisco Franco, to produce hydroelectric power and store water. As the reservoir filled, the valley floor disappeared. Farmland went under. So did old paths, field walls... and this ring of stones. For decades, the dolmen became a local memory rather than a place you could visit.

Here is something many visitors miss. The dolmen was not the only ancient monument in the flooded zone. A short distance away stood the Roman town of Augustobriga. Before the water rose, part of its Roman temple, known locally as Los Mármoles, was taken down and rebuilt on higher ground. The dolmen received no such rescue. It simply stayed where it was, and the reservoir covered it.

The stones came back into public view in 2019, during a long, severe drought. As the water level dropped, the tops of the granite slabs broke the surface, and then the whole structure stood clear. Photographs travelled around the world. A local cultural group launched a campaign asking the authorities to protect the site, and some people even proposed moving it to dry ground. The debate about the best way to protect it has continued ever since.

Now look closely at the layout in front of you. The monument has two main parts. There is an oval chamber, made of large upright slabs set close together. And there is a long corridor, a narrow passage that leads into that chamber. Archaeologists call this design a passage grave. The corridor gave people a way to enter the burial space, perhaps for ceremonies, perhaps to add new remains over many generations.

Count the stones if you have time. There are roughly a hundred and fifty of them, all granite, a stone that is common in this part of Extremadura. Some slabs are taller than an adult. Near the entrance stands a single upright stone, a menhir, with carved lines on its surface. Some researchers have suggested the carving could show a serpent. Others have proposed that it shows a winding river, possibly an early map of the Tagus itself. Nobody can say for certain which reading is correct.

The water has left its own marks. Look at the surfaces of the stones. Many show pitting, cracks, and a pale crust of mineral deposits. Granite is hard, but the cycle of wetting and drying wears it down over time. Experts have warned that each new exposure to sun and air, after years underwater, puts extra stress on the stone. That is why every visitor matters here. Please do not climb on the slabs, and do not touch the carved menhir.

Take a moment to look beyond the stones as well. The Valdecañas reservoir spreads across the valley, its colour changing from grey to green to silver with the light. The banks around it are low, with oak and scrub on the higher ground. Birds work the shoreline, and in summer the heat comes up off the dry lakebed in shimmering waves. In wet years, all of this lies under many metres of water, and the dolmen cannot be seen at all.

There is something unusual about meeting a monument this way. Most ancient sites stay put and wait for you. This one appears and disappears with the climate, with rainfall, and with how much water the dam holds back. Whether you see it at all depends on the year you happen to come. In a sense, the dolmen now keeps two calendars: the slow one of the Stone Age builders, and the fast one of modern water management.

Before you walk back across the cracked ground, stand still for a minute. Look at the corridor, pointing towards the chamber, as it has for thousands of years. Notice the line on the stones where the water usually sits. Feel the wind coming off the reservoir. When you turn away, look back once more from a distance. The ring will seem small against the wide valley... and that is exactly how its builders would have seen it too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check reservoir water levels before travelling, the site may be submerged.
Wear sturdy boots for the walk across dry, uneven lakebed.
Bring plenty of water and sun protection in summer.
Stay off the stones to avoid damaging fragile granite.
Come early morning for softer light and fewer visitors.
