The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Traffic hums along the wide avenue below, but up here, the sound changes. A bell sounds from the tower. Candle smoke drifts out through heavy doors. Above you, five golden domes catch the Ural light. Even on a grey afternoon, they seem to hold a little warmth.

You are standing at the Church on the Blood, often called the Church of All Saints. Its full dedication honours all the saints who shone in the Russian land. But most people know this hill for a darker reason. This is the place where the last Russian emperor, Nicholas the Second, died with his family.

Before the church, a private house stood here. It belonged to a merchant and engineer named Nikolai Ipatiev. It was a solid, two-storey home, comfortable and respectable. In the spring of 1918, during the Russian Civil War, the Bolshevik authorities took the house from its owner. They gave it a cold new name: the House of Special Purpose.

The former imperial family arrived as prisoners. Nicholas, his wife Alexandra, their four daughters, and their young son Alexei lived here under guard. Several loyal servants and their family doctor stayed with them. The windows were painted over. A high fence went up around the house. For weeks, the family lived in near isolation, while war moved closer to the city.

In the early hours of 17 July 1918, the family and their servants were woken and led down to a basement room. There, they were shot. Their bodies were taken out of the city into the forest. For decades, the full story stayed buried — in the ground, and in official silence. Today, the lower part of the church marks the place where that basement room once stood.

Here is something many visitors miss. The house survived the revolution by nearly sixty years. Through the Soviet period it held offices and, for a time, a small museum. But it also drew quiet pilgrims, and the authorities did not welcome that attention. In 1977, the house was demolished on orders from Moscow. The regional party leader who carried out the order was Boris Yeltsin — the same man who later became the first president of the new Russia.

For years after the demolition, this site was an empty patch of ground. Then the Soviet Union ended, and attitudes began to change. Believers placed a wooden cross here. In 2000, the Russian Orthodox Church declared the imperial family saints. It honoured them as passion-bearers — a special category for people who faced death with humility and faith, rather than for their deeds in life. Construction of this church followed soon after.

The building you see now was completed in the early 2000s and consecrated in 2003. Its style is often called Russian-Byzantine. That means it borrows from the old churches of Byzantium, the eastern Roman Empire, and from medieval Russian church building. Look at the white walls and the five gilded domes. In Orthodox tradition, five domes often stand for Christ and the four Gospel writers.

Now step closer to the entrance. A bronze group of figures shows the family on a staircase, moving down. The image recalls their final walk to the basement. The sculptor gave each figure a calm, quiet expression. Many visitors stop here first. Some leave flowers at the base. Others simply stand and look for a while before they go inside.

The church has two levels, and they feel very different. The upper church is tall and filled with light. Gold, icons, and painted walls rise towards the central dome. The lower church is lower, darker, and more intimate. It holds the memorial to the place of execution. People tend to speak in whispers there, or not at all. If a service is taking place, the singing carries through both floors.

A second surprise waits outside, on the calendar. Every July, on the night of the anniversary, thousands of pilgrims gather here for an all-night service. Before dawn, they set out on foot to Ganina Yama, a forest site outside the city. That is where the bodies were first taken. The walk takes many hours. Locals call these days the Tsar's Days, and the city fills with pilgrims from across Russia.

Take a moment to look around the hill itself. Close by stands the older Church of the Ascension, with its pale classical lines. This rise is called Ascension Hill, and it was once one of the most respected addresses in the city. Nearby buildings belong to the church complex, including a museum about the imperial family. Below you, the city stretches towards the Iset River and its long city pond.

Yekaterinburg itself sits on the eastern side of the Ural Mountains, near the line between Europe and Asia. It grew in the eighteenth century as a centre for iron and metalwork. Through the Soviet years, it carried another name — Sverdlovsk. That layered past is easy to feel here. A merchant's house, a revolution, a demolition, and a rebuilding all happened on one small piece of land.

Before you leave, turn back and look up at the domes one more time. Notice how the gold changes as clouds move across the sky. Listen for the bells, or for the soft shuffle of feet on the steps. Whatever you believe, this is a place where people come to remember. As you walk back down the hill, you carry a little of that quiet with you.
