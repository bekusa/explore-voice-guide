The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the eastern end of Lenin Avenue and look straight ahead. The long avenue runs out of road here. In front of you, a wide square opens up, and beyond it rises a pale, columned façade that seems to stop the whole street in its tracks. Listen, and you will probably hear the same thing you would hear at any campus in the world: students talking, laughing, hurrying with bags on their shoulders toward the doors.

This is the main building of what many people in Yekaterinburg still call simply UPI. Those three letters stand for the Ural Polytechnic Institute, the name this school carried for most of the twentieth century. Later it became Ural State Technical University. Today it forms the heart of Ural Federal University, one of the largest universities in Russia. But for older residents of the city, the building in front of you is still UPI, and the nickname has never really gone away.

The story begins in 1920, in the hard years just after the Russian Civil War. The new Soviet state needed engineers, and it needed them badly. The Urals had been a mining and metalworking region for two centuries, with ironworks and mines spread across the mountains. But the region had very few places to train the specialists who would run them. A polytechnic school here was not a luxury. It was a plan to rebuild an entire industrial economy.

In its early years, the institute moved between different buildings and changed its structure several times. In the 1930s, it took the name of Sergei Kirov, a senior Communist Party leader who was assassinated in 1934. That was a common practice in the Soviet era. Factories, cities and schools across the country received the names of political figures. For decades, the full official name of this school carried Kirov's name with it.

Now look again at the façade. Notice how the architecture works. The heavy columns, the broad steps, the strict symmetry — all of it follows the language of Soviet monumental neoclassicism, a style that borrowed from ancient Greek and Roman temples to project power and permanence. The message is clear. Education here is presented as something grand, almost sacred. A student walking up these steps is meant to feel that they are joining something much larger than themselves.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The placement of the building is not an accident. Lenin Avenue is the main east-west axis of central Yekaterinburg. City planners placed the institute so that it closes the view at the end of that axis. Stand in the middle of the avenue some distance back, and the building sits directly in your line of sight. In a sense, the city's main street was designed to lead you to a school of engineering.

The district around you has its own name: Vtuzgorodok. Roughly translated, it means the town of the technical college. In the late 1920s and 1930s, this area grew as a planned neighbourhood for the institute, with student housing and homes for teachers. Walk the streets nearby and you will find blocks from that era, some in the clean, geometric style of Soviet constructivism, an early modernist movement that favoured function and simple forms over decoration.

Now for the name you may already associate with this place. Boris Yeltsin, the first President of the Russian Federation, studied here. He trained as a civil engineer and graduated in the 1950s. Before politics, he worked on construction sites in the region. Today the federal university that includes this campus carries his name. Whatever your view of his presidency, it is a striking thought. The man who signed the documents of a new Russia once walked these same steps as a young student with a construction textbook under his arm.

And he was not the only one. Nikolai Ryzhkov, who became head of the Soviet government in the 1980s, is also among the graduates of this institute. So in the final years of the Soviet Union, two of the most powerful men in the country had both come out of the same engineering school in the Urals. That says a great deal about how central industry, and the people trained to manage it, were to Soviet life.

The name changed again in the early 1990s. As the Soviet Union came apart, the institute became Ural State Technical University. Then, in 2011, it merged with Ural State University, the region's classical university focused on the sciences and humanities. Together they formed Ural Federal University. So the building you see is now one part of a much larger institution, with campuses and faculties spread across the city.

If you climb the steps and peer toward the entrance, keep in mind that this is a working university, not a museum. Classes run, exams happen, and people are going about their day. Be respectful and quiet near the doors. Security may limit who can enter, so do not count on going inside. The exterior and the square, however, are open to everyone, and they are where the real sense of the place lives.

Take a moment to watch the square itself. In warm months, students sit on the steps between lectures. In winter, the snow piles up along the edges, and the columns look even paler against a grey Ural sky. Graduation season brings families, flowers and photographs on these same steps. It is a place where the city meets its young people, generation after generation, in exactly the same spot.

Before you walk away, turn around and face west, back down Lenin Avenue. The street stretches toward the historic centre, toward the old dam and the city pond. From here you can see the idea behind the plan: the city on one end, the school on the other. Notice the trams, the traffic, the ordinary rhythm of a Yekaterinburg afternoon. And as you go, glance back once more at those columns, still standing where the avenue ends.

Practical tips
