The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the sea moves in and out of the shingle with a slow, steady hiss. Salt hangs in the air, and the limestone above you is pale, almost honey-coloured where the sun touches it. Look at the great opening in the cliff, a tall, dark mouth cut into the eastern face of the Rock of Gibraltar. People sheltered inside that darkness for a very, very long time. Many of them were not quite like us.

This is Gorham's Cave. It sits close to sea level on the quieter, eastern side of the Rock, away from the town and the harbour. The cave takes its name from a British officer, Captain Gorham, who entered it in the early twentieth century. He thought he was among the first to explore it. He was wrong by tens of thousands of years. The real first visitors had left their traces deep in the floor.

Those visitors were Neanderthals. They were an ancient human species who lived across Europe and western Asia long before modern humans arrived. Excavations here have uncovered layer upon layer of their stone tools, hearths, and food remains. The deposits show that Neanderthals returned to this cave again and again over an enormous span of time, likely more than one hundred thousand years. For them, this was not a single campsite. It was a familiar place, visited across countless generations.

Now, here is the first surprise. When you look out from the cave today, you see open water. But during the colder phases of the last Ice Age, so much water was locked in ice that sea levels were far lower. In front of this cave lay a wide coastal plain, several kilometres across. There were dunes, wetlands, pine and scrub. Deer, ibex and wild horses moved across it. The Neanderthals who lived here looked out over land, not sea.

That landscape fed them well. Archaeologists at Gorham's Cave and its neighbour, Vanguard Cave, found evidence that Neanderthals ate shellfish gathered from the shore. They also ate seals, dolphins, fish, and many kinds of birds. For a long time, scientists assumed that only modern humans made good use of the sea. The finds from this stretch of coast helped to change that view. Neanderthals here were skilled, flexible foragers who understood both the land and the shoreline.

There is a stranger detail too. Researchers studying Gibraltar's caves found that Neanderthals took birds of prey and crows, and they seem to have been interested in the wing feathers. Some scientists think the dark feathers were removed and worn, perhaps as decoration. That interpretation is still debated. But it raises a powerful question. Did these people care how they looked? Did they mark themselves out in some way? The cave holds the hints, not the full answer.

And then there is the engraving. Deep inside the cave, excavators uncovered a patch of bedrock carved with a pattern of deep, crossing lines, like a rough grid or hashtag. The layer of sediment that covered it was dated to before modern humans are thought to have reached this area. This makes it one of the strongest cases for an abstract design made by Neanderthals. The lines were cut deliberately, with repeated strokes. Someone wanted that mark to last.

Gorham's Cave is also famous for a long-running argument about time. Some excavation results suggested that Neanderthals survived here later than almost anywhere else, possibly into a period when they had already vanished elsewhere in Europe. Other scientists question those dates and the methods behind them. The debate continues. Still, many researchers agree that the southern tip of Iberia, with its mild climate and rich coast, may have been one of the last strongholds of the Neanderthal world.

Here is a second surprise, and it comes from much closer to our own era. Long after the Neanderthals were gone, the cave found a new purpose. Sailors from the Phoenician and later Carthaginian world, seafaring peoples from the eastern and southern Mediterranean, used it as a sacred place. Excavators found pottery, small amulets, and other offerings deep inside. Imagine ships rounding this headland, and their crews stopping to leave gifts in the dark, perhaps praying for a safe passage through the strait.

Gibraltar has another link to the story of Neanderthals. In the middle of the nineteenth century, a fossil skull was found in a quarry on the northern side of the Rock. Nobody understood it at the time. Only later did scientists realise it was a Neanderthal skull, found years before the famous discovery in Germany's Neander Valley that gave the species its name. In a way, this small territory met the Neanderthals first, without knowing it.

Because of all this, Gorham's Cave is part of a protected landscape called the Gorham's Cave Complex. It includes several neighbouring caves, cliffs and the sea in front of them. In 2016 UNESCO added the complex to its World Heritage List, recognising it as an exceptional record of Neanderthal life. The caves are fragile and research is still active. Access inside is carefully managed, usually through guided visits arranged by the Gibraltar National Museum, so plan ahead if you hope to enter.

If you do go inside, notice how the light changes as you move deeper. The bright, sea-reflected glare at the entrance fades into soft grey, then real darkness. The air grows cooler and stiller. The floor is uneven, and in places you can see the cut edges of excavation trenches, with bands of sediment in different colours. Each band is a slice of time. The lower you look, the further back you are seeing.

Wherever you stand now, take one more moment. Look at the cliff face, the dark opening, the line of water where once there was a wide plain. Listen to the waves again. The sound is not so different from what a Neanderthal family might have heard on a stormy night, if the sea came close. As you walk away, glance back once. That doorway in the rock has been a home, a larder, a shrine, and now a library of deep time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a guided cave visit ahead through the Gibraltar National Museum.
Wear sturdy shoes with grip for uneven, rocky ground.
Bring binoculars to study the cliff caves from afar.
Visit in morning light when the eastern cliffs are lit.
Carry water, as the eastern coast has few facilities.
