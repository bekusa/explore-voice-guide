The guide

The story

Run your hand along the wall here. The surface is rough and slightly grainy. It is not neat cut stone. It feels more like old, sun-baked concrete, with small pebbles and lime pressed into it. Above you, a tall, square, honey-coloured tower rises from the slope of the Rock. Below, the roofs of Gibraltar fall away towards the harbour. On a windy day you can hear gulls, distant traffic, and sometimes the rumble of a plane on the runway beyond the town.

This is the Moorish Castle. Its most visible part is the great square keep, usually called the Tower of Homage. For centuries it was the first thing sailors saw as they approached the northern face of the Rock. Even now, with apartment blocks and shipyards around it, the tower holds its ground. It is a reminder that Gibraltar was a fortress long before it became a British territory, a tax haven, or a cruise ship stop.

To understand why a castle stands here, start with the name of the place itself. In 711, a Berber commander called Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the strait from North Africa and landed near this Rock. That crossing began the Muslim conquest of much of the Iberian Peninsula. The mountain became known as Jabal Tariq, the Mountain of Tariq. Over the centuries, that Arabic name was worn down in Spanish mouths until it became Gibraltar.

The first true town on the Rock came much later, in the twelfth century, under the Almohads, a North African dynasty that ruled large parts of Morocco and Spain. They founded a fortified settlement here. But most of what you see today is younger. Historians generally believe the great tower above you dates from the fourteenth century, rebuilt after a period of hard fighting over this strategic Rock.

And the fighting was relentless. Castilian forces from Christian Spain captured Gibraltar in the early fourteenth century. A few decades later, in 1333, the Marinids, a Moroccan dynasty, took it back after a long siege. The castle was strengthened again and again. Then, in 1462, Castile captured the Rock for good. Whoever held this slope could watch the narrow sea lane between the Mediterranean and the Atlantic. That view was worth fighting for... repeatedly.

Now look closely at the building material. The walls are largely made of a technique called tapial, a form of rammed earth. Builders packed a mix of earth, lime, and small stones into wooden frames, then removed the frames once the mix had set. In places you can still see faint horizontal lines and small holes. These are traces of the wooden shuttering and the beams that held it. It is a construction method used across Moorish Spain and North Africa.

Here is something many visitors miss. The face of the tower is not smooth. Scan it slowly and you will notice pits, gouges, and scarred patches. These are generally understood to be the marks of cannon and musket fire from the many sieges Gibraltar endured. The Rock was besieged more than a dozen times over the centuries. The tower simply absorbed the punishment. Those wounds are part of its surface now, as much as the original walls.

The castle was never one single building. It was a complex of walls, gates, and towers that once ran down the hillside towards the sea, enclosing the medieval town. Much of that circuit has gone, swallowed by later fortifications and the modern streets below. But if you follow the lines of old walls with your eyes, you can still sense how the defences stepped down the slope, layer after layer, towards the water.

After 1704, when an Anglo-Dutch fleet captured Gibraltar, the British took over the castle. They adapted it, as they adapted almost everything on the Rock. Parts of the complex later served as a prison. In fact, Gibraltar's prison operated within the castle grounds until the early twenty-first century, when a new facility opened elsewhere. So for many local families, this place was not a romantic ruin. It was simply the jail on the hill.

There is another surprise in the flag above you. The Union Flag flies from the top of the tower. Locals often point out that a British flag has flown over this keep since the early eighteenth century. In the long years of siege that followed, the flag on the tower was a visible signal to both sides. While it stayed up, the Rock was still holding out.

Now turn your back to the wall and take in the view. To the north is the flat strip of the airport runway and, beyond it, the border with Spain. To the west, across the bay, you can see the Spanish town of Algeciras and the hills behind it. On clear days, the mountains of Morocco rise to the south. From here, the logic of the castle becomes obvious. Every ship entering or leaving the bay passed under this tower.

The castle sits on the lower slopes of the Upper Rock, so you are close to the nature reserve that covers much of the mountain. Keep an eye open for Barbary macaques, the tailless monkeys of Gibraltar. They sometimes wander down towards these roads. You may also smell wild herbs and pine in the warm air, and feel the wind change as clouds gather on the summit above.

Before you move on, look up at the tower one more time. Notice how the late afternoon light turns the walls from pale gold to a warmer amber. Listen to the town below — the engines, the voices, the gulls over the harbour. The tower has watched this same bay through conquests, sieges, and centuries of ordinary days. As you walk away, glance back once. It will still be there, square and patient, looking out to sea.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes; the approach roads are steep and uneven.
Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the walls.
Combine your visit with the Upper Rock Nature Reserve.
Keep food out of sight to avoid curious macaques.
Bring water; the slope gets hot and exposed in summer.
