The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Pine needles shift underfoot, a wood pigeon calls somewhere in the trees, and the air smells of resin and warm limestone. Rising above the treetops is a square, honey-coloured building with towers at each corner. It looks like a fortress. It was built as a place to rest. This is Verdala Palace, on the high ground south of Rabat, in the heart of Malta.

To understand the palace, start with the island's rulers in the late sixteenth century. These were the Knights of Saint John, a military and religious order of noblemen from across Catholic Europe. They had come to Malta in 1530. In 1565 they survived the Great Siege, when a huge Ottoman fleet attacked the islands and failed to take them. After that victory, the Knights began to build with confidence.

Most of that building happened in Valletta, the new fortified capital on the harbour. Valletta was crowded, hot, and very public in summer. The leader of the Order, called the Grand Master, wanted a retreat in the cooler, greener countryside. In 1586, Grand Master Hugues Loubenx de Verdalle, a Frenchman, ordered a new residence here. The palace still carries a version of his name.

The design is traditionally credited to Girolamo Cassar, the Maltese architect who shaped much of early Valletta. Look at the building's outline. It stands on a square plan, with a solid tower at each corner. A dry ditch, like a moat without water, runs around it. Here is the first surprise. These defences were mostly a statement. By the 1580s, a country house like this could not stop a real army. The towers said power. They did not say war.

That mix of fortress and villa is what makes Verdala unusual. Outside, it is closed and severe. The walls are thick, the windows are measured, and the stone is plain. Inside, according to descriptions of the interiors, the rooms are decorated with painted scenes linked to Verdalle's life and career. One feature people often mention is an elegant oval staircase. It turns gracefully through the building — a soft curve hidden inside a hard square.

Verdalle's own story adds a twist. Not long after the palace was built, the Pope made him a cardinal. So the man who built a fortified hunting lodge in a Maltese wood also wore the red robes of the Church. This was not unusual for the Knights. They were soldiers and monks at the same time. Their buildings often show both sides — prayer and display, discipline and comfort.

Now turn your attention to the trees around you. These are the Buskett Gardens. The name comes from the Italian word for a small wood. The Knights shaped this valley as a hunting ground and a shaded garden. That is the second surprise. Malta is a dry, rocky island with very little natural forest. So this green, shaded valley is rare. Much of it exists because people planted and protected it over centuries.

On a hot afternoon, you can feel the difference straight away. The temperature drops under the trees. The light turns green and broken. In winter and spring, water can run through the valley floor, and the ground becomes softer. Orange and lemon trees, pines, and other planted trees grow here together. Families from across Malta come to picnic. That everyday use is part of the place, not a distraction from it.

The palace did not stay a quiet summer house. When Napoleon's French forces took Malta in 1798, the Maltese rose against them, and British forces later arrived to support the islanders. During that troubled period, the building was used to hold French prisoners. A Grand Master's retreat became a place of confinement. It is a reminder that grand buildings often outlive the purpose they were made for.

Under British rule in the nineteenth century, the palace had a difficult phase and fell into poor condition. Later it was repaired, and British governors of Malta used it as a summer residence. They followed the same logic as the Knights. They wanted cool air, open views, and distance from the busy harbour. The building had found its old role again — just under a different flag.

Today, Verdala Palace is the official summer residence of the President of Malta. That is why it usually feels so private. It is a working state building, used for official guests and state occasions. Most visitors see it from the outside, from the gardens and the road. On certain special occasions it has opened to the public, but you should not expect to walk inside on an ordinary day.

That limitation has a quiet benefit. You see the palace as it was meant to be seen — from below, through the trees, as a firm block of stone above the soft green valley. Walk a little along the paths and watch how the view changes. From one angle, a corner tower appears between the pines. From another, the whole square mass sits on the ridge, sharp against the sky.

The wider landscape matters too. Rabat and the old walled city of Mdina are a short drive away. The high Dingli Cliffs are also nearby, where the land drops steeply to the sea. This part of Malta feels very different from the crowded coast. The roads are narrow, the fields are divided by low rubble walls, and the colours are dusty gold and grey-green. The palace belongs to this countryside.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute in the shade. Notice the stone again — the same pale limestone used across the island for churches, houses, and walls. It holds the warmth of the day long after the sun drops. Listen to the birds and the voices of families under the trees. Four centuries ago, a Grand Master chose this spot for the same reasons you might enjoy it now. Cool air. Green shade. A little distance from everything.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan to view the palace from Buskett Gardens outside.
Watch for rare public open days announced in advance.
Come on a hot afternoon to enjoy the gardens' shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven paths through the woodland.
Combine your visit with nearby Mdina or Dingli Cliffs.
