The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the sea and moves through the dry scrub on this ridge. The ground underfoot is pale and dusty. Below you, the Mediterranean spreads out in deep blue. In front of you stand blocks of honey-coloured stone, worn soft, pitted, and rounded at the edges. These stones were set here by people who lived on Malta long before the pyramids of Egypt were built.

This is Ħaġar Qim, on the southern coast of Malta. It lies a short drive south of Rabat, near the village of Qrendi. The name comes from Maltese and is usually translated as 'standing stones' or 'worshipping stones'. Even before anyone dug here, the tallest blocks rose above the fields. Farmers and travellers knew this hill held something old. For centuries, nobody could say exactly how old.

Today archaeologists date the main building to roughly the second half of the fourth millennium before Christ. That is more than five thousand years ago. The builders were farming communities who had crossed to these islands from Sicily centuries earlier. They had no metal tools. They worked with stone, bone, and wood. With those simple tools, they shaped and raised slabs that weigh many tonnes. Nobody knows their own name for themselves, or for this place.

Here is the first surprise. Malta has several temple sites like this one, and together they form one of the oldest groups of free-standing stone buildings in the world. They are listed by UNESCO as the Megalithic Temples of Malta. Ħaġar Qim belongs to that group. The word 'megalithic' simply means built from very large stones. These temples are older than Stonehenge in its stone form, and they are much more complex inside.

Look at the stone itself. The builders used globigerina limestone, the soft local rock that forms much of Malta. It is easy to carve when freshly cut, which helped the builders shape doorways and decoration. But it is also soft against wind, salt, and rain. That is why the outer walls look so smooth and eroded. Five thousand years of sea weather have rounded every edge. In some places the surface has flaked away in thin layers.

That weathering explains the large white canopy above you. In the late 2000s a protective shelter was built over the site. It is designed to keep direct rain and harsh sun off the stone. Some visitors find it strange at first, because it breaks the view of the open sky. But it slows the damage. Under its shade, the stones stay cooler and drier, and they will last longer for the people who come after us.

Now walk toward the main façade. The entrance follows a classic Maltese temple pattern. Two upright stones carry a horizontal stone across the top. This is called a trilithon, a doorway made of three stones. Beyond it, the inside opens into rounded chambers, often called apses, linked by passages. From above, the plan looks a little like a cluster of lobes. Many people see a human body in that shape, though nobody knows if the builders intended this.

As you move through, watch for the openings cut straight through solid slabs. These are known as porthole doorways. A rectangular or oval hole is carved into a single stone, so you step or crawl through it. They feel deliberate, almost theatrical. Passing through a narrow opening into a darker room changes how a space feels. It is easy to imagine that only certain people were allowed to go further in.

Here is the second surprise, and it involves light. In one of the chambers on the northern side, a small hole is cut through the stone wall. Around the summer solstice, the longest day of the year, early morning sunlight passes through this hole. It throws a spot of light onto a slab inside. Whether the builders planned this exactly is still debated. But many researchers believe these people watched the sun closely, and marked its seasons in stone.

The site was first properly excavated in 1839. Since then, it has produced some of the most famous objects from prehistoric Malta. Among them are stone and clay figures with broad, rounded bodies. They are often called the 'fat figures'. Some may represent a fertility deity, while others may show people, or neither. Their meaning is still open. The originals are kept in the National Museum of Archaeology in Valletta, which is worth a visit if you want to see them close up.

The builders also decorated their temples. At Ħaġar Qim, archaeologists found carved altars and slabs covered with drilled dots. One well-known pillar altar shows a plant-like design growing from a pot. Many original carvings are now protected indoors, and copies help you picture them in place. Animal bones found at Maltese temple sites suggest that sacrifice and feasting took place. What prayers or words went with them, we will never know.

Around 2500 BC, the temple culture across Malta came to an end. The reason is still unclear. Archaeologists have suggested drought, famine, disease, or the arrival of new people. Perhaps it was several of these together. Whatever happened, the temples fell silent. Soil and stones slowly buried much of the site. For thousands of years, only the tallest uprights stood clear of the earth, waiting on this ridge above the sea.

Before you leave, follow the path that runs down the slope toward the sea. A few minutes' walk away lies Mnajdra, another temple complex from the same culture. The walk gives you the same view the builders had. Offshore, you can see the small, flat islet of Filfla rising from the water. On a clear day, the light here is sharp and white, and the wind carries salt and the smell of wild thyme.

So take one last look at the façade. Run your eyes along the joints between the stones, and the soft hollows left by the weather. People stood on this same ground more than five thousand years ago. They watched the same sea and the same rising sun. As you walk away, listen again to the wind across the ridge. It is one of the few things here that has hardly changed at all.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid midday heat on the exposed ridge.
Wear sturdy shoes for the dusty, uneven stone paths.
Walk the downhill path to visit nearby Mnajdra temples.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is limited outside.
Visit Valletta's archaeology museum to see the original finds.
