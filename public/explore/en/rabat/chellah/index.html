Chellah

34.0103° N · 6.8177° W · Ruins

Chellah is an archaeological site south of the medina, featuring Roman-era remains (the ancient city of Sala Colonia) overlaid with medieval Islamic structures. It is in Rabat, Morocco. Most visitors spend 1–1.5 hours here. The free Lokali audio guide runs 5 minutes.

The story

Pass through the tall gate in the red wall and stop. The noise of the city falls away behind you. Ahead, stone ruins lie scattered among tall grass and trees. Storks call from huge nests on the old towers. The air smells of earth and flowers. This is Chellah, a place of many layers. Walk in slowly and let your eyes adjust to the green.

Chellah sits on a slope above the Bou Regreg river, on the southern edge of Rabat. People have used this ground for a very long time. The earliest traces suggest that Phoenician traders set up a small post here, almost three thousand years ago. They came by sea, looking for trade along the coast. This was always a place between the land and the water.

Later the Romans built a real town on the same spot. They called it Sala Colonia. It was part of their distant province of Mauretania Tingitana. This was a working port city. Diggings have uncovered the main road, a forum where people gathered, and the base of a triumphal arch. Trade goods, grain, and people once passed through this port. The sea was its lifeline.

Walk down the path and you can still trace the Roman plan. The streets were laid out in an orderly grid. You can see the lines of old foundations in the grass. Columns lie where they fell long ago. For a while this was a busy place of trade and government, far from Rome itself. Stand on the old main street and look along its straight line. Two thousand years seem to fall away.

Then the town was slowly abandoned. The people left. The buildings fell into ruin. For a long stretch of time the site sat empty, with grass and weather working on the stone. What had been a Roman city became a quiet field of fallen walls above the river. Birds and wild plants took over where markets and temples had once stood.

Centuries later the site was given a new purpose. In the late thirteenth century, a Moroccan dynasty called the Marinids chose this ground for their royal burials. It became a necropolis, a resting place for sultans and their families. The old ruins now held new tombs among them. Stone that once served Rome was used again for a new faith and a new dynasty.

One ruler in particular shaped what you see. The sultan Abu al-Hassan enclosed part of the site with strong walls of red stone. He added a religious complex inside. In the year 1339 he built a zawiya here, a place for prayer and for housing the Sufi pilgrims who came to visit.

Walk among the Marinid remains and the mood changes again. There are tombs, a prayer hall, and the broken tower of a minaret. Old tilework still clings to some surfaces. The stone is warm in color. Plants grow through the cracks. Roman and medieval ruins now stand side by side in the same garden. Bees move over the wildflowers. The smell of fig and herb hangs in the warm air.

Look up and you will almost certainly see the storks. Great untidy nests sit on top of the walls and the old minaret. The birds clatter their bills and glide overhead. They have nested here for many generations. Their calls are one of the first things people remember about Chellah. Stand still and listen to the clatter and the wind for a while.

There is water here too. A spring feeds a small pool near the lower walls. By old tradition, people have come to these waters hoping for blessings and good fortune. Eels are said to live in the pool. Whatever you believe, the corner is cool, green, and very still. Reeds grow at the edge. Frogs and birds keep up a soft, steady sound.

Over the later centuries the necropolis fell into decline. Earthquakes shook the walls. Looters took what they could carry. Yet the great red gate and much of the enclosure survived. Today the ruins are protected, and the grounds are kept as a garden that is open to visitors. Cats doze in the sun on the warm fallen stones.

In 2012, Chellah was included in the UNESCO World Heritage site of Rabat. That recognition reaches across all of its layers at once. The Phoenician trace, the Roman town, the Marinid tombs, and the wild garden are all valued together as one long human story.

Find a bench in the shade before you go. Sit for a moment and look at it all. A Roman street, a sultan's tomb, a stork on a ruined tower, a green pool below. Few places hold so many centuries so gently within one single quiet view.

Practical tips

Visit in spring when the storks are nesting.
Wear comfortable shoes for the uneven stone paths.
Bring water, as there is little shade at midday.
Allow about an hour to walk all the ruins calmly.
