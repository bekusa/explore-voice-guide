The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in the square. The limestone under your feet is pale and smooth, worn by centuries of shoes. The light here is soft and golden, because almost everything around you is cut from the same honey-coloured stone. Listen... the noise of traffic from Rabat fades behind the old walls. In front of you rises a broad Baroque façade, two bell towers, and behind them a red-and-gold dome. This is Saint Paul's Cathedral, the heart of Mdina.

Mdina is often called the Silent City, and you can feel why. Cars are mostly kept outside the gates. The streets are narrow and they curve, so sound does not travel far. For most of its history this hilltop town was Malta's capital and the seat of its old noble families. Rabat grew up just outside its walls. Even today the two places sit side by side, one quiet and enclosed, one busy and open.

The story of this site begins with a shipwreck. According to the Acts of the Apostles, Saint Paul was shipwrecked on Malta while being taken to Rome as a prisoner, around the middle of the first century. The text says the island's chief man, Publius, welcomed him and gave him shelter. Christian tradition holds that this cathedral stands on the place where Publius had his house. That link is tradition rather than proven archaeology, but it has shaped Maltese faith for centuries.

Locals also say that Publius became the first bishop of Malta after Paul healed his father of a fever. Again, this is a story passed down through the church, not something historians can confirm. What is certain is that Christians have worshipped on this hilltop for a very long time. A church stood here in the Middle Ages. Over the centuries it was rebuilt and enlarged, and it became the cathedral of the Maltese bishops.

Then, in 1693, the ground moved. A powerful earthquake struck Sicily and eastern parts of the Mediterranean, and Malta felt it hard. The old cathedral in Mdina was badly damaged. Parts of it collapsed. For a small island, the loss of its mother church was a serious blow. But the response was quick. Within a few years, a new building was already rising on the same site.

The man chosen to design it was Lorenzo Gafà, one of Malta's leading architects of the time. He worked on the new cathedral in the closing years of the seventeenth century, and the main structure was finished around 1702. Gafà was known for his domes, and this one is often counted among his finest. Baroque, by the way, is a style of art and architecture from that era which loves drama, curves, light and rich decoration.

Now look at the façade itself. It is calm and balanced rather than wild. Columns and pilasters frame the doors in layers, and the two bell towers stand at each corner like guards. The stone is Maltese limestone, soft enough to carve with fine detail but quick to weather. If you look closely, you can see patches where newer stone replaces old. The colours differ slightly, a quiet record of repair across three centuries.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look at the clocks on the bell towers. Malta has a long folk tradition of churches with more than one clock face. Locals say that one clock was meant to confuse the devil, so he would never know the true time of Mass. It is a story rather than a rule, and not every clock follows it. But it is worth a glance, and a smile, before you go inside.

Step through the doors and the temperature drops. Your eyes take a moment to adjust. Then the floor catches your attention. It is covered with inlaid marble tombstones, marking the graves of bishops, clergy and Maltese nobles. Each slab is a small picture made of coloured stone, with coats of arms, skulls, crowns and Latin words. You are walking across a map of the island's old families. Tread gently, and look down as often as you look up.

Now the second surprise. When the earthquake brought down the old cathedral, a few treasures survived. One is a set of carved wooden doors from the old building, made of Irish oak and now used at the sacristy, the room where priests prepare for services. Another survivor is the area around the apse, the curved end behind the high altar. Its painting of the Conversion of Saint Paul, by the Italian painter Mattia Preti, came through the disaster.

Preti spent much of his later life in Malta, and his work fills churches across the island. Above the apse, a large fresco shows the shipwreck of Saint Paul, the very event that ties this hill to the saint. Let your eye travel from the altar up to the dome. The light falls from above, through windows high in the drum. On a bright day, the interior seems to glow from within, warm gold against deep red.

This cathedral shares its role with another great church. Saint John's in Valletta is also a co-cathedral, and the Archbishop of Malta has his seat in both. Mdina's cathedral is the older title, rooted in the island's first Christian story. Across the square, a former seminary now holds the Cathedral Museum. It keeps church silver, manuscripts, coins and art. If you have time, it adds depth to what you see here.

As you leave, pause again in the square. Notice how the cathedral fills almost the whole view, yet the space around it stays intimate. Small palaces and quiet houses press close on every side. Bells may ring from the towers above you. Walk a few streets toward the walls, and the island opens below you, green and tan, stretching toward the sea. Somewhere out there, tradition says, Paul came ashore. And here, on this hill, the story has stayed.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly with shoulders and knees covered inside.
Visit early morning when Mdina's streets are quietest.
Combine your visit with the Cathedral Museum across the square.
Walk to the city walls afterwards for views over Malta.
Keep voices low during services and private prayer.
