The guide

The story

Feel the stone under your shoes first. It is smooth and slightly worn, a pale honey colour that warms as the sun climbs. Up here the wind moves freely, and below you the rooftops of the town spread out in every direction. You are standing on the highest ground in the heart of Gozo, Malta's smaller sister island, inside the fortress that locals simply call il-Kastell.

A quick note on names, because they can confuse visitors. This Rabat is not the capital of Morocco. It is the main town of Gozo. In 1887 the British renamed it Victoria, to mark Queen Victoria's jubilee. Road signs and maps use that name. But many Gozitans still call their town Rabat, as they have for centuries. The Cittadella sits on top of it, like a crown on a head.

People chose this hill long before anyone built these walls. Archaeologists have found evidence that the site was settled as far back as the Bronze Age. Later, the Phoenicians and then the Romans used the hilltop as a fortified centre. The reason is easy to understand when you look around. From here, you can see almost the whole island, and you can see danger coming from the sea long before it arrives.

That danger was real. For centuries, the islands of the central Mediterranean lived in fear of raids. The worst came in 1551, when an Ottoman fleet landed on Gozo and laid siege to this citadel. The old walls could not hold. When the fortress surrendered, thousands of people were carried away into slavery. Historians generally describe it as almost the entire population of the island. Gozo was nearly emptied in a single summer.

The walls you see today are largely a response to that disaster. The Knights of Saint John, the military religious order that ruled Malta at the time, rebuilt and strengthened the defences in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. Notice the thick bastions and the steep, angled faces of the stone. They were designed to deflect cannon fire, not arrows. This was a fortress built for a new age of gunpowder warfare.

For a period after the raid, the island's people were required to spend their nights inside the citadel walls. By day they farmed the fields below. By night they climbed back up for safety. Only as the threat of raids faded did families move out and build the town at the foot of the hill. The Rabat around you grew, in a sense, from people who finally felt safe enough to sleep outside.

Now turn toward the centre of the citadel, to the cathedral with its broad flight of steps. This is the Cathedral of the Assumption, dedicated to the Virgin Mary. It was built at the turn of the eighteenth century to a design by Lorenzo Gafà, a Maltese architect who shaped many of the islands' great Baroque churches. Baroque is the dramatic, theatrical style that spread across Catholic Europe in that period. The facade is calm and balanced, almost restrained.

Here is the first surprise. From outside, the cathedral seems to have no dome. And it does not. Plans for one were never carried out. Instead, inside, the flat ceiling was painted to look like a dome, with columns and light rising into an imaginary space. Stand near the centre of the nave and look up. Then walk slowly toward the altar. The illusion holds, then shifts, then breaks. Many visitors only notice the trick when they move.

The cathedral steps are not just an entrance. In mid August, during the feast of the Assumption, they become a stage. Crowds gather in the square below. A statue of Mary is carried out in procession, bells ring across the town, and fireworks crack over the rooftops. If you come at another time of year, the square is quieter. But the steps still feel built for an audience.

The second surprise is easy to miss. Look at the lanes beyond the cathedral, where the ground rises and the buildings grow rougher. Some of this area was left in ruins for a long time. You can still see foundations, broken walls and the outlines of old houses. These are traces of the medieval settlement that once filled the whole citadel, when hundreds of people lived packed inside these walls.

Several small museums now sit within the fortress. There is a museum of archaeology, a museum of natural science, and a cathedral museum. One of the most personal is the old prison. On its stone walls, prisoners carved graffiti over the centuries. You can find ships, crosses, hands and simple marks counting the days. They are small, rough lines. But they are some of the most direct human voices left in this place.

In recent years the citadel went through a major restoration. The walls were repaired, the walkways improved, and a visitor centre was added near the entrance. Some people miss the rougher, crumbling look of earlier decades. Others are glad the stone has been made safe for the next generations. Either way, the work means you can now walk much of the circuit of the walls, which is the best way to understand the place.

So take that walk if you can. On a clear day, the island opens up around you. You can pick out village church domes rising from the countryside, terraced fields, flat-topped hills, and the line of the sea. Each village is a small cluster of honey-coloured stone around its church. It is a view that explains Gozo better than any map. A small island, farmed and defended, with faith and survival close together.

Before you go, pause for a moment at the edge of the walls. Listen to the wind, the church bells, the traffic and voices drifting up from the town below. The people who once slept here every night looked out at these same fields. As you walk back down into Rabat, notice how the streets still curve toward this hill. The town grew outward from the citadel, and it still turns toward it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full circuit of the walls for island-wide views.
Visit in the morning for softer light on the stone.
Allow extra time if you plan to enter the small museums.
Wear shoes with good grip for the smooth, worn paving.
Come in mid August to see the Assumption feast celebrations.
