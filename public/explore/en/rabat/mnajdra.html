The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes up from the sea in long, steady pushes, and the dry scrub around you rustles and ticks. Underfoot, the path is pale dust and broken limestone. Below, the Mediterranean spreads out in a hard, bright blue. Ahead of you, under a wide protective canopy, rows of grey and honey-coloured stones stand in curved walls. You have reached Mnajdra, on the southern coast of Malta, a short drive from the old inland town of Rabat.

These stones are older than most of what people think of as ancient. The earliest building here dates to the fourth millennium before Christ, roughly five and a half thousand years ago. That makes parts of Mnajdra older than the great stone circle of Stonehenge in its stone phase. It is also older than the pyramids of Egypt. The people who raised these walls had no metal tools and no writing. They had stone, rope, timber, and a great deal of shared effort.

Mnajdra is not one temple. It is three, set side by side and facing a shared open space. Archaeologists usually call them the upper, middle, and lower temples. The upper one is the smallest and the oldest, and it is the most worn by time. The lower temple came later and is the most carefully built. The middle temple was squeezed in between them later still. So when you look along the row, you are looking at several centuries of building in one glance.

Look closely at the stone itself, because there are two kinds. The rough outer walls use coralline limestone, a hard, grey rock that stands up well to wind and salt. The inner slabs and doorways are mostly globigerina limestone, softer and warmer in colour. The builders chose this softer stone where they wanted fine shaping and decoration. That choice has a cost today. The soft stone wears away faster, which is one reason the site needs so much protection.

That protection is the large canopy above your head. It was put up in the late 2000s, over Mnajdra and its neighbour, Ħaġar Qim. Some visitors find it strange at first, a modern roof over a prehistoric ruin. But it shields the stones from heavy rain, from the hottest sun, and from the salt that the wind carries off the sea. Under its shade, the light becomes softer and more even, and the details on the stones become easier to see.

Walk into the lower temple and notice the shape of the rooms. Each temple is built as a series of rounded chambers, often called apses, arranged on either side of a central passage. Seen from above, the plan can look a little like a cloverleaf, or like a body with rounded limbs. Nobody knows for certain what this shape meant to the builders. Some researchers connect it to fertility and the human figure. Others see it simply as a practical way to roof and divide space.

Now comes the detail most visitors miss. The lower temple faces east, and it is aligned with the sun. Heritage researchers have recorded that at sunrise on the spring and autumn equinoxes, the first light passes straight through the main doorway. It runs along the central passage and reaches the inner chamber. At the summer and winter solstices, the light falls instead on the edges of stone slabs beside the inner doorway. Whether this was a calendar, a ritual, or both, the alignment is real and can still be watched today.

Look at the surfaces of the slabs around the doorways. Many are covered with small, neat holes drilled into the stone in rows and patterns. This is called pitted decoration, and it appears in several of Malta's temples. Making each hole by hand, with stone tools, took patient work. In some walls you will also see small openings cut through a slab, linking one room to another. People sometimes call them oracle holes. Their real purpose is not known, so treat that name as a modern guess, not a fact.

Here is a second surprise. In the middle temple, one upright stone carries a faint engraving. It appears to show the front of a temple building, with its facade and doorway. If that reading is right, it is one of the oldest known drawings of a building in this part of the world. Think about that for a moment. The people who used this temple may have carved a picture of a temple, standing inside a temple, more than five thousand years ago.

The temples were largely forgotten for a very long time. Soil and rubble covered much of them, and only the tops of some stones showed above the ground. Serious excavation began in the nineteenth century, around 1840. Since then, archaeologists have found pottery, stone tools, animal bones, and carved objects here. Many of the finest finds from Malta's temples are now kept in the national archaeology museum in Valletta, the capital. What stays here is the architecture itself, in the place where it was built.

Mnajdra is part of a group of sites listed together by UNESCO as the Megalithic Temples of Malta. The word megalithic simply means built with very large stones. You may already have this temple in your pocket without knowing it. The Maltese euro coins of one, two, and five cents show an image of Mnajdra. Check any small change you collect on the island. It is a quiet sign of how much these stones mean to Maltese identity.

Before you leave, turn around and look out to sea. The small, flat island you can see offshore is Filfla, an uninhabited rock and nature reserve. It is easy to imagine that the builders chose this slope partly for the view, with open water and that lonely islet in front of them. We cannot know their reasons for sure. But the setting is part of the experience. The temple, the cliffs, and the sea all belong to the same picture.

As you walk back up the path, the canopy will drop behind you, and the wind will take over again. Notice the stone walls along the fields, the wild thyme, the small birds in the scrub. Stop for a moment and look back at the grey curve of the walls against the blue. The sun that rises through that doorway at the equinox is the same sun on your face right now. Five thousand years ago, someone stood here and watched for it too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with Ħaġar Qim along the connecting path.
Wear sturdy shoes for the dusty, sloping limestone path.
Bring water and a hat for the exposed coastal walk.
Visit near an equinox sunrise to watch the solar alignment.
Save time for the archaeology museum in Valletta afterwards.
