The guide

The story

Listen first. A low wind moves across flat fields, and somewhere a tractor hums. The air smells of turned earth and, on a warm day, faintly of the sea. Underfoot the ground is soft and level, a wide plain between two rivers. This is not the setting most people picture for an ancient city. Yet here, close to the shore of the Gulf of Taranto, Greek settlers once laid out streets, temples and a marketplace. Welcome to Metapontum.

The settlers came from Achaea, a region in the northern Peloponnese of Greece. Ancient writers place the foundation in the seventh century before Christ, during the great wave of Greek colonisation along the coasts of southern Italy. The Romans later called this whole region Magna Graecia — Great Greece — because so many Greek cities rose here. Metapontum sat between the Bradano and Basento rivers. That location gave it two gifts: fresh water and some of the most fertile soil on this coast.

And the city built its identity on that soil. Look at almost any coin from Metapontum and you will see the same image — a single ear of barley, its grains drawn with care. It was the city's emblem for generations. Ancient sources also say the people of Metapontum sent a remarkable offering to the sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi: a harvest made of gold. Whatever its exact form, the message was clear. This was a city that lived from the field.

Those fields were not random. Archaeologists, especially teams working here from the 1970s onward, traced long, regular land divisions across the surrounding countryside. Greeks called this farmland the chora — the territory that fed the city. Excavations uncovered farmhouses, small rural shrines and family burial grounds scattered through it. Together they show a planned landscape. Plots were measured, roads ran straight, and ordinary farmers lived out on the land rather than only inside the walls.

Now turn your attention to the urban centre. Inside the archaeological park, the ground plan of the old city opens out before you. You can follow the line of the sacred area, where several temples once stood side by side. Only their foundations remain — long rectangles of pale stone set into the grass. Nearby lay the agora, the public square where citizens traded, argued and voted. Here, too, stood a large gathering place for assemblies, later rebuilt as a theatre.

The largest temple in the sanctuary is linked to Apollo, the god of light, music and prophecy. Apollo Lykeios was a favourite in this region. Other foundations belonged to shrines for different gods, including Hera, queen of the gods. As you walk, notice how close the buildings sit to one another. This was a crowded holy precinct, not an empty lawn. Picture painted roof tiles, bronze offerings and the smoke of sacrifices rising over the plain.

Here is something many visitors miss. The ground at Metapontum is low, and the water table lies close to the surface. Over the centuries, rising groundwater and river silt have covered and soaked the ancient remains. In wet seasons, parts of the excavated area can hold standing water. That is one reason the land later became marshy and unhealthy, and why the city faded. It is also why the ruins sit so quietly in the grass today.

Now for the name that gives this place its strangest fame. Pythagoras, the philosopher and mathematician we remember for a triangle theorem, spent his final years at Metapontum. He had founded a community of followers at nearby Croton. After political trouble there, ancient tradition says he moved here. Many centuries later the Roman orator Cicero wrote that he visited Metapontum and saw the place associated with Pythagoras. Even in antiquity, people came here to stand where a famous mind had lived.

The city's later history was harder. During the long wars between Rome and Carthage, the Carthaginian general Hannibal used Metapontum as a base in southern Italy. When his campaign failed, the city lost much of its power. Under Roman rule it shrank. Malaria and flooding pressed in from the lowlands. By late antiquity the once confident colony was a small settlement, and the fields slowly swallowed its streets.

About three kilometres north of the city centre, near the Bradano river, stands the most striking survivor. Locals call it the Tavole Palatine. It is a Doric temple — Doric is the plain, sturdy style of Greek column, with no base and a simple cushion-shaped top. Fifteen columns still stand, in two rows along the long sides. Scholars generally connect the temple with Hera. If you have time, it is worth the short drive to see them rising from the plain.

Legend has given these columns other stories. The name Tavole Palatine is often explained as the tables of the paladins — the knights of medieval romance. One popular tale links the ruins to these knights meeting there. There is no evidence for it, of course. But it shows how people in later centuries looked at these columns, standing alone in farmland, and wove their own meaning around them.

Many of the finest objects from this territory now rest in the national archaeological museum in the modern village of Metaponto. Its galleries hold pottery, terracotta figures, coins and grave goods from the city and its farms. Seeing the museum alongside the park makes the ruins easier to read. The foundations in the grass show you the scale. The objects behind glass show you the people — their gods, their meals, their jewellery and their hopes.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look around. The same flat horizon is in front of you. The same rivers still run toward the same sea. Fields of grain and orchards still cover the plain, just as the barley on those old coins promised. As you walk back across the soft ground, listen again to the wind over the fields. It is, in its own way, the oldest sound at Metapontum.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Metaponto museum before the park for context.
Drive to the Tavole Palatine; it sits north of the park.
Wear sturdy shoes; the ground can be soft or muddy.
Bring sun protection; the plain has very little shade.
Come early morning for cooler air and softer light.
