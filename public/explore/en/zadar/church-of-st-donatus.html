The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the outer wall. The stone is rough and uneven, warm in the afternoon and cool in the morning. Above you, a great round drum of masonry rises with almost no decoration at all. There are no carved saints and no painted portals. There is only weight, curve and shadow. Around you, the old Roman forum of Zadar opens out in pale paving stones, and somewhere beyond the rooftops you can hear the Adriatic moving against the shore.

This is the Church of Saint Donatus. For more than a thousand years, it has been the shape that people think of when they think of Zadar. It is a round church, built in the early Middle Ages, in a style that historians call pre-Romanesque. That simply means it belongs to the centuries before the great Romanesque churches of Europe, with their heavy arches and carved doorways. Here in Croatia, it is usually described as the largest building of its kind.

The church was built around the ninth century. At that time, Zadar sat on a delicate line between two powers. To the east was the Byzantine Empire, ruled from Constantinople. To the west was the growing Frankish realm of Charlemagne. Zadar was a Byzantine city on the Adriatic coast, and its leaders had to speak carefully to both sides. Trade, faith and survival all depended on that balance.

One of those leaders was a bishop named Donatus. Historical sources record him as a diplomat as well as a churchman. He travelled as an envoy in the negotiations between the Byzantines and the Franks in the early ninth century. Tradition links the building of this church to him. Interestingly, it did not carry his name at first. The church was originally dedicated to the Holy Trinity. Only centuries later, around the fifteenth century, did people begin to call it after the bishop himself.

Now look down, toward the base of the walls. This is the first surprise most visitors miss. The builders did not quarry fresh stone for the foundations. They used what was already lying around them... the ruins of Roman Zadar. You can see fragments of Roman columns, pieces of cornice and blocks with Latin letters set sideways or upside down. Old temples and monuments became the footings of a Christian church.

This was not unusual in the early Middle Ages. Roman cities across Europe became quarries for the people who came after. But here the practice is unusually easy to see. The church stands directly on the paving of the Roman forum, the old public square at the heart of the town. In the twentieth century, the medieval floor inside was removed. Beneath it, the original Roman paving stones appeared again, and today you walk on the same surface that Roman citizens once crossed.

Step inside, if the doors are open. The space feels much taller than it looks from outside. A circular central area rises toward the roof, and around it runs a ring-shaped passage. Above that is an upper gallery, reached by stairs. In many early churches, galleries like this were used to separate groups of worshippers. Stand still for a moment and let your eyes adjust. The light comes in softly, and the walls are almost entirely bare.

That bareness is part of the experience. There is very little ornament here — no gold, no frescoes covering every surface. Instead, you see the structure itself. Thick piers carry the upper level. Arches spring from them in simple curves. Toward the east, three apses push outward from the round body of the church. An apse is a rounded recess, usually where the altar stood. From outside, these three curves give the building its distinctive, clustered silhouette.

Here is a second surprise. This building has not been used as a church for about two hundred years. In the early nineteenth century, after the end of Venetian rule over Zadar, it stopped serving as a place of worship. For a time it was used for very practical purposes, including storage. Later it became part of the city's archaeological collections. Today it is protected as a monument rather than a parish.

And yet the building has found a new voice. The round interior and hard stone surfaces give it remarkable acoustics. For decades, Zadar has used it as a concert space, especially for early and classical music. Musicians often speak about the long, clear resonance of a single note under this roof. If you ever have the chance to hear a performance here, the sound fills the space in a way no modern hall quite matches.

Walk back outside and turn slowly around. Next to the church stands the Cathedral of Saint Anastasia, with its tall bell tower. Nearby is the Archaeological Museum, which holds many finds from this very square. On the forum itself, look for a single standing Roman column. Locals call it the Pillar of Shame. In the Middle Ages, wrongdoers were chained to it and displayed to the public, so a Roman monument became a medieval punishment post.

Scattered around the square, you will also see loose Roman stones, column fragments and carved blocks lying in the open. Children sit on them. Tourists lean against them to check their phones. Cats sleep on them in the sun. There are very few places where two thousand years of history sit so casually in public space. Here, the past is not behind glass. It is simply part of the furniture of the town.

Before you leave, find a spot a little distance away and look at the church again. Notice how its round body and three apses catch the light differently as the sun moves. Notice the Roman stones at its feet, the cathedral tower beside it, and the open square in front. As you walk on toward the waterfront, you are leaving a building made from older buildings — a place that Zadar has rebuilt, renamed and reused, and still keeps at its centre.
