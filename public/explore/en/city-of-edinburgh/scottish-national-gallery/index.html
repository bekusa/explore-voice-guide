The guide

The story

Sandstone columns rise in front of you, pale and warm in the northern light. Below, traffic moves along the streets of the city. You are standing on The Mound, a ridge of earth that links the old town and the new town of Edinburgh. The gallery sits here at the center, between two worlds. Its honey-colored stone holds the soft grey light of the sky.

The building first opened in 1859. The architect was William Henry Playfair, a man who shaped much of the city. He worked in the neoclassical style. That means tall columns, clean straight lines, and shapes borrowed from the temples of ancient Greece. Look at the Ionic columns at the entrance. Their tops curl over like scrolls. They give the building a calm and balanced face that has not changed in more than a century.

Step inside, and the noise of the streets falls away. The walls of the rooms are painted in deep red and green. Paintings hang in heavy gold frames, lit from above. The light is low, soft, and even. This is the home of Scotland's national collection of fine art. Entry to the main rooms is free, open to everyone who walks through the door.

The gallery holds work by many of the great painters of Europe. You can find Titian, who painted in Venice five hundred years ago. You can find Raphael from Italy, and rooms full of Dutch art, including work by Rembrandt. There is Spanish painting by Velazquez. Each room carries you into a different country and a different century, all under one roof.

Move slowly through the rooms and watch how the art changes. The oldest paintings often show religious scenes. Saints, angels, and still figures fill the frames in gold and blue. As you walk forward in time, the subjects shift. You begin to see portraits of real people, wide landscapes, and quiet scenes from daily life. The art follows the slow turning of European thought.

One part of the gallery belongs to Scotland alone. Here hang the works of Scottish painters who recorded their own land and their own people. Look for portraits of serious men and women in dark clothes. Look for wide views of hills, lochs, and heavy grey skies. These paintings show how the country once saw itself, and how it wished to be seen.

A small painting often draws a quiet crowd. It shows a minister skating on a frozen loch. He leans back, his arms folded, gliding across the ice in a black coat and hat. The work is long attributed to the Scottish painter Henry Raeburn. Its simple shape and calm mood have made it one of the best loved images in all of Scotland.

The gallery does not stand alone. Beside it sits another building with columns, the Royal Scottish Academy. The same architect, Playfair, designed them both. A lower floor now links the two underground. You can pass from one building to the other without stepping outside into the rain. Together they form a single home for art at the heart of The Mound.

Around the stone walls lie the Princes Street Gardens. Long ago, a lake of dark water filled this valley below the castle. The city drained it away in the early nineteenth century. Green lawns, trees, and winding paths took its place. From the steps of the gallery, you can look out across the gardens to the castle standing high on its rock of black stone.

Take your time with a single painting. You do not need to see every room in one visit. Choose one work and stand quietly with it. Notice the colors the painter chose. Notice the way the light falls and the shadow gathers. Notice the small details hidden in a corner. A few minutes of close and patient looking can stay with you for many years.

The gallery is open on most days, and entry to the main collection costs nothing at all. There is a cafe where you can sit and rest your feet. Special exhibitions sometimes ask for a ticket. Large bags may be checked at the door for safety. Quiet voices are welcome here, but the rooms ask for a gentle and unhurried kind of attention.

When you are ready to leave, step out onto The Mound once more. The sound of the city returns around you. The light shifts again on the pale stone. You carry with you the faces and colors you have seen inside. For a short while, the gallery held them still and silent for you. Now they travel on, quietly, within your own memory.
