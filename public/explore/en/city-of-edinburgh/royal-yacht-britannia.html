The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps softly against a steel hull, and somewhere a gull calls over the docks of Leith. The deck under your feet is pale teak, scrubbed smooth by decades of hands and brushes. Above you, the dark blue hull rises with a thin gold line along its side. Brass catches the light. For more than forty years this ship carried a queen around the world. Now she rests here, at the edge of Edinburgh's old port, quiet and still.

This is the Royal Yacht Britannia. She was built on the River Clyde, at the John Brown shipyard in Clydebank, a yard that also launched great ocean liners. Queen Elizabeth the Second launched her in 1953, the year of the coronation. The ship entered service the following year. For the young Queen, Britannia became something rare in a royal life. She was a floating palace, a working ship, and, for a few weeks at a time, something close to a private family home.

Britannia was not the first royal yacht. A long line of earlier vessels came before her, stretching back several centuries. But she would be the last. Over her career she sailed more than a million nautical miles. She called at ports on every inhabited continent. She carried the royal family on state visits, official tours and long summer cruises around the coast of Scotland. Wherever she anchored, she brought a piece of Britain with her.

Here is a detail that surprises many visitors. The ship had a second purpose, planned from the start. In wartime, Britannia was meant to become a hospital ship. That plan shaped parts of her design. She never served in that role. Yet in 1986, during civil war in Yemen, she helped evacuate more than a thousand people from the shore near Aden. Many of the people she rescued were not British. A royal yacht became, for a few tense days, a lifeboat.

As you move through the ship, notice how two worlds sit side by side. On the upper decks are the royal apartments, calm and carefully kept. Below are the crew's quarters, the engine rooms, the laundry and the mess decks. The crew were Royal Navy sailors, known affectionately as yachtsmen. Several hundred of them lived aboard on a long voyage, along with a band of Royal Marines. Space was tight, and the work never stopped.

The yachtsmen worked under some unusual rules. Near the royal apartments, crew tried to move as quietly as possible. Many orders on the upper deck were given with hand signals, not shouted commands. Sailors wore soft-soled shoes to keep the noise down. Work near the royal rooms was often done early in the morning, before the family woke. The aim was simple. The family should feel at rest, even on a busy naval vessel.

Now look at the royal rooms themselves. They are elegant, but they are not grand in the way of a palace. The furniture is comfortable. The fabrics are soft and the colours are calm. The Queen reportedly said that Britannia was one of the few places where she could truly relax. The Sun Lounge, with its wide windows and light, cane-style furniture, is often described as one of her favourite rooms on board.

The royal bedrooms hold a second surprise. They are modest, and they contain single beds. For most of the ship's life, there was no double bed on board at all. One was brought aboard in 1981, when Prince Charles and Princess Diana spent their honeymoon on the yacht. They were not the only royal couple to do so. Princess Margaret and Princess Anne also began their married lives at sea on Britannia.

The State Dining Room shows the ship's public face. Here the long table was laid for official banquets during state visits. The guests over the years included prime ministers, presidents and royalty from around the world. Winston Churchill, Ronald Reagan and Nelson Mandela all came aboard. Setting the table was a precise job. Crew members used measuring sticks to place each glass and piece of cutlery in exactly the right spot.

One more detail is easy to walk past. On board there is a garage, and inside it a royal Rolls-Royce. The car travelled with the family, so they could drive in style when the ship reached port. Getting it in and out was not simple. The crew had to lift it with care, and space around it was very tight. It is a small, practical sign of how far the planning went.

Britannia's last great public duty came in 1997. She carried Prince Charles to the handover of Hong Kong to China, one of the final moments of the British Empire. Later that year, the ship was decommissioned. At the ceremony, the Queen was seen to wipe away a tear. It was a rare public show of emotion. Many people saw it as a sign of how much this ship meant to her.

After decommissioning, the yacht came north to Leith, the historic port of Edinburgh. She opened to visitors soon after, and today she is kept much as she was in service. Throughout the ship, the clocks have been stopped. They show the time when the Queen last stepped ashore. It is a quiet, precise way to mark the end of a long story, and it is worth a second look.

As you finish your visit, stand for a moment on the deck again. Look across the water at the docks and the new buildings of modern Leith. Feel the teak under your feet and hear the gulls overhead. This ship once crossed oceans, hosted world leaders and offered a family some privacy. Now she stays in one place. When you walk away, look back once at that dark blue hull and its fine gold line.
