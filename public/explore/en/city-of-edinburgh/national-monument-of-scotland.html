The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here on Calton Hill, it rarely stops. It comes off the Firth of Forth, crosses the rooftops, and moves through a row of huge stone columns that hold up nothing at all. The grass is short and springy underfoot. Somewhere below, a bus changes gear on Princes Street. And in front of you stand twelve grey pillars on a stone platform, open to the sky. Welcome to the National Monument of Scotland, a temple that was never finished.

The idea began in the years after the Napoleonic Wars. Britain had fought France for more than two decades, and many Scottish soldiers and sailors had died. Scotland wanted a national memorial to honour them. The plan was ambitious. This would not be a simple statue or an obelisk. It would be a full-scale copy of the Parthenon, the great temple on the Acropolis in Athens. Scotland would build its own Greek temple, high on a hill above its capital.

Why Greece? In the early nineteenth century, Edinburgh had a strong sense of itself as a city of learning. It had universities, philosophers, scientists and writers. People began to call it the Athens of the North. The comparison was partly about ideas, and partly about landscape. Edinburgh has a rocky hill crowned with old buildings, much as Athens has the Acropolis. A Parthenon on Calton Hill would make that link visible to anyone looking up from the streets below.

The design came from two architects. Charles Robert Cockerell was an English architect who had studied ancient Greek buildings at first hand. William Henry Playfair was one of the most important architects in Edinburgh at the time. Look around this hilltop and you will see more of Playfair's work. The foundation stone was laid in 1822, during the visit of King George the Fourth to Scotland. It was a grand royal occasion, full of ceremony and public excitement.

Then came the hard part — paying for it. Construction began in the mid-1820s, funded by public subscription. That means ordinary donors and wealthy patrons were asked to contribute. But the money never reached the amount needed. Building a Greek temple out of massive stone blocks was enormously expensive. By 1829 the funds had run out, and the work stopped. What you see now is what stood at that moment: the base, a row of twelve columns, and the beam of stone across their tops.

Here is something many visitors miss. Edinburgh did not quietly accept this. For years, people mocked the half-built temple. It picked up nicknames such as Scotland's Disgrace and Edinburgh's Folly. A national memorial that could not be finished felt, to some, like a public embarrassment. Over time, though, the feeling changed. The unfinished columns became one of the most recognisable shapes on the city skyline. The so-called folly turned into a landmark people love.

Walk closer and look at the columns themselves. They are in the Doric style. Doric is the oldest and plainest of the classical Greek styles. The columns have no base of their own and they have simple, cushion-shaped tops. Run your eye up the shafts and you will see vertical grooves, called fluting, cut into the stone. These grooves catch the light and make each column look taller and slimmer than it really is.

Now consider the scale. Stand at the foot of a column and look up. Each one is built from several stacked drums of stone, not a single piece. Those blocks had to be quarried, carved and hauled up this steep hill, long before modern cranes or lorries. That effort is part of the reason the money ran out so quickly. You can see the joints between the drums if you look carefully. Notice too how the stone has darkened and weathered with two centuries of Scottish rain.

The platform the columns stand on is wide and solid, and it has become a place to sit. On a fine day you will find people here with flasks of coffee, sandwiches, and cameras. Students climb up to talk. Couples sit with their legs over the edge. It is one of the few national monuments where people actually spend time inside the structure. The temple was meant to be solemn. Today it is also a place for lunch and conversation.

Turn around now and take in the view. To one side rises Arthur's Seat, the old volcanic hill in Holyrood Park. Below it lies the area around the Palace of Holyroodhouse and the Scottish Parliament. Look the other way and you will find the long line of Princes Street and the Old Town on its ridge, with the castle at its end. On a clear day you can see across the Firth of Forth to the coast of Fife.

The monument has neighbours worth noticing. Close by stands the Nelson Monument, a tall tower shaped like an upturned telescope, built to remember Admiral Nelson. There is also the Dugald Stewart Monument, a small round temple designed by Playfair, which appears in countless photographs of the city. And there is the walled City Observatory, a reminder that this hill was once a centre for watching the stars. Together they make Calton Hill a kind of open-air gallery of ideas.

Over the years, there have been suggestions to finish the temple at last. None of them succeeded. And perhaps that is fitting. The monument now tells two stories. One is about the soldiers and sailors it was built to honour. The other is about ambition, money, and a city that wanted to be seen as a modern Athens. The empty space behind the columns is part of the memorial too. It shows the gap between what Edinburgh hoped to build and what it could afford.

Before you leave, stand for a moment between two of the columns and look out through the gap. The stone frames the city like a window. Listen to the wind again, and to the voices of people sitting on the steps. Notice how the light shifts across the fluting as the clouds move. As you walk back down the hill, turn once and look up. Twelve columns, still standing, still unfinished, and still part of Edinburgh's skyline.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come on a clear day for views to Fife.
Bring a windproof layer; the hilltop is very exposed.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep climb and grass.
Sit on the platform steps to rest and watch the city.
Visit near sunset for warm light on the columns.
