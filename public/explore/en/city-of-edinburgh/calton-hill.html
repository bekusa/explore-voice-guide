The story

The climb is short, but the air changes as you rise. Steps lead up from the street, and then the ground opens flat at the top. Calton Hill is one of several hills in Edinburgh, and one of the easiest to reach on foot. From here the whole city lies around you, and a strange set of monuments stands on the grass.

The first thing many people notice looks out of place. A row of tall stone columns stands alone, holding nothing up. This is the National Monument. It was begun in 1826 to honour Scottish soldiers and sailors who died in the wars against Napoleon. The plan was grand, modelled on an ancient temple in Athens.

Then the money ran out. Only twelve columns were ever raised before the work stopped for good. Some people mocked it and called it the city's folly. Others slowly grew fond of it. Today it stands unfinished, and that is its character. Climb up onto the base, and the columns frame the sky and the sea beyond.

Nearby rises a tall, dark tower shaped like an upturned telescope. This is the Nelson Monument, built to remember Admiral Nelson after the battle of Trafalgar. You can climb the narrow stair inside to a balcony near the top. The view rewards the effort, reaching out over the rooftops to the wide water beyond.

Look closely at the very top of the tower. A large ball sits on a mast there. Once a day it rises and then drops. This is the time ball. It falls at one o'clock, at the same moment the gun fires at the castle across the valley. Together they once gave ships two signals, one to see and one to hear.

A round building with columns stands a little apart from the rest. People often photograph it against the city behind. This is the Dugald Stewart Monument, raised for a professor of philosophy. Its simple circle of stone has become one of the shapes most closely linked with Edinburgh. At sunset the light gathers behind it.
