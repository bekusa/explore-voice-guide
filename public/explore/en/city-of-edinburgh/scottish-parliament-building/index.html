The story

At the foot of the long hill, where the old street finally ends, the buildings suddenly change. Curves and sharp angles replace the straight stone fronts. Pale concrete, oak, and granite fold together in shapes that surprise the eye. This is the home of the Scottish Parliament, and it looks like nothing else around it.

The location matters a great deal. The building sits at Holyrood, at the bottom of the Royal Mile, directly across from the old royal palace. So the country's newest seat of power faces its oldest. Behind it rises the green slope of the surrounding park. The architect wanted the building to grow out of this meeting of city and land.

That architect was Enric Miralles, who came from Catalonia in Spain. He won the competition to design the building and filled it with ideas drawn from the Scottish landscape, from boats pulled up on a shore, and from leaves and stems. He did not live to see it finished. He died in 2000, four years before the doors opened.

The building opened in 2004. Its construction proved difficult, and it cost far more than first planned, which led to much public argument at the time. Yet once it was complete, opinion slowly began to shift. In 2005 it won the Stirling Prize, a major award for architecture. Many who disliked the idea came to admire the result.

Look at the offices where the members work. Each one has a window that pushes out from the wall, with a built-in seat beneath it. These shapes step along the front of the building in a long row. They were made as quiet spots for reading and thought, and people often call them the contemplation windows.

Along one edge runs a low wall that faces the street. Set into it are pieces of stone from across Scotland, each carved with words. The quotations come from writers, from scripture, and from ordinary speech. Visitors stop to read them one by one. The wall ties this modern building to the country's own many voices.

Inside, the debating chamber is the heart of the building. The seats curve in a wide half-circle, rather than facing off in two opposing rows. Above them, a roof of oak beams and steel braces spreads across the room. Natural light falls from windows set high overhead. The shape was meant to encourage discussion over conflict.

Throughout the building, the same materials repeat again and again. Oak, granite, and concrete appear over and over, both indoors and out. Small ponds and channels of water sit among the paths outside. Grass grows over some of the lower roofs, so that parts of the building seem to rise straight out of the ground.

The design carries meaning in its details. Curved forms echo upturned boats. Branching shapes recall leaves and trees. Not everyone reads these the same way, and some shapes simply please the eye. The architect wanted a building rooted in its own place, rather than a copy of older parliaments built elsewhere.

The Parliament is open to the public. People can visit the main hall, join a tour, or sit in the gallery to watch a debate when the members meet. Entry is free of charge. There are usually checks at the door, as in any working seat of government, but visitors are genuinely welcome inside.

Step outside again and compare the two buildings. The palace across the road is old and orderly, built around a square courtyard. The Parliament beside it is restless and modern, full of movement. The contrast is not an accident. It shows a country holding its long past and its present government side by side, within a single view.

One older building hides within the complex itself. Queensberry House, a mansion from the seventeenth century, was kept and carefully worked into the modern design rather than torn down. Its plain older stone walls now sit among the new concrete and oak. The choice was a deliberate one. It let the Parliament hold on to a thread of the past inside a building that otherwise looks firmly toward the present and the years ahead.

Stand on the path between the water and the wall, and read one of the carved stones once more. The words belong to people long gone, set into a building made for the living to argue and decide. Behind you the hill is green and quiet. You finish reading, look up at the strange roofline, and move on.

Key facts

Designed by the Catalan architect Enric Miralles.
Miralles died in 2000, before the building opened.
The Parliament building opened to use in 2004.
It won the Stirling Prize for architecture in 2005.
It stands directly opposite the Palace of Holyroodhouse.

What to look for

Contemplation windows jutting out from the office walls.
Carved quotation stones set in the low street wall.
Branching, boat-like shapes across the rooflines.
Grass roofs that blend into the hillside behind.
