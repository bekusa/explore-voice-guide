The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves across an open square in the centre of Mariupol, a port city on the northern shore of the Sea of Azov. Salt air drifts in from the water, and the light here is wide and pale. In front of you is the building known for decades as the Donetsk Regional Academic Drama Theatre. Most people simply call it the Mariupol Drama Theatre. Few buildings in modern Europe carry as much weight as this one.

Before we come to the hardest part of this story, let us begin with what the theatre was meant to be. It was a civic building, made for the whole city. Families came here for holiday performances. Students saw their first serious plays here. On ordinary evenings, people crossed the square in their best coats, talked under the columns, and went inside to sit together in the dark. For generations, that was the life of this place.

The building you see dates from the middle of the twentieth century. It opened around 1960, in the late years of a grand Soviet architectural style. That style borrowed heavily from classical Greece and Rome. Look for the strong symmetry, the heavy columns, and the pale facade. Architects of that era wanted public buildings to look permanent and serious, almost like temples. A theatre was a temple to culture, in their view, and it was placed at the heart of the city plan.

The theatre company itself is older than the building. Its roots go back long before this structure existed. Like many regional companies across the former Soviet Union, it staged classic Russian and Ukrainian drama, European plays, and works for children. Over time, Ukrainian-language productions became a stronger part of its identity. By the early twenty-first century, the theatre was a familiar part of everyday life for many people in Mariupol, and a point of local pride.

Now, the square itself. Notice how the theatre stands slightly apart, with open space around it. This was deliberate. The square gave crowds room to gather before and after performances. It also gave the building a stage of its own, so it could be seen from a distance. Trees, paths, and benches once framed the approach. In good weather, this was simply a pleasant place to sit, to meet a friend, or to watch children run across the paving stones.

In February 2022, Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Mariupol was quickly surrounded. Within days, the city lost electricity, heating, water, and most communication. The siege lasted for weeks. Many residents lost their homes, and the city centre came under constant fire. People needed somewhere solid to shelter. The theatre, with its thick walls and large basement, became one of the main refuges in the city.

Hundreds of civilians moved inside. Many reports suggest the number was well over a thousand at some points. Families slept in corridors, in the basement, and on the stage. People cooked over open fires outside, because there was no gas. The building also served as a point where people gathered in hope of evacuation corridors out of the city. A place built for audiences had become a home for those who had nowhere else to go.

Here is a detail that many visitors know from photographs. People sheltering inside painted a single word in huge white letters on the ground, both in front of the building and behind it. The word was the Russian for children. It was written large enough to be read from the air. The message was a plea to anyone flying overhead: there are children here. Satellite images taken in those days showed the word clearly.

On 16 March 2022, the theatre was struck by a powerful Russian airstrike. A large part of the building collapsed, including the central section above the stage. The exact number of people killed remains uncertain, and estimates differ widely. An investigation by the Associated Press suggested the toll could be in the hundreds. Other investigators gave lower figures and noted the difficulty of counting under siege. What is clear is that many civilians died here, and that the building was a known shelter.

After weeks of fighting, Mariupol came under Russian occupation in spring 2022. Later that year, the occupation authorities began clearing the ruined theatre. Much of the damaged structure was taken down, and reconstruction work followed. Many Ukrainians, and many international observers, criticised this. They argued that clearing the site removed evidence and erased the memory of the dead before any full investigation could take place. The building you see today exists inside that disagreement.

So there is something surprising to keep in mind as you look at it. The facade in front of you may look complete, even fresh. But the shape is a reconstruction of what stood before, not the same walls that sheltered those families. And the theatre company did not disappear with the building. Members of the troupe left the city and continued to perform elsewhere in Ukraine. In a real sense, the theatre of Mariupol lives on in two places at once.

If you are standing here today, you are on ground that means very different things to different people. For some, this is simply a rebuilt landmark in a city centre. For many Ukrainians and for families of the victims, it is a site of mourning. A respectful visit is quiet. Many people choose to pause, to look at the space in front of the entrance where the word was painted, and to think about the people who sheltered here.

Before you move on, look once more across the square. Notice the distance between the columns and the open ground in front of them. Imagine those white letters on the paving, large enough to be read from the sky. Feel the wind coming in from the sea. As you walk away, you may notice how ordinary the surrounding streets appear. That ordinariness is part of the story too... and it is worth carrying with you.
