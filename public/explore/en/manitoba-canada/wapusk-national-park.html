The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the wind is often the loudest thing for many kilometres. It moves across low willow, lichen, and pale grass. It pushes against the windows of your vehicle and carries the cold, salty smell of Hudson Bay. Under all that open sky, the ground looks flat and empty. But this land is not empty. It is one of the most important places on Earth for a single animal... the polar bear.

Welcome to Wapusk National Park, on the shores of Hudson Bay in northeastern Manitoba. The name comes from the Cree language. Wapusk means white bear. It is a simple name, and an honest one. Parks Canada created the park in 1996 to protect this coastline and the land behind it. It covers more than eleven thousand square kilometres, and no public road crosses any of it.

That last detail surprises many visitors. You cannot simply drive into Wapusk. There are no road links to the park at all, and most people reach the nearby town of Churchill by train or by plane. Access to the park itself is limited. Most visitors come with licensed operators, by special vehicle, by aircraft, or on guided trips. That distance is part of what keeps the park wild.

Look at the land around you. This is part of the Hudson Bay Lowlands, one of the largest wetland regions in the world. The ground is a patchwork of bog, fen, shallow ponds, and raised ridges. Much of it sits on permafrost — soil that stays frozen all year below the surface. In summer, only the top layer thaws. Water cannot drain down, so it gathers in thousands of small, still pools.

This park also sits on a boundary. To the south, the boreal forest begins, with its dark spruce and tamarack. To the north and along the coast, open tundra takes over. Wapusk lies in the transition between the two. As you travel, watch the trees. They grow shorter, thinner, and farther apart. Some lean away from the wind, with branches mostly on one side. That shape shows the force of the winter storms.

Now, to the bears. Polar bears hunt seals on the sea ice. But Hudson Bay freezes and melts every year. When the ice breaks up in summer, the bears must come ashore. Many of them land along this coast. Here they wait, often resting and using as little energy as possible. In autumn, as the bay begins to freeze again, they gather near the shore, ready to return to the ice.

Here is something most visitors never see. Wapusk protects one of the largest polar bear maternity denning areas known anywhere. Pregnant females move inland and dig dens, often in banks of peat and soil. In the middle of winter, they give birth inside these dens. The cubs are tiny at birth. Mother and cubs usually stay hidden until late winter or early spring, when they come out into the snow and light.

That makes this land a nursery. Some of the bears that walk the coast of Hudson Bay began their lives somewhere in these quiet ridges. The dens themselves are hard to spot. They are small openings in the ground, often covered with snow. Scientists have studied this area for decades, because the health of these bears tells us a great deal about the health of the sea ice.

The ice is the key to the whole story. Researchers have documented that the ice season on western Hudson Bay has changed over recent decades. When the ice melts earlier and forms later, the bears spend longer on land without their main food. So when you watch a bear resting on the shore, you are watching an animal that is waiting. Its life depends on the timing of the cold.

Bears are not the only residents. Caribou move across this landscape, and the area is home to a herd known as the Cape Churchill caribou. Arctic foxes, with thick white winter coats, trot across the snow. Red foxes live here too. In the warmer months, huge numbers of birds arrive to nest in the wetlands. Snow geese, shorebirds, and ducks fill the ponds with sound for a short, busy season.

People have a long history on this land as well. Cree and Dene peoples, and Inuit along the coast, have travelled, hunted, and lived across this region for a very long time. The Cree name of the park is one reminder of that connection. Later, fur traders of the Hudson's Bay Company used the waters of the bay and the nearby rivers. The land holds traces of both stories, though many are not easy to see.

If you are here during the bear season, notice the quiet behaviour around you. Guides keep vehicles and people at a careful distance. Bears are powerful and fast, and they are not tame. Inside a vehicle or with a trained guide, you can watch them safely. Keep your voice low and your movements small. Often the best moments come after a long wait, when a bear lifts its head and turns toward the wind.

Before you leave, take one more look. See how the light lies flat across the land. Notice the colours — grey water, brown peat, pale lichen, and the white line where sky meets snow or ice. Listen again to the wind. Somewhere out there, perhaps under your feet in a frozen bank, a bear may be sleeping. As you turn back toward Churchill, remember that this quiet land is a nursery, and a waiting room, at the edge of the sea.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book access through a licensed operator before you travel.
Reach Churchill by train or plane, not by road.
Dress in windproof layers even on clear days.
Bring binoculars to watch wildlife from a safe distance.
Keep voices low and follow your guide's instructions.
