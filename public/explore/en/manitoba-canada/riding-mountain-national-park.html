The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the aspen leaves with a dry, papery rustle, quite unlike the sound of pine or spruce. Somewhere nearby, a raven calls. Underfoot, the ground is soft with fallen needles and dark soil. The air here is cooler than on the open farmland you crossed to get here. It smells of resin, damp moss, and lake water. Welcome to Riding Mountain National Park, a raised island of forest in the middle of the Manitoba prairie.

That word, island, is not just a figure of speech. Look at a satellite image of this region and the pattern is clear. Wheat fields and canola stretch out in neat squares on every side. Then, abruptly, the squares stop, and a large dark green shape takes their place. That shape is the park. Farms press right up against its edges, so the boundary often runs as a sharp line between cultivated land and wild forest. Few protected areas in Canada show their borders so plainly.

So where does the mountain come in? Manitoba is famous for flatness, and nothing here looks like the Rockies. The answer is the Manitoba Escarpment, a long, uneven slope of land that runs across the province. On the eastern side of the park, this escarpment climbs steeply from the lowlands below. From the plain, it looks like a wall of forest rising out of the fields. Drive up the escarpment and you will feel your ears adjust and notice the temperature drop.

The name has a practical origin. According to the commonly told account, fur traders travelling west by water reached this high ground and changed their way of moving. They left their canoes and continued on horseback, so the uplands became the riding mountain. It is a modest, working name. It describes what people did here, not how the place looked. That fits a landscape shaped as much by travel and trade as by scenery.

Long before the fur traders, this was Anishinaabe land, the homeland of Ojibway peoples who hunted, gathered, and travelled through these hills. The creation of the park in the early twentieth century displaced people from places they had long used. The Keeseekoowenin Ojibway First Nation, whose community sits near the park, lost land and access when the boundaries were drawn. In recent decades, Parks Canada and First Nations have worked on a more cooperative relationship. That history is part of what you are standing on.

Now here is something many visitors miss. Riding Mountain sits at a meeting point of three very different landscapes. From the north and east comes the boreal forest, with dark spruce, fir, and tamarack. From the south and west comes open grassland, with rough fescue prairie. Between them lies aspen parkland, a mix of poplar groves and meadows. In a single afternoon, you can walk from one world into another. Watch how the trees change as you gain or lose height.

That mix of habitats supports a wide range of animals. Elk move through the meadows, especially at dawn and dusk. Moose feed in wetlands and along quiet lakeshores. Black bears live throughout the forest, and wolves hunt across the park, though they rarely let people see them. Beavers have reshaped many streams with their dams. Each animal uses a different edge of these overlapping landscapes, which is why the park feels so full of life.

One animal has a special place here: the plains bison. Bison once moved in enormous herds across the surrounding prairie before they were hunted almost to extinction in the nineteenth century. Today, a small herd lives inside a large fenced range near Lake Audy, on the western side of the park. From the viewing area, you can watch these heavy, shaggy animals graze much as their ancestors did. Bring binoculars. The herd often stays some distance from the road.

A second surprise involves a famous name. In 1931, the writer and conservationist known as Grey Owl came here to work for the national parks service. He lived in a small log cabin beside a quiet lake, with beavers as his companions. His time at Riding Mountain was short, and he soon moved on to Saskatchewan. Later, people learned that Grey Owl was an Englishman who had adopted an Indigenous identity. A hiking trail still leads to his cabin today.

Many visitors start or end their day at Wasagaming, the small townsite on the shore of Clear Lake. The lake earns its name. On calm days, you can look down from the dock and see stones on the bottom well below the surface. In summer, the town fills with families, ice cream, and canoes. Notice the older buildings, many of them made from heavy logs and fieldstone. That rustic style was a deliberate choice, designed to suit the forest around it.

Some of that work dates from the Great Depression of the 1930s. During those hard years, government relief programs put unemployed men to work here, building roads, shelters, and structures by hand. The log Visitor Centre at Wasagaming is one result of their labour, and it is now recognised as a national historic site. Run your hand along the timber and you can almost feel the axe work. These buildings record a difficult decade through very solid craftsmanship.

The park also belongs to something larger. In 1986, the park and the farmland and communities around it were named a UNESCO biosphere reserve. A biosphere reserve is an area where people try to balance nature protection with farming, towns, and daily life. That idea makes particular sense here, where the forest and the fields sit so close together. What happens on a farm just outside the boundary can affect the elk, the wolves, and the water inside it.

If you have time, follow one of the many hiking trails or drive the highway that crosses the park from north to south. Along the way, stop at a viewpoint on the escarpment. On a clear day, the farmland spreads out below like a vast patterned blanket. In autumn, the aspens turn bright gold against the dark green of the spruce. In winter, the park grows very quiet, and snowshoe tracks replace hiking boots on the trails.

Before you move on, pause wherever you are standing. Notice the light as it falls through the leaves and lands on the forest floor. Listen again for the aspens, the birds, perhaps the slap of water against a shore. This high green island has carried traders, hunters, relief workers, and families on summer holidays. Now it carries you. As you leave, watch for the moment the trees give way to open fields again.

Practical tips

Visit the bison range near Lake Audy at dawn or dusk.

Stop at an escarpment viewpoint on a clear day.
