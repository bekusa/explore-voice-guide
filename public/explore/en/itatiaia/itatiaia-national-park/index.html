The guide

The story

Listen first. Water is running somewhere below you, over stone, through ferns. The air is cool and damp, and it smells of wet leaves and moss. Light reaches the trail in pale green patches, filtered through layer after layer of forest. Welcome to Itatiaia National Park, high in the mountains where three Brazilian states meet. This was the first national park Brazil ever created. People still come here for the same reasons the early naturalists did: clean water, cold air and rock.

The park sits in the Serra da Mantiqueira, a long mountain range in the southeast of the country. The park straddles the border between Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais, and it lies close to São Paulo state. On a map it looks small beside the Amazon reserves. On foot it feels enormous. In a short distance the ground climbs from warm, humid forest to open, windswept highlands. That climb is the heart of this place. Every few hundred metres of height brings different plants, different birds and a different kind of weather.

The name Itatiaia comes from Tupi, one of the Indigenous language families of Brazil. It is usually translated as something like stone full of points, or jagged rock. When you see the summits, that meaning makes sense. The high peaks here are not smooth domes. They are broken, ridged and sharp, with deep grooves cut into their sides. The name describes the landscape well. It was given long before roads, hotels or park gates existed.

Brazil declared this area a national park in 1937. Before that, scientists and botanists had already noticed how unusual the mountain was. They found plants at the top that grow nowhere near the lowland coast. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Brazil is famous for its tropical heat, yet the high plateau of Itatiaia regularly sees frost in winter. On some cold mornings, the grass up there turns white. So plants and animals on the summits must survive conditions that feel almost alpine.

The park has two quite different faces, and it helps to know which one you are standing in. The lower part is reached from the town of Itatiaia, near the main highway between Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo. This side is green, shaded and full of water. Paved roads and short trails lead to waterfalls, pools and viewpoints. The upper part, often called the plateau, is reached by a separate mountain road from the other side of the range. It is open, rocky and much colder.

If you are in the lower section, you are walking through Atlantic Forest. This is the forest that once covered much of Brazil's coast. Most of it has been cleared over the centuries for farms, cities and plantations. What survives in places like this is precious. Look at the trunks around you. Many carry bromeliads, plants that hold small pools of rainwater in their leaves. Orchids, mosses and lichens grow on almost every branch. A single tree here can support a small world of other plants.

The forest is also full of sound, if you stop moving long enough to hear it. Hummingbirds pass close to your head with a sharp buzz. Larger birds call from the canopy, and you may hear monkeys moving through the branches before you see them. Insects add a steady hum in the warmer months. Many visitors walk quickly from one waterfall to the next. Try standing still for two full minutes at some point. The forest becomes much louder than you expect.

Water shapes this whole side of the park. Streams start high on the mountain and fall in steps through the forest, cutting pools into the rock. Some waterfalls drop in thin white ribbons. Others slide down wide, smooth slabs of granite-like stone. The water is clear and very cold, even in summer. Locals and visitors swim in some of the pools on hot days. If you put your hand in, you will feel how quickly the mountain chills everything that flows from it.

Now think about the rock itself, because here is the second surprise. The Itatiaia massif is made of an unusual type of rock. It formed when molten material pushed up from deep underground and cooled slowly beneath the surface, millions of years ago. Over a very long time, rain and frost wore away the softer layers around it. What remained are the hard, strange summits you see today. Geologists come here to study this rock, because formations like it are rare in Brazil.

The highest point is the peak called Agulhas Negras, which means Black Needles. It rises to around 2,790 metres and is one of the tallest mountains in Brazil. Its dark rock is scored with deep vertical channels, cut by water over countless years. Nearby stands another famous formation called Prateleiras, or Shelves. Its giant blocks of stone look as if someone stacked them by hand. Reaching these summits is a serious hike, and some routes require scrambling and a qualified guide.

The plateau feels like a different country from the forest below. Trees give way to grasses, low shrubs and wildflowers that can handle wind and cold. Fog can roll in very quickly and hide the trail within minutes. On clear days the views reach across valleys toward distant ranges. The light is sharper up there, and the sky often looks a deeper blue. Many hikers start very early, before clouds gather around the peaks in the afternoon.

People have shaped this park as well. Hotels, mountain lodges and small houses sit along the edges of the lower section. Some of them have welcomed walkers and families for generations. Rangers, guides and researchers work here throughout the year. Weekend visitors from Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo come for fresh air and cool nights. The park has to balance all of this human use against protecting the forest, the springs and the rare plants on the summits.

Before you move on, look around you once more. Notice the colour of the light through the leaves, the shine of wet stone and the sound of the nearest stream. Somewhere above you, the jagged peaks are standing in cloud or sun. This mountain has been here far longer than any park or name. As you walk on, keep your steps on the trail and your eyes open. The smallest details here, a flower or a bird, are often the most rare.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring warm layers; the high plateau can see winter frost.
Start upper plateau hikes early, before afternoon fog builds.
Hire a qualified guide for the Agulhas Negras summit.
Wear grippy shoes; wet rock near waterfalls is slippery.
Check which park section your route uses before driving.
