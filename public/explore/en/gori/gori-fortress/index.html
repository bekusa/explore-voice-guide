The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. The path up this hill is rough and uneven, a mix of worn stone, packed earth and loose gravel that shifts as you climb. Wind often moves across the summit, even on still days in the town below. As you rise, the noise of traffic fades. What replaces it is the sound of birds, the crunch of your own steps, and the dry rustle of grass growing between old stones.

You are standing at Gori Fortress, known in Georgian as Goris Tsikhe. The fortress sits on a rocky hill in the very centre of Gori, the main town of Shida Kartli, which means Inner Kartli. This is the heart of eastern Georgia. The hill rises sharply from flat land, and that simple fact explains almost everything about why people built here. Whoever held this rock could see anyone approaching from far away.

Look at the shape of the walls. They do not form a neat square or a perfect circle. Instead, they follow the natural crest of the hill, bending and stretching along the rock. The builders worked with the land, not against it. This gives the fortress a long, irregular outline. Walk the perimeter slowly, and you will notice how each section of wall rises directly from the stone beneath it, as if the hill itself grew upward.

The history of this site is long, and parts of it are uncertain. A fortified place on this hill appears in medieval written sources. However, much of what you see today belongs to later centuries, especially the period of repeated rebuilding in the early modern era. Fortresses like this one were rarely built once and left alone. They were damaged, repaired, extended and repaired again. So the walls in front of you are layered, the work of many generations.

Think about the location on a map. Gori lies in a broad valley where important roads crossed Kartli, linking east and west across Georgia. Two rivers meet near the town — the Mtkvari, which flows on to Tbilisi, and the Great Liakhvi, which comes down from the mountains to the north. Control of this crossing mattered. Over the centuries, Georgian kings, Persian forces and Ottoman armies all contested this region, and the fortress changed hands more than once.

Now look closely at the materials. The walls are made largely of rough stone, set in thick mortar. In some places you can see sections of brick, or patches where the masonry changes style. These differences show different periods of repair. Some stones are carefully squared. Others are rough and irregular, pushed into place wherever they fit. Run your eyes along a single stretch of wall, and you may count several building campaigns in just a few metres.

Here is something many visitors miss. Part of the damage you see was not caused by war at all. In 1920, a strong earthquake struck the Gori area and badly damaged the fortress. Earthquakes are a real force in the Caucasus, a young and active mountain region. So when you see a cracked tower or a collapsed section of wall, remember that the ground itself has been one of this fortress's enemies.

Turn now toward the view. From the summit, the whole town spreads out below you. You can see red and grey rooftops, Soviet-era apartment blocks, church domes and the green lines of trees along the rivers. On a clear day, look north. The snow-covered peaks of the Greater Caucasus can appear on the horizon, sharp and white above the haze. Many visitors come up for the history and stay for this wide, open view.

As you look over the town, remember that Gori is known around the world for one reason above all others. Joseph Stalin was born here, in the late nineteenth century, in a small house that still stands in the town. A large museum dedicated to his life is located nearby. For many Georgians, this connection is complicated and uncomfortable. The fortress, however, is much older than that story. It belongs to a past that stretches far beyond the twentieth century.

Gori has also seen more recent conflict. In August 2008, during the war between Georgia and Russia, the town was bombed and briefly occupied. The border of the breakaway region of South Ossetia lies not far to the north. From this hill, you are looking toward land that is very close, yet difficult to reach. For local people, these memories are still fresh, and the fortress carries a new layer of meaning as a symbol of endurance.

At the foot of the hill, look for a memorial to Georgian warriors. It is a group of bronze figures, dressed in armour and arranged together in a quiet circle. The figures have a heavy, solemn presence. Their dark metal contrasts with the pale stone of the fortress above. The memorial honours those who fought for Georgia across many centuries. Visitors often pause there, either before the climb or after coming down, and it is worth a few quiet minutes.

If you have more time in the area, the fortress makes a good starting point for exploring Shida Kartli. Not far to the east lies Uplistsikhe, an ancient rock-cut town carved directly into a cliff above the Mtkvari River. Its caves, halls and passages are among the oldest urban sites in Georgia. Seeing both places on the same day gives you a strong sense of how long people have lived, built and defended themselves in this valley.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the top of the wall. Listen to the wind and the distant hum of the town. Notice the light on the old stones, and the way it shows every crack and patch of repair. As you walk back down, look at the path one more time. Countless soldiers, traders and townspeople have climbed this same rock. Now you have joined them, and the fortress continues its long watch over Gori.
