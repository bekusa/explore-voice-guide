The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. Under your shoes, the rock is soft and pale, worn smooth by many centuries of feet. Here and there you will notice shallow grooves cut into the stone. The wind comes up off the river below and carries dust across the hillside. There are no walls of brick or timber around you. Almost everything you see was carved straight out of the cliff... rooms, steps, halls, and streets, all shaped from one living mass of rock.

Welcome to Uplistsikhe, an ancient rock-cut town in the heart of Kartli, the historic central region of Georgia. You are a short drive east of the town of Gori. Below you runs the Mtkvari River, which many maps also call the Kura. The name Uplistsikhe is usually translated as the Lord's Fortress. It is one of the oldest urban settlements in the country. People lived on this ridge long before Georgia became a Christian kingdom.

Archaeologists date the earliest traces of settlement here to the first millennium before Christ, in the early Iron Age. Over the following centuries, the site grew from a hilltop community into a real town. Its location made sense. The river gave water and a natural line of defence. The soft sandstone was easy to cut with simple tools. And the valley below formed a corridor for travellers moving east and west across the Caucasus.

Take a moment to look at the rock itself. It is a fairly soft sandstone, warm in colour, ranging from honey to grey. This softness is the whole secret of the place. Builders could carve it with patience rather than great force. The same softness has a cost, though. Wind, rain, frost, and earthquakes have eroded much of the town. Many rooms that once had sharp edges now look rounded, almost melted, like shapes in sand.

In the centuries before Christianity, Uplistsikhe was an important religious centre. Scholars believe it held pagan temples, and that worship here included rituals connected to the sun and to the forces of nature. Some of the larger chambers you will pass were probably sacred spaces rather than homes. Small niches and platforms in the walls may have held offerings. We do not know every detail of these ceremonies, and it is honest to say that much remains uncertain.

As you walk, follow the main street. It climbs through the town, cut directly into the slope. Look down at the surface, and you may see channels carved along the edges. These helped carry rainwater away and protected the rooms from flooding. Picture this path crowded with traders, animals, and priests. Uplistsikhe sat near routes that later formed part of the wider network of trade between Asia and Europe, often called the Silk Road.

One of the most striking rooms is a large hall with a carved ceiling. Its ceiling is cut into square, sunken panels, a style called coffering, which imitates the wooden roofs of buildings from the same era. Locals often call this space Queen Tamar's Hall, after Georgia's most celebrated medieval ruler. This name is a popular tradition, not a proven fact. The hall itself is much older than her reign.

Here is something many visitors miss. Several rooms in the town contain round holes cut into the floor. These held qvevri, the large clay vessels that Georgians have used for making and storing wine for thousands of years. In Georgian, a wine cellar is called a marani. So even in this ancient stone town, wine was part of daily life. That tradition continues today in villages all around you.

Now, a second surprise. A tunnel runs from the town down toward the river. It was cut through the rock so that people could reach water safely, even during a siege. Enemies outside the walls could not easily see who was coming and going. Today a section of this passage is open, and walking through it gives you a sense of how carefully the residents planned for danger.

The arrival of Christianity changed Uplistsikhe. Georgia adopted Christianity as its state religion in the fourth century, and the old pagan sanctuaries lost their purpose. Yet the town did not disappear. In the early medieval period, when Arab forces held Tbilisi, Uplistsikhe gained new importance. It became a stronghold for Georgian Christian rulers in Kartli. For a time, this rocky ridge was a centre of political life again.

Look up toward the highest point of the site. There you will see a small church built of stone and brick, standing above the carved rooms. It is known as the Uplistsulis Eklesia, which means the Church of the Lord's Fortress. It dates from roughly the ninth or tenth century. It is a basilica, a church with a long central hall, and it sits on top of older pagan ground. Two faiths, layered one above the other.

The town's long life came to an end in the thirteenth century. Mongol invasions swept across Georgia, and Uplistsikhe was badly damaged. After that, the site slowly emptied. Shepherds and monks may have used some rooms, but it never again became a living town. In the modern era, archaeologists have excavated and studied the ruins. Uplistsikhe is now on Georgia's tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage status.

Before you leave, find a quiet edge and look out over the valley. Watch the river bend through the green fields. Listen to the wind moving across the rock, and the voices of other visitors echoing from the carved rooms. Touch the stone if you like, gently. As you walk back down, notice the small details... a step worn into a hollow, a niche in a wall, a groove for water. Each one was cut by hand, a very long time ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for smooth, sloping rock.
Bring a hat and water; the ridge has little shade.
Visit early morning to avoid tour groups from Tbilisi.
Combine your trip with a visit to nearby Gori.
Watch your step near unfenced edges and steep drops.
