The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go in. Bolzano is a quiet hum of bicycles and Italian and German voices, and somewhere a church bell sounds across the old town. Inside the museum the noise drops away. The air feels cooler, the light is low, and people talk in near-whispers. Many of them are here for one person. He has lain in the mountains, in a laboratory, and now in this building for more than five thousand years.

He is known as Ötzi, the Iceman. The name comes from the Ötztal, a valley in the Alps near where he was found. A journalist created the nickname soon after the discovery, and it stuck. Scientists date his life to around 3300 BCE. That makes him older than the pyramids of Giza and older than Stonehenge in its familiar stone form. He belongs to the Copper Age, the period when people in Europe first learned to work metal.

The story of his discovery begins in September 1991. Two German hikers, Helmut and Erika Simon, were crossing a high pass in the Ötztal Alps, more than 3,200 metres above sea level. They saw a brown shape in a melting gully of ice. At first they believed it was a modern climber who had died in an accident. They took a photograph and reported it. Nobody guessed the true age of the body for several days.

That early mistake had consequences. The first recovery team treated the site like a modern accident scene. They used tools, including a pneumatic drill, to free the body from the ice, and the drill damaged his hip. Some of his equipment was scattered or broken in the rush. Only when an archaeologist examined the axe did the truth become clear. The blade was copper, and the man was prehistoric. The world's oldest natural human mummy from Europe had just been pulled from a glacier.

There was another surprise. The site lies very close to the border between Austria and Italy. The body was first taken to Innsbruck in Austria. Careful surveys then showed that he had died on the Italian side, inside South Tyrol, by only a short distance. So Ötzi came to Bolzano. This museum opened in 1998, built around the task of keeping him safe and letting the public see him.

That task is harder than it sounds. The Iceman stays in a special cold cell, kept at about minus six degrees Celsius with very high humidity. These conditions copy the glacier that protected him for millennia. You see him through a small window. The opening is modest, almost private, and people often step back afterwards in silence. Take your time here. Notice the colour of his skin, and the position of his arm across his body.

For ten years, people assumed he had died of cold or exhaustion. Then, in 2001, an X-ray showed something everyone had missed. A flint arrowhead sits in his left shoulder. The arrow struck from behind and likely damaged a major artery. Later studies also found signs of a blow to the head. Ötzi was not simply lost in a storm. He was almost certainly killed, and investigators still discuss this as one of the oldest murder cases in the world.

The objects he carried may be even more remarkable than the body. Look for his copper axe, with a handle made of yew wood. Copper was precious, so this tool tells us he had some status. There is also a longbow, still unfinished, and a quiver with arrows. Some were ready to use and some were not. He carried a small flint dagger, a backpack frame, and tinder fungus for starting fires. It is a complete travel kit from the Copper Age.

His clothes survived too, and they change how we picture prehistoric people. He wore leggings and a coat made from the hides of several animals, stitched together in strips. His cap was made of bear fur. His shoes had soles of bearskin and were stuffed with dry grass to keep his feet warm. A mat of woven grass may have served as a cloak. These were not crude rags. They were practical garments, made by people who understood mountain weather very well.

Science has learned a lot from his body. His stomach held the remains of his last meal, including ibex meat and an early type of wheat called einkorn. He was probably in his mid-forties, short by modern standards, with worn joints. His skin carries dozens of small tattoos, made of lines and crosses. Many of them sit over joints that likely caused him pain. Some researchers think the tattoos were a kind of early medical treatment rather than decoration.

On one floor you will meet him face to face, in a sense. Two Dutch artists, the brothers Alfons and Adrie Kennis, built a life-size reconstruction of the Iceman. They based it on scans and scientific data. He looks weathered and alert, with deep lines around his eyes. Many visitors find this figure more moving than the mummy itself. It turns a scientific specimen back into a man who walked these valleys.

The museum is about more than one person. Other rooms cover the long prehistory of South Tyrol, from early hunters to later farming communities. There are tools, pottery, and reconstructions of daily life. And there is one story that belongs to folklore, not science. Some people speak of an Iceman's curse, linking him to the deaths of people involved in the discovery. It is a popular tale, and nothing more. Scientists point out that coincidence explains it easily.

When you leave, the streets of Bolzano will feel bright and loud again. Look up toward the mountains that rise around the city. Somewhere beyond them is the high pass where a man walked alone more than five thousand years ago, with an axe on his belt and an arrow behind him. The ice kept him for us. As you walk away, think of the slow work of that ice, and of how close we came to never knowing his name.
