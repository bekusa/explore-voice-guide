The guide

The story

Listen first. A low wind moves across open water and stirs the dry grass. Somewhere out on the lagoon, hundreds of wading birds murmur and splash. The air smells of salt and warm mud. Underfoot, or under the wheels of your jeep, the ground is pale and sandy. Thorny scrub presses close on either side. This is Bundala, on the southern coast of Sri Lanka. It is a landscape of shallow water and wide skies, and it belongs mostly to the birds.

Bundala sits in Sri Lanka's dry zone, the part of the island that receives far less rain than the green hills of the interior. That changes everything about the place. Instead of thick jungle, you see low, spiky bushes, cactus-like plants and scattered trees bent by the coastal wind. Between them lie a chain of shallow lagoons. They fill and shrink with the seasons. Sometimes the water sits only ankle-deep across wide stretches, and that shallow water feeds an enormous number of birds.

Here is the first fact that surprises many visitors. Bundala was the first wetland in Sri Lanka to be listed under the Ramsar Convention. That is an international treaty, signed by many countries, that protects wetlands of global importance. Sri Lanka added Bundala to that list in 1991. The site had already been protected as a sanctuary for birds for decades. Later it was upgraded to a national park. Today it is also part of a UNESCO biosphere reserve, a status that links nature protection with the lives of people nearby.

The lagoons each have their own names, and your driver may point them out. Bundala Lagoon gives the park its name. Others include Embilikala, Malala and Koholankala. They are not deep, dramatic lakes. They are flat, glassy sheets of water, often edged with mud flats and salt crust. In the dry months, the edges pull back and leave cracked ground. In the wet months, they spread out again. The birds follow these changes closely.

And the birds are the reason most people come. Bundala is one of the most important places in southern Sri Lanka for waterbirds. Watch for painted storks, with their pale bodies and pinkish wing feathers. Look for herons standing very still at the water's edge, and for spoonbills sweeping their flat bills from side to side. Small sandpipers and plovers run along the mud in quick bursts. Pelicans sometimes drift across the open water in slow, heavy groups.

Then there are the visitors from far away. Many birds that breed in northern Asia and beyond travel south to escape the cold. Some of them spend the northern winter right here, on these lagoons. This makes the cooler months, roughly from late in the year into the early spring, the busiest season for birdwatchers. On a good day, the mud flats fill with flocks of small waders. Some of them have flown thousands of kilometres to reach this coast.

The most famous of these winter guests is the greater flamingo. Not every year is the same. Some seasons bring large flocks, others very few, and flamingos are never guaranteed. But when they are present, the sight is hard to forget. A long line of pink birds stands in the shallow water, heads down, feeding. They filter tiny creatures from the mud. Through binoculars, you can see their bent beaks working in the water.

Birds are not the only story. Bundala is also home to crocodiles. Both the mugger crocodile, a broad-snouted freshwater species, and the larger saltwater crocodile live in this area. On warm afternoons they often lie on the banks with their mouths open. This helps them release heat. You may also see monitor lizards walking slowly through the scrub, flicking their forked tongues. Grey langur monkeys and smaller macaques sometimes watch from the trees.

Here is a second surprise. Elephants live in Bundala too, although in smaller numbers than in some other parks. Because the scrub is low and open, a single elephant can appear suddenly between the bushes, very close to the track. Sightings are not certain, and that unpredictability is part of the experience. Keep your voice low if one appears. Your driver knows the right distance to keep. These are wild animals, and the park belongs to them first.

The park also meets the sea. Its southern edge runs along a long, empty beach on the Indian Ocean. This coast is an important nesting place for sea turtles. Several species come ashore here, mostly at night, to dig nests in the sand and lay their eggs. Most visitors never see this, because the park is closed after dark and the beach is protected. But it is worth knowing that this quiet shoreline plays a role in the survival of these ancient animals.

People have shaped this land too. Near the park, you may notice wide, flat salt pans, called salterns. Seawater is let into these shallow pools and left to evaporate under the strong sun. White salt remains behind. Salt making has a long history on this part of the coast. The salterns affect the water in nearby lagoons, and they also attract birds that feed in the salty shallows. Here, industry and wildlife sit side by side.

The surrounding area tells its own story. Villages lie close to the park boundaries. Farmers grow rice and other crops, and fishermen work the coast and the lagoons. The towns of Hambantota and Tissamaharama are not far away. Many visitors combine Bundala with the larger and better known Yala National Park to the east. Bundala feels different, though. It is quieter, flatter and more open, and it rewards patience more than speed.

A safari here moves slowly. Jeeps follow sandy tracks between the lagoons and stop often. The best moments usually come when the engine is off. Then you hear the birds again, the wind in the scrub, perhaps the distant sound of the sea. The light matters a great deal. Early morning and late afternoon are softer and cooler. The midday sun turns the water bright and white, and many animals rest in the shade.

So take a moment now. Look out across the water in front of you. Notice how flat it is, how the sky seems to fill most of what you see. Watch one bird for a full minute, the way it moves and where it steps. As you leave, listen for the calls fading behind you. This shallow, salty, sun-baked coast feeds a whole web of life. It asks only that we pass through it gently.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the cooler months for migratory birds.
Go early morning or late afternoon for softer light.
Bring binoculars to watch waders on distant mud flats.
Carry a hat and water against the dry-zone sun.
Ask your driver to switch off the engine at stops.
