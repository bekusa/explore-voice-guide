The guide

The story

Listen to the silence first. Out here, the wind moves over warm stone, and a raven calls somewhere above the ridges. The ground under your shoes is coarse red sand, loose and dry. Around you, the rock rises in shapes that look half-melted: swirls, waves, rounded humps the colour of rust and brick and salmon. On a clear morning, the light hits these walls and they seem to glow from inside. That glow gave this place its name.

You are standing in Valley of Fire State Park, in the Mojave Desert, roughly an hour's drive northeast of Las Vegas. It is Nevada's oldest state park, set aside in 1935. In those years the country was deep in the Great Depression, and Nevada was a thinly populated state. Even so, people understood that this landscape was worth protecting. Today the park covers a large stretch of desert, with Lake Mead not far to the east.

So why is the rock red? The answer is time and sand. Most of the formations here are Aztec Sandstone. This stone began as enormous sand dunes during the Jurassic period, the age of the dinosaurs, more than a hundred and fifty million years ago. Wind piled the dunes up, layer upon layer. Over ages, they were buried, pressed, and cemented into rock. Iron in the sand oxidised — in simple words, it rusted — and that rust gives the stone its warm colour.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look closely at the faces of the rock and you will often see fine diagonal lines crossing each other. These are not cracks. They are the slopes of those ancient dunes, frozen in stone. Geologists call this cross-bedding. When you trace one of those lines with your eyes, you are following the shape of a desert that blew across this region long before there were any humans to see it.

Wind and water did not stop working when the dunes became stone. They kept carving. Over millions of years, they hollowed out small caves, pockets, and arches. Some formations have earned nicknames. Elephant Rock, near the eastern entrance, really does look like an elephant dipping its trunk to the ground. The Beehives are rounded, layered domes that resemble old woven hives. The Seven Sisters stand as a row of separate red towers along the main road.

People have lived with these rocks for thousands of years. Archaeologists link early use of the valley to ancient desert cultures, including Basketmaker people and later Ancestral Puebloans, who farmed along the nearby river valleys. The Southern Paiute, whose homeland includes this region, also knew the valley well. The area was likely used for hunting, gathering, and ceremony rather than year-round living, because reliable water is scarce in this heat.

Those earlier visitors left messages. On many dark rock surfaces you can find petroglyphs, images pecked or scratched into the stone. The dark coating they cut through is called desert varnish, a thin film of minerals that builds up very slowly. Scratch it away, and the lighter rock underneath shows through. The figures include bighorn sheep, human shapes, spirals, and patterns whose meaning is still debated. Please look, photograph, and admire — but never touch them.

One of the best places to see these images is Atlatl Rock. A metal staircase climbs up the side of a cliff to a panel of carvings. Among them is a figure that appears to show an atlatl. An atlatl is a spear-throwing tool, a kind of handle that lets a hunter throw a dart with much more force. That tool was used in the Americas long before the bow and arrow arrived, which hints at the great age of some of this art.

Another rewarding stop is Mouse's Tank. A short, sandy trail runs through a narrow canyon lined with petroglyphs to a natural basin in the rock. After rain, water can collect there and stay for weeks. According to a well-known local story, the place takes its name from a Southern Paiute man nicknamed Mouse, who is said to have hidden in this canyon in the late nineteenth century, surviving on the hidden water. Treat it as part history, part legend.

In the 1930s, workers from the Civilian Conservation Corps came to the valley. This was a federal programme that gave jobs to young men during the Depression, building roads, trails, and shelters in parks across the United States. Here they built simple stone cabins from local sandstone, meant as rest stops for travellers. You can still visit them. Their walls blend into the landscape so well that, from a distance, they almost disappear.

The colours and strange shapes have drawn filmmakers too. The park has served as a backdrop for westerns, commercials, and science fiction, standing in for distant planets. Fans of Star Trek may know that scenes from the film Star Trek Generations were shot among these rocks. It is easy to understand why. In the late afternoon, with long shadows and no buildings in sight, the valley can feel less like Nevada and more like another world.

The desert here is not empty. Look for desert bighorn sheep on the ridges, especially early or late in the day. Lizards dart across warm stone, and the protected desert tortoise lives in this region, though it is rarely seen. Plants survive with very little water: creosote bush, with its sharp, rain-like scent after a storm, and spiny cholla cactus. In spring, after a wet winter, small wildflowers can add yellow and purple to the red ground.

A word of honest caution. This is one of the hottest places in a hot state. In summer, the rock absorbs the sun and gives the heat back, and conditions on exposed trails can become dangerous quickly. Carry more water than you think you need, wear a hat, and plan walks for the cooler hours. Popular routes such as the Fire Wave and White Domes are short, but there is very little shade.

Before you move on, stand still for one more moment. Look at the nearest rock face and find the thin crossing lines of the old dunes. Then look for a darker patch of varnish, and imagine a hand, long ago, tapping a picture into it. Notice how the colour shifts as a cloud passes. As you walk back toward the road, listen again for the wind over the stone. It has been shaping this valley for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start hikes at sunrise to avoid the dangerous midday heat.
Carry far more water than you expect to drink.
Wear a wide-brimmed hat; trails offer almost no shade.
Return near sunset when the red rock glows most strongly.
Look for bighorn sheep on ridges early or late.
