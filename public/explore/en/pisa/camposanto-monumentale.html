The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out on the lawn, the crowds laugh and call to each other beside the leaning tower. In here, the sound drops away. Your footsteps tap on old marble slabs. Many of those slabs are tombstones, worn smooth by centuries of shoes. Pale light falls through tall arched windows onto a square of quiet grass. This is the Camposanto Monumentale, the monumental cemetery of Pisa. It is the long white wall on the north side of the cathedral square, the one most visitors walk straight past.

The name Camposanto simply means holy field. Work on this enclosed cemetery began in 1278, at a time when Pisa was a powerful sea republic. The building grew slowly over the next two centuries. Its plan is simple: a long rectangle of covered walkways around an open lawn. Think of a monastery cloister, but made for the dead. The outer wall is almost blank. All the beauty faces inward, as if the place keeps its treasures for those who step inside.

Now look at the grass in the centre. According to a popular legend, the soil here is not from Tuscany at all. Locals say that a Pisan archbishop returned from the Crusades with ships full of earth from Golgotha, the hill outside Jerusalem where Christ was crucified. The tale adds that this holy soil could reduce a body to bare bones within a single day. It is folklore, not documented fact. But it shows how sacred this ground felt to the people of medieval Pisa.

Turn to the arches. They are tall and rounded, and each one holds delicate Gothic tracery — thin stone ribs that divide the opening into slender windows with small rosettes above. Gothic is the pointed, airy style of late medieval Europe. Here it meets the calmer, rounder forms that Pisa loved. The result feels light. Walk slowly and notice how the shadows of the tracery move across the floor as the sun shifts.

Under your feet lies another surprise. Pisan families were buried beneath these floors for centuries. Many slabs still carry carved coats of arms, names, and dates. Some are so worn that only a faint outline of a shield or a helmet remains. You are walking on the city's family tree. Try to find a slab where the carving is still sharp... and then one where it has almost vanished.

Along the walls you will see rows of ancient Roman sarcophagi — stone coffins carved with battles, hunts, and myths. The medieval Pisans collected them and reused them for their own important dead. To them, Rome was a proud ancestor. These carvings also mattered to art. Historians often connect them with the sculptor Nicola Pisano, whose pulpit stands in the baptistery nearby. His figures have the same solid, classical weight.

Now raise your eyes to the walls themselves. For centuries they were covered with enormous frescoes, painted from the fourteenth century onward. A fresco is painted onto wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall. Visitors came from across Europe to see them. The cycle included scenes from the Old Testament by the Florentine painter Benozzo Gozzoli, and many other works by leading artists of the time.

The most famous fresco is the Triumph of Death. It is usually attributed to a painter named Buffalmacco, though scholars still debate the name. In one corner, a group of elegant riders out hunting meets three open coffins with corpses in different stages of decay. Nearby, young lovers sit in a garden, unaware of the figure of Death flying toward them. It is often said that this image inspired the composer Franz Liszt to write his dark piano work called Totentanz, or Dance of Death.

Then came the war. On 27 July 1944, during fighting between Allied and German forces, an artillery shell struck the Camposanto. A fire broke out. The lead roof melted, and the molten metal ran down the walls over the frescoes. Much of the painted surface was destroyed or badly scarred. In a single afternoon, one of the great painted interiors of Italy was almost lost.

What happened next is a long story of patience. Restorers detached the damaged frescoes from the walls to save them. Behind the paint, they found something unexpected — the sinopie. These are the reddish preparatory drawings that artists sketched on the rough plaster before painting. Normally no one sees them. Today many of these sketches hang in the Museum of the Sinopie, on the other side of the square. Restoration here has continued for decades, and some great frescoes have returned to their walls in recent years.

As you walk, look for a set of heavy iron chains. They are linked to the harbour of Porto Pisano. In the Middle Ages, chains like these could close the harbour to enemy ships. After Pisa lost a great sea battle to Genoa in 1284, the Genoese took the chains away as trophies. Pieces were returned to Pisa many centuries later. They hang here as a quiet symbol of a lost maritime power.

The cemetery also holds small chapels and later monuments from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. You will find marble busts, carved angels, and memorials to scholars and citizens. Some styles clash with the medieval walls, and that is part of the story too. This place was never frozen. Each generation added something, removed something, and repaired something.

Before you leave, stand at one corner and look down the full length of a walkway. Notice the rhythm of the arches, the pale stone, the patches of surviving colour high on the wall. Outside, the square is full of noise and photographs. In here, the air is still. As you step back into the light, look once more at that plain white outer wall — and remember what it keeps behind it.
