Toronto Islands

The city drops away the moment the ferry pulls out.

⏱ 5 min

The story

The city drops away the moment the ferry pulls out. Engine hum, gulls, and the slap of water on the hull. Behind you the towers shrink. Ahead, a low green line of trees rises from the lake. In about ten minutes you trade traffic for the sound of wind in the cottonwoods. The air feels cooler here, off the open water.

The Toronto Islands are a chain of small islands in Lake Ontario, just offshore from downtown. You reach them only by boat. Ferries run from the terminal at the foot of the city, and the crossing is short. Once you land, there are no cars. People move by foot, by bicycle, and by the slow drift of a rented paddleboat.

The islands were not always islands. For a long time this was a sandy peninsula curving out from the mainland. In 1858 a powerful storm cut through the neck of that peninsula. The water opened a gap, and the land beyond became a set of islands. What you walk on today is the work of waves and one violent night.

The chain has several parts, linked by paths and bridges. Centre Island sits in the middle, with gardens and a small amusement park built for young children. Ward's Island and Algonquin Island, at the eastern end, hold a quiet community of homes. Hanlan's Point lies to the west, with a beach and open lawns.

One of the oldest structures in Toronto stands here. The Gibraltar Point Lighthouse was built in 1808, of stone, to guide ships toward the harbor. It no longer works as a light, but it still stands among the trees, short and solid, far older than almost anything across the water in the city. A long-told legend says its first keeper vanished one night long ago, and some still call the tower haunted. The story is only that, a story, but it has held to the place for generations.

The beaches draw people all summer. Some are busy and easy to reach. Others take a longer walk and stay calm. The water is fresh, not salt, and cold even in July. On a hot day the shoreline fills with swimmers, picnics, and the smell of sunscreen and barbecue smoke.

For many visitors, the real reason to come is the view back. From the island shore, the whole downtown skyline lines up across the water. The tall tower in the center, the bank towers, the spread of the waterfront. At sunset the glass catches the light, and the city glows orange against the dusk.

The small amusement park on Centre Island is built to a gentle scale. A carousel, a little train, a flume ride, a Ferris wheel. It is made for families with young children rather than thrill seekers. The rides have run here for generations, and many adults remember coming as kids.

Renting a bicycle changes the day. The islands stretch several kilometers from end to end, and a bike lets you reach the quiet corners. Flat paths run past lagoons, gardens, and stands of willow. You can pedal from the busy center to a still beach in a matter of minutes.

The residential community is one of the oldest of its kind in the area. A few hundred people live in small houses on the eastern islands, with no cars and narrow lanes. Walk through and you find gardens, bicycles leaning on fences, and a pace of life that feels far from the towers across the bay.

Plan your time around the ferry. The last boats back fill quickly on summer evenings, and missing one means a long wait. Bring water, a hat, and food, since services are limited once you are out here. Weather off the lake can shift fast, so a light layer helps even on a warm day.

The outer edges of the islands stay wild. Boardwalks cross marshy ground, and tall reeds hide herons and other birds. In spring and fall, birdwatchers come for the many species that rest here on their long flights along the lake. Away from the amusement park and the main beaches, you can find stretches where the loudest sound is the wind in the grass and the water against the shore.

The strange gift of this place is distance. You are barely a kilometer from one of the largest cities in the country, yet here there is grass underfoot and water on every side. You can see the city clearly. You just cannot hear it. Sometimes the best view of a place is the one from just outside it.
