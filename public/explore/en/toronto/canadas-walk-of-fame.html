The guide

The story

Look down. Under your feet, the grey pavement of King Street West gives way to something polished. A star sits flush with the sidewalk, smooth enough to catch the light from passing streetcars. Around it, the city moves fast. Theatre-goers hurry past, office workers balance coffee cups, and the rumble of traffic never quite stops. Most of them walk straight over these stars without slowing down. You, though, have stopped. And that is exactly the right way to meet this place.

This is Canada's Walk of Fame. It is not a museum and it has no doors. It is a stretch of public sidewalk in Toronto's Entertainment District, mostly along King Street West and around Simcoe Street. Each star carries the name of a Canadian whose work travelled far beyond the country's borders. The idea is simple. Instead of putting these names behind glass, the city set them into the ground, where anyone can walk among them, any time.

The Walk of Fame began in 1998. Many people compare it to the Hollywood Walk of Fame in Los Angeles, and the comparison makes sense. But there is one important difference. Hollywood mostly honours the film and entertainment industry. This walk is broader. It honours actors and musicians, yes, but also athletes, writers, scientists, business leaders, and humanitarians. That mix tells you something about how Canada chose to see itself... not only as a place of entertainers, but as a place of builders and thinkers too.

As you walk, you will start to recognise names. Wayne Gretzky, often called the greatest hockey player who ever lived, has a star here. So does Céline Dion, the singer from Quebec whose voice filled stadiums around the world. Michael J. Fox, born in Edmonton, is here as well. He is known for his films, and also for his long public work on Parkinson's disease research. Each name is a small doorway into a much larger story.

Here is something many visitors miss. Several of these people became famous far from Canada. They built careers in Los Angeles, New York, or London. To many audiences abroad, they simply seemed American or British. This sidewalk quietly corrects that. It says — these people came from here. For Canadians, that can be a point of real pride. A country that often lives in the shadow of a much louder neighbour has written its own list, in stone.

Now lift your eyes from the pavement for a moment. The location was not chosen by accident. This is Toronto's theatre heart. The Royal Alexandra Theatre stands on King Street West, an elegant early twentieth century playhouse with a richly decorated facade. Nearby, the Princess of Wales Theatre hosts large touring musicals. On many evenings, crowds gather on these sidewalks before curtain time. The stars sit right where audiences wait, talk, and look around.

At the corner of King and Simcoe, you will see Roy Thomson Hall. It is hard to miss. The concert hall is wrapped in a curved skin of glass, and at night it glows from within. It is home to the Toronto Symphony Orchestra. In daylight, the glass reflects the sky and the towers around it. It makes a strong contrast with the older brick and stone of the theatres just along the street.

A little further along King Street, near John Street, is TIFF Bell Lightbox. This is the home of the Toronto International Film Festival, one of the most important film festivals in the world. Every September, this district fills with film crews, red carpets, and fans waiting behind barriers for a glimpse of a star. In those weeks, the stars in the pavement have living company. The real thing walks right past them.

New names join this sidewalk regularly. The Walk of Fame holds induction events that have been broadcast on Canadian television over the years. Inductees are celebrated with music, speeches, and tributes from friends and colleagues. Later, their stars take their place here. So this is not a finished monument. It is still growing. If you return in a few years, the walk you see may be a little longer than the one in front of you today.

Take a moment to notice the stars themselves. They are set level with the sidewalk, so they are part of the street, not raised above it. Look for the maple leaf, Canada's national symbol, worked into the design. Rain, snow, salt, and thousands of footsteps all pass over these stars. Toronto winters are hard on pavement. Some stars look freshly polished, while others carry the soft wear of many seasons.

There is a small human ritual you may see here. People crouch down beside a star, sometimes kneeling right on the sidewalk, to take a photograph. Fans look for one name in particular — a singer they grew up with, an athlete their parent loved. Sometimes you will see someone touch a star lightly with their hand. It is a quiet gesture, in the middle of a loud street. And nobody around them seems to mind.

It is worth thinking about who chooses these names. The selection reflects public suggestions and the work of the organisation behind the walk. Like any list of honours, it starts debates. Why this person and not that one? Who is still missing? Those conversations are part of the point. A walk like this is never only about the past. It is also about what a country decides to remember, and how those choices change over time.

If you have a little time, walk the full stretch slowly rather than searching for one star. Read the names you do not know, too. You may find a scientist, a novelist, or a comedian you had never connected with Canada. Each name is short. The life behind it is not. Later, you can look up the ones that caught your eye, and the walk will keep going long after you leave.

So stand here for one more moment. Hear the streetcar bell, the voices outside the theatres, the steady footsteps all around you. Most of those footsteps will pass over these stars without a pause. Yours do not have to. As you walk on along King Street, keep your eyes down for a few more steps. Another name is probably waiting just ahead.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full stretch slowly instead of hunting one name.
Visit in early evening when theatre crowds fill the sidewalk.
Come during September to catch the film festival atmosphere.
Wear grippy shoes in winter; polished stars can be slippery.
Crouch low and shoot from the side to avoid shadows.
