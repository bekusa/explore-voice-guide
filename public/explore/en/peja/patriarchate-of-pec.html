The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close, water is running fast over stone. That is the Bistrica river, coming down cold from the mountains just behind you. The air here is cooler than in the town. It smells of cut grass, wet earth and old wood. Ahead, behind a high wall, stand several church domes painted a deep, warm red. They look almost soft against the dark green slopes. You have arrived at the Patriarchate of Peć.

This place sits at the mouth of the Rugova Gorge, where the mountains open onto the plain of Peja. The setting was chosen with care. Monks wanted water, shelter and distance from the noise of the road. The cliffs rise steeply behind the walls, and the gorge runs on toward the border with Montenegro. Even today, the town feels far away once you pass the gate.

The complex is a working monastery of the Serbian Orthodox Church. A community of nuns lives here, and they keep the daily rhythm of prayer. Because of the tensions of recent decades, the site is protected. At the entrance you will usually meet a security check, and staff may ask to see your passport or identity card. This is normal here. It is part of what it means to visit this monastery in Kosovo today.

Now look at the main building. It is not one church. It is four churches joined together, side by side, under one roof line. From the outside they read as a single red mass with several domes. Inside, they open into one another through doorways and a shared entrance hall. This cluster grew over roughly a century, as successive church leaders added their own chapel to the old core.

The oldest part is the Church of the Holy Apostles, from the thirteenth century. At that time, the head of the Serbian Church moved his seat here from the monastery of Žiča, further north. Peć was safer, deeper in the hills. In the fourteenth century, more churches followed. One is dedicated to Saint Demetrius. Another is dedicated to the Holy Mother of God Hodegetria — a Greek title meaning "she who shows the way." A small chapel of Saint Nicholas completes the group.

In front of these churches stands a wide entrance hall, called a narthex. It is traditionally linked to Archbishop Danilo the Second, a writer and builder of the fourteenth century. This hall tied the separate churches into one plan. Walk through it slowly. The light is dim, and your eyes need a moment. Then the walls begin to fill with figures, colours and faces.

Here is something many visitors miss. The red colour outside is not the original look of medieval stone. The red paint belongs to a later phase of the building's life. Underneath, the walls are stone and brick, laid in bands. Some visitors find the colour surprising. Others feel it gives the monastery its warmth. Either way, it makes the complex easy to recognise from far up the valley.

Inside, the frescoes are the real treasure. They cover walls, arches and domes. They were painted over several periods, from the thirteenth century onward, so you can see changes in style as you move from room to room. Earlier figures are calm and heavy. Later scenes are busier, with more movement and detail. Look up into the domes. Christ, the prophets and the saints look down from a deep blue ground.

In 1346, the Serbian archbishopric was raised to the rank of a patriarchate. This happened under Stefan Dušan, who was crowned emperor in the same period. From then on, the head of the Serbian Church carried the title of Patriarch. That is where the name of this place comes from. The leaders of the Church were enthroned here, and several of them are buried inside these churches.

The Ottoman conquest brought hard times. The patriarchate lost its status for a period. Then, in 1557, it was restored, with the support of the Ottoman grand vizier Mehmed Pasha Sokolović, who had Serbian roots. His relative Makarije became patriarch. This is one of the surprising turns of Balkan history — a powerful Ottoman statesman helping to revive an Orthodox church seat. The patriarchate was abolished again in 1766, and this time it stayed closed for a long time.

In 2006, the Patriarchate of Peć was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list. It belongs to a group called the Medieval Monuments in Kosovo, together with Dečani Monastery, Gračanica and the Church of the Virgin of Ljeviša in Prizren. The group is also on the list of World Heritage in Danger. That status reflects concerns about protection and the political situation, not only the age of the buildings.

Before you leave, walk slowly around the courtyard. Look for the old mulberry tree, with its thick, twisted trunk. Locals say it has stood here for many centuries, and it has become a symbol of the monastery. You may also see neat gardens, beehives or vegetables tended by the nuns. These small, daily tasks are part of the place. This is a home, not only a monument.

Now stand still for a moment. Listen again to the river behind the wall. Feel the cool air coming down the gorge. Look once more at the red walls and the grey mountains above them. People have prayed here for more than seven hundred years, through empires and wars. As you walk back toward the gate and the town, notice how the sound of the water follows you... and then slowly fades.

Combine your visit with a walk into Rugova Gorge.
