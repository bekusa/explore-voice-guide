The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Just behind you, a tram bell rings on the Ring, and car tyres hiss over the cobbles. Here on Minoritenplatz, though, the sound drops away. The square is calm, framed by pale palace walls and government offices. In the middle stands a long grey church of rough stone, plain and heavy, like a ship pulled up on dry land. This is the Minoritenkirche, one of the oldest Gothic churches in Vienna.

The name comes from the people who first lived here. The Minorites were a branch of the Franciscan friars, followers of Saint Francis of Assisi. They called themselves the lesser brothers, and they took vows of poverty. They arrived in Vienna in the early thirteenth century, when the city was still a walled ducal town. The ruling duke gave them land here, close to the ducal residence. That residence later grew into the Hofburg palace, only a few minutes' walk away.

The building you see today dates mostly from the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. An earlier church on this site burned, and the friars rebuilt in the new Gothic style. Tradition says that King Ottokar the Second of Bohemia, who ruled Vienna for a time before the Habsburgs, laid the foundation stone. Whatever the exact story, the timing matters. Saint Stephen's Cathedral was still mostly a Romanesque church at the time. Here, the new pointed Gothic style arrived early.

Now look at the tower. Something seems wrong with it, and you are right to notice. It rises as an eight-sided Gothic tower, and then it simply stops. There is no tall pointed spire, only a low cap. The tower top was damaged during the Ottoman sieges of Vienna, when cannon fire hit the city. The tall spire never returned. So the church has carried this shortened, almost cut-off silhouette for centuries.

Walk to the main entrance on the west front. Above the doors, the stone portal is the finest piece of carving on the outside. Look up into the pointed arch above the doors. That arch is called the tympanum, and here it shows the Crucifixion, with figures standing on either side of the cross. This portal was made in the fourteenth century. It is linked with a friar who had trained in France, and you can see the elegant French Gothic style in the slim figures and soft folds of cloth.

As you go inside, the first surprise is the shape of the space. Many Gothic churches are tall and narrow, and they pull your eye forward to the altar. This one feels wide and almost square. It is a hall church, which means the side aisles rise nearly as high as the central nave. The columns stand like trees in an orderly stone forest. The light falls evenly across the whole room, not just down a central corridor.

The interior is also calmer than you might expect in Vienna. This city loves gold and marble and painted clouds. Over the centuries, the church did receive baroque decoration in that style. Then, in the late eighteenth century, architects removed much of it and returned the building to a cleaner Gothic look. So what you see is partly medieval and partly an eighteenth-century idea of the Middle Ages. It is a careful restoration, but also a statement of taste.

That same period brought another major change. In 1784, Emperor Joseph the Second gave the church to Vienna's Italian community. Joseph was a reforming ruler who closed or reorganised many religious houses. The friars left, and the building became the Italian national church, dedicated to Our Lady of the Snows. For generations, Italian merchants, artists and court musicians worshipped here. The Italian poet Metastasio, who wrote opera texts for the Habsburg court, was buried in this church.

Now turn to the left wall, and you will find the second big surprise. A huge copy of Leonardo da Vinci's Last Supper fills the wall. Step closer, and you will see that it is not painted at all. It is a mosaic, built from many thousands of tiny pieces of coloured glass. From a few steps away, the pieces blend into soft shading. Up close, the faces break into small, separate dots of colour.

The story of this mosaic runs through European politics. Napoleon ordered it in the early nineteenth century. At that time, he controlled Milan, where Leonardo's original fades on a monastery wall. The Italian mosaic artist Giacomo Raffaelli worked on it for years. By the time it was finished, Napoleon had fallen from power. The Austrian emperor Francis the First acquired it instead. The mosaic travelled to Vienna, and eventually it came to rest here, in an Italian church in the Habsburg capital.

Think about what that means. Leonardo's original in Milan has suffered from damp, war and old restorations. Its colours are pale and damaged. This glass copy was made two centuries ago, and its colours do not fade in the same way. So in some ways, this mosaic keeps a clearer picture of how people saw Leonardo's work around 1800. Look at the gestures of the apostles. Each man reacts in shock to Christ's words that one of them will betray him.

Before you leave, walk slowly around the outside of the church. The stone walls show patches of different colour and texture, from many centuries of repair. Look at the tall buttresses, the stone supports that hold up the walls, and the pointed window frames between them. Around the square, the palaces and ministries are much younger and smoother. Austria's government still works in these streets, and the federal chancellery stands nearby. Among all that official power, this old Franciscan church looks humble and rough.

When you step back onto the cobbles, look once more at that shortened tower against the sky. It has stood through sieges, reforms and changes of language and congregation. Friars once walked here in simple robes. Later, Italian families gathered here far from home. Today, office workers cross the square with coffee in hand, and many never look up. You did. Take that quiet with you as you walk back toward the noise of the city.

What to look for

Columns of nearly equal height creating a wide hall interior.
Patched stone of different shades along the outer walls.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning when Minoritenplatz is at its quietest.
Step back across the square to see the cut-off tower.
Respect services in progress and keep voices low inside.
Combine the visit with a walk to the nearby Hofburg.
Bring zoom or binoculars for details in the portal tympanum.
