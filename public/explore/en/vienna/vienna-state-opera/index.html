The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of the Ringstrasse for a moment and listen. Trams grind past on their rails. Horse-drawn carriages clatter over the stones. Above all of it rises a long, pale facade of arched windows and heavy stone. In the evening, light spills from the upper loggia, and people in dark coats gather beneath it with tickets in their hands. This is the Vienna State Opera, and for more than a century and a half it has been one of the places the city cares about most.

To understand why it stands here, picture Vienna in the middle of the nineteenth century. The old city walls had come down, and Emperor Franz Joseph ordered a grand new boulevard to take their place. That boulevard is the Ringstrasse, the ring road you are standing on. Ministries, museums and a parliament would all follow along its curve. But the opera house came first. It was the first great public building finished on the new ring, a statement that music sat at the centre of Viennese life.

The design came from two architects who worked as a team, August Sicard von Sicardsburg and Eduard van der Nüll. They chose a style called Neo-Renaissance, which borrows the arches, columns and calm proportions of Italian buildings from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Look at the facade and you can see that idea at work. The ground floor carries rounded arcades. Above it, the open loggia frames five large arches. Everything is built to feel solid, balanced and dignified.

And yet... the Viennese did not love it at first. Critics mocked the building. Some said it looked heavy. Others complained that the street level around it had been raised, so the opera seemed to sink into the ground. The two architects took this criticism very hard. Van der Nüll took his own life in 1868, and Sicardsburg died only weeks later. Neither man lived to see his building open its doors.

There is a well-known story connected to that sad episode. Locals say that Franz Joseph had made a sharp remark about the building, and that the tragedy shocked him deeply. According to the tale, for the rest of his long reign he answered almost every new thing with the same polite phrase: it was very nice, it pleased me very much. Whether every detail is true is hard to know. But the story has survived in Vienna for generations, and people still repeat it.

The house opened in 1869 with Mozart's Don Giovanni. That choice was no accident. Mozart had lived and worked in Vienna, and the city saw him as one of its own. From that first night, the opera became a stage for the greatest names in music. Composers, singers and conductors from across Europe came here to be heard by an audience that knew its music well and did not hide its opinions.

One name stands out above the others. At the end of the nineteenth century, the composer Gustav Mahler became director of the house, and he held the post for about ten years. Mahler was demanding and often difficult. He insisted on serious rehearsal and close attention to drama as well as sound. Latecomers were not allowed to walk in during the music. He also dimmed the lights in the auditorium, so the audience watched the stage instead of each other. Many of his reforms shaped how opera is performed today.

Now for a surprise that many visitors miss. Much of what you see inside today is not the original nineteenth-century building. In March 1945, near the end of the Second World War, bombs struck the opera. Fire swept through the auditorium and the stage. The great hall where audiences had sat for decades was destroyed. Yet the front of the building survived. So did the grand staircase, the main foyer and a few elegant reception rooms. When you walk up those stairs, you are walking through the old house that came through the fire.

Rebuilding took about ten years. For the people of Vienna, it was more than a construction project. The city was occupied and poor after the war, and the restored opera became a symbol of recovery. The house reopened in 1955 with Beethoven's Fidelio. That opera tells the story of a prisoner rescued and set free. In the same year, Austria regained its full independence. The choice of music carried a clear meaning for everyone in the hall.

Inside, the auditorium follows the classic horseshoe shape. Tiers of boxes rise in curves of red and gold and cream, all facing the stage. The orchestra pit sits low in front of it. Here is a second detail many visitors do not realise. Musicians from the opera's own orchestra also form the Vienna Philharmonic, one of the most respected orchestras in the world. The players you hear in the pit at night are often the same people who perform in the city's famous concerts.

The opera works on a repertory system. That means it does not run one production for weeks. Instead, it performs many different operas and ballets across a season, often changing the programme from night to night. Behind the scenes, crews move huge sets in and out of storage constantly. Once a year, the house changes completely. For the Opera Ball, seats in the stalls are removed and a dance floor joins the auditorium to the stage. Debutantes in white open the evening with a waltz.

The opera is not only for those with expensive seats. For a long time, the house has sold standing places on the day of the performance, and students and music lovers line up for them. On many evenings in the season, performances are also shown on a large screen outside, on the square beside the building. People bring a coat, stand on the stones, and watch the opera under the open sky, with the traffic of the ring moving behind them.

If you have time, step back across the street before you leave. Look up at the loggia and its arches, at the stone figures and the bronze along the roofline. Think of the two architects who never saw this view, of the fire in 1945, and of the night in 1955 when the music returned. Then listen again — to the trams, to the carriages, to the people talking on their way in. The house is still doing what it was built to do.
