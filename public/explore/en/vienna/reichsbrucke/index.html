The guide

The story

Stand at the railing and feel it first. A low hum rises through the steel under your feet. That is the underground train passing on the deck below you. Wind comes off the water, wide and steady, and it carries a faint river smell of wet stone and diesel. Ahead, the Danube runs broad and grey-green, much wider than most visitors expect. This is the Reichsbrücke, the Imperial Bridge, and it is one of the main crossings between the old city and the new districts to the east.

First, a word about where you are. Behind you, on the Leopoldstadt side, lies the second district of Vienna. Ahead, past the long island in the middle of the river, lies Donaustadt, the twenty-second district. For much of the city's history, this side of the river felt like the edge of town. Floodplains, gravel banks and side channels spread out here. The bridge connects these two worlds, and its story follows the city's slow move toward the water.

For centuries, the Danube near Vienna was not one river. It was a wandering web of arms and islands. It flooded often and changed course without warning. In the late nineteenth century, engineers cut a new, straight main channel for the river. That project made a permanent bridge possible here. The first bridge on this spot opened in 1876. It was named after Crown Prince Rudolf, the heir to the Habsburg throne.

The name did not last. After the end of the monarchy, the crossing became the Reichsbrücke. In the 1930s, the city replaced the old structure with a new chain bridge. It was elegant, heavy and very much a statement of confidence. Its massive piers stood in the river, and its chains hung between tall pylons. For decades, Viennese families crossed it on foot to reach the beaches and meadows along the water.

Here is the first surprise. During the final battle for Vienna in April 1945, many bridges across the Danube were destroyed. The Reichsbrücke was not. It survived the fighting and came out of the war standing. For many people in the city, it became a quiet symbol of endurance. A bridge that had lived through the worst of the century seemed almost permanent... and that belief makes the next chapter even more striking.

Early on a Sunday morning, on the first of August 1976, the old bridge collapsed. It happened just before five in the morning, when the city was mostly asleep. One of the river piers failed, and the whole structure fell into the Danube within moments. A bus was on the bridge at the time. Its few passengers survived, which many people still describe as close to a miracle. One driver crossing the bridge was killed.

The timing mattered enormously. On a weekday, or a summer afternoon, the bridge would have carried trams, cars and crowds of people. The collapse shocked Vienna and all of Austria. Investigations focused on the condition of the pier, and the event led to new attention to how bridges were inspected and maintained. For a generation of Viennese, the morning the Reichsbrücke fell is a moment they remember clearly, like a family story.

The bridge you are standing on now is the replacement. It opened to traffic in 1980. Its design is very different from the old chain bridge. There are no pylons and no hanging chains. Instead, it is a long, flat, practical structure with two levels. Cars, cyclists and pedestrians use the upper deck. Below them, hidden inside the bridge, runs the U1 underground line. That is the source of the hum you felt at the start.

Here is the second surprise. There is an underground station on the bridge itself, above the water. It is called Donauinsel, after the island in the middle of the river. Few cities have a metro station that sits in the middle of a major river. Step down to the platform and look out of the windows. You will see the Danube on both sides of you, and trains arriving from the city and from the east.

That long island below is the Danube Island. It is not a natural feature. Engineers built it in the 1970s and 1980s as part of a large flood protection project. A second channel, the New Danube, runs on the far side of it. Today, the island is one of the city's favourite places to relax. In summer, people swim, cycle, grill and sunbathe along its banks. Every year, a large free open-air music festival fills it with crowds.

Now turn and look east. The tall, curved towers in the distance belong to the Vienna International Centre, often called UNO City. It is one of the official seats of the United Nations. The bridge leads almost directly toward it. This road connects the historic heart of Vienna with a district of modern offices, apartment towers and international organisations. In one walk across the river, you move from the nineteenth century into the late twentieth.

Look back toward the Leopoldstadt end. There you will see Mexikoplatz, the square where the bridge begins. A large brick church with towers rises above the trees. It is the Church of Saint Francis of Assisi, built around the start of the twentieth century. Its reddish brick and pointed shapes make it easy to spot from the bridge. The square around it has long been a place of shops, river traffic and arrivals from the water.

The river below is still a working river. Watch for long, flat cargo barges moving slowly upstream or downstream. They carry goods between Germany, Austria, Hungary and further toward the Black Sea. Passenger ships also pass here, especially in the warmer months. The Danube is one of Europe's great transport routes, and this bridge sits directly above it. Watching a barge slide underneath you is a quiet, steady pleasure.

Before you move on, take a moment at the railing. Listen to the wind and the traffic, and feel the low vibration of the next train below. Many people cross this bridge every day without a second thought. But you now know the story under your feet: an early morning collapse, a city in shock, and a new bridge built to carry trains over water. Look down at the current, then follow it with your eyes toward the horizon.
