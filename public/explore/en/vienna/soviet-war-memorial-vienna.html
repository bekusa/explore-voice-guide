The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, trams grind and chime around the curve of Schwarzenbergplatz. In front of you, water falls in a steady, cooling rush from a wide fountain. Beyond the spray, a pale stone colonnade curves like an open arm. At its centre, a tall column carries a single soldier. He stands high above the traffic, with a flag in one hand and a gilded helmet catching the light.

This is the Soviet War Memorial. Viennese often call it the Heroes' Monument of the Red Army, or simply the Russian monument. It honours the Soviet soldiers who died in the battle for Vienna in April 1945, in the final weeks of the Second World War in Europe. The figure faces the city centre. It feels as if he is still watching over the streets his army fought through.

To understand why this monument stands here, picture Vienna in the spring of 1945. Austria had been absorbed into Nazi Germany in 1938. By early April 1945, the Red Army was pushing in from the east and south. The fighting moved through the suburbs, along the Danube Canal and into the heart of the city. It lasted roughly two weeks, and it was bloody. Commonly cited figures put the Soviet dead in the battle at around seventeen thousand soldiers.

The speed of what came next is striking. The battle ended in mid April. Yet the memorial was unveiled in August of that same year, only a few months later. The Soviet command wanted a statement in stone, and it wanted it quickly. Much of the labour and material came from a war-damaged city that was still short of food, fuel and glass for its windows. Think about that as you look at the clean lines of the colonnade.

Now look more closely at the soldier himself. He wears a helmet covered in gold, and on his chest sits a gilded shield bearing the Soviet coat of arms. His pose is typical of Soviet monumental sculpture of the period — upright, forward-facing, confident and calm. This style later became known as socialist realism. It was the official Soviet art style, and it aimed to show ideal heroes in a clear, heroic way.

Behind the column, the semicircular colonnade forms a kind of stone backdrop. Its pillars carry inscriptions in Russian. They honour the fallen and celebrate the victory over fascism. Most visitors cannot read the Cyrillic letters, so the words become texture rather than message. If you can read Russian, take a few minutes along the curve. The carved lines give the memorial its full voice.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. For around ten years after the war, this square was not called Schwarzenbergplatz at all. During the Allied occupation, when Vienna was divided into sectors like Berlin, the square was renamed Stalin Square. The old name returned only in the mid 1950s, after the occupation ended. The street signs changed, but the soldier stayed exactly where he was.

That permanence was written into law. In 1955, Austria signed its State Treaty with the four Allied powers and regained full independence. As part of that agreement, Austria committed to protect and maintain Allied war graves and memorials on its soil. That is why this monument has survived the Cold War, the collapse of the Soviet Union and decades of changing political moods. Removing it is not simply a matter of local opinion.

And local opinion has never been simple. For many Austrians, the Red Army brought liberation from Nazi rule. For many others, especially those who lived through 1945, it also brought fear, looting and violence against civilians. Vienna's dark humour found its way into a nickname. Some locals called this the monument to the unknown plunderer. Both memories — liberation and suffering — sit side by side on this one square.

Over the years the memorial has also become a target. It has been splashed with paint and defaced at different times, and it has been cleaned and repaired again. In recent years, especially since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the square has also drawn protests and quiet gatherings. Some people come to lay flowers for the dead of 1945. Others come to make a point about the present. The stone carries all of it.

Turn around now and look at the fountain between you and the traffic. This is the Hochstrahlbrunnen, the High Jet Fountain. It is older than the memorial by many decades. It was built in the late nineteenth century to celebrate Vienna's first great water pipeline, which brought fresh spring water down from the Alps. On summer evenings it is lit in changing colours. From many angles, the spray partly hides the soldier from the street.

That layering is part of the experience here. In one view you can see an imperial-era fountain, a Soviet war memorial, the trees and walls of the baroque Palais Schwarzenberg behind it, and modern Vienna rushing past on the Ring nearby. Few places in the city put so many periods into a single frame. Walk slowly around the edge of the fountain. Watch how the soldier appears and disappears behind the water.

Before you leave, step back toward the tram stop and take one last look. Notice the gold on the helmet against the grey stone. Hear the water and the bells of the trams. People sit on the fountain's edge, eat ice cream and check their phones, often without looking up at the soldier at all. As you walk back toward the Ring, you carry the same question many Viennese do — how should a city live with a memory it cannot easily agree on?
