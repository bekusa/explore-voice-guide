The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, trams grind and chime along the Ring. In front of you, water roars from a tall fountain, and spray drifts on the wind. Past that sound, behind a screen of trees and a quiet forecourt, sits a long, pale Baroque palace. It feels almost withdrawn from the city around it. That calm is not an accident. When this house was planned, this ground lay outside Vienna's walls, among gardens and open fields.

This is Palais Schwarzenberg, one of the great summer palaces of Baroque Vienna. Around the year 1700, the nobility began to build country retreats just beyond the fortifications. The Ottoman siege of 1683 had ended in defeat for the attackers, and the land outside the walls suddenly felt safe. Wealthy families raced to claim it. They wanted fresh air, formal gardens, and a stage to show their status to the court.

The palace did not begin with the Schwarzenbergs. The first owner was Heinrich Franz von Mansfeld, Prince of Fondi, a senior figure at the imperial court. He commissioned the young architect Johann Lukas von Hildebrandt, who later designed the Belvedere palaces just up the slope from here. Building started in the late 1690s. But Mansfeld died in 1715, before his house was truly finished. The unfinished estate soon found a new and very determined buyer.

That buyer was Prince Adam Franz von Schwarzenberg, who acquired the property in 1716. He brought in Johann Bernhard Fischer von Erlach, the most celebrated architect of imperial Vienna. Fischer von Erlach is the man behind the Karlskirche, the great domed church a short walk west of here. After his death, his son Joseph Emanuel continued the work. So the building you see carries the hands of two generations and two rival architectural minds.

Now for a story that most visitors never hear. Adam Franz did not live long to enjoy his palace. In 1732, he joined Emperor Charles the Sixth on a stag hunt in Bohemia. During the hunt, a shot went wrong. The bullet that killed the prince came from the emperor's own gun. A ruler had accidentally killed one of his most loyal nobles, and the court was shaken. The Schwarzenberg family, however, kept the palace and kept their place at court.

Look at the shape of the building. It does not stand tall and square like a town house. Instead, it stretches wide and low, with wings that reach out toward the forecourt. This was a garden palace, designed to open toward green space rather than toward a narrow street. Behind it, the gardens once ran up the hillside in terraces, with sculptures, clipped hedges and water features. Part of that green space still survives, although the city has pressed in around it.

Inside, the palace once held grand reception rooms with painted ceilings. The Austrian painter Daniel Gran worked on frescoes here, and they were among the finest decorative paintings of their time. The Second World War brought bombing to Vienna, and the palace was badly damaged. Some of the painted ceilings were lost forever. What visitors can see today is a building that has been repaired, adapted and repaired again over many decades.

Here is a second surprise. In 1798, this palace hosted the first performance of Joseph Haydn's oratorio The Creation. An oratorio is a large musical work for orchestra, choir and solo singers, often on a religious theme. The premiere was private, for an invited audience of aristocrats who supported the composer. Accounts from the period describe a crowd gathering outside, simply hoping to hear something through the walls. Haydn later presented another major oratorio, The Seasons, in this same house.

Now turn and look back toward the square. Schwarzenbergplatz takes its name from the family, and especially from Field Marshal Karl Philipp von Schwarzenberg. He commanded the allied armies against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813. His bronze statue on horseback stands at the far end of the square, near the Ring. So the square tells the family story from both ends: a soldier at one side, and the family palace at the other.

Between the statue and the palace, you will notice two later additions. The first is the High Jet Fountain, built in 1873 to celebrate Vienna's new mountain water supply. Clean water from the Alps changed public health in the city, and this fountain was its proud announcement. Just behind it stands the Soviet War Memorial, raised in 1945 after the Red Army entered Vienna. The column and the soldier figure still stand today. Together, they make this one of the most layered views in the city.

In the twentieth century, the palace found a new life. For several decades, part of it operated as a luxury hotel and restaurant. Guests could sleep in a noble residence and eat on a terrace above the old gardens. That chapter has since closed, and the building has been quieter in recent years. The Schwarzenberg family still owns it. Access to the interior has been limited, so for most visitors, this is a place to view from the outside.

That outside view still rewards patience. Walk slowly along the forecourt and notice how the façade moves in and out, with a raised central section that catches more light. Look at the window frames, the cornice line along the roof, and the stone details over the doors. These elements are typical of Austrian Baroque, a style that loved balance and theatre. Then step back toward the fountain and see how the palace sits as a backdrop to the square.

It is worth thinking about what this place has seen. A prince who never finished his house. An heir who died from an emperor's bullet. A composer hearing his greatest work for the first time in these rooms. Bombs falling, then hotel guests, then quiet. Through all of it, one family name has stayed attached to the walls. Few private palaces in Vienna can say the same after more than three centuries.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment between the fountain and the palace gates. Notice how the noise of the Ring fades behind you, and how the trees soften the light on the pale stone. Picture this ground as open country, with carriages arriving from the city walls. Then walk on toward the Belvedere or back toward the square... and listen to the water as it follows you away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the Belvedere palaces just uphill.
Stand beside the High Jet Fountain for the widest palace view.
Plan to view the palace mainly from outside.
Walk the full length of Schwarzenbergplatz from the Ring.
Visit on a calm day when fountain spray stays low.
