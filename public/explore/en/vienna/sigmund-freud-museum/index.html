The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go in. Berggasse is a quiet street of pale stone facades, and it runs gently downhill toward the Danube Canal. The name means, roughly, mountain lane, and you feel that slope in your legs. Trams hum somewhere nearby. Doors close with a heavy wooden sound. It is an ordinary Viennese street in the ninth district, Alsergrund. That is the first surprise. One of the most influential ideas of the twentieth century did not grow in a palace or a university hall. It grew here, behind a plain doorway, at number nineteen.

Sigmund Freud moved into this building in 1891. He stayed for nearly half a century. Here he raised six children with his wife, Martha. Here he saw patients, wrote his books, and smoked his cigars late into the night. The apartment was both his home and his workplace. Patients climbed the same staircase you are about to climb. Some arrived anxious, some curious, some desperate. Many of them came with problems that the medicine of the day could not explain. Freud listened to them. That simple act — careful, patient listening — became the start of psychoanalysis.

As you walk up, notice the stairwell itself. The stone steps are worn smooth in the middle. The light falls in from tall windows. This is a typical Viennese apartment house of the late nineteenth century, built for the comfortable middle class. Doctors, lawyers, and merchants lived in buildings just like this across the city. Nothing on the outside announced that a revolution in thinking was happening upstairs. Today a modest sign marks the entrance. For decades, though, a passer-by would have seen only another respectable address on a sloping street.

Inside, the rooms feel surprisingly bright and open. This is partly the result of a major renovation, completed in 2020. The museum now includes the former private apartment of the Freud family, not only the practice rooms. You move from space to space as Freud's patients and relatives once did. The floors creak a little. The windows look down onto the street and the courtyard. Try to picture the noise of a large family here — children, visitors, servants, and the steady arrival of patients through the day.

Now for the detail that surprises many visitors. The famous couch is not here. Freud's analytic couch, covered with its patterned rug, now stands in London. So does most of his collection of antiquities — the small statues of gods and animals that crowded his desk. They travelled with him when he left Vienna. What you find here instead is absence, and the museum uses that absence on purpose. The empty spaces ask you to think about why these objects left, and what happened to the people who stayed behind.

The reason is 1938. In March of that year, Nazi Germany annexed Austria. Within weeks, Jewish life in Vienna became dangerous. Freud was over eighty and seriously ill with cancer of the jaw. His apartment was searched. His daughter Anna was taken away for questioning by the Gestapo and later released. Only after intense pressure from friends and supporters abroad was the family allowed to leave. In June 1938, Freud departed for London. He died there the following year, in September 1939, shortly after the Second World War began.

Not everyone escaped. Four of Freud's sisters remained in Vienna. All four were later murdered in the Holocaust. This part of the story is easy to miss if you come only for the history of ideas. The museum does not let you miss it. Berggasse nineteen is a place of intellectual history, but it is also a place of loss. The building carries the story of a Viennese Jewish family, and of a city that turned against many of its own citizens.

One room deserves your full attention: the waiting room. Its furniture is original. Decades after the war, Anna Freud helped return these pieces to Vienna, and the museum opened here in 1971. Look at the chairs, the small tables, the heavy fabrics. Patients sat here before their sessions. So did the members of a small circle that began meeting in the apartment in 1902, on Wednesday evenings. They called themselves the Wednesday Psychological Society. From those talks grew the international psychoanalytic movement.

Another quiet surprise is a set of photographs. Shortly before the family left in 1938, a young photographer named Edmund Engelman took pictures of the apartment. He recorded the desk, the consulting room, the couch, and the shelves full of figures. These images became the main record of how the rooms looked when Freud worked in them. Much of what we know about the original arrangement comes from his camera. As you walk, you can compare the photographs with the rooms in front of you. The gap between them tells its own story.

It helps to remember what Freud wrote within these walls. His book The Interpretation of Dreams appeared at the turn of the twentieth century. In it, he argued that dreams carry meaning, and that the mind holds wishes and fears we do not see directly. Ideas such as the unconscious, repression, and the talking cure entered everyday language. Many of his theories are still debated, and some are rejected. Yet it is hard to discuss the human mind today without using words that passed through this apartment.

The museum also looks beyond Freud himself. Anna Freud became a major figure in child psychoanalysis. Her work, and the work of many other analysts, spread from this circle to cities around the world. Exhibitions here often explore that wider network. You may also find contemporary art among the displays. The museum has long collected conceptual artworks connected to memory, language, and the mind. This mix can feel unexpected at first. But it fits a place where people first tried to put hidden thoughts into words.

Before you leave, stand by one of the windows and look down at Berggasse. Freud saw this view thousands of times. He saw it in quiet years, when he walked out each day through the neighbourhood. He saw it again in 1938, when the street was no longer safe for him. The view itself has not changed very much. The same stone, the same slope, the same rhythm of footsteps. That ordinary continuity is part of what makes the place so moving.

When you step back outside, take a moment on the pavement. Look up at the windows on the upper floor and count them. Somewhere behind that glass, a doctor once sat out of sight behind his patients and listened. Then walk slowly down the hill toward the canal. Notice the doorways you pass, the people inside the cafes, the voices in the street. Every one of them carries thoughts no one else can see... and that, in the end, is the question this quiet address first asked.
