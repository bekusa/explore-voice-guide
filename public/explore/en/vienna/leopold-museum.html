The guide

The story

Stand in the courtyard for a moment and look at the walls in front of you. The building is a pale, almost creamy cube of limestone, smooth and quiet. Across the square, its neighbour rises in dark grey volcanic stone. One white, one black... and between them, the old baroque stables of an emperor. Footsteps echo on the paving. Someone laughs on the bright courtyard furniture. This is the MuseumsQuartier, and the white cube is the Leopold Museum.

The white stone was a deliberate choice. The architects, the brothers Laurids and Manfred Ortner, designed the two museums as a pair of opposites. The dark block holds the modern and contemporary collection, known as mumok. The light one holds Vienna around 1900. Notice how both buildings stay low enough to sit behind the long baroque front. The old imperial stables were built in the eighteenth century. The new museums opened in 2001, set into the space behind them.

Now, the surprise most visitors miss. This museum did not begin with a government or an aristocrat. It began with an eye doctor. Rudolf Leopold was a young Viennese medical student in the 1950s. He saw paintings by Egon Schiele and became obsessed. Back then, Schiele was not fashionable, and his work could still be bought by a determined young man with little money. Leopold kept buying for the rest of his life.

He did not collect alone. His wife, Elisabeth Leopold, was also a doctor, and she shared the passion. Together they filled their home with paintings, drawings, furniture, and objects. Over the decades, the private collection grew into one of the most important groups of Austrian art anywhere. In 1994, the couple and the Austrian state created a foundation. The state helped to secure the collection, and the collection got a permanent public home in this white building.

So what will you find inside? The heart of the museum is Egon Schiele. The Leopold holds the largest collection of his work in the world. Schiele drew bodies that are thin, twisted, and exposed. His lines are sharp and nervous. Skin turns green, orange, and bruised purple. Many of his self-portraits show a young man staring straight back at you. They can feel uncomfortable, and they were meant to.

Look out for his self-portrait with a Chinese lantern plant, painted in 1912. The small orange seed pods glow beside his pale, angled face. Nearby, if it is on display, is a portrait of Wally Neuzil, his companion and model during those years. That second painting carries a long story. In 1998, while on loan to an exhibition in New York, it was held by the authorities over a claim from the heirs of its pre-war Jewish owner.

The dispute lasted more than a decade. It was finally settled in 2010, when the museum paid the heirs and the painting returned to Vienna. The case became one of the best-known examples of art taken during the Nazi era. It changed how many museums look at the history of their own collections. Today, provenance research — the study of who owned a work, and when — is a serious part of museum work in Austria.

Schiele is not alone here. Gustav Klimt is present too, with his large painting Death and Life. On one side, a crowd of sleeping people, wrapped in colourful patterns, drift together. On the other side, Death stands alone, covered in dark crosses, watching them. It is a quieter Klimt than the golden portraits many people expect. Both Klimt and Schiele died in the same year, 1918, as the First World War ended.

That year matters. Schiele was only twenty-eight when the Spanish flu killed him, days after his pregnant wife. An empire collapsed around them. When you walk these rooms, you are seeing a city at a turning point. Vienna around 1900 was full of new ideas in art, medicine, music, and psychology. Look for the work of Oskar Kokoschka and Richard Gerstl too, two other painters who pushed against polite, decorative style.

The museum is also about everyday objects. Vienna's designers believed that a chair, a lamp, or a spoon deserved the same care as a painting. The Wiener Werkstätte, which means the Vienna Workshops, was founded on that idea. Designers such as Josef Hoffmann and Koloman Moser made furniture with clean lines, black and white grids, and careful proportions. Some of these pieces look surprisingly modern, even today.

As you move through the floors, pay attention to the building itself. The interior is calm and full of light. Large windows appear at certain points, framing views of the city outside. These pauses are useful. After an hour of intense faces and twisted bodies, your eyes need rest. Stand at a window, look at the rooftops of Vienna, and then return to the art with fresh attention.

The collection also reaches beyond 1900. Rudolf Leopold collected older Austrian painting and later work too, including landscapes and portraits from the nineteenth century. Special exhibitions change through the year, so the rooms are never quite the same twice. The permanent heart, though, stays with Vienna's great moment of change. Rudolf Leopold died in 2010. Elisabeth continued to support the museum long after, and the collection still carries their personal taste.

When you leave, step back into the courtyard and turn around. Look again at that pale stone cube, and at the dark one facing it. Behind you are the long yellow walls of the old stables. In front of you, people sit and talk, children run, and the city carries on. Somewhere inside those white walls, a young man with a thin face and orange seed pods beside him is still staring out... waiting for the next visitor.
