The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere ahead a peacock lets out a sharp, almost human cry. Old trees throw shade across the paths. The air smells of cut grass, warm hay, and the faint sweetness of the palace gardens just beyond the walls. You are standing inside an imperial park, and the animals around you live in a space that was first laid out for an emperor's pleasure. Let's walk through it together.

This is Schönbrunn Zoo, known in German as Tiergarten Schönbrunn. It was founded in 1752, and it is widely recognised as the oldest zoo in the world that is still operating today. That is a remarkable thread of continuity. Empires have risen and fallen around this patch of ground, and through most of it, the gates have opened and people have come to look at animals. Few places anywhere can say the same.

The man behind it was Francis Stephen of Lorraine, who became Holy Roman Emperor as Francis the First. He was the husband of Maria Theresa, the powerful Habsburg ruler who reshaped Austria in the eighteenth century. Francis had a strong interest in natural science, collections, and curiosities. A menagerie, a collection of living animals kept for display, was a fashionable sign of status for European courts at the time. Here, it became something that lasted far longer than fashion.

Now find the octagonal pavilion at the centre of the old zoo. This elegant building was the imperial breakfast pavilion. The design is clever. The original animal enclosures were arranged around it in a ring, radiating outward like slices of a round cake. From inside, the imperial family could take breakfast and turn their heads from one enclosure to the next. The animals were the entertainment. The view was the whole point.

That radial plan is still easy to read on the ground today. Walk a little way and notice how the paths curve around the centre, and how the older enclosures sit in a circle. It is a piece of baroque thinking, the style of grand symmetry and theatre that shaped European palaces in that era. Order, control, a single privileged point of view. The modern zoo has grown far beyond that ring, but the original geometry remains at its heart.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In 1828, a giraffe arrived in Vienna as a diplomatic gift, and the city went a little wild. Giraffes were almost unknown to most Europeans. Viennese society responded with a craze for everything giraffe — patterned fabrics, hairstyles, gloves, and pastries styled after the animal. For a short season, a single long-necked arrival set the tone for fashion in an imperial capital.

Over the following century, the menagerie slowly changed character. It stopped being a private court amusement and became a public institution, open to ordinary people. After the end of the Habsburg monarchy in 1918, it passed into the hands of the new Austrian republic. The twentieth century was hard on it. The Second World War brought damage and heavy losses, and the zoo had to rebuild, almost from the ground up, in the years that followed.

The most dramatic transformation came in recent decades. Old iron cages gave way to larger, more natural spaces. Look for the glass houses that hold whole climates under one roof. The Rainforest House is warm and humid, with dense green growth and birds moving overhead. Step inside and your glasses may fog. The Desert House sits in a historic glasshouse, and it holds dry-land plants and small desert animals in a much warmer, drier world.

Second surprise. Schönbrunn became one of Europe's best-known homes for giant pandas. A pair arrived from China in 2003, and in 2007 a cub was born here. The birth drew international attention, because successful panda breeding in zoos was still rare at that time. Pandas here remain on loan from China, part of a wider conservation agreement. If the panda enclosure is busy, give it a few minutes. These animals often rest, then suddenly move.

Not everything here is exotic. Look for the Tyrolean farmhouse, a traditional alpine farm building brought to the zoo. Around it live older breeds of farm animals, some of them now rare in the Austrian countryside. It is a quiet corner, and it smells honestly of a farmyard. Children often love it most. It is also a reminder that conservation includes the domestic animals farmers once depended on.

The zoo also sits on a slope. Uphill, through the trees, you may catch sight of the Gloriette, the colonnaded structure that crowns the hill in the palace gardens. The whole palace and park complex, including the zoo, is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, the list of places protected by the United Nations for their cultural value. That status helps explain the care taken with old buildings, even as the enclosures behind them modernise.

As you walk, notice the layers. A baroque pavilion with painted walls. Nineteenth century iron and glass. Concrete from the post-war years. And newer enclosures shaped with rock, water, and planting. You can read more than two and a half centuries of changing ideas about animals in the architecture alone. First the animal as spectacle. Then the animal as a scientific specimen. Now, at least in intention, the animal as something to protect.

Pause for a moment wherever you are standing. Listen to the mix of sounds — children calling out, birds in the old trees, gravel under strollers, perhaps the low call of an animal you cannot yet see. Look back toward the octagonal pavilion if you can find it. An emperor once sat there and looked out at a ring of animals. Today, you are the one doing the looking, on paths open to everyone. Take your time as you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see animals before midday crowds build.
Wear comfortable shoes for gravel paths and sloping ground.
Pair your visit with a walk up to the Gloriette.
Bring a cloth for fogged glasses in the Rainforest House.
Wait patiently at the panda enclosure for animals to move.
