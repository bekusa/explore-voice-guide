The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. Smooth stone paving, wide open, and scattered across it are large, bright, angular loungers. Some are pink, some are mint green, some are another colour entirely this year. People lie on them with coffee, with books, with their eyes closed. Around you rise long baroque facades, pale and orderly. And in the middle of all that symmetry sit two plain cubes, one white and one almost black. Welcome to the MuseumsQuartier in Vienna, where an old imperial building and modern art share a single courtyard.

To understand this place, begin with horses. These long wings were built in the early eighteenth century as the imperial court stables. They served the Habsburgs, the dynasty that ruled from the palace just across the road. The design is credited to Johann Bernhard Fischer von Erlach, one of the great architects of Austrian baroque. His son, Joseph Emanuel, carried the work forward. So the dignified frontage you see along the main street was made to impress, even though it housed animals, carriages and grooms.

Think about what that says about the court. Horses were not a small matter here. They meant ceremony, power and display. The emperor's carriages left from these buildings for processions through the city. Grooms, coachmen and stable staff lived and worked inside. If you stand still and imagine the courtyard filled with hooves on stone and the smell of hay, the long, low proportions of these wings start to make sense. They were built for movement and for space.

Then the empire ended, after the First World War, and the stables lost their purpose. In the twentieth century the complex took on a very different life. It became a trade fair site, known in Vienna as the Messepalast, the fair palace. Exhibitions, sales halls and commercial shows filled the old spaces. For decades, people came here to look at goods rather than paintings. That practical chapter is easy to forget, but it kept the buildings in use.

The idea of a museum district took a long time to settle. Plans were drawn, argued about and redrawn. One early design included a tall tower that would have risen above the old roofline. Many Viennese objected. They did not want anything to compete with the baroque skyline around the Hofburg palace. The tower was dropped. The final design, by the architects Laurids and Manfred Ortner, kept the historic frontage and placed the new buildings inside, in the courtyards. The district opened in 2001.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. From the main street, you can walk right past and see only an eighteenth-century facade. The modern architecture is almost hidden. It is only when you pass through the central gateway that the cubes appear. That is deliberate. The old city keeps its face toward the street, and the new city lives behind it. Vienna often works like this. Courtyards hold surprises that the outer walls do not show.

Now look at the white cube. That is the Leopold Museum, clad in pale limestone. Inside is one of the most important collections of Austrian art from around 1900. It holds the largest collection of works by Egon Schiele anywhere. Schiele was a young painter whose twisted, nervous figures shocked Vienna. He died in 1918, at only twenty-eight. You will also find Gustav Klimt and furniture and design from the Vienna Secession, the movement that broke with academic art.

Turn to face the dark cube. That is mumok, the Museum of Modern Art Ludwig Foundation Vienna. Its walls are dressed in dark grey volcanic stone, and the building looks heavy and closed, like a block of rock set down in the yard. Inside, the focus moves into the twentieth and twenty-first centuries: pop art, conceptual art, and the radical performance movement known as Viennese Actionism. The contrast between the two cubes is the whole point. Light and dark. Around 1900 and after.

Between and around them are smaller institutions. Kunsthalle Wien shows changing contemporary exhibitions and uses the former winter riding hall, where horses once trained when the weather was cold. There is an architecture centre, a dance and performance house, and ZOOM, a children's museum where young visitors are invited to touch and make things. Here is the second surprise. The quarter is not one museum. It is a small neighbourhood of many, sharing one address.

And yet a great many people come here without entering any museum at all. The courtyards are public space. On a warm evening, students, office workers and families gather on the loungers. Cafes and bars spill out under the arcades. In colder months, the courtyard often changes again, with seasonal installations and stands. The loungers themselves have become a small local symbol. Their colour changes from time to time, so the yard rarely looks exactly the same from one year to the next.

Step back out toward the main entrance and look across the road. Directly opposite is Maria-Theresien-Platz, with its twin museum palaces, the Art History Museum and the Natural History Museum. Beyond them lies the Hofburg, the old imperial palace. Stand at the gateway and you can hold the whole story in one line of sight. The emperor's home. His great collections. His stables, now turned into a place for contemporary culture. It is a single axis across three centuries.

It is worth walking the edges of the quarter, too. The complex runs back toward the Spittelberg area, a district of narrow lanes and old townhouses. Small passages lead out of the courtyards into the surrounding streets. Little details sit in corners and archways: art installations, unexpected doors, small shops. The quarter was planned to be crossed, not only visited. People use it as a shortcut between neighbourhoods, which keeps the place feeling lived in rather than formal.

Before you leave, find a spot in the main courtyard and simply sit for a minute. Look at the white stone, then the dark stone, then the pale baroque wings that frame them both. Listen to the voices, the clink of glasses, the footsteps across the paving. Where carriages once rolled out for the emperor, people now lie back and watch the sky. As you walk away through the gateway, glance back once. The old facade closes behind you, and the cubes disappear again.

Practical tips
