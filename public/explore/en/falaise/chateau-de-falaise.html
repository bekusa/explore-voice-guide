The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the rock under your feet. This is not a hill of soft earth. It is a hard, grey spur of stone that rises sharply above a narrow valley. Below you, a small river called the Ante runs past old wash-houses and gardens. Above you, pale walls climb straight out of the cliff, as if the rock simply decided to become a fortress.

This is the Château de Falaise, in the heart of Normandy. The name of the town says a great deal. In French, falaise means cliff. The castle and the cliff are almost the same thing here. Builders used the natural edge of the rock as their first line of defence, and then they stacked stone upon stone to make the drop even more frightening for anyone who tried to attack.

This castle is famous for one birth. Around 1027 or 1028, a boy was born here who would change the history of two countries. His name was William. Today, the world knows him as William the Conqueror — the Duke of Normandy who crossed the Channel in 1066, won the Battle of Hastings, and became King of England. Many visitors arrive knowing the battle. Fewer know that the story starts on this rock.

The story of how William came to be born is part history and part legend. His father was Robert, Duke of Normandy, sometimes called Robert the Magnificent. His mother was a local woman named Herleva, often called Arlette in French. Tradition says she was the daughter of a tanner, a man who worked animal hides down by the river. She was not the duke's wife — and so their son was first known by a much harsher name: William the Bastard.

Locals love to tell how the couple met. According to the popular tale, the young duke looked down from the castle walls and saw Arlette washing clothes, or perhaps dancing, in the stream below. He was struck at once. Whether it happened exactly like that, nobody can prove. But the spring at the foot of the cliff still carries her name, the Fontaine d'Arlette. When you walk down later, you can stand at the very spot where the legend is set.

Now look at the castle itself. The largest square block is the great keep. It was built in the twelfth century, after William's lifetime, under his son Henry the First, who ruled both England and Normandy. So the building you see is not the room where William was born. It is the stronger, grander castle his family raised afterwards, on the same rock. That is a surprise for many visitors, and it is worth keeping in mind as you explore.

Notice the stone. It is a light, creamy limestone, laid in neat courses. Look closely at the walls and you will see narrow windows, thick buttresses, and small changes in colour where stone was repaired over the centuries. Beside the great keep stands a smaller keep, added later to give extra living space and defence. Together they form a compact, heavy group, built to impress as much as to protect.

Then your eye goes to the round tower. It rises tall and slender beside the square keeps, and it looks quite different. That is because it comes from a different power. In 1204, the French king Philip Augustus took Normandy from the English crown. Falaise changed hands, and the French added this round tower. Round towers were harder to undermine and had no weak corners for attackers to target. The shape itself is a lesson in medieval warfare.

The tower is called the Talbot Tower. The name comes from John Talbot, an English commander during the Hundred Years' War, the long conflict between England and France in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Falaise was once again in English hands for a time, and Talbot's name stuck to the tower. So in one view you can see Norman, English, and French chapters of history standing shoulder to shoulder on the same rock.

The castle saw more fighting long after the knights were gone. In 1590, during the French Wars of Religion, the future King Henry the Fourth besieged Falaise. Then came the twentieth century. In August 1944, the area around the town became the scene of one of the decisive battles of the Normandy campaign, known as the Falaise Pocket. Much of the town below was badly damaged. The castle on its rock came through, scarred but standing.

As you move inside, you will notice something unexpected. Among the old stones, there are modern additions — concrete, steel, glass, and clean modern floors. These came from a restoration in the late twentieth century. The architect chose not to fake medieval details, but to show clearly what is new. Some people find this bold and honest. Others find it jarring. Look at it yourself and decide. It is one of the most debated restorations in France.

Inside the keeps, the rooms are high and bare, and sound carries off the stone. Climb where you can and look out through the narrow openings. You will see the roofs of the town, the church towers, and the green valley folding away into the Norman countryside. From up here, you understand why someone chose this rock. Every road, every field, every movement in the valley could be watched from these walls.

When you leave, walk down into the town square. There you will find a bronze statue of William on horseback, raised in the nineteenth century, surrounded by figures of the earlier dukes of Normandy. It is a proud, theatrical monument, very much of its time. Turn around from there and look back up at the castle. The view of the keeps above the cliff is one of the best in Falaise.

Before you go, take one last look at the rock itself. The walls have changed many times — Norman, English, French, and modern. But the grey stone under them has not moved. A tanner's daughter washed clothes below it. A duke watched from above. A boy was born here who would become a king. Listen for the river as you walk away... it is still running at the foot of the cliff, just as it did then.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk down to the Fontaine d'Arlette below the cliff.
View the keeps from the town square for the best angle.
Wear sturdy shoes for stone stairs and uneven paths.
Climb to the upper windows for views across the valley.
Allow extra time to explore the town after the castle.
