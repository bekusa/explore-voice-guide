The story

The ground rises unevenly beneath your feet — layers of earth and memory compressed into a space no larger than a city block. Twelve thousand headstones lean against one another in the Old Jewish Cemetery, some tilted at impossible angles, others nearly horizontal, all crowded so tightly that walking between them is almost impossible. The stone surfaces are worn smooth by centuries of rain, the Hebrew inscriptions softened to shadows.

This cemetery served Prague's Jewish community for over three hundred years, from the early fifteen hundreds until 1787. Jewish law forbids disturbing the dead, so when the cemetery filled, the community could not expand outward — the Christian city hemmed them in on all sides. Instead, they built upward. New soil was brought in, and the dead were buried in layers, one atop another, sometimes as many as twelve bodies deep. The headstones were moved upward with each new layer, creating the dense forest of markers you see today.

The oldest legible stone dates to 1439, marking the grave of the poet Avigdor Kara, who survived the pogrom of 1389 and wrote an elegy for the dead. Most of the prominent markers belong to rabbis and scholars — the cemetery became a map of the community's intellectual life. Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel, known as the Maharal, lies here beneath a stone perpetually covered in small rocks and written prayers. He died in 1609 and remains Prague's most famous rabbi, associated in legend with the creation of the Golem, a clay figure brought to life to protect the ghetto.

Look closely at the carvings on the stones. A pair of hands forming a blessing gesture marks a Cohen, a descendant of the priestly line. A jug or basin indicates a Levite, responsible for ritual washing. Grapes, a lion, a stag, a pine cone — each symbol tells something about the deceased, their name, their trade, their lineage. The stone of Hendl Bassevi, who died in 1628, bears a lion — her husband was the first Jew in Bohemia granted a title of nobility.

The cemetery sits in Josefov, the old Jewish quarter, named after Emperor Joseph the Second who eased restrictions on Prague's Jews in the late seventeen hundreds. Before that, the quarter was walled and gated, its residents subject to curfews and occupational limits. The synagogues that surround this cemetery — the Old-New Synagogue just next door is Europe's oldest active synagogue, dating to the late thirteenth century — formed the heart of one of Europe's most vibrant Jewish communities.

By the mid-nineteenth century, the cemetery was closed and the ghetto walls came down. The Jewish quarter was largely demolished in a sweeping urban renewal project around nineteen hundred, replaced with the Art Nouveau apartment blocks you see in the surrounding streets. The cemetery and six synagogues were preserved, becoming a museum complex. During the Nazi occupation, the community was deported and murdered — of the fifty-five thousand Jews living in Prague before the war, fewer than ten thousand returned.

The Nazis intended this cemetery to become part of a museum of an extinct race. They collected Jewish artifacts from across Bohemia and Moravia, concentrating them here. After the war, the collection remained, and the cemetery became a memorial not just to the individuals buried here but to the entire destroyed world of Central European Jewry.

Stand still for a moment and notice the elder trees growing between the stones, their roots weaving through the layers below. In summer, the shade is dense and cool. In winter, snow settles in the gaps between the markers, softening the crowded angles. The silence here is profound, broken only by the rustle of leaves and the occasional distant tram on Široká Street.

As you leave, glance back at the mass of stone — the sheer density of it, the way the markers seem to hold one another upright. It is a place where physical space was never enough, where a community made room for its dead by building a city of memory in miniature, layer upon patient layer.
