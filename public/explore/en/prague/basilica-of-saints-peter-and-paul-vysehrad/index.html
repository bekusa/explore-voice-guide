The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here on Vyšehrad, the noise of Prague drops away. You hear gravel under your shoes, wind moving through old trees, and now and then the bells above you. Look up at the two dark spires. They rise high over the hill, and on a clear day you can see them from the river far below. This is the Basilica of Saints Peter and Paul, one of the oldest sacred sites in the city.

The hill itself matters as much as the church. Vyšehrad means something like "the upper castle" or "the high fortress" in Czech. It sits on a rock above the Vltava River, on the southern edge of the historic centre. For centuries it was a royal seat and a fortress. Later it became something different — a place of national memory for the Czech people.

The story of this church begins in the eleventh century. Vratislaus the Second, a Czech ruler of the Přemyslid dynasty, founded a church here dedicated to Saint Peter and Saint Paul. The Přemyslids were the first ruling family of the Czech lands. Vratislaus moved his court to Vyšehrad for a time. Some historians see this as a political choice, a way to keep some distance from the powerful bishop at Prague Castle.

The building you see today is not that first church. Over the centuries this site went through many changes. There were fires, wars, and rebuilding in Gothic and later Baroque styles. What stands in front of you now is mostly the result of a large neo-Gothic reconstruction. Neo-Gothic means a nineteenth-century revival of medieval Gothic forms — pointed arches, tall towers, and vertical lines. That work took place around the end of the nineteenth century and the start of the twentieth.

Those twin spires are the clearest sign of that rebuilding. They were completed in the early 1900s. So when you look at them, remember — the hill is ancient, but the towers are younger than many buildings in the Old Town. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The silhouette that now defines Vyšehrad from the river is barely more than a century old.

Now walk toward the main entrance and look above the doors. The carved stone tympanum, the half-moon panel over the portal, shows the Last Judgment. In this scene, the souls of the dead are judged at the end of time. It was a common theme over medieval church doors. It reminded people of their choices as they stepped inside. Take a moment to follow the figures from one side to the other.

Inside, the mood changes completely. Many people expect dark stone and grey shadows. Instead, the walls and pillars are covered in painted decoration. Plants, flowers, and curling lines climb the surfaces in soft greens, reds, and golds. This painting is in the Art Nouveau style, the decorative movement of the years around 1900. It is a second surprise of the basilica. The structure looks back to the Middle Ages, but the colours belong to the early twentieth century.

Walk slowly along the nave, the long central space of the church. Let your eyes move from the floor up to the vaulted ceiling. The painted patterns seem to grow upward like a garden. Notice how the light from the stained glass falls on these colours. The effect is different in the morning and late afternoon. It is a quiet place, so speak softly. This is still an active church, and services take place here.

In 2003, the church received the title of minor basilica from the Pope. This is an honour the Catholic Church gives to churches of special historical or spiritual importance. It does not change the building itself. But it shows how much this place still means, almost a thousand years after the first church rose on this hill.

Vyšehrad is also full of legends, and you should hear them as legends, not history. Locals say that the princess Libuše once stood on this rock. According to the old tale, she looked across the river and foresaw a great city whose glory would reach the stars. That city was Prague. No archive records this moment. But the story shaped how Czechs think about this hill.

Another tale stays close to the church. Not far from here, in the park, lie pieces of an old stone column known as the Devil's Column. Legend has it that a priest made a bet with the devil. The devil had to bring a column from Rome before the priest finished Mass. According to the story, the devil came too late, threw the column down in anger, and it broke into pieces. Look for it as you walk the grounds.

Next to the basilica is the Vyšehrad Cemetery. Many of the most important figures in Czech culture are buried here. They include the composers Antonín Dvořák and Bedřich Smetana, the artist Alfons Mucha, and the writer Karel Čapek, who gave the world the word "robot". Smetana even wrote a piece of music called "Vyšehrad". It opens his famous cycle of symphonic poems about his homeland, "Má vlast", which means "My Country".

The church and cemetery belong together. The cemetery arcades run along the side of the basilica, and the graves carry beautiful sculpture and mosaics. Walking from the church into the cemetery, you move from faith to national memory. For many Czechs, coming here is like visiting the family tree of the nation. People leave flowers and candles on the graves of writers and musicians they love.

Before you leave, step back to a spot where you can see both spires at once. Look at the dark stone against the sky. Listen again to the wind in the trees and the city far below. Then walk to the old walls and look out over the Vltava. The river, the rooftops, and the distant castle lie in front of you. This is the view the legends speak of — and it is waiting for you now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the adjoining Vyšehrad Cemetery right after the church.
Walk the fortress walls for wide views over the Vltava.
Speak quietly inside, as services still take place here.
Come in late afternoon for warmer light on the façade.
Listen to Smetana's "Vyšehrad" before or after your visit.
