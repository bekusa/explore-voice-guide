The guide

The story

Stand at the bottom of the steps for a moment. Behind you, trams grind and clatter along Újezd Street. In front of you, a pale concrete staircase climbs toward the green slope of Petřín Hill. On those steps, bronze men are walking down toward you. Look closely at the nearest figure. Then let your eyes move up the stairs, one figure at a time, and notice that something is wrong with each of them.

This is the Memorial to the Victims of Communism, in the Malá Strana district, the Lesser Town, on the left bank of the Vltava. It was unveiled in 2002, a little more than twelve years after communist rule ended in Czechoslovakia. The memorial does not show leaders or heroes. It shows ordinary men, thin and bare, with no names and no uniforms. That choice is deliberate. The monument is meant for everyone whose life was damaged by the regime.

The sculptor was Olbram Zoubek, one of the best-known Czech sculptors of the twentieth century. He worked on the site with two architects, Jan Kerel and Zdeněk Hölzel. Zoubek had a personal link to this history. In January 1969, a student named Jan Palach set himself on fire in Wenceslas Square to protest the Soviet-led occupation of the country. Zoubek made Palach's death mask. For years after that, the authorities kept the sculptor out of official artistic life.

Now look at the figures again. The man closest to the street is almost whole. As you climb, the bodies break apart. One figure is missing an arm. Another has lost part of a leg. Further up, the bronze opens into gaps and splits, as if the body is coming apart from the inside. The highest figures are the most broken — some are hardly more than fragments. The idea is simple and hard to forget. The system did not always kill people. Often it wore them down, piece by piece.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The figures are not climbing up. They are coming down, toward the street and toward you. Some people read this as the victims returning to the living city. Others see men walking out of the hill, out of the past, so that we cannot ignore them. The sculptor left the meaning open. Stand halfway up and look back at the street, and the effect changes again.

Between the figures, a narrow bronze strip runs along the centre of the steps. Bend down and read it. It carries numbers, not words of comfort. More than 205,000 people were arrested. Around 170,000 left the country into exile. 248 people were executed. About 4,500 died in prisons. 327 were shot while trying to cross the border to the West. Each figure on the stairs stands for many thousands of real lives.

To understand those numbers, it helps to know the timeline. The Communist Party took full power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948. The early 1950s brought show trials, labour camps and uranium mines where political prisoners worked in brutal conditions. In 1968, a period of reform called the Prague Spring tried to create a more open socialism. Warsaw Pact tanks ended it in August of that year. Then came two decades of quiet pressure, surveillance and fear.

That later period was less violent, but it shaped daily life in deep ways. A wrong word at work could end a career. Children of known critics could lose their place at university. Neighbours sometimes reported on neighbours. Many people chose silence simply to protect their families. A plaque at the site makes this point directly. It says the memorial honours not only those who were jailed or executed, but also those whose lives were ruined in quieter ways.

The regime finally fell in November 1989, in what became known as the Velvet Revolution. It was called velvet because it happened almost without violence. Students marched, crowds filled the squares, and people rattled their keys in the cold air as a signal that it was time for the rulers to leave. Within weeks, the playwright and dissident Václav Havel became president. This memorial came years later, as the country slowly worked out how to remember.

Notice the setting itself. The steps cut into the base of Petřín Hill, with trees and rough stone walls above. The memorial is not in a grand square or behind a gate. It sits right beside a tram stop, a few steps from cafés and the lower station of the funicular railway that climbs the hill. Locals walk past it on their way to work. That ordinary location is part of its power. The past is placed directly inside everyday life.

The light here changes the work. In the morning, the hill can leave the steps in soft shade, and the bronze looks dark and heavy. In the afternoon, sunlight falls across the figures and makes the gaps in their bodies easier to read. After dark, small lights set into the steps light the strip of numbers from below. At night the figures look thinner, almost like shadows, and the street noise seems further away.

If you visit with others, you may notice that people go quiet here. Some leave flowers or candles at the base of the steps. Older Czech visitors sometimes stop for a long time in front of one figure. For them, this is not abstract history. It is their parents, their teachers, or their own younger selves. Give the space a little respect. Speak softly, and let others take their moment too.

Before you leave, walk back down to the street and turn around once more. From here, all the figures line up on the stairs, the whole one at the front and the broken ones behind. The trams keep running. People keep walking past with shopping bags and phones. That is exactly the point the memorial makes — life goes on beside it. As you head toward the river or up the hill, take one of those numbers with you, and think of one name you will never know.

Before you go

Practical tips

Read the bronze number strip from the bottom upward.
Return after dark to see the steps lit from below.
Use the Újezd tram stop at the foot of the memorial.
Combine with the funicular ride up nearby Petřín Hill.
Lower your voice; locals often come here to remember.
