The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The tram bells from the river fade behind you, and the street noise softens. Look up. Above an old Gothic church, two white concrete curves rise into the sky and cross each other like a pair of wings. Most visitors stop right here, a little puzzled. Is this medieval or modern? The honest answer is both. This place, the Emmaus Monastery, carries more than six centuries of Prague's story in one view.

The monastery stands in the New Town, a short walk south of Charles Square. The New Town was planned in the fourteenth century as a bold expansion of the city. Its founder was Charles the Fourth, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor. He was the ruler who gave Prague its university, its great stone bridge, and much of its medieval shape. In 1347 he founded this monastery. He had a very particular purpose in mind.

Here is the first surprise. Charles did not invite ordinary Latin-speaking monks. He brought Benedictine monks from Croatia who celebrated the liturgy in Old Church Slavonic. That is the old written language of the Slavic church, the language of Saints Cyril and Methodius. These monks wrote in Glagolitic, an early Slavic alphabet with rounded, almost geometric letters. For this reason, Czechs often call the place Na Slovanech, which means roughly "At the Slavs".

Why would an emperor do this? Historians usually see a mix of faith and politics. Charles wanted to honour the Slavic heritage of the Czech lands. He also wanted a bridge between the Latin West and the Slavic East. The monastery became a centre of learning and copying. Manuscripts written here travelled far beyond Prague. One of them later reached France, where French kings are said to have taken oaths on it during their coronation ceremonies.

The church was consecrated in 1372, on Easter Monday. On that day, the Gospel reading tells the story of Emmaus. Two travellers walk with a stranger, and only later do they recognise him as the risen Christ. The monastery took its name from that reading. It is a quiet, fitting story for a place built to connect people of different languages. Keep that image of travellers on a road in mind as you explore.

In the fifteenth century, Bohemia was shaken by the Hussite wars. The Hussites were religious reformers who followed the teaching of the preacher Jan Hus. Many monasteries across the country were attacked and burned. Emmaus was a rare exception. Its monks joined the reform side, and the community survived. In the centuries that followed, ownership changed several times. Spanish Benedictines took over in the seventeenth century, and the church was given Baroque details.

In the late nineteenth century, a new chapter began. Benedictine monks arrived from the Abbey of Beuron in southern Germany. They brought their own artistic style, known as the Beuron school. It is calm, symmetrical, and almost Egyptian in its flat figures and soft colours. The monks repainted parts of the interior and restored the buildings in a neo-Gothic spirit. If you look closely inside, you may still find traces of their careful, stylised work.

Now comes the darkest moment. On 14 February 1945, near the end of the Second World War, American bombers struck Prague. The crews believed they were over Dresden in Germany, which lies not far to the north. Bombs fell across this part of the New Town. The monastery was badly hit. Its roof and towers were destroyed, and fire damaged the church and the cloister. Prague was otherwise spared heavy bombing, so this event still feels shocking to many locals.

For years after the war, the church stood in a damaged state. The question was how to rebuild it. One option was to copy the old Gothic towers exactly. Instead, a bolder idea won. The architect František Maria Černý designed the two concrete shells you see above you. They were completed in the 1960s. Each curve leans toward the other, and their tips meet high above the roof. The result is light, sharp, and very modern for its time.

People still argue about those spires. Some find them strange against the Gothic stone. Others see them as one of the most honest repairs in the city. The new roof does not pretend that the war never happened. It shows that the building was wounded and then given a new life. Stand back and compare the rough old walls with the smooth white concrete. The two ages sit side by side, clearly separate.

Inside the cloister, look for the medieval wall paintings. They date from the fourteenth century and are among the most important Gothic murals in Central Europe. The paintings pair scenes from the Old Testament with scenes from the New Testament. Each pair shows how an older story was read as a promise of a later one. Many sections are faded or missing after centuries of damage. Even so, faces, robes, and gestures still appear clearly in places.

The second surprise is how quiet the monastery feels. During the communist period, the monks could not live here freely. Various institutions used the buildings instead. After 1989, when the political system changed, Benedictines were able to return. Today it is again a working religious community, alongside cultural use of the space. Speak softly in the church and the cloister. You may hear only footsteps on stone and the distant hum of the city beyond the walls.

When you leave, walk slowly along the street and look back once more. From the river side, the white spires catch the light and stand out above the rooftops of the New Town. Think of the Croatian monks with their Glagolitic letters, the reformers, the Spanish and German brothers, and the night of the bombs. All of them shaped what stands in front of you now. Like the travellers to Emmaus, you may only understand the place fully once you have walked on.
