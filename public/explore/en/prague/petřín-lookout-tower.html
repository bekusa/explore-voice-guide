The guide

The story

The path up the hill smells of cut grass and old trees. Wind moves through the branches above you. Ahead, a tower of iron rises over the green slope, its open frame letting the sky show through. This is the Petřín Lookout Tower. From the foot of it, you tilt your head back and watch the metal climb toward the clouds. The closer you stand, the larger it grows.

The tower stands on Petřín hill, one of the highest points in the city. The hill rises more than one hundred meters above the river below. Because the tower sits so high, its top reaches almost the same height above the sea as the Eiffel Tower in Paris. Visitors often notice the family resemblance at once, and they are right to. The likeness was planned from the start.

That resemblance is not an accident. Members of a Czech hiking club traveled to Paris and saw the Eiffel Tower at the world fair of 1889. They came home wanting something of their own. Two years later, in 1891, they raised this tower for a large exhibition in Prague. The work took only a few months from start to finish. For its time, that was a remarkable speed.

The tower stands about sixty-three meters tall. It is far smaller than its cousin in Paris, but the hill makes up the difference in height. Seen from the streets far below, the iron frame looks light, almost like lace against the sky. Up close, though, the bolts and beams feel heavy and firm under your hand. The whole frame seems to hum faintly in the wind.

To reach the top, you can climb the stairs. There are two hundred ninety-nine of them, winding up in a tight spiral. The metal rings softly under your feet as you rise. Each turn of the staircase opens a fresh slice of the view through the frame. Those who would rather not climb can take a small lift inside the central shaft instead.

At the top, the city opens in every direction. Red roofs cover the old streets in a warm blanket. The river bends through the center in a wide, slow curve of silver, crossed by a line of old bridges. On a clear day the view reaches far past the rooftops to the green hills beyond. The wind is stronger up here, and it pulls at your coat.

Getting to the foot of the tower is part of the pleasure. A funicular railway carries visitors up the steep slope in small cars. They climb slowly through the gardens, clicking over the rails. You can also walk up the winding paths instead, passing orchards that turn white and pink with blossom in the spring. The climb on foot is gentle and shaded.

The hill itself has a long story. For hundreds of years people have come here to gather, to walk, and to rest among the trees. Vineyards once covered these slopes and gave the city its wine. Today orchards grow in their place, and in spring whole families climb the hill simply to stand among the flowering branches. The scent of blossom fills the air.

The slopes around the tower are full of gardens. In early summer a rose garden opens near the top, and the air turns sweet with its scent. Couples walk the paths slowly, in no hurry at all. Children run ahead toward the tower, eager to start counting the steps. Benches sit in the shade for those who only want to rest and watch the city.

Near the tower stand other small wonders. One building, shaped like a little castle gate, holds a maze of mirrors that turns your reflection strange and tall and wide. Nearby, an old observatory lets visitors look through its telescopes at the sun and the stars. Together they make the hilltop feel like a place built for curiosity and quiet play.

The tower has watched over the city for more than a hundred years. It stood through storms and through the rise and fall of empires. For part of its life it carried radio and television signals out across the rooftops to homes far away. Now it serves mainly as a place to climb, to look, and to wonder at the wide view below.

Many people climb the tower near the end of the day. As the sun drops, the light turns the red roofs to gold and warm copper. The river catches the glow for a few minutes and seems to burn. Then the streetlamps blink on, one by one, across the darkening city. Watching the day close from up here is a quiet joy.

Climbing back down, the trees close in around you again. The city slowly returns to its normal size, street by street, until it is just the ground beneath your feet. But the memory of that high, open view stays with you. It is a quiet reminder that a short climb, on the right hill, can change the way you see a place.
