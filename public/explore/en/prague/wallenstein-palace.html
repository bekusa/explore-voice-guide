The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere behind the clipped hedges a peacock lets out a sharp, almost human cry. The air smells of cut grass and damp stone. Above the garden wall, the spires of Prague Castle rise against the sky. You are standing in one of the quietest places in the Lesser Town. Yet it was built by one of the loudest, most feared men in seventeenth-century Europe.

That man was Albrecht von Wallenstein. He was a Bohemian nobleman who became supreme commander of the Habsburg imperial armies during the Thirty Years' War. That war was a long and brutal conflict that tore Central Europe apart between 1618 and 1648. Wallenstein grew enormously rich during it. He gained land, titles, and power on a scale few subjects of the emperor had ever held. This palace was his statement to the city, and to the emperor himself.

He built it in the 1620s, and he built it fast. To clear space, dozens of existing houses, gardens, and workshops on this slope below the castle were bought and pulled down. Think about that as you look around. The calm, open lawns in front of you were once a crowded neighbourhood of ordinary homes. One man's ambition turned them into a private world of fountains, statues, and long avenues.

Wallenstein hired Italian architects and artists, which was the fashion of the time for anyone who wanted to look powerful. The result is one of the first large early Baroque palaces in Prague. Baroque is the dramatic style of the seventeenth century, full of movement, scale, and theatre. Here it is still young and a little restrained. You can see Renaissance order in the straight lines of the garden, with Baroque ambition pushing at its edges.

Now turn toward the great open loggia at the end of the garden. This is the sala terrena — a garden hall, open on one side, built so that the house could breathe into the landscape. Its three tall arches frame the space like a stage. Step inside if you can and look up. The vaulted ceiling is covered with painted scenes and stucco work. On a summer evening, the shade here feels cool after the heat of the gravel paths.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. The bronze statues lining the main avenue are not the originals. The first set was made by Adriaen de Vries, a Dutch sculptor who was one of the finest bronze casters in Europe. In 1648, at the very end of the war, Swedish troops plundered Prague. They carried the statues away as war loot. The originals still stand in Sweden today. What you see here are careful copies, placed back where the originals once stood.

Walk along the side of the garden and you will reach a long, strange wall. It looks as if the stone has melted and dripped. Grey, lumpy forms hang down like stalactites in a cave. This is the dripstone wall, sometimes called the grotesque wall. It was designed to feel wild and mysterious, like a grotto in a garden of ancient Rome. Stand close and let your eyes adjust to the shapes.

This is the second surprise. Locals say that if you look long enough, faces and creatures appear in the stone — a snake, a frog, a grimacing head, small animals hidden in the folds. Some people swear they find dozens. Whether every figure was intended, or whether our eyes simply want to see them, is part of the fun. Families often turn it into a quiet game, pointing and whispering as each new shape appears.

Not far from the wall, there is a small aviary where owls sit very still behind the mesh. Peacocks walk freely across the lawns, and you may spot a white one among them. They are not shy. They wander between visitors, drag their long tails over the gravel, and sometimes open their feathers without warning. Their sudden calls echo off the walls, and they remind you that this garden was built for display.

In the middle of the garden, a rectangular pond holds still green water. A statue of Hercules stands at its centre. In the old stories, Hercules was the strongest of heroes, a figure that rulers loved to compare themselves with. Wallenstein went further inside the palace. In the main hall, a ceiling painting shows him as Mars, the Roman god of war, riding in a chariot. Few men would dare to paint themselves as a god. He did.

That pride did not end well. The emperor began to fear that his general had grown too powerful and too independent. In 1634, Wallenstein was murdered in the town of Cheb, in western Bohemia, by officers acting on the emperor's wishes. He spent very little time in this palace. He built a paradise and barely lived to enjoy it. Every statue and fountain here is, in a way, a monument to a life that ended suddenly.

Today the palace has a very different role. It is the seat of the Senate of the Czech Republic, the upper house of the country's parliament. So the rooms built for one man's glory now host public debate and lawmaking. Some interior rooms open to visitors on certain days. The former riding school, at the edge of the complex, is used for art exhibitions. The garden itself stays a place for slow walks, reading, and rest.

Before you leave, find a spot on the main avenue and look back toward the sala terrena. Then lift your eyes to the castle above the wall. The view joins two kinds of power, the royal and the military, in one frame. Listen again for the gravel, the fountain, the peacocks. When you step back into the narrow streets of the Lesser Town, notice how quickly the noise returns. Behind you, the garden stays exactly as quiet as Wallenstein's money made it.
