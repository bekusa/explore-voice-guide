The guide

The story

Listen first. In front of the palace, a bronze fountain drips water onto metal. The drops fall from one bowl onto another, and the bronze rings softly, like a faint, uneven bell. Around you, gravel crunches under shoes, and the clipped hedges of the Royal Garden smell green and slightly sweet. Ahead stands a long, pale building with slender columns and a curved green roof. This is Queen Anne's Summer Palace, which many people in Prague simply call the Belvedere.

The palace sits at the far eastern end of the Royal Garden, on the north side of Prague Castle. The garden itself was laid out in the 1530s, under King Ferdinand the First of the Habsburg family. At that time, a garden like this was a statement. It showed that the new ruler of Bohemia knew the latest fashions from Italy, with exotic plants, ordered paths, and places built purely for pleasure, not for defence or government.

Ferdinand dedicated this summer palace to his wife, Anne of Bohemia and Hungary. Their marriage was a political alliance, but by many accounts it was also a close and affectionate one. They had a large family together. Construction began in 1538. The building was meant as a place for leisure — for walks, music, conversation, and looking out over the garden. It was a retreat only a few minutes on foot from the business of the castle.

Here is the sad part of the story. Anne never saw the palace finished. She died in 1547, while work was still going on. Building took decades, slowed by money problems, a serious fire at the castle, and changes of architect. So the palace that carries her name became, in a sense, a memorial. Ferdinand continued the project after her death, and the building was finally completed in the 1560s.

Now look closely at the arcade, the open gallery of columns that runs around the whole ground floor. The first design came from an Italian architect, Paolo della Stella, and his team of stonemasons. They carved the slender columns, the light arches, and a long series of relief panels. Architectural historians often describe this building as one of the purest examples of Italian Renaissance architecture north of the Alps. In other words, a piece of early sixteenth-century Italy was built here, in the heart of Bohemia.

Walk slowly along the arcade and look at the carved panels between the arches. They show scenes from ancient mythology, battles, hunting, and figures from classical stories. These carvings reward patience. The details are small, and the stone is weathered in places. One relief is often pointed out by guides: it shows Ferdinand offering a flower to Anne. It is a gentle, personal image on a building full of heroes and gods. Try to find it as you walk.

After Paolo della Stella, the work passed to another architect, Bonifaz Wolmut. He designed the upper floor and the roof. And the roof is the part most visitors remember. It is covered in green copper, and its curved shape looks like the hull of a ship turned upside down. Some people say it looks like a giant upturned boat resting on top of a Renaissance villa. In the 1500s, no other building in the city had a roof quite like it.

The upper floor once held a large hall for festivities, with tall windows looking out on all sides. Over the centuries, the building changed roles many times. Under the Emperor Rudolf the Second, who made Prague the capital of his empire around 1600, the palace has long been linked with astronomy. His court attracted famous scientists, and the high, open position of the Belvedere made it a natural place to watch the sky. Later centuries brought far less elegant uses, including military ones.

In the nineteenth century, people began to value the building again as a historic monument. It was restored, and the upper hall was decorated with large paintings of scenes from Czech history. Today the palace is mainly used for exhibitions, so what you find inside depends on when you visit. Some days the doors are open. On other days you can only enjoy the outside. Either way, the arcade and the garden around it are the heart of the experience.

Now come back to the fountain in front of the palace. It is known as the Singing Fountain. It was designed in the mid sixteenth century and cast in bronze by a master founder named Tomáš Jaroš. Here is the surprise that many visitors miss. The singing is real. Water falls from the upper bowl onto the lower bronze basin, and the metal produces a soft ringing sound. Crouch down beneath the lower bowl, close to the rim, and you will hear it clearly.

Turn around and look back towards the west. From the edge of the garden you can see the long silhouette of Prague Castle and the dark Gothic towers of Saint Vitus Cathedral rising above the trees. Between the garden and the castle lies a deep, green ravine called the Stag Moat. In Ferdinand's time, this was a hunting ground. The view shows two worlds side by side: the heavy stone of royal power, and the light, open lines of a pleasure palace.

As you walk back through the Royal Garden, notice how the paths are laid out in straight, calm lines. Look for the long building with decorated walls further along the garden. This is the Ball Game Hall, built in the same century for a sport similar to early tennis. Spring brings flowerbeds full of colour, and summer brings deep shade under the old trees. In winter, the bare garden makes the palace's columns and copper roof stand out even more sharply.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the corner of the arcade. Look along the line of columns and watch how the light falls between them, making a rhythm of bright stone and shadow. Listen once more for the bronze notes of the fountain behind you. Nearly five hundred years ago, a king built this place for a queen who never lived to walk here. The gravel under your feet, the hedges, and that quiet ringing water are still here for everyone else.

Before you go

Practical tips

Crouch beneath the fountain's lower bowl to hear it ring.
Check exhibition schedules before visiting; the interior opens irregularly.
Walk the full arcade slowly to study the carved reliefs.
Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the columns.
Combine with a walk through the Royal Garden and Ball Game Hall.
