The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go in. Outside, the lane is loud with rolling suitcases, café chatter, and tour guides calling out in a dozen languages. Then the door closes behind you, and the noise drops away. The air feels cooler. The light is low and warm. You are a few steps from Old Town Square, yet the building around you feels quiet and private. That shift in sound is the first thing this place gives you.

You are standing in the Sex Machines Museum, on Melantrichova, the narrow lane that links Old Town Square with the old market streets to the south. It is an unusual neighbour for medieval cellars and Gothic towers. The museum opened in the early 2000s. Its focus is narrow and clear. It does not try to cover the whole history of love or desire. It collects machines, devices, and contraptions built for intimacy, and it shows them as objects of design and engineering.

A word before we go further. This museum is for adults only, and some displays are explicit. This guide stays thoughtful rather than graphic. Think of the visit as a walk through a cabinet of curiosities. Many objects here say less about romance and more about invention — and about how people in different centuries tried to control, measure, or improve the human body.

The collection spreads across several floors. As you climb, look at the building itself. Prague's Old Town is layered. Many houses on these lanes have medieval foundations, later Baroque fronts, and modern interiors added on top. Here, dark walls and spotlights pull your eye to each object, one at a time. The result feels closer to a jewellery shop than to a museum hall. Every device sits under its own small pool of light.

The first surprise is how mechanical everything is. Before electricity, an inventor had springs, levers, pulleys, leather straps, carved wood, and brass. Many pieces here look like they belong in a clockmaker's workshop. Watch for gears, hand cranks, and pedals. Some contraptions look almost like furniture — chairs, frames, and benches with odd attachments. Without the labels, you might guess they were tools for a dentist or a tailor.

That link to medicine is real, and it is worth a pause. In the nineteenth century, the line between medical device and private gadget was often blurry. Doctors and inventors sold machines that promised health, calm nerves, or better circulation. Some of the most famous stories from this period are now debated by historians. So read the labels with a curious mind. Treat the bold claims of old advertisements as claims, not as settled fact.

The same caution applies to one of the most talked-about objects here — the chastity belt. Many people picture knights riding off to war and locking their wives away. Legend has it that this was common in the Middle Ages. Many historians now doubt it. They suggest that many so-called medieval belts were made much later, as curiosities or jokes. So when you stand in front of one, you may be looking at a myth made of metal, as much as a piece of history.

That is the second surprise of this collection. Many objects here show how stories travel. A device appears. Someone attaches a dramatic tale. Collectors repeat the tale. Over time, the story becomes harder to separate from the object. This museum is a good place to practise a simple habit. Look closely at the material, the wear, and the construction. Then ask yourself what is shown and what is only told.

Somewhere in the building you will also find a small screening room. It shows early erotic films from the first decades of cinema. The flicker of black-and-white images matters here. These short films are historical documents. They show the clothes, the rooms, and the camera tricks of their time. Notice how slowly the scenes move and how theatrical the gestures are. Film was new, and everyone, in front of the camera and behind it, was still learning the language.

As you walk on, notice the mood of the other visitors. Few museums produce so many nervous laughs. Couples nudge each other. Groups of friends read labels out loud. Solo visitors move quietly from case to case. That mix of embarrassment and curiosity is part of the experience. Humour is often how people approach a subject they rarely discuss in public. Here, the laughter makes the subject easier to look at.

It also helps to think about why a museum like this sits in Prague. The city has a long tradition of collectors, alchemists, and makers of strange mechanisms. In the late sixteenth century, the court of Emperor Rudolf the Second gathered clocks, automata, and wonders from across Europe. Automata are moving mechanical figures. A few minutes' walk from here, the astronomical clock still performs its mechanical show every hour. This museum belongs, in its own cheeky way, to that same love of clever machinery.

Before you leave, go back to one object that puzzled you. Look at it again for a full minute. Look at the joints, the stitching, and the screws. Think about the person who designed it, and the person who first used it. Behind each curious machine stands a real human worry. It might be loneliness, shame, health, or simple boredom. The machines are strange, but the needs behind them are familiar.

Now step back out onto Melantrichova. The noise returns at once — footsteps on cobblestones, the smell of roasting sugar from street stalls, bells somewhere above the rooftops. Turn north, and the lane opens towards Old Town Square. Turn south, and it leads to the market stalls. As you walk, you may notice how many doors on this street hide a different world behind them. This was one of them. Take a breath, find the light, and carry the questions with you.
