The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of the city fades here, behind a long wall the colour of warm butter. Gravel shifts under your feet. Somewhere in the garden, water runs. And above it all rises a church front of pale stone and curved lines, calm and quiet. You are in Břevnov, a district on the western side of Prague, beyond the Castle. This is one of the oldest monasteries in the Czech lands. Monks have prayed on this ground for more than a thousand years.

The story begins in 993. That year, Adalbert, the Bishop of Prague, founded a community of Benedictine monks here. Czechs know him as Saint Vojtěch. He worked with the ruling duke of Bohemia, Boleslaus the Second. The Benedictines follow a sixth-century rule of life, built on prayer, study and work with the hands. Břevnov became the first monastery for men in Bohemia. From here, learning and farming knowledge spread across the young Christian country.

There is also a legend about the founding. Locals say the bishop and the duke each had the same dream, and then met by chance at a spring in this valley. According to the tale, a wooden beam lay across the water. In Czech, a beam is a "břevno". People say this gave the place its name. It is folklore, not documented history. But the spring is real. It still flows in the garden, and a small Baroque pavilion now stands over it.

The church in front of you is the Basilica of Saint Margaret. At first glance, it looks like a single elegant building from the eighteenth century. That is true above ground. But below your feet lies something much older. Under the choir, archaeologists uncovered a Romanesque crypt. Romanesque is the heavy, round-arched style of Europe around a thousand years ago. These thick walls and low vaults survive from the early church that stood here before the Baroque one.

This is the first surprise most visitors miss. The graceful church you see is only the newest layer. The old monastery suffered badly in 1420, during the Hussite Wars. The Hussites were religious reformers, and their armies attacked many monasteries across Bohemia. Břevnov was burned and its monks were scattered. For a long time afterwards, the community was small and poor. Recovery took generations, and the real rebirth came only in the Baroque age.

That rebirth arrived in the early eighteenth century. The abbots hired the Dientzenhofer family, a father and son who shaped much of Baroque Prague. Christoph Dientzenhofer and his son Kilian Ignaz rebuilt the church and the monastery buildings. Look at the facade. It does not sit flat. The surfaces bend gently in and out, so the light moves across the stone through the day. This curving style became a signature of Prague's Baroque architecture.

Step inside, if the church is open. The interior has one long nave, without side aisles. The space is shaped by overlapping ovals, so the walls and vaults seem to flow into each other. The colours are soft — white, cream, gold and pale marble. Painted ceilings show the life of Saint Margaret, the patron of the church. Sit for a minute on a wooden bench. The silence here is deep, and every footstep carries.

The monastery building holds another treasure. On a guided tour, you can usually enter the grand ceremonial room called the Theresian Hall. Its ceiling fresco is the work of the Bavarian painter Cosmas Damian Asam. It shows a miracle linked to Blessed Gunther, a monk connected with Břevnov. According to the story, Gunther was offered meat during a fast, and a roasted bird flew away from the plate. Look up and try to find the bird.

Now for the second surprise. In the twentieth century, this place almost lost its monks completely. In 1950, the Communist government shut down monasteries across Czechoslovakia. The Benedictines were forced out. Several were imprisoned. The buildings were taken over by the state and used for offices and archives. For four decades, the Rule of Saint Benedict was not lived here. The church survived, but the community did not.

After the Velvet Revolution of 1989, everything changed. The monks came back and began the long work of repair. In 1993, the monastery celebrated its first thousand years. Pope John Paul the Second came to Břevnov for the anniversary. Today, a Benedictine community lives here again. The monastery is a working religious house, so please respect quiet zones and closed doors. You may see a monk in a black habit crossing the courtyard.

The Benedictine motto is "pray and work". Here, work has always included brewing. Monasteries across Central Europe made beer for centuries, and Břevnov was no exception. In recent years, a monastery brewery has opened again inside the complex. You can taste beer brewed on the site of a thousand-year-old tradition. There is also a restaurant in the old buildings. For many locals, this is simply a pleasant place to sit on a summer evening.

Take time for the gardens. They climb gently up the slope behind the monastery. Paths lead past orchards, old walls and quiet lawns. Look for the small pavilion over the spring, the place where the legend begins. From higher up, you can look back at the red roofs and the church tower. The view is calm, and few tourists come this far, so you will often share it only with local families and dog walkers.

Before you leave, stand once more in the main courtyard. Look at the church front, with its soft curves and saints carved in stone. Then think of what lies beneath it — the Romanesque crypt, the old foundations, a thousand years of prayer. Listen again for the water in the garden. It was running here before the first monk arrived. As you walk back to the tram, notice how the noise of the city slowly returns.
