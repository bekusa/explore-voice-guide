The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the traffic of Prague fades into a low hum. Gravel crunches under your shoes. Wind moves through old chestnut trees, and somewhere below, a tram rattles along the river. You are standing on a rocky hill above the Vltava, inside the walls of Vyšehrad. The name means something close to "the high castle". From this height, the city spreads out to the north. Far downstream, on the opposite bank, you can see the outline of Prague Castle. Vyšehrad is its quieter, older-feeling sibling.

Before the history, there is the legend. Czech tradition places one of its founding stories right here. According to the old tale, a wise princess named Libuše lived on this rock. She had the gift of prophecy. Legend has it that she stood on the cliff, looked across the forested valley, and foresaw a great city whose glory would touch the stars. She also chose a husband, a ploughman named Přemysl, and from that marriage came the Přemyslid dynasty — the family that would rule Bohemia for centuries.

Historians treat that story as myth, not record. But the hill itself is real, and it was important. A fortified settlement stood here in the early Middle Ages. In the late eleventh century, Vratislaus the Second, who became the first king of Bohemia, made Vyšehrad his seat. For a time, power in this land lived on this rock, not across the river. Later rulers returned to Prague Castle, and Vyšehrad slowly drifted from the centre of politics into the centre of memory.

Walk toward the small round stone building near the edge of the grounds. This is the Rotunda of Saint Martin. It dates from the Romanesque period — the style of thick walls, small windows, and rounded arches that came before Gothic. The rotunda is usually described as the oldest surviving building of its kind in Prague. It is tiny. A simple cylinder, a small apse, a conical roof, a little lantern on top. After nearly a thousand years, it still stands on its original ground.

Now look at the walls around you. The massive brick ramparts you walked through are much younger than the legends. In the seventeenth century, under Habsburg rule, Vyšehrad became a military fortress. Engineers wrapped the hill in thick Baroque fortifications — angled bastions, deep walls, and heavy gates. Baroque here means the dramatic, ornamental style of that era, even on military buildings. If you came in through the Leopold Gate, notice its carved stone and its formal symmetry. Even a fortress gate was meant to impress.

Here is something many visitors miss. Under your feet, inside those walls, runs a network of brick tunnels called casemates. Soldiers once used them for storage and movement. Today, part of this underground space holds something surprising: several original Baroque statues from Charles Bridge. The figures you see on the bridge today are largely copies. Some of the originals were brought here to protect them from weather and pollution. Guided visits into the casemates run at certain times, so ask at the information point.

The twin dark spires rising above the trees belong to the Basilica of Saint Peter and Saint Paul. A church has stood on this spot since the early days of Vyšehrad. It was rebuilt many times. The form you see today is neo-Gothic, a nineteenth-century revival of medieval Gothic style. The towers were completed around the turn of the twentieth century. Step close to the main doors and look at the carved stone. Inside, the walls are covered in painted floral and geometric patterns in the Art Nouveau style, soft and flowing, full of greens and golds.

Beside the basilica lies the place that makes Vyšehrad so personal for Czechs. The Vyšehrad Cemetery is the resting place of many of the country's writers, composers, painters, and scientists. Walk slowly under the arcades. You will find the grave of the composer Antonín Dvořák. Nearby rests Bedřich Smetana. The writer Karel Čapek is here — the man who gave the world the word "robot". The painter Alfons Mucha, famous for his elegant Art Nouveau posters, lies here too.

At the far end of the cemetery stands the Slavín, a large shared tomb. It was created in the late nineteenth century as a national monument for distinguished Czechs. In those years, the Czech national revival was in full force. People wanted to celebrate their own language and culture inside the Austrian empire. Burying artists and thinkers together, on the hill of Libuše's legend, was a clear statement. This is where the nation keeps its memory. Many graves carry fine sculpture, so take your time.

Smetana deserves one more moment. He wrote a famous cycle of symphonic poems called My Country. The first piece in that cycle carries the name of this hill: Vyšehrad. It opens with the sound of a harp, like a bard recalling old glories. The music then rises and falls, as if telling the story of the castle's greatness and its decline. If you have headphones, this is a good place to listen to it. The composer now lies only a few steps away from the hill he described.

In the gardens, look for a group of large stone statues. They show pairs of figures from Czech legend, including Libuše and Přemysl. They are the work of the sculptor Josef Václav Myslbek. These statues once stood on Palacký Bridge in the city below. The bridge was damaged by bombing in 1945, near the end of the Second World War. Afterwards, the sculptures were moved up here. Now the legendary characters stand on the very hill where their stories are set.

Near the basilica, you may notice three broken stone columns lying together. Locals call this the Devil's Column. According to one popular tale, a priest made a bet with the devil. The devil had to bring a column from a church in Rome before the priest finished Mass. Saint Peter interfered, the devil lost, and in anger he threw the column down here, where it broke. Nobody really knows where the stones came from. That uncertainty is part of the fun.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the ramparts above the river. On the cliff below stands the ruin of an old guard building. Tradition calls it Libuše's Bath, though it was almost certainly part of the fortifications. Lean on the wall and look down. The Vltava bends past the rock. Boats move slowly. Railway tracks and roads run along the bank. To the north, the towers of the Old Town and the castle rise in layers. This is one of the calmest views in Prague.

So take a breath here. Feel the cool stone of the wall under your hands. Behind you are a thousand years of churches, soldiers, kings, and legends. In front of you is a modern city going about its day. As you walk back toward the gates, listen again. The gravel, the trees, the bells of the basilica, if you are lucky. Vyšehrad does not ask you to hurry. It simply lets you stand on the hill where, according to the story, Prague began.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ask at the information point about casemate tour times.
Bring headphones to hear Smetana's Vyšehrad on the ramparts.
Walk the outer ramparts for river views toward the castle.
Wear comfortable shoes for gravel paths and steep slopes.
Visit the cemetery quietly; it is still an active burial ground.
