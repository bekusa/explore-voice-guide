The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Maiselova Street carries the sound of footsteps on cobblestones, tour groups speaking a dozen languages, and the soft clatter of café cups. Then you look up at a pale facade with tall, pointed windows and a crest of small stone pinnacles along the roofline. It looks almost like a small church from a Victorian storybook. But this is the Maisel Synagogue, one of the oldest Jewish prayer houses in Prague, and its story is older than the building in front of you.

You are standing in Josefov, the old Jewish Town of Prague. For centuries, the Jewish community of this city lived inside a tight cluster of streets near the bend of the Vltava River. Several synagogues, a town hall, and the Old Jewish Cemetery survive here today. Together they form the Jewish Museum in Prague. The Maisel Synagogue is one part of that museum, and it holds one of its main exhibitions. So this building is both a monument and a doorway into a much longer history.

The synagogue carries the name of the man who paid for it. Mordechai Maisel was a leader of the Jewish Town in the late sixteenth century. He was also a financier, and he did business with the court of Emperor Rudolf the Second, the Habsburg ruler who made Prague his capital and filled it with artists, astronomers, and alchemists. Maisel became very wealthy. And he spent a great deal of that wealth on the community around him.

Maisel did not only build this synagogue. He also funded the Jewish Town Hall, paved streets, and supported a bathhouse and other community buildings. In the early 1590s, he received permission from the emperor to build a private prayer house of his own. That permission mattered. In this period, Jewish communities needed royal approval for almost everything, from building to trading. The synagogue was completed in 1592, and people remembered it as one of the most splendid buildings in the ghetto.

Here is the first surprise. The building you see today is not the Renaissance synagogue that Maisel knew. In 1689, a great fire swept through the Jewish Town and destroyed a large part of it. The Maisel Synagogue burned too. It was rebuilt afterwards, but in a smaller and simpler form. So the original interior, with all the decoration that impressed people in Maisel's lifetime, survives only in descriptions and memory, not in stone.

The second surprise is the facade itself. Those pointed windows and Gothic-style details are not medieval. They come from a rebuilding at the end of the nineteenth century and the start of the twentieth. At that time, the city cleared most of the old Jewish Town. Crowded lanes and old houses were demolished, and new apartment blocks went up in their place. A few key buildings, including this synagogue, were kept. The Maisel Synagogue was redesigned in the neo-Gothic style, which means a revival of medieval Gothic forms.

Think about what that means as you look along this street. Many of the elegant buildings around you, with their balconies and carved stone, belong to that same period of clearance. The old ghetto was a maze of narrow passages. Today the streets are wide and orderly. The synagogues and the cemetery are among the few places where the older layout of the Jewish Town still shows. This building is a survivor, but it has also been reshaped by every century it passed through.

The twentieth century brought the darkest chapter. During the Nazi occupation of Czech lands in the Second World War, Jews from Bohemia and Moravia were deported, and most were murdered. Their homes and synagogues were emptied. The Maisel Synagogue was used as a warehouse for confiscated Jewish property. Objects from many communities across the region were brought to Prague and stored. It is a painful fact. But it is also part of the reason why so many ritual objects from Bohemia and Moravia survive today.

After the war, those collections became the foundation of the Jewish Museum. Under the communist government, the museum had a difficult existence, and the buildings were not always well cared for. After 1989, the museum returned to the ownership of the Jewish community, and the synagogues were repaired and reopened. Today, inside the Maisel Synagogue, you will find an exhibition about the history of Jews in Bohemia and Moravia, from the early medieval period up to the age of emancipation.

When you step inside, notice how the light changes. The street is bright and noisy. The interior is quieter, with tall windows and a long hall that draws your eye forward. Display cases hold ritual objects in silver and textile. You may see Torah ornaments, which are the crowns, pointers, and shields that decorate the scroll of Jewish law. Take your time with the labels. Each object came from a real family or a real congregation, often from towns that no longer have a Jewish community at all.

The exhibition also tells the story of Maisel himself, and it has a sad ending. Maisel died at the beginning of the seventeenth century. Although he had left instructions for his estate, the imperial treasury seized much of his fortune. So the man who gave so much to his community could not pass his wealth on as he wished. He was buried in the Old Jewish Cemetery, only a short walk from here. Many visitors look for his grave among the crowded stones.

If you want to understand this building fully, see it as one stop on a longer walk. The Pinkas Synagogue, the Klausen Synagogue, the Spanish Synagogue, and the Old Jewish Cemetery are all close by. The Old-New Synagogue, still an active house of prayer, stands just a few minutes away. Each one shows a different moment in the life of this community. Together they show how a small district carried centuries of faith, work, loss, and return.

When you come back out onto Maiselova Street, pause for a moment before you join the flow of people again. Look once more at the pointed windows and the pale stone. The street carries the name of the man who built the first synagogue here, more than four hundred years ago. The building has burned, been rebuilt, been redesigned, and been used for things its founder never imagined. And still it stands here... quietly holding the memory of the people of this town.

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid crowds along Maiselova Street.
Combine your visit with the nearby Old Jewish Cemetery.
