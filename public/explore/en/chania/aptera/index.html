The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here on the plateau, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through dry grass and olive leaves. Cicadas buzz in the warm months. Underfoot, the path is pale earth and loose stone, and the smell of wild thyme rises when the sun is on it. Far below, the water of Souda Bay shines like hammered metal. You are standing on the site of one of the great city-states of western Crete.

This is Aptera. Today it is a quiet archaeological site on a hilltop east of Chania. For more than a thousand years it was a living city with its own laws, its own temples and its own coins. The people of Aptera chose this high ground carefully. From here they could watch every ship that entered the bay, and they could see trouble coming long before it arrived.

The name itself carries a story. In Greek, "aptera" means "wingless". According to an old legend, the Muses and the Sirens met near here for a singing contest. The Muses won. The defeated Sirens tore the feathers from their own wings and fell into the sea. Locals say their white bodies became the small islands you can see in the bay below. It is a myth, of course — but look down at those islets, and the story suddenly feels very close.

The city is old. Scholars often link Aptera to a place name that appears on clay tablets from the Bronze Age palace of Knossos, written in the early Greek script known as Linear B. That connection is debated, so treat it carefully. What is certain is that Aptera grew into a powerful independent city in the centuries after classical Greece, during the period historians call Hellenistic, after the conquests of Alexander the Great.

In those centuries Crete was not one country. It was a patchwork of rival city-states that fought, made treaties, and fought again. Aptera held a strong position among them. It controlled farmland on the plateau and access to the harbour below. Like other proud Cretan cities, it minted its own silver and bronze coins. Some of them show a warrior figure. Coins like these travelled far beyond Crete, carrying the city's name across the Mediterranean.

As you walk, notice the long lines of heavy stone that appear and disappear in the grass. These are parts of the city walls. Huge blocks were fitted together without mortar, held in place by their own weight and careful cutting. The circuit once ran for several kilometres around the plateau. In places the wall still stands well above head height. Run your eyes along the joints and you can see how precisely the masons worked.

Now the surprise that most visitors remember. Beneath your feet, the Romans built enormous underground cisterns to store water. Crete came under Roman rule in the first century before Christ, and Aptera kept prospering. One cistern is shaped like a long letter L. Another is divided into three vaulted halls by rows of arches. Step inside and the temperature drops at once. Sound echoes softly off the walls, and the light falls in pale shafts.

Why so much effort for water? A hilltop city has no river. Rain falls in winter, and summer is long and dry. These cisterns collected and kept enough water to supply public baths, fountains and homes through the hottest months. You can still see the thick waterproof plaster on the walls. In places it carries a faint reddish tint. Engineers today still admire how well these chambers have survived.

Not far away, the Romans also built baths, and the city had a small theatre cut into a natural slope. Archaeologists have excavated the theatre and restored part of its seating. Sit for a moment on the stone rows. Imagine the audience here on a warm evening, with the stage below and the bay beyond. Nearby are the foundations of a small temple, often linked to the goddess Demeter, protector of grain and harvest.

The city did not last forever. In late antiquity, earthquakes and raids from the sea struck Crete hard. Aptera shrank, and in time its people moved away. The great buildings collapsed. Soil and plants slowly covered the stones. For centuries, local farmers grazed animals and planted olives among ruins that few outsiders knew about. Much of the city still lies under the ground, waiting for future excavation.

Life did return in a quieter form. In the middle of the site stands the Monastery of Saint John the Theologian. It belonged to the famous monastery on the island of Patmos, far away in the Aegean. Its plain buildings and courtyard sit right among the ancient ruins. Monks once worked this land. Look closely at the walls and you may spot older cut stones, reused from the Roman and Greek city around it.

At the edge of the plateau there is one more layer of history. In the nineteenth century, during Ottoman rule, the Turks built a small fortress here to watch the bay — the same reason the ancient citizens chose this hill. A larger Ottoman fortress stands lower down, close to the water. Walk to the viewpoint and you will see why. The whole bay opens below you, with the White Mountains rising to the south.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute. Feel the wind and look at the water, the islets and the mountains. Greek walls, Roman cisterns, a Christian monastery and an Ottoman fort all share this one hilltop. Each group came for the same view and the same safety. As you walk back along the path, listen again to the grass and the cicadas. They were here long before the city — and they are still here after it.
