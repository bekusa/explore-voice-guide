The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything clearly, you hear it: a soft, dry clatter, like wooden wind chimes. It comes from thousands of rosaries hanging on thousands of crosses, knocking together in the breeze. The sound never stops. It rises when the wind picks up across the flat fields, and it settles into a murmur on still days. Underfoot, the gravel path crunches. Ahead of you, a low mound looks almost furry from a distance, covered in a dense, uneven forest of wood and metal.

This is the Hill of Crosses, known in Lithuanian as Kryžių kalnas. It sits in open countryside roughly twelve kilometres north of the city of Šiauliai, in northern Lithuania. The hill itself is modest. It is not a mountain, and it is not even very tall. What makes it extraordinary is what people have placed on it. Crosses of every size stand shoulder to shoulder, from tiny ones you could hold in your palm to carved monuments several metres high. Nobody knows the exact count, but estimates run well over a hundred thousand.

The mound is believed to be an old hillfort — a defended earthwork from the time before Christianity reached this region. Lithuania was the last country in Europe to officially convert, in the late fourteenth century. So this ground may have carried meaning for people long before the first cross arrived. Archaeologists have studied the site, but much of its early story remains uncertain. What is clear is that the place you are walking toward has been shaped by many generations of hands.

When did the crosses begin? The most widely told account links them to the nineteenth century, when Lithuania was part of the Russian Empire. After the failed uprisings of 1831 and 1863, many families lost sons and husbands. Bodies were often never returned. Historians generally believe that people began placing crosses here as memorials for those they could not bury. Written mentions of crosses on the hill date from around this period, though the first one may be older.

Local legends offer other origins. According to one popular tale, a father sat by the bed of his gravely ill daughter. In a dream, a figure told him to make a cross and carry it to the hill. He did, and when he returned home, his daughter was well. Another legend says a castle once stood here and sank into the earth. These stories are folklore, not documented history — but they show how deeply people have wanted to explain this place.

Now look closely at the crosses themselves. Many are not simple shapes. Lithuanian cross-crafting is a distinct folk art, with carved sunbursts, small roofs, tiny shrines, and floral patterns. In 2001, UNESCO recognised this tradition of cross-crafting and its symbolism as part of humanity's intangible heritage. So the hill is more than a pile of offerings. It is also an open-air gallery of a craft passed from master to apprentice for centuries.

Watch for one recurring figure. It is a small, seated Christ, resting his head in one hand, looking tired and thoughtful. Lithuanians call him Rūpintojėlis, which roughly means the Worrier, or the One Who Cares. He is one of the most beloved images in Lithuanian folk art. He does not look triumphant. He looks like someone sharing your troubles. Once you notice him, you will start to see him everywhere on the hill.

The hill's most remarkable chapter came in the twentieth century. After the Second World War, Lithuania was absorbed into the Soviet Union. The Soviet state promoted atheism, and the hill became a quiet symbol of faith and national identity. Authorities cleared it several times. Crosses were removed, burned, or flattened by bulldozers. Some accounts say officials even considered flooding the area. Each time, the hill was stripped bare.

And each time, the crosses came back. People slipped in at night, often at real personal risk, and planted new ones. Sometimes the hill was full again within weeks. This quiet, stubborn rebuilding became a form of peaceful resistance. Nobody marched. Nobody gave speeches. People simply kept returning with a piece of wood and a prayer. When you stand here, you are standing on ground that refused to stay empty.

Lithuania restored its independence in 1990. A few years later, in September 1993, Pope John Paul the Second visited the hill and celebrated Mass here. It was a powerful moment for a nation emerging from decades of Soviet rule. He later gave a crucifix to the site. Not long after, a Franciscan hermitage was built close by. As you look around, you may spot its simple modern building, set a short walk away across the fields.

Today, people of every background come here. Some are devout Catholics. Some are tourists who leave a cross as a souvenir of their journey. Others come to remember someone they have lost. You will see crosses carrying names, dates, photographs, and messages in many languages. There are wedding mementos, children's drawings, and military tags. Each object is a private story placed in a very public space.

As you walk the narrow paths that wind across the hill, take your time. The crosses press in on both sides, and the ground is uneven. Notice how the older wooden crosses have weathered to silver grey, and how moss grows on their bases. Notice the bright newer ones beside them. Notice the layers: crosses hung on crosses, rosaries hung on those. The hill is never finished. It changes a little every single day.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and turn back toward the mound. Let the sound reach you again — that wooden, rattling hum moving across the open fields. Behind each tiny cross is a person who walked this same gravel path, carrying something they wanted to set down. As you walk back to the road, listen to how the clatter slowly fades... until only the wind and the fields remain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a small cross from home to leave yourself.
Wear sturdy shoes for the narrow, uneven paths.
Visit early morning for quieter, more reflective walks.
Walk across to the nearby Franciscan hermitage afterward.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed open fields.
