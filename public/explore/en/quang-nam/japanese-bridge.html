The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, the boards are worn smooth and slightly uneven, polished by centuries of sandals, bare feet and bicycle tyres. Above you, a roof of curved clay tiles holds back the heat. Below, a narrow creek slides toward the river. Shopkeepers call out on either bank, and the air carries incense from somewhere close by. You are standing on the Japanese Covered Bridge in Hội An, a short wooden crossing that has become the emblem of this whole old town.

The bridge is small. You can walk from one end to the other in less than a minute. That is part of its charm, and part of its surprise. Many visitors arrive expecting something grand, and instead they find a modest covered walkway with a tiny temple tucked into one side. Yet this structure links two neighbourhoods, two communities and several centuries of trade. Take a moment to look along its length before you move on.

To understand why a Japanese bridge stands in central Vietnam, picture Hội An around the late sixteenth century. The town was a busy port on the Thu Bồn River. Merchant ships from Japan, China, and later Europe arrived with the seasonal winds. They stayed for months, waiting for the winds to turn. Japanese traders settled on one side of this creek. Chinese merchants lived on the other. Tradition holds that the Japanese community built this bridge to connect the two quarters.

The most commonly cited date is the 1590s, though that comes from local tradition rather than a surviving building record. What is certain is that the bridge is old, and that it has been repaired many times. Wood does not last forever in a hot, wet climate with yearly floods. Each generation replaced beams, tiles and posts. So the bridge you see today carries the shape of the original idea, while most of its individual pieces are much younger.

Now look at the two ends of the bridge. At one end, you will find a pair of statues of monkeys. At the other end, a pair of dogs. They are easy to miss, so search for them. Locals say construction began in a year of the monkey and finished in a year of the dog. In the East Asian zodiac, each year belongs to an animal. If the story is true, the work took about two years.

There is a stranger legend too, and it is clearly folklore. According to one popular tale, a giant underground monster lay beneath the region. Its head rested in India, its tail in Japan, and its back lay under Vietnam. When it moved, the land shook and floods came. The bridge, people say, was built like a sword driven into the creature's back, to pin it down and keep the town safe. It is a story, not history. But it shows how deeply people trusted this crossing.

Halfway across, on the north side, step into the small temple. This is why the Vietnamese name for the bridge is Chùa Cầu, which means Bridge Pagoda. The temple was added after the bridge itself. It honours Trấn Võ Bắc Đế, a Taoist guardian deity of the north. Taoism is a Chinese tradition of spiritual practice, and this figure is linked to weather and protection. Sailors and merchants who depended on winds and calm water had good reason to ask for his help.

Above the temple doorway, look for a carved wooden plaque with Chinese characters. It gives the bridge a formal name: Lai Viễn Kiều. That roughly means the bridge for travellers from afar. The name was given in 1719 by Nguyễn Phúc Chu, one of the Nguyễn lords who ruled this part of Vietnam. It is a fitting name for a port town. Here is a quiet surprise: the bridge is called Japanese, yet its official name, its temple and many of its decorations follow Chinese and Vietnamese style.

That mix is the real story of this place. Over the centuries, the Japanese community in Hội An declined. Japan closed itself to most foreign trade in the seventeenth century, and its merchants could no longer sail freely. The Chinese community grew instead, and so did Vietnamese influence. Repairs gradually changed the look of the bridge. During the French colonial period, the roadway was flattened so vehicles could cross. Later restorers brought back its gentle arched shape.

Here is another detail many visitors miss. Pull a Vietnamese 20,000 dong banknote from your wallet, if you have one. Turn it over. The building printed on it is this bridge. Millions of people across the country carry its image every day. For a structure you can cross in a few dozen steps, that is a remarkable reach. Try holding the note up against the real bridge for a photo.

In 1999, UNESCO, the United Nations cultural agency, added the Hội An Ancient Town to its World Heritage list. The bridge sits at the centre of that protected area. In recent years, it went through a major restoration to deal with decay and leaning timbers. Some visitors noticed that the bridge suddenly looked brighter and newer, and this caused debate. It is a real question for any old building: how much should you repair, and how much should you leave as it is?

The best way to enjoy the bridge is to see it twice. Come early in the morning, when the street is quiet and the light is soft on the yellow walls nearby. Then return after dark. In the evening, silk lanterns glow along the riverfront, and their reflections move on the water. Small boats drift past. The bridge is lit warmly, and the crowds feel less hurried. Stand on one of the banks rather than on the bridge itself for the clearest view.

Before you walk away, pause on the bridge one last time. Rest your hand on the wooden rail. Listen to the voices on both sides, speaking Vietnamese, English, Korean, Japanese and more. Four hundred years ago, merchants from distant ports crossed here too, carrying silk, ceramics and spices. The trade has changed, but the purpose has not. This is still a place where travellers from afar meet. Now, step off the boards, and notice how the town opens up around you.
