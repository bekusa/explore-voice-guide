The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under your feet, the ground crunches like broken pottery. The air smells faintly of sulphur and warm stone. All around you, the land rises in shades of rust, charcoal, ochre and deep red. There are no trees and almost no sound. Only wind moving across the cinders. This is Timanfaya, the Fire Mountains of Lanzarote.

First, a quick note on where you are. Las Palmas is the name of the province that includes the eastern Canary Islands. Its capital city sits on Gran Canaria. But Timanfaya lies on Lanzarote, the island closest to the coast of Africa. Here, in the southwest of the island, the land looks very new. And in geological terms, it is.

In 1730, the earth here split open. The eruptions began that year and continued, on and off, until 1736. That is about six years of fire. Dozens of new cones grew out of the fields. Lava flowed across farmland and covered villages. Ash fell over much of the island. It is one of the longest volcanic eruptions in recorded history, and the people of Lanzarote lived through every month of it.

We know about this because one man wrote it down. The parish priest of Yaiza, a village just to the south, kept a diary of the early eruptions. He described the night the ground opened, the burning rivers of rock, and families leaving their homes. His account is still one of the main written sources for the disaster. Many families eventually left the island altogether. The land they left behind is the land you see now.

The volcanoes were not finished. In 1824, a smaller eruption broke out in the same region. It lasted only a few months, but it added more fresh lava to the landscape. Since then, the volcanoes have stayed quiet on the surface. Underneath, though, the heat never left. That heat is the first surprise of Timanfaya, and you can feel it with your own hands.

At the visitor area on the Islote de Hilario, the staff give small demonstrations. They push dry brushwood into a shallow pit in the ground. Within moments, it smokes and catches fire. No match is used. Then they pour a bucket of water into a pipe set in the rock. A second later, a column of steam shoots out with a loud roar. The temperature only a few metres down is several hundred degrees.

The name Islote de Hilario comes from a local story. Legend has it that a hermit called Hilario lived on this hill with his camel, long after the eruptions. Locals say he planted a fig tree here. According to the tale, the tree flowered but never gave fruit, because its roots reached down towards the fire. Nobody can prove the story. But standing on warm ground, it is easy to see why people believe it.

Now look at the round building beside you. This is the El Diablo restaurant, designed by César Manrique. Manrique was an artist and architect from Lanzarote. He spent much of his life fighting to protect the island from high-rise tourism. He wanted buildings to sit low and quietly in the landscape. Here, he used stone, glass and simple lines. Inside, the kitchen cooks meat over a grill that sits on a natural vent. The heat comes straight from the volcano.

Manrique also drew the park's symbol: a small, playful devil holding a pitchfork. You will see it on road signs as you enter the park. It is a fitting mascot. For centuries, people on the island linked these smoking mountains with the devil. Manrique took that old fear and turned it into something friendly. That small figure now marks the entrance to one of the strangest landscapes in Europe.

The land here is fragile, so you cannot walk freely across it. Most visitors see the heart of the park from a coach on the Route of the Volcanoes. The road was built to follow the lava fields closely. It passes craters, collapsed tubes and walls of frozen rock. A recorded commentary plays as you go. Look out of the window at the textures. Some lava is smooth and rope-like. Other lava is broken into sharp, jagged blocks.

Here is the second surprise. This place is not dead. Look closely at the dark rocks and you may see pale green, grey or orange patches. These are lichens. They are among the first living things to grow on new lava. Slowly, over many years, they help to break the rock down. Scientists study Timanfaya to understand how life returns to land after a volcano. Every patch of colour on these stones is part of that slow return.

The area was declared a national park in 1974. Later, the whole of Lanzarote became a UNESCO biosphere reserve. That is an international title for places where people and nature try to live in balance. The rules here are strict for good reason. A single footprint in loose cinder can last for many years. Driving, hiking and collecting stones are tightly controlled. What looks empty is, in fact, carefully protected.

Outside the park, you can see how islanders learned to live with all this ash. Near the park entrance, camel rides take visitors along the slopes. Camels were once working animals on Lanzarote's farms. Further east, in the La Geria area, farmers grow vines inside small pits dug into the black ash. Low stone walls shelter each vine from the wind. The ash holds the night dew and feeds it to the roots. Disaster became a way of farming.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the colours again: red, black, brown, and a little green. Feel the warmth coming up through the soles of your shoes. Listen to the wind and nothing else. Nearly three hundred years ago, this was farmland, with houses and fields. Then the earth opened. Today, you are standing on the newest ground in the island's story... and it is still warm.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a windproof layer; the hilltop wind can feel cold.
Wear closed shoes with firm soles for the cinder ground.
Sit on the coach's window side for clearer crater views.
Stay on marked paths; footprints in cinder last for years.
Arrive early to avoid queues at the park entrance road.
