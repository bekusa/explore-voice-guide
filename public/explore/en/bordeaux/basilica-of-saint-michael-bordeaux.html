The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the square and listen. Pigeons shift on warm stone ledges. A café spoon rings against a saucer somewhere behind you. And above all of it, a single spire rises so high that you have to tilt your head right back to find the top. This is the Basilica of Saint Michael, and its pale limestone has watched over this corner of Bordeaux for more than six hundred years.

Before we look closer, notice something unusual. The tall spire is not attached to the church. It stands on its own, a short walk away across the square. Most visitors walk toward the church, then turn around and realise the tower was never part of the building at all. That separation is the first small surprise of this place, and we will come back to it.

First, the church itself. Work on the basilica began in the fourteenth century and continued, in stages, into the sixteenth. That is a long time to build anything. Generations of masons lived and died while these walls went up. Wars interrupted the work. Money ran out and came back again. What you see today carries all of those pauses, layered into a single body of stone.

The style is Flamboyant Gothic, the final and most decorative phase of Gothic building in France. The word flamboyant comes from the French for flame. Look at the tracery in the upper windows and you will see why. The stone curves and twists into shapes like the tips of flickering fire. Earlier Gothic churches used straighter, more geometric patterns. Here, the masons let the lines move.

The church grew alongside its neighbourhood. In the late Middle Ages, this district sat close to the river and the busy port. Sailors, dockworkers, coopers who made barrels for wine, and traders lived in these streets. Many of the side chapels inside were funded by trade guilds, the craft associations of their day. Each group wanted its own place to pray, and its own saint to watch over its work.

The basilica also stands on a much older road. For centuries, pilgrims walked through Bordeaux on their way to Santiago de Compostela, the great shrine of Saint James in northwest Spain. Because of that link, this church is listed by UNESCO as part of the pilgrim routes through France. Look for the scallop shell, the old pilgrim symbol. It still appears on markers and in details around the city.

Now, back to that solitary spire. Locals simply call it the flèche, which means the arrow. It was built in the fifteenth century as a separate bell tower. Builders often kept heavy bell towers apart from the main church. The swinging bells shook the stone, and a tall tower carried enormous weight. Keeping them apart protected the church walls from cracks and strain.

The spire rises well over one hundred metres, and it is one of the tallest in France. On a clear day, it is a landmark you can pick out from across the river. The spire you see today is not entirely medieval. Storms and age damaged the original top, and much of the upper section was rebuilt during the nineteenth century, when France began restoring its great Gothic monuments.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a strange one. Beneath the tower lies a crypt. In the eighteenth century, when an old cemetery nearby was cleared, workers found bodies that had dried out naturally in the soil. They became known as the mummies of Saint Michael. For many years, they were displayed in the crypt, and curious visitors came down to see them by candlelight. They were finally removed from public view in 1990.

Writers and travellers in the nineteenth century described that crypt with a mix of horror and fascination. Some of the stories told about individual mummies, their names and how they died, belong to local legend rather than documented record. What is certain is that the crypt gave this quarter a slightly eerie reputation. Ask an older Bordeaux resident, and they may still remember hearing about it as a child.

Step inside the church if it is open. The nave is long and tall, and the light changes as you walk. Much of the old stained glass was lost over the centuries, including damage during the Second World War. A large part of the glass you see is from the twentieth century. The colours are bolder and more modern than you might expect in a medieval church, and they throw soft patches of colour across the floor.

Take a slow walk along the side chapels. Each one has its own character, with carved stone, painted altarpieces, and memorial plaques. Some are quiet and dim. Others glow with candles left by people from the neighbourhood. This is still a working parish church, not only a monument. You may share the space with someone praying, so keep your voice low and your phone on silent.

When you come back outside, look at the square around you. The Saint Michael quarter is one of the most mixed parts of Bordeaux. On market days, stalls spread across the cobbles, and secondhand dealers lay out old furniture, books, and dishes. You hear French, Arabic, Spanish, and Portuguese in the same few minutes. The church has always belonged to a working neighbourhood, and that is still true today.

Before you leave, find a spot where you can see both the church and the spire together. Notice how the light moves across the limestone, turning it from grey to honey to gold as the day goes on. Six centuries of hands shaped these stones. As you walk away down the narrow streets, glance back once more. The arrow will still be there, rising above the rooftops, the way it has for every traveller before you.
