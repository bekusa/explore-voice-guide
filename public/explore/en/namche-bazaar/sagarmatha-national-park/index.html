The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below you, the Dudh Koshi river runs grey-white over boulders, a steady roar that never quite fades. Closer by, a yak bell rings once, then again. Prayer flags snap in the wind above the rooftops of Namche Bazaar. The air is thin and cold, and every breath feels a little shorter than it should. You are standing inside one of the highest protected landscapes on Earth, and the mountains have not even shown themselves yet.

This is Sagarmatha National Park. Nepal created it in 1976 to protect the upper Khumbu region, the high valleys below Mount Everest. Only a few years later, in 1979, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List. The park covers more than eleven hundred square kilometres. Almost all of it sits above three thousand metres. Deep river gorges, glaciers, moraines and some of the tallest peaks on the planet all fall inside its boundary.

The name itself is worth a moment. Sagarmatha is the Nepali name for Everest. It is often translated as forehead of the sky, or head of the sky. On the Tibetan side of the border, the same mountain is called Chomolungma, usually rendered as mother goddess of the world. The English name came from a British surveyor in the nineteenth century. Three names, three cultures, one summit — and locals here use the first two far more than the third.

Namche Bazaar is the gateway to all of this. The town sits at roughly 3,440 metres, built into a steep, curving bowl on the hillside. Its stone houses rise in terraces, like seats in a theatre. Look at the roofs. Many are painted blue, green or red, bright against grey rock. For centuries this was a trading point. Goods moved between the lowlands of Nepal and the high plateau of Tibet, carried on the backs of people and animals.

The people who made this landscape home are the Sherpa. Their ancestors are generally believed to have migrated from eastern Tibet several centuries ago. The word Sherpa is often explained as people from the east. Today the name is famous because of mountaineering. But for most of their history, Sherpa families were farmers, herders and traders. They grew potatoes on these slopes and moved yaks between summer and winter pastures, a rhythm that still shapes life in the valleys.

Here is something many visitors miss. This national park is not an empty wilderness. People live inside it. Villages like Namche, Khumjung and Khunde sit within the protected area, and their fields, monasteries and trails are part of what is being protected. UNESCO recognised the landscape partly for this reason. The culture and the mountains are treated as one thing, not two separate things placed side by side.

That culture is Tibetan Buddhist, and you can see it everywhere along the trails. Look for large stones carved with prayers and painted in white and black. These are mani stones. Look for white dome-shaped shrines called stupas, and for prayer wheels set into walls. Local custom asks you to walk past all of them on your left side, moving clockwise. Spinning a prayer wheel as you pass is welcome. Sherpa guides do it without breaking stride.

Now, a short walk uphill. Above the town, on a ridge, sits the park headquarters and visitor centre. On a clear morning, this ridge offers one of the first chances to see Everest. It appears as a dark, wind-scoured pyramid, often with a plume of snow streaming from its top. It is partly hidden behind the long wall of Nuptse and Lhotse. To its right, the sharp, elegant peak of Ama Dablam is impossible to mistake.

Everest was first climbed in 1953 by Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa, and Edmund Hillary, from New Zealand. Their ascent changed the Khumbu forever. Hillary later returned to support the region, and the trust he founded helped build schools and health clinics in Sherpa villages. The school in nearby Khumjung is one of the best known. For many families here, that legacy is as important as the climb itself.

The park is not only rock and ice. Lower down, forests of pine, fir and birch cover the slopes. In spring, rhododendrons bloom red, pink and white — the rhododendron is Nepal's national flower. Keep your eyes on the hillsides for Himalayan tahr, a wild goat with a thick, shaggy coat. You may also spot the Himalayan monal, Nepal's national bird, its feathers shining green, blue and copper. Snow leopards and red pandas live here too, but sightings are rare.

The park also carries real pressure. Every season, tens of thousands of trekkers and climbers pass through these trails. That brings income, but also rubbish, firewood demand and crowded paths. Local organisations and the park authorities have worked on waste collection and on reducing wood burning. You can help simply. Carry your waste out, refill a water bottle instead of buying plastic, and stay on the marked trail.

One practical word about the altitude. Namche is the place where most trekkers stop for an extra day to let their bodies adjust. The usual advice is to climb high during the day and sleep lower at night. Headaches, nausea and unusual tiredness can be early signs of altitude sickness. Drink water, walk slowly, and tell your guide or lodge host if you feel unwell. In the mountains, slowing down is not weakness — it is good judgement.

So pause here for a moment. Feel the cold on your face and the pull in your lungs. Look at the stone walls, the stacked firewood, the prayer flags fading slowly in the sun. Every colour and every carved stone around you was carried up by someone, step by step. As you walk on, listen again for the river below and the bells of the yaks. The mountains will still be here tomorrow... and so, quite likely, will the people who live beneath them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan an acclimatisation day in Namche before climbing higher.
Walk to the ridge above town early for clear mountain views.
Pass mani stones and stupas keeping them on your left.
Carry a refillable water bottle to reduce plastic waste.
Arrange your park entry permit before heading up the trail.
