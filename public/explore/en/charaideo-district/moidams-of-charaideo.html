The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here the loudest sounds are birds in the trees, wind moving through tall grass, and perhaps the soft crunch of your own feet on a path. In front of you rise smooth, rounded hills covered in green. They look natural at first glance. They are not. Each one was raised by human hands, and each one was built to hold a king, a queen, or a noble of a dynasty that ruled this valley for about six hundred years.

These are the Moidams of Charaideo, in Upper Assam, in the far northeast of India. A moidam is a burial mound. Underneath the earth sits a chamber, often vaulted, where the dead were laid to rest. Above it, workers piled soil into a hemispherical mound. Around many of them runs a low wall, frequently eight-sided. Some people call this place the pyramids of Assam. The comparison is loose, but it captures the idea: royal tombs, built to last, shaped to be seen.

The people behind these mounds were the Ahoms. Their founder, a Tai prince called Sukaphaa, crossed the mountains from the east in the thirteenth century. He and his followers came from Tai-speaking lands that today lie around Myanmar and southern China. They settled in the Brahmaputra valley. Over the following centuries, the Ahom kingdom grew into one of the longest-ruling dynasties in South Asian history. It lasted until the early nineteenth century.

Charaideo was their first capital. Even after the royal court moved to other cities, this ground kept a special place. It remained the sacred burial ground of the royal family. Think of it as the spiritual home of the dynasty. Kings could rule from elsewhere, but many still came back here in death. That connection is why you see so many mounds gathered in one landscape, rather than scattered across the region.

In 2024, UNESCO added the Moidams to its World Heritage List. The official name describes them as the mound-burial system of the Ahom dynasty. That wording matters. UNESCO did not only recognise a group of tombs. It recognised a whole system — a way of choosing the site, shaping the land, building the chamber, and tending the mound. This was the first cultural World Heritage Site in India's northeast. For many people in Assam, it was a moment of real pride.

Now look closely at the shape of the nearest mound. Its size tells you something. In general, a larger mound and a taller structure point to a person of higher rank. The power of the person buried inside was written into the height of the earth above them. Walking between the mounds, you can compare them. Some are modest. Others stand well above head height, and rise like small hills against the sky.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The green slope you see is only the outer skin. Beneath it, builders used brick, stone, and earth in layers. The inner chamber often had a vaulted roof. On top of the mound, some moidams had a small pavilion-like structure called a chow chali. Over time, many of these top structures were lost. What remains now is mostly the mound itself, softened by grass and centuries of rain.

What went inside the chambers? Accounts describe the dead buried with objects they might need in the afterlife — personal belongings, ornaments, and other goods fit for royalty. Some older accounts also mention attendants and animals buried alongside rulers in early times. Historians treat the details with care, because records vary. What is clear is that these tombs held valuable things. That made them a target. Many moidams were opened and looted over the centuries, long before they received modern protection.

The second surprise is about change in belief. The early Ahoms followed their own Tai religious traditions. Over the centuries, the royal family gradually adopted Hinduism. In Hindu practice, the dead are usually cremated rather than buried. Yet the moidam tradition did not simply stop. In later periods, cremated remains were often placed inside moidams. So these mounds hold two faiths at once — an old Tai custom of burial, carried forward by rulers who had embraced new ways of honouring the dead.

The tradition is still alive in another form. Ahom communities keep a ritual of ancestor worship called Me-Dam-Me-Phi. The name comes from the Tai language, and it refers to offerings to the dead and to the gods. Families and community groups gather to remember their forebears. When you stand here, you are not only looking at an archaeological site. For many local people, this is a place connected to their own family lines and their sense of who they are.

Take a moment to notice the landscape around the mounds. The ground rolls gently toward the hills. Tall trees stand between the moidams, and shade falls across the paths. In the wet season, everything turns a deep, heavy green, and the air feels thick. In the dry months, the light is clearer and the shapes of the mounds stand out more sharply. Either way, the site feels quiet and spread out, more like a park than a museum.

As you walk, keep your voice low and stay on the paths. The mounds are protected heritage, and they are also graves. Climbing them damages the soil and grass that hold their shape. You may see caretakers at work, cutting grass or clearing fallen leaves. That simple maintenance is part of what keeps the mounds intact. Without it, roots and rain would slowly pull them apart.

Before you leave, stand still once more and look across the nearest mound. Picture a royal funeral procession moving along this ground, centuries ago — the sound of drums, the smell of smoke, the slow work of raising earth over a chamber. Then come back to what is here now. Grass. Birdsong. A green hill holding a quiet secret. As you walk away, glance back once. From a distance, the mounds blend into the land again... and that, perhaps, is exactly how their builders meant them to rest.

What to look for

Low eight-sided walls ringing the base of some mounds.
Differences in mound height reflecting the buried person's rank.
Exposed brickwork where the grass covering has worn away.
Small remains of structures on top of certain mounds.
Tall shade trees standing between the grassy burial mounds.
Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning for softer light and cooler air.
Stay on marked paths and do not climb the mounds.
Wear comfortable shoes for walking between scattered mounds.
Carry water and insect repellent, especially in wet season.
Combine your visit with Ahom monuments around Sivasagar.
