The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below you, a small train is grinding up the slope, its rack wheels biting into the track. The air carries the smell of damp leaves and warm stone. Through the trees, a cluster of pale towers and steep slate roofs rises out of the hillside. It looks like a castle from a fairy tale. And that is exactly what it was meant to look like.

This is Schloss Drachenburg, on the slopes of the Drachenfels above Königswinter. The broad Rhine runs below, and the wooded hills of the Siebengebirge, the Seven Mountains, roll away behind you. But here is the first surprise. Despite its towers and battlements, this is not a medieval castle. It was built in the early 1880s, in only a few years. It is a rich man's dream of the Middle Ages, not the real thing.

That rich man was Stephan von Sarter. He came from a modest Bonn family, and he made a fortune on the Paris stock exchange. His wealth was linked in part to investments in the Suez Canal era. Later he received the title of baron. With his new money and his new name, he wanted a residence that announced his arrival. So he chose one of the most famous hillsides in the Rhineland.

And here is the second surprise. Baron von Sarter never lived here. He spent most of his life in Paris, and the castle he paid for remained largely empty while he was alive. Imagine it. All these painted halls, all this carved wood, all this coloured glass... and the owner stayed far away. The house was a statement, more than a home. Locals still enjoy this irony today.

Look at the building as a whole. Its style is called historicism. This was a nineteenth century fashion that borrowed freely from earlier periods. Here you can see Gothic pointed arches, Renaissance details, and romantic towers side by side. A purist might call it a costume. But that mix was the point. It was built to impress, and to make visitors feel the drama of the old German past.

Why this hill? The answer lies in the name. Drachenfels means Dragon's Rock. According to legend, the hero Siegfried killed a dragon on this mountain. He then bathed in its blood and became almost invulnerable. This story belongs to the world of the Nibelungen, the great medieval German epic. It is folklore, not history. But in the nineteenth century, these tales became a powerful symbol of German identity, and this hill became a place of pilgrimage for romantic travellers.

Above you, at the summit, stand the ruins of a real medieval castle, Burg Drachenfels. Its broken walls are much older than the villa beneath them. The mountain itself has another story too. For centuries, workers quarried its volcanic stone, called trachyte. Some of this stone went down the river to Cologne, where it was used in the great cathedral. So part of this hill now stands in another city.

Now step inside, if you can. The interiors are the real reward. Look for the wall paintings with scenes from German legend and history. Look for the dark carved wood, and for the patterned floors underfoot. Above all, watch the light through the stained-glass windows. On a bright day, the colours fall across the floors in soft patches of red, blue and gold. Many of these windows were restored or recreated in recent decades, following old designs.

The castle had a busy and difficult life after the baron's death. Over the twentieth century it passed through many hands. It served as a school, and during the Nazi period it was used for training young people in that regime's ideology. War brought damage. After the war it was used for other training purposes, and for some years it fell into poor condition. Parts of it were close to being lost.

Then came a long rescue. A private owner began repairs in the 1970s. Later, a foundation of the state of North Rhine-Westphalia took over the castle. Over many years, craftspeople worked on roofs, walls, paintings and glass. The restoration was slow and careful. What you see today is the result of that patient work. The building looks newer in places than you might expect. That freshness comes from restoration, not from neglect.

Walk out onto the terraces. From here, the Rhine opens below you in a wide silver curve. On a clear day, you can follow the river north towards Bonn. Barges move slowly along the water. Across the valley, small towns and church towers sit on the far bank. This view is the reason the baron built here. The castle was designed to be seen from the river, and to look out over it.

Before you leave, visit the outer buildings, the forecourt area below the main house. One of them holds a museum about the history of nature conservation in Germany. This is fitting. The Siebengebirge became one of the country's early protected landscapes, partly because people wanted to stop quarrying from destroying hills like this one. The romantic love of ruins and legends helped to save the forest around you.

Take one last look back at the towers as you walk away. Notice how the pale stone stands against the dark green trees. Listen again for the little train on its way up or down the mountain. You are standing between three ages here. A medieval ruin above, a nineteenth century dream around you, and the modern river traffic below. Each one, in its own way, came here for the view.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the Drachenfels rack railway; it stops near the castle.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep walking path.
Visit on a sunny day for stained-glass colours indoors.
Continue uphill afterwards to the medieval Drachenfels ruins.
Save time for the nature conservation museum below.
