The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps gently against the hull of the little boat, and somewhere behind you a windmill creaks in the breeze. The lagoon around you is shallow and very still. In places it is barely deeper than your knees. Along the shore, low white mounds of salt sit in their pans like small snowfields under the Sicilian sun. Ahead, a low green island rises from the water, covered in pines, vines and scrub. This is Motya. Today it is quiet, almost sleepy. Nearly three thousand years ago, it was one of the busiest trading ports in the Mediterranean.

The lagoon you are crossing is called the Stagnone, which means something like "the big pond". Several small islands sit inside it, sheltered from the open sea by a long, low barrier island. That shelter is the reason Motya existed at all. Ancient sailors wanted a safe harbour, an easy place to land cargo, and a site that was hard to attack. A small island in a protected lagoon gave them all three. Keep that in mind as you step onto the jetty. Geography made this place.

The people who settled here were Phoenicians. They were a seafaring people from the coast of what is now Lebanon, famous for trade, purple dye and the alphabet that later shaped Greek and Latin writing. From around the eighth century before Christ, they built a network of ports across the western Mediterranean. Motya became one of their most important outposts in Sicily. It looked toward Carthage, the great Phoenician city in North Africa, just across the water. Ships, metals, pottery and ideas moved constantly through this small island.

Here is the first surprise. Motya was not only connected by sea. The Phoenicians built a road across the lagoon to the Sicilian mainland. It ran just under or just above the water line, depending on the tides and the centuries. On a calm, clear day, people say you can still trace its line beneath the surface toward the northern shore. Carts once rolled across it. Imagine wheels moving over the water, with the island walls rising ahead of them.

Those walls once ringed the whole island. As you walk the paths, look for the stone foundations near the old gates, especially on the north side, where the causeway arrived. The stone is pale, rough and heavily weathered, and in places it shows centuries of repair and rebuilding. Inside the walls stood houses, workshops, sanctuaries and shrines. Archaeologists have found traces of industry here too, including the production of purple dye from sea snails. Some of the craftsmen who made it may have worked within sight of where you are standing.

Near the southern edge of the island is a rectangular stone basin known as the Kothon. For many years scholars called it an artificial harbour, a small dock for boats. More recent excavations suggest a different idea. The basin may have been a sacred pool, linked to a temple and to the water that fed it. The debate is a good reminder that archaeology is not a finished story. Experts still argue about what they are looking at, and new seasons of digging keep changing the picture.

Another area carries a heavier weight. The tophet was a sacred precinct where the Phoenicians buried urns containing the cremated remains of infants and young animals, alongside carved stone markers. Ancient Greek and Roman writers accused the Phoenicians of child sacrifice. Modern scholars remain divided. Some see evidence of ritual killing, while others think these were mainly burials of children who died young. Standing here, you are close to one of the most disputed questions in Mediterranean archaeology. It is worth a quiet moment.

Motya's prosperity did not last. In 397 BC, Dionysius the First, the ruler of the Greek city of Syracuse in eastern Sicily, came with a large army and fleet. Ancient accounts describe a fierce siege. The Greeks built siege works, brought up war machines and fought their way into the town street by street. The city fell, and much of it was destroyed. Many inhabitants were killed or enslaved. Motya never recovered its old importance.

Here is the second surprise, and it brings you back to the mainland. Survivors and refugees moved to a new site on the coast nearby, a fortified headland called Lilybaeum. That city later became Marsala, the town whose rooftops you can see across the lagoon. So in a real sense, Marsala is Motya's child. The island was abandoned as a major city, but its people carried their life a short distance across the water.

For many centuries afterwards, the island was mostly farmland and silence. Then, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, it caught the attention of Joseph Whitaker. He came from an English family deeply involved in the Marsala wine trade, the fortified wine that made this coast known in Britain. Whitaker bought the island, studied its birds and its ruins, and organised excavations. Today a foundation carrying his family name looks after Motya, and his old house holds the island's museum.

Inside that museum is the island's most famous object. It is a marble statue of a young man, found here in 1979 and usually called the Motya Charioteer, or the Youth of Motya. He stands tall and confident, wearing a finely pleated, close-fitting tunic. The carving is Greek in style, from the fifth century before Christ, and it is extraordinary work. Nobody knows for certain who he is, or how a Greek masterpiece came to stand in a Phoenician city. Look closely at the folds of the cloth. They look almost wet against the body.

Outside again, walk slowly. The paths take you past vineyards, low trees and open fields where the ruins come and go among the grass. Near one of the old houses you can see a floor laid with small black and white pebbles, set into patterns of animals. Lizards run across the warm stones. Birds call from the reeds near the shore. In summer the heat rises quickly, and the smell of dry herbs and salt water hangs over everything. Find some shade, and let the island set the pace.

Before you leave, stand at the water's edge and look back toward Sicily. The salt pans shine white. The windmills turn, or stand still, depending on the wind. Marsala sits low on the horizon. Below you, the shallow water hides the old causeway and holds the reflections of the clouds. Ships from Lebanon, Carthage and Greece once crowded into this lagoon. Now there is only a small boat, a few visitors, and the soft sound of water against the jetty... the same sound that greeted the first traders here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Catch the small boat from the jetty by the salt pans.
Bring water and a hat; shade on the island is limited.
Visit the museum first to understand the ruins outside.
Allow a few hours to walk the island's full perimeter path.
Stay for sunset over the salt pans after returning to shore.
