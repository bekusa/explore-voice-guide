The guide

The story

Listen first. Traffic hums along the boulevards nearby, but here the sound softens. Pigeons scrape across old paving stones. A tram bell rings somewhere in the distance. In front of you stands a long, low church of dark red brick. The walls are rough and uneven, with thick bands of mortar between the bricks. There is no gold here, no glossy paint. This plain building is one of the oldest churches in Sofia, and it gave the whole city its name.

Before we go inside, consider that name. Sophia is a Greek word, and it means wisdom. This church is not dedicated to a woman called Sophia. It is dedicated to Holy Wisdom, an idea from early Christian thought. In that tradition, divine wisdom is linked to Christ himself. The same dedication appears in the great Hagia Sophia in Istanbul. So when people say the name of this capital, they are saying the name of an idea.

Long before Sofia was Sofia, this place was Serdica. It was a Roman city, and an important one. Several Roman emperors spent time here. According to a popular tradition, Emperor Constantine once called Serdica his Rome. Historians treat that line with some caution, but it shows how much the city mattered. Under your feet, and around this church, lies part of that Roman world.

The ground beneath the church was once a burial ground. In Roman times, cemeteries sat outside the city walls, and this hill was one of them. Christians later built small chapels and churches among the graves. Over the centuries, one church replaced another on this same spot. Each new building rose on the ruins of the one before. The church you see today is the last in that long line.

The present building dates to the sixth century. That was the age of the Byzantine Empire, the eastern half of the Roman world, which ruled from Constantinople. Many scholars link it to the reign of Emperor Justinian, a great builder of churches. Look at the shape. The church is a basilica, a long hall divided into three aisles. A shorter arm crosses it, so from above the building forms a cross. A dome rises where the arms meet.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The city did not always carry this church's name. For centuries it was known by other names, including Sredets. By the fourteenth century, the name Sofia began to appear, taken from this very church. Slowly the new name replaced the old one. Few capitals in Europe can point to a single building and say, the city is named after that.

The history of the church did not stay peaceful. In the late fourteenth century, the Ottoman Empire took control of these lands. It ruled here for roughly five hundred years. In the Ottoman period, the church was turned into a mosque. A minaret, the slender tower used for the call to prayer, was added to the building. Frescoes on the walls were covered over. For generations, Muslim worshippers prayed under this dome.

Then the ground moved. In the nineteenth century, strong earthquakes struck Sofia. They damaged the building badly, and the minaret fell. After that, the structure was largely abandoned. For a time it stood empty and broken, a shell of brick in a changing city. Only in the twentieth century did serious restoration begin. Builders repaired the walls and the dome, and the church returned to Christian worship. Today it is an active Bulgarian Orthodox church.

Now step inside, if the doors are open. The air is cool, and the light is soft and low. The interior is simple — bare brick, high arches, a few icons near the altar. You will not find walls covered in bright frescoes. That emptiness is part of its character. Notice how the arches carry the weight of the dome. Notice the quiet. Even when tourists pass through, people often lower their voices here without being asked.

The most surprising part of this place lies below the floor. Beneath the church, archaeologists uncovered layers of the old cemetery and the earlier churches. Today this underground space is open as a museum, with its own entrance and a separate ticket. Walkways pass over Roman tombs. Some tombs still show painted decoration inside. You can also see fragments of mosaic floors from the older churches, with geometric patterns in stone. It is one of the clearest ways to see how this city grew, layer upon layer.

Back outside, walk along the side of the church. Near its wall you will find the grave of Ivan Vazov. He is one of Bulgaria's most loved writers, often called the patriarch of Bulgarian literature. His novels and poems helped shape how Bulgarians tell the story of their nation. He asked to be buried beside this church. Look for the simple stone. People sometimes leave flowers there, a small sign that his words still matter here.

Against another wall of the church stands the Monument to the Unknown Soldier. An eternal flame burns in front of it. A stone lion, a symbol of Bulgaria, lies close by. This memorial honours soldiers who died for the country. Official visitors and ceremonies often come here. It is a quieter, more solemn corner than the busy square across the street. Pause for a moment, and watch the flame move in the wind.

From here, turn your head toward the large cathedral nearby, with its golden domes. That is Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, built much later, in a time of national revival. The contrast says a lot. One building is grand and shining. The other is old, plain, and patient. Both are part of Sofia's story. But this brick church was here first, by well over a thousand years, and it carries the name the city still uses.

Before you move on, look once more at those red walls. Run your eyes along the uneven bricks and the thick lines of mortar. Hands in the sixth century laid many of them. Earthquakes shook them, empires came and went, and the building stayed. As you walk away, listen to the city around you, and remember. Every time someone in this capital says where they live, they are saying the name of this church.
