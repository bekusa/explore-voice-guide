The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind comes up from the sea, crosses miles of olive groves and juniper, and lifts over this limestone shelf. Underfoot the ground is pale and dusty, scattered with broken column drums. Below you the land falls away in long green steps toward the Mediterranean. Somewhere nearby, water trickles out of the rock. That small sound is the reason this city exists.

You are standing in Cyrene, one of the greatest Greek cities ever built in Africa. It sits on the edge of the Jebel Akhdar, the Green Mountain, just outside the modern town of Al Bayda. For more than a thousand years, people walked these streets speaking Greek, then Latin. They argued philosophy, traded grain, and worshipped Apollo. Today the ruins spread across the hillside in every direction, much larger than most visitors expect.

The founding story comes to us from the Greek historian Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE. He tells of settlers from Thera, the island we now call Santorini. According to his account, the oracle at Delphi ordered them to found a colony in Libya. They hesitated, struggled, and tried more than once. A leader named Battus finally brought them here in the late seventh century BCE. Parts of the tale are clearly legend, but the Theran origin of the city is widely accepted.

Why this spot? Look for the spring. Water flows from the cliff face in the Sanctuary of Apollo, and the Greeks linked it to a nymph called Kyrene. The city took her name. On a dry coast, a reliable spring on a high, fertile terrace was precious. The settlers could farm the plateau, catch the sea breeze, and watch the coast from above. Their port, Apollonia, lay down on the shore below.

The Sanctuary of Apollo is the heart of the lower city. Here the ground is crowded with temples, altars, and fountains packed close together. The Temple of Apollo itself was rebuilt several times over the centuries. Walk slowly and you will see foundations from different eras lying side by side. Greek blocks sit beside Roman repairs. The whole terrace shows how one sacred place was used, damaged, and rebuilt again and again.

Now for something most visitors never hear about. Cyrene grew rich on a plant. It was called silphium, a wild herb that grew only in this region. The ancient world used it as a seasoning and a medicine, and valued it highly. Cyrene stamped silphium on its coins. Then, by Roman times, the plant became very hard to find. Ancient writers already described it as nearly gone. Scientists still debate exactly which plant it was.

Up on the higher ground, to the northeast, stands the Temple of Zeus. It was one of the largest Greek temples ever built, often compared in size with the Parthenon in Athens. Its massive Doric columns, a plain and sturdy Greek style, were thrown down in ancient times. In the twentieth century, archaeologists lifted many of them back into place. Standing among them, you feel how small a person is beside this stone.

Cyrene was also a city of ideas. The philosopher Aristippus was born here. He founded a school of thought which taught that pleasure is the highest good. The poet Callimachus came from Cyrene, and so did Eratosthenes. He was the scholar who later ran the great library in Alexandria and estimated the size of the Earth. Imagine these minds growing up on this windy hillside, looking out at the same sea.

Power here changed hands many times. After its Greek kings, Cyrene came under the Ptolemies, the Greek dynasty that ruled Egypt after Alexander the Great. Then Rome took control in the first century BCE. The city became part of a group of five towns known as the Pentapolis. Roman builders added baths, a forum, and grand public spaces. You can see their brick and marble layered over the older Greek stone.

The city also appears in the Christian story. The Gospels name Simon of Cyrene as the man who helped carry the cross of Jesus. A large Jewish community lived here in Roman times. In the early second century CE, a major Jewish revolt broke out across the region, and Cyrene suffered heavy damage. The city recovered, but a powerful earthquake in the fourth century struck it hard again. Slowly, life drained away.

Before you leave, look beyond the city walls. The hills around Cyrene hold one of the largest ancient cemeteries in the Mediterranean world. Thousands of tombs are cut straight into the rock. Some have carved facades like small temples. Others are simple dark openings in the cliff. Shepherds have sheltered in them for centuries. Driving along the roads nearby, you will see these doorways staring out from every slope.

Modern excavation began in earnest in the early twentieth century, led largely by Italian archaeologists. Much of what you see standing today was cleared, studied, and partly rebuilt in that period. In 1982, Cyrene was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Since 2016, it has also been on the list of World Heritage in Danger. Erosion, construction, and the region's recent instability all threaten these ruins.

So take one more moment here. Feel the wind again, the same wind the settlers from Thera felt when they first climbed this slope. Listen for the water still running from the rock. Look down toward the coast, where ships once carried silphium and grain. As you walk back, notice the broken stones at your feet. Each one was cut by hand, carried, and placed by someone who called this city home.
