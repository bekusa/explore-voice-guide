The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the bare rock, and somewhere above you a bell rings once and stops. The stone under your feet is pale and worn smooth by countless shoes. Ahead, high walls rise straight out of the cliff, so solid that they look more like a fortress than a church. This is the Monastery of Our Lady of Saidnaya. It sits on a rocky height above the town, in the dry hills north of Damascus.

Before we climb, it helps to know what kind of place this is. This is not a museum. It is a working convent of the Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch, one of the oldest Christian communities in the world. Nuns live, pray, and work here. So the quiet you hear is not staged for visitors. It is daily life. Walk gently, speak softly, and you will see the place as it really is.

Now, the founding story. Pay attention here, because this is tradition, not documented history. Legend has it that the Byzantine emperor Justinian, who ruled in the sixth century, was hunting in these hills. He chased a gazelle up to this rocky summit. Just as he raised his bow, the animal changed into a shining figure of the Virgin Mary. She told him not to kill, and asked him to build a church on this very spot.

The story goes further. According to the tale, Justinian ordered his builders to begin, but they could not settle on a plan. Then the Virgin appeared again, this time in a dream, and showed him exactly how the church should look. Whether you take this as faith or as folklore, it explains something you can feel on site. The monastery does not sit politely in the valley. It claims the highest, hardest ground — as if someone was told to build exactly here.

Even the town's name carries a puzzle. People explain the word Saidnaya in two ways. Some connect it to an old Aramaic phrase meaning Our Lady. Aramaic, by the way, is the ancient language of this region, close to the language Jesus spoke. Others link the name to a word for a hunting place, which fits Justinian's chase. Scholars and locals still disagree. And so the name itself holds both the faith and the legend.

Look up as you start to climb. Stairways turn back and forth across the rock face. The walls are thick and plain, with few decorations on the outside. This is deliberate. Over many centuries, religious buildings in this region often needed protection as much as beauty. Much of what you see today has been rebuilt and repaired many times. So the stones you touch come from many different generations, laid one layer on top of another.

At the top, the mood changes. Courtyards open out, and the light becomes softer. You may smell candle wax and incense drifting from a doorway. Pilgrims move slowly here. Some are local families. Some have travelled from across Syria and Lebanon. Many walk in silence. Others whisper prayers as they go. Take a moment in the courtyard before you go further. Let your eyes adjust to the shade.

The heart of the monastery is a small, dark room. It holds the most treasured object here — an icon of the Virgin called the Shaghoura. The word is usually translated as the famous one, or the renowned one. Tradition says that Saint Luke the Evangelist painted it. Historians cannot confirm that claim. But for believers, the icon is the reason this place exists, and it has drawn pilgrims for many centuries.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. You will probably not see the Shaghoura itself clearly. The icon is kept inside a shrine, behind a screen and covered in silver. People do not come to look at it like a painting in a gallery. They come to be near it. That is why the room feels so crowded with objects — lamps, offerings, small silver plaques, and candles. Each one was left by someone who came here with a hope.

Now the second surprise. This sanctuary is not only for Christians. Muslim families also come to pray here, especially women hoping for a child or for healing. In this part of the world, the Virgin Mary is honoured in Islam as well, and she is named in the Quran. So you may see two faiths standing side by side in the same small room. Nobody makes an announcement about it. It simply happens, quietly, every day.

The fame of this place once reached far beyond Syria. In the Middle Ages, travellers from Europe wrote about a miraculous icon at Saidnaya. Some of these accounts claimed that a fragrant oil came from the image. Pilgrims carried small amounts of it home as a blessing. Treat the miracle stories as part of the legend. But the travellers' records show something real: for centuries, this remote hilltop was known across the Christian world.

The busiest day of the year comes in early September. On the feast of the Nativity of the Virgin, the celebration of Mary's birth, crowds fill the town and climb these stairs. Fires and candles light the night, and the hillside fills with the sound of hymns. If you come on an ordinary day, the contrast is strong. You may share the courtyards with only a few people, a cat in the sun, and the sound of a broom on stone.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the terrace and look out. Below you, the town spreads across the slopes, with flat roofs, church towers, and a few minarets. Beyond it, the hills turn brown and gold, with long folds of rock. On a clear day, the air here feels thinner and cooler than in Damascus. You can see why someone, long ago, believed this summit was chosen.

So take one last look at the walls. Notice how the afternoon light catches the pale stone. Notice the stairs you climbed, worn into shallow dips by many generations of feet. As you walk back down, listen again. You may hear the bell, a quiet prayer, or only the wind across the rock. All of these sounds belong here — and for a few minutes, so did you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered inside the convent.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep stone stairways.
Keep your voice low near the shrine room.
Ask before photographing nuns, pilgrims, or the shrine.
Visit in early September to see the main feast.
