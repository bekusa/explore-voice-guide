The guide

The story

Start with your hand on the wall. The concrete is cool and slightly rough, and it does not stand straight. It leans, it tilts, it angles away from you. Above, the roof slopes down toward the grass like a hillside. On a quiet day you can hear the wind off the Ottawa River and very little else. This building was designed to feel as if it rose out of the earth itself. It feels a little unsettled, and that is deliberate.

Welcome to the Canadian War Museum. It sits on LeBreton Flats, on the south bank of the Ottawa River, a short walk west of Parliament Hill. The current building opened in 2005. Before that, the national collection lived in a much older and more cramped building on Sussex Drive. The roots of the collection go back much further, to the late nineteenth century. The museum has grown from a small military collection into one of the country's major national museums.

The ground under your feet has its own story. LeBreton Flats was once an industrial neighbourhood of lumber mills, rail yards and workers' homes. In 1900 a great fire swept through Ottawa and Hull and destroyed much of it. In the 1960s the federal government cleared the remaining neighbourhood, and the land sat empty for decades. So this museum about destruction and recovery stands on a site that knows both. That is a coincidence few visitors notice.

The architect was Raymond Moriyama, a Canadian designer with a deep personal link to the subject. As a boy of Japanese descent, he was held in an internment camp in British Columbia during the Second World War. He spoke about this building as a place of regeneration. He did not want it to celebrate war. Look at the low, grassy roof. It suggests life returning to a battlefield, the way grass slowly grows back over shell holes.

Now for the first surprise. Look up at the long line of narrow windows along the roof. They are not random. The pattern of short and long openings spells out a message in Morse code. In English it reads Lest We Forget. In French it reads N'oublions jamais, which means the same thing. Most people walk past without realising that the building carries those words in light, in both of Canada's official languages.

Inside, the galleries move in rough chronological order. They begin with conflict on this land long before Canada existed, including warfare among Indigenous nations and the struggles between France and Britain. They continue through the War of 1812, the South African War, and the two World Wars. The later rooms cover the Cold War, peacekeeping, and Canada's mission in Afghanistan. The path is long, so let yourself drift. You do not need to read every panel.

The First World War galleries are often the most powerful. Canada had a population of only a few million people then, and yet hundreds of thousands served overseas. The museum shows uniforms, letters, weapons and recreated trench spaces. Pay attention to the small personal objects. A pocket diary or a photograph carried in a tunic can say more than a large gun. Many visitors slow down here without quite deciding to.

Here is the second surprise, and it depends on the calendar. Find Memorial Hall, a quiet, bare concrete room. It holds one object: the original headstone of Canada's Unknown Soldier. In 2000, the remains of an unidentified Canadian soldier were brought home from France and buried at the National War Memorial downtown. The old headstone came here. A single window is placed so that at 11 o'clock in the morning on November 11, Remembrance Day, sunlight falls directly on it.

Next, look for Regeneration Hall. It is a tall, angled space that narrows as it rises. At the far end, a window frames a view toward the Peace Tower of Parliament, the symbol of Canadian democracy. Inside the hall stand large plaster figures. They are working models made by the sculptor Walter Allward for the Canadian National Vimy Memorial in France. That memorial marks the 1917 Battle of Vimy Ridge, a key moment in how Canadians remember the First World War.

The museum is also home to one of the most important collections of war art in the country. Much of it comes from official programs that sent artists to record the wars as they happened. You will see dark, muddy battlefields, ruined towns, and quiet portraits of nurses and soldiers. Some paintings feel almost like documents. Others feel like private grief on canvas. If you like art, give yourself extra time in these rooms.

Then there is the LeBreton Gallery, a huge open hall that feels almost like a hangar. Tanks, artillery pieces and military vehicles stand in long rows under a high ceiling. Children often love this space, and so do many adults. You can walk around the machines and see their size up close. The scale is the point. Steel that looks abstract in photographs looks very heavy and very real when you stand next to it.

The collection includes some objects that people do not expect to find in Canada. One of the best known is a large Mercedes-Benz parade car once used by Adolf Hitler. It is shown as a historical object, not as a trophy. The museum does not hide uncomfortable history. Throughout the galleries, it also tells stories of loss at home, of protest against war, and of people whose service was overlooked for many years.

Before you leave, step back outside and turn around once more. Look at the sloping walls, the grass on the roof, and the narrow windows with their hidden message. To the east you can see the towers of Parliament Hill. The river moves on beside you, as it did before the mills and before the fire. Take a slow breath here. This place asks you to remember, and then it lets you walk back into an ordinary, peaceful afternoon.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least half a day for the chronological galleries.
Visit Memorial Hall near 11 a.m. on November 11.
Walk the riverside path afterward toward Parliament Hill.
Bring children to the LeBreton Gallery's large vehicles.
Expect large crowds around Remembrance Day each November.
