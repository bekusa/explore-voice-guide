The guide

The story

The steps down are steep and narrow, and the air grows warmer with every one. The bright desert light falls away behind you. Then the small burial chamber opens around you, and the walls are almost glowing. They are covered in warm golden yellow, deep reds, and black outlines as clean as if they were painted last season. You are standing inside one of the best preserved private tombs in Egypt, made by a man who decorated tombs for a living.

This is TT1, the first entry in the official numbering of the Theban Tombs. It belonged to Sennedjem, who held the title Servant in the Place of Truth. That title marked him as a member of the elite crew who cut and decorated the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings. He lived during the Nineteenth Dynasty, in the reigns of Seti the First and Ramesses the Second. That is roughly the thirteenth century before Christ.

To understand this chamber, it helps to picture the village outside. Deir el-Medina was a planned settlement for these royal craftsmen and their families. Above ground you can still trace the stone footings of their houses, packed tightly along a single main street inside a boundary wall. Stonecutters, draughtsmen, and painters lived here for generations. They walked over the hills each working week to the royal valley, and they came home to build tombs for themselves.

That is the first surprise of this place. The men who worked here had the best training in the kingdom, and they also had access to the finest pigments. When they turned that skill to their own burials, the results could match royal work in quality, if not in scale. Sennedjem's tomb is small, but every surface was planned by someone who knew exactly how the gods should look, and exactly how the journey after death should unfold.

Look up first. The ceiling is not flat. It curves in a low vault, and the painters divided it into panels like the pages of an open book. Each panel holds a scene or a hymn, framed in bands of colour and lines of hieroglyphs. In one of these scenes, Sennedjem and his wife kneel in adoration before the gods. The curve of the vault makes the chamber feel like the inside of a coffin, which is exactly what it was meant to suggest.

Now find the long wall showing the Field of Reeds. This was the Egyptian vision of paradise, a well-watered land where the blessed dead farmed forever. Here Sennedjem guides a plough pulled by a pair of oxen, and his wife Iyneferti walks behind him, scattering seed. Further along, they harvest tall golden wheat with sickles. Water channels frame the scene in blue, and date palms and other fruit trees stand heavy with fruit.

Pause on that image for a moment. These were not farmers. Sennedjem spent his life with chisels and brushes in the dark of royal tombs. Yet his idea of eternity was a sunlit field, with his wife at his side and good soil underfoot. It is one of the most human scenes in Egyptian art. It shows how deeply the rhythm of the Nile harvest shaped what people in this valley hoped for after death.

Other walls follow the darker parts of the journey. You will see Anubis, the jackal-headed god of embalming, bending over the mummy as it lies on a lion-shaped bed. There are guardians of the gates of the underworld, figures the dead had to pass by speaking the right words. And there are columns of text from the collection of spells we now call the Book of the Dead, which helped the soul find its way.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look for a cat sitting beside a tree, holding a knife, and cutting into a large serpent. The cat is a form of the sun god Ra. The serpent is Apep, the great enemy of the sun, who tried each night to swallow the light. By painting this fight beside his burial, Sennedjem placed himself on the side of the sunrise. It is a fierce little image, and it is easy to walk past.

The story of the tomb's discovery is the second surprise. In 1886, local villagers from the area found the entrance and alerted the Egyptian Antiquities Service. When the chamber was opened, it was intact. Inside lay the burials of Sennedjem and members of his family, with coffins, furniture, tools, and offerings. Very few private tombs in Egypt have been found in this condition, and the discovery gave scholars an unusually complete picture of a craftsman's household.

Those objects are no longer here. They were removed and are now held in museums, mainly the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, with some pieces in collections abroad. One of the most famous is the painted wooden door that once sealed this chamber. Its panels show Sennedjem and his family playing a board game called senet, a game linked to passing safely into the afterlife. The empty room you see now is the painted shell that once held all of that.

Before you climb back up, stand still and let your eyes adjust. Notice how the figures overlap neatly at the edges, and how the hieroglyphs stay crisp even in the corners. The dry air of this desert valley helped protect the colours for more than three thousand years. The space is small and visitors usually rotate through quickly, so take your moment in the centre of the room, where you can see the walls and the vault together.

Outside, look at the low structure above the tomb shaft. Tombs at Deir el-Medina were often topped by a chapel with a small mudbrick pyramid, where the family could leave offerings. Some of these have been rebuilt from their remains, so you can see how the hillside looked when it was covered with them. From here, the ruins of the village sit just below you, and nearby are other painted tombs belonging to Sennedjem's neighbours.

As you step back into the sunlight, notice the colours of the hillside itself. The pale limestone, the dusty gold of the rock, the deep shadow in each doorway. Those same tones are on the walls below. The painters lived inside this landscape, and they carried it down into the dark with them. When you walk the village street, you are walking where Sennedjem walked home in the evening, after a day spent working for a king.
