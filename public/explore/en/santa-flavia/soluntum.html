The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here on the slope of Monte Catalfano, the wind comes off the sea and moves through dry grass and wild fennel. Underfoot, the paving stones are worn smooth and uneven. Lizards slide between the blocks. Below you, the Gulf of Palermo opens out in a wide sheet of blue, and the headland of Capo Zafferano rises to the east. This is Soluntum, an ancient city built on a mountainside, and you are walking its streets.

The name comes in several forms. The Greeks called the place Solous, the Romans called it Soluntum, and Italians today call it Solunto. Behind all three sits an older Phoenician name. The Phoenicians were seafaring traders from the coast of what is now Lebanon, and they settled the western end of Sicily long before Rome mattered here. The Greek historian Thucydides writes that the Phoenicians gathered in three Sicilian strongholds: Motya, Panormus, which is modern Palermo, and Solous. That first Solous was not exactly where you stand.

The earliest settlement probably lay closer to the shore. In the fourth century before Christ, during the long wars between Carthage and the Greek city of Syracuse, the town was rebuilt up here on the mountain. The reason is easy to feel. From this height, nobody could approach by land or sea without being seen. The slope was steep enough to defend. And the view over the gulf gave the people who lived here a direct line of sight toward their Phoenician neighbours in Palermo.

Now look at the streets themselves. Here is the first surprise. This hillside city follows a strict grid. Long main streets run along the contours of the mountain, and narrower lanes cut straight up the slope at right angles. Greek town planners favoured this kind of regular layout. So you have a city with Phoenician roots, built with a Greek plan, on a Sicilian mountain. Soluntum was a meeting point of cultures long before the Romans arrived.

Those cross streets climb hard. Many of them are stepped, because no cart could manage the gradient. As you walk, notice how the houses sit on terraces, each one cut into the rock above the last. Residents carried water, oil and grain up and down these steps every day. The main street that runs along the slope is wider and flatter. It was the city's spine, and it leads toward the public heart of the town.

Water was the constant problem on this dry mountain. There are no rivers up here. So the people of Soluntum caught the rain. Look down as you go, and you will see openings in the rock and the floors of houses. These are cisterns, underground tanks cut into the stone and lined to hold water. Roofs and courtyards were shaped to send rainwater into them. Every house depended on what fell from the sky in winter.

One of the most admired homes on the site is known as the House of Leda. It takes the name from a painted scene of Leda, a figure from Greek myth, that once decorated its walls. Traces of coloured plaster and floor mosaics survive in this part of the city. They show that the wealthier families here lived with real comfort. These were rooms arranged around open courtyards, with decoration that followed the tastes of the wider Greek and Roman world.

Here is the second surprise. Near the main street stands a row of columns, partly raised again by modern restorers. For a long time it was called the Gymnasium, after an inscription found nearby. But archaeologists now understand that this building was not a gymnasium at all. It was a large private house, with columns around an inner courtyard. The old name stuck, and you will still see it on many signs. It is a good reminder that ruins are read and reread over time.

Keep walking toward the agora, the open public square where markets, meetings and announcements took place. Around it were civic buildings, places of worship and long porches where people could stand in the shade. Higher on the slope, the remains of a theatre and a smaller council hall show that this was a real city with its own public life. Stop for a moment in the square. Imagine voices, traders, animals, and the same wind you can feel now.

In the third century before Christ, during the First Punic War between Rome and Carthage, Soluntum passed into Roman hands. Life continued. The city kept its houses, its streets and its temples, and Roman habits joined the older Phoenician and Greek ones. But over the following centuries, people slowly drifted away from this exposed height. By the Roman imperial period the town was emptying, and in time the mountain took it back under soil and scrub.

The ruins stayed hidden for a very long time. Systematic excavation began in the nineteenth century, and work has continued in stages since then. Many of the finest objects found here, including sculpture and painted fragments, are now kept in museums, especially the regional archaeological museum in Palermo. A small museum at the entrance of the site, called the Antiquarium, holds material from the excavations and helps explain what you are seeing on the slope.

As you move around, pay attention to the stone. Much of the city was built from local limestone, pale grey and warm yellow, and you can see how walls were made from rough blocks fitted closely together. In some places, thresholds still carry grooves where doors once turned or slid. These small details tell you more about daily life than any grand monument. Someone stepped over that stone every morning, more than two thousand years ago.

Before you leave, find a place where the city drops away and the gulf fills your view. Below you lie Santa Flavia, the fishing harbour of Porticello, and the long coast curving toward Palermo. Fishing boats still cross the same water that Phoenician ships once sailed. Take a breath of the salt air, listen to the wind in the grass, and look back once along the stepped street behind you. The city is quiet now, but its lines are still clear on the mountain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven stepped streets.
Visit the Antiquarium first to understand the site layout.
Bring water and a hat; shade is limited on the slope.
Come in late afternoon for softer light over the gulf.
Pair the visit with Palermo's regional archaeological museum.
