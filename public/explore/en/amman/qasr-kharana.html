The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, east of Amman, the wind moves across the flat gravel plain with almost nothing to stop it. The ground is pale and stony, scattered with dark flint that crunches underfoot. Then the building appears, square and solid, the colour of dry sand. It sits alone in open desert. There is no village around it, no river, and no trees. For many visitors, the first question is simple. Why would anyone build something this large in a place this empty?

This is Qasr Kharana, one of the so-called desert castles of eastern Jordan. The word qasr is Arabic and usually means castle or palace. Most of these buildings date from the Umayyad period. The Umayyads were the first great Islamic dynasty. They ruled a vast empire from Damascus in the late seventh and early eighth centuries. Kharana is one of the best preserved of these desert buildings, and it is one of the easiest to reach from the capital.

Take a moment to look at the outside walls. Each side is roughly thirty-five metres long. Round towers stand at the four corners. Half-round towers sit along the walls between them. Two rows of narrow slits run across the face of the building. From here, it looks exactly like a fortress built to hold off an army. Many travellers assume this was a military outpost guarding the desert. That is the first surprise. It almost certainly was not.

Look closely at those narrow slits. Inside, many of them are placed too high, or at angles that make them useless for archers. Some open into rooms where a defender could never stand properly. Scholars now generally believe they were for light and air, not for weapons. In summer, the desert heat is intense. Narrow openings let a breeze move through the rooms while keeping out the worst of the sun. The towers are also solid at the base, so they could not shelter soldiers either.

There is only one way in, through a doorway in the south wall. As you pass through it, notice how the noise of the wind drops away. The thick walls hold the cool air. You arrive in a central courtyard, open to the sky. Long rooms line the ground floor on every side. Many experts think these were used for animals or storage. In the middle of the courtyard, look for traces of a basin. It once collected rainwater, which was precious in this landscape.

Now look at how the walls were built. The builders used rough, uneven blocks of local stone, set in thick mortar. Between them, you can see flat bands of stone and fired brick laid in neat horizontal lines. These bands added strength, and they also make an attractive pattern. In some places, the outer surface was once covered with plaster. Where the plaster has fallen away, the rough stone shows through. The mix of rough and careful work gives the building its rugged, handmade feel.

Climb the stairs to the upper floor. This is where Kharana changes character completely. The rooms up here feel lighter and more carefully finished. Look for rows of shallow arches set into the walls, with small half-domes above them. In the corners of some rooms, you will see clever curved supports. Builders used these to carry a round dome on top of a square room. These techniques have links to building traditions in Persia and Mesopotamia, to the east.

Now look up at the decoration. In several rooms, round medallions and rosettes in carved plaster, called stucco, still cling to the walls and ceilings. Some show simple geometric patterns. Others look like stylised flowers. They are small, and easy to walk past. Yet they show that someone important used these upper rooms. A storehouse or barracks would not need this kind of care. This was a space meant for guests, for meetings, and perhaps for comfort.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a remarkable one. In one of the upper rooms, someone once painted an inscription on the wall in ink. It is written in early Arabic script, above a doorway. The text records a date that corresponds to the year 710. It names a man called Abd al-Malik ibn Ubayd. This short line of writing is one of the most important clues we have. It tells us the building was standing by that date, although it may be somewhat older.

So what was Qasr Kharana for? The honest answer is that nobody knows for certain. For a long time, people thought it was a caravanserai, a roadside inn for traders and their camels. But there is no natural water source nearby, and it does not sit clearly on a major trade route. Many historians now suggest a different idea. The Umayyad rulers may have used it as a place to meet the leaders of local Bedouin tribes, the nomadic peoples of the desert.

This idea makes sense when you think about the politics of the time. The Umayyads depended on good relations with the desert tribes. These tribes controlled grazing land, routes, and fighting men. A grand meeting place in the open desert would be neutral ground, and it would also impress visitors. The strong walls and towers sent a message of power, even if they were not truly built for war. In this sense, the building was a statement, as much as a shelter.

Kharana is also part of a wider story. Further east along the same road stands Qusayr Amra, a small Umayyad bathhouse famous for its painted walls. Beyond that lies the fortress of Azraq, beside an oasis. Many travellers visit these places together in one day. Each site shows a different side of life in the early Islamic desert. Kharana is the most fortress-like of them, and in many ways the most mysterious.

Before you leave, walk out a little way from the entrance and turn around. Look at the building against the wide sky. Notice how the light changes the colour of the stone. Feel the wind again on your face, and listen to the silence of the plain. People stood here thirteen centuries ago, looking at these same walls. They came for reasons we can only guess. As you walk back toward the road, the castle keeps its secret, solid and quiet in the open desert.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning to avoid the strongest desert heat.
Bring water and a hat; shade is limited outside.
Combine with Qusayr Amra and Azraq for a day loop.
Wear sturdy shoes for the stone stairs and uneven floors.
Hire a car or driver; public transport is limited here.
