The guide

The story

Start with the colour. Above the slow green water of the Siverskyi Donets river, a cliff of pure white chalk rises out of the pine forest. It is so bright on a sunny day that you may want to narrow your eyes. Gold domes sit along its edges and on its shoulders. Below, the river moves without hurry, and the air smells of resin and damp stone. This is the Sviatohirsk Lavra, the monastery of the Holy Mountains.

The name says almost everything. Sviatohirsk means, roughly, the place of the holy mountains. The town takes its name from the monastery, not the other way round. A lavra is a special title in Orthodox Christianity. It is given only to the largest and most important monasteries. In Ukraine, only a handful of communities hold it. The Holy Mountains received this rank in the early 2000s, joining the great lavras of Kyiv and Pochaiv.

But the story begins long before any title. Nobody knows exactly when the first monks came to these cliffs. The soft chalk was easy to carve, and the forest offered isolation. Hermits seem to have cut small cells and passages into the rock. Travellers mentioned a monastery here by the sixteenth century. At that time, this was open frontier steppe. Raiders crossed it regularly, so a hidden life inside a cliff made practical sense as well as spiritual sense.

Those cave passages still exist, and here is the first surprise for many visitors. The chalk hill is not solid. It holds a network of narrow tunnels, small chapels, and old burial niches. Some passages are so low that you must bend your head. The walls are cool and slightly powdery to the touch. Candles provide much of the light. Inside, the sounds of the outside world disappear quickly, and you may hear only footsteps and quiet prayer.

Look up at the very top of the cliff. There, a small church seems to grow straight out of the white rock. It is the Church of Saint Nicholas, and part of it is literally carved into the chalk. From the riverbank, it looks almost impossible, like a white building balanced on a white wave. Climbing up to it means stairs and paths, but the view across the river valley rewards the effort.

The monastery's history has not been one smooth line. In the late eighteenth century, the Russian Empress Catherine the Second took many church lands into state hands. In 1787, the Holy Mountains monastery was closed as part of this policy. For more than half a century, the cliffs stood without their community. Then, in 1844, the monastery was restored, and a long period of building and growth followed.

Much of what you see at ground level comes from that nineteenth century revival. The largest church in the main courtyard is the Dormition Cathedral. Dormition is the Orthodox name for the death and heavenly rising of the Virgin Mary. Its tall, pale walls and clustered domes follow the grand church style of the Russian Empire at that time. Around it stand residential buildings, gates, and smaller churches, arranged between the river and the foot of the cliff.

The monastery also drew writers. The playwright and story writer Anton Chekhov visited the Holy Mountains in 1887. He spent time among the crowds of pilgrims who came for church holidays. The trip gave him material for a story about wandering people met on the road. He described the river, the chalk, and the noise of pilgrims sleeping wherever they could find space. His visit is one reason educated Russian readers knew this place well.

The twentieth century was harder. After the revolution, the Soviet authorities closed the monastery in the early 1920s. The buildings were used for other purposes, including a rest home. Religious life here stopped for about seventy years. In Soviet times, the town even carried a different name. On a hill across the river, a giant concrete statue of the revolutionary known as Artem was raised. It still faces the monastery today, a strange neighbour from a very different belief system.

The monks returned in 1992, soon after Ukraine became independent. Over the following years, they repaired churches, reopened caves, and brought back pilgrimages. Here is a second detail many visitors miss. Higher in the forest, the community built a wooden church in the old northern style, made entirely of logs. For years it was one of the most photographed buildings on the hill, dark timber standing among the pines.

Then came the war. In 2022, Sviatohirsk was close to heavy fighting, and the monastery was hit several times by shelling. Monks, nuns, and lay people sheltered inside the buildings and caves. Some of them were killed. The wooden church in the forest burned. The town was later freed by Ukrainian forces, but the damage remains visible. Broken windows, scarred walls, and repair scaffolding are now part of the scene.

So if you stand here today, you are seeing a place in the middle of recovery. Notice how the old and the new damage sit side by side. Chalk weathered over centuries sits next to fresh cracks from recent years. Gold leaf may shine above a wall that is still waiting for repair. The community continues its daily rhythm of services, bells, and work, even in difficult conditions. Be respectful of that rhythm, and of the people still living here.

For a final moment, walk back toward the water if you can. Turn around and look at the whole picture again. The river at your feet. The forest, dark and green. The white cliff with its small church on top. The domes catching the light. Monks have looked at this same view for at least five centuries, through raids, closures, and war. Listen for the bells, or simply for the river. Then take that quiet with you as you leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current safety advice before travelling near the front line.
Dress modestly; women usually cover their heads inside churches.
Wear sturdy shoes for the cliff stairs and paths.
Duck carefully in the low, narrow cave passages.
Keep voices low during services and ask before photographing monks.
