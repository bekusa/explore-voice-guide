The guide

The story

The ground here is soft and pale, almost like sand. Pine trees stand in loose rows, and the wind moves through their high branches with a dry, steady sound. Underfoot there are needles, cones, and patches of thin grass. It is a quiet forest. For most of its life, it was simply that: a place at the edge of a town where people walked, gathered mushrooms, or passed on their way somewhere else.

This guide is a little different from most. You are not at a monument to a distant age. You are at a place connected to events from very recent years, in the war that followed Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. The burial site lies in a pine forest on the outskirts of Izium. If you came here from Sviatohirsk, you have travelled through the same wider landscape of rivers, chalk hills, and forests along the Siverskyi Donets.

So please take what follows at your own pace. If you would rather stand in silence for a while, that is a fitting way to be here too. What we can do together is understand, carefully and honestly, what happened in this place, and why so many people from Ukraine and beyond have come to stand where you are standing now.

In the spring of 2022, Russian forces took control of Izium after weeks of heavy fighting. The town sits on an important route through the region, and much of it was badly damaged. For months, the people who remained lived under occupation. Communication with the rest of Ukraine was cut or very limited, and families outside the town often did not know if their relatives were alive.

In September 2022, Ukrainian forces regained the town during a rapid counteroffensive across the Kharkiv region. Soon afterwards, investigators came to this forest. They found a large burial area: hundreds of graves, most of them marked with simple wooden crosses. Ukrainian officials reported more than four hundred graves at this site. Many crosses carried only a number instead of a name.

The exhumations began that same month. Forensic teams, police, and prosecutors worked among the trees, often in protective suits, while journalists and international observers watched. Ukrainian authorities stated that most of the people buried here were civilians, including women, children, and elderly residents. Some died during the shelling and the hardship of the occupation. Ukrainian investigators also reported that some bodies showed signs of violent death. A separate mass grave held Ukrainian soldiers.

It is worth pausing on what this work means. Each grave was opened so that a person could be identified, so that a family could know, and so that evidence could be gathered. Relatives came to the forest and to the morgues to give samples for identification. Some found the people they were looking for. Some are still waiting. The investigations by Ukrainian and international bodies into what happened here have continued long after the trees went quiet again.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Not every grave in this forest held a victim of violence in the narrow sense. Some people died because there was no medicine, no hospital care, or no way to leave. With the regular cemetery hard to reach and the town under fire, neighbours and local workers sometimes buried the dead wherever they could. The forest held both kinds of loss: deliberate violence and the slower damage of war.

A second detail is easy to miss. The numbers on the crosses were not meant to erase anyone. In many cases, they were a record, a way to match a grave to a list, so that a name might return later. That quiet act of keeping track, in a time of chaos, later helped investigators and families. A number on a wooden board became a path back to a person.

The town itself carries the same story in its streets. Izium is an old settlement, overlooked by a high hill above the river, and it was once a small, ordinary regional town with schools, markets, and apartment blocks. Much of that was hit during 2022. Sviatohirsk, too, was occupied and damaged, including parts of its monastery complex below the white chalk cliffs. Across this region, rebuilding and remembering happen side by side.

If you visit, treat the forest as a cemetery, because that is what it is. Speak quietly. Do not step on or between graves, and do not move objects that people have left behind. Flowers, candles, photographs, and small toys may rest at the foot of a cross. Families still come here. Your presence is welcome, but this place belongs first to them and to the people buried in this soil.

There is also a practical side. This region remains close to the war, and much of the Kharkiv area has been contaminated with mines and unexploded ordnance. Stay on clear paths and marked roads. Follow every sign and every instruction from local authorities or police. Before you travel, check the current security situation and whether access to the site is permitted. Conditions can change quickly, and local advice matters more than any guide.

As you stand here, notice the ordinary things. The light falls between the pine trunks in narrow bands. The sand is pale where the earth was turned. The crosses are made of plain wood, and the wood weathers grey with time. Birds still call from the canopy. Nothing about this forest looks dramatic, and perhaps that is the point. Terrible events often happen in places that look exactly like this.

When you are ready to leave, take a moment at the edge of the trees. Look back at the rows of crosses and the tall, straight pines above them. Each mark in the ground stands for one life, one name, one family. Carry that with you as you walk back toward the town, past the people who live here still, and who chose to rebuild.
