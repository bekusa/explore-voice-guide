The guide

The story

Start with the feel of the stone under your hand. It is warm, slightly rough, and worn smooth at the edges where people sat for centuries. Above you, the curved rows of seats climb the hillside in a great half circle. Below, the stage area opens toward the city. Listen for a moment. A voice from the stage floor still carries surprisingly well up these steps, which is exactly what the Roman builders intended.

You are standing in one of the most remarkable rediscoveries in modern Spain. For a very long time, nobody in Cartagena knew this theatre was here. It lay buried under houses, streets and later buildings, hidden in plain sight in the heart of the old town. Then, in 1988, excavations for a new regional crafts centre uncovered the first traces. What began as a routine dig turned into years of careful work. Slowly, an entire Roman theatre came back into the light.

To understand why a theatre this size was built here, think about the city itself. Cartagena was founded by the Carthaginian general Hasdrubal in the late third century before Christ, and it was called Qart Hadasht, meaning New City. After Rome took it during the war with Carthage, the Romans called it Carthago Nova. Its deep, sheltered harbour and nearby silver mines made it one of the most valuable ports in Roman Spain.

The theatre dates to the time of the emperor Augustus, at the very end of the first century before Christ. This was a period when Rome used architecture as a message. A grand theatre told everyone that this town belonged to the Roman world — its culture, its order, its loyalty to the emperor. Here is the first surprise. Inscriptions found during the excavation show the building was dedicated to Gaius and Lucius Caesar, the two grandsons Augustus had adopted as his heirs.

That dedication carries a quiet sadness. Augustus planned for these young men to succeed him. Both died young, years before the old emperor himself. So this theatre honours a future that never happened. When you look at the altars and inscriptions in the museum, remember that they were carved with great hope, for two princes who would never rule.

Now look at how the seating is built. The Romans did not construct every row from scratch. They used the natural slope of the hill, cutting part of the seating directly into the rock. This saved material and gave the structure a solid base. The seating is divided into sections, with the best places closest to the stage. In Roman society, where you sat showed your rank, and the front rows were kept for the city's leading citizens.

The theatre could hold several thousand spectators. Imagine those rows full, the crowd moving, talking and calling out. Performances might include comedies, tragedies, mime and music. Behind the stage rose the scaenae frons — the tall decorated wall that served as a permanent backdrop. It had two levels of columns, and some of them have been raised again, so you can picture its height and rhythm.

Notice the colours in the stone. The column shafts are a warm, reddish stone, while the bases and capitals are pale marble. Some of that marble was brought from Italy, which shows how much money and effort went into this project. The contrast between red and white was deliberate. Under the strong Mediterranean sun, it would have made the stage wall glow.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it. Look up at the top of the seating. Above the theatre stand the ruined walls of an old church, often called the Old Cathedral, Santa María la Vieja. For centuries, that church sat right on top of the Roman seats, and nobody realised what lay beneath. Its broken walls, damaged in the twentieth century, now frame the theatre like a second stage.

The theatre did not simply fall silent and disappear. In late Roman times, as the city changed, the building lost its original purpose. Its stones were reused, and a market area was built over part of it. Later still, houses spread across the site. Layer by layer, the city grew over the theatre. Archaeologists had to read all these layers carefully before they could reach the Roman level.

The way you arrive here is part of the story. The museum was designed by the Spanish architect Rafael Moneo and opened in 2008. Visitors begin near the town hall square and move through modern galleries, then pass underground, beneath streets and old buildings. You travel through the city's history in the same direction the archaeologists did. Then, at the end, the theatre opens up in front of you.

Take a moment to look beyond the stage. From the upper rows, you can see the rooftops of the old town and, in the distance, hints of the harbour. That harbour is the reason this city mattered to Carthage, to Rome, and to every power that came later. The theatre and the port belong to the same story of a city facing the sea.

Before you leave, sit down on one of the stone rows. Feel how high and steep they are, and how close the stage seems. Notice the sounds of the modern city drifting in — traffic, voices, perhaps church bells. For centuries, this place was forgotten under people's homes. Now you can sit where Roman citizens once sat, and look out at the same harbour light.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the museum galleries before reaching the theatre itself.
Climb to the upper rows for views over the harbour.
Wear shoes with grip for the steep stone steps.
Bring water and a hat on sunny summer afternoons.
Test the acoustics by speaking softly from the stage.
