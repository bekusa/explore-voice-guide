The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Below you, the Menai Strait moves fast between Anglesey and the Welsh mainland. The water is green-grey, and the tide pulls hard through the narrows. Above it, traffic runs steadily across a high road deck. Every so often, a train passes beneath that road with a long, metallic rumble. Two journeys share one bridge. Two centuries of engineering sit stacked on top of each other.

This is the Britannia Bridge. It links the island of Anglesey to the rest of Wales, a short distance from the village with the famously long name. The bridge takes its name from Britannia Rock, a small outcrop in the middle of the strait. That rock gave the engineers a footing in deep, restless water. The great central tower stands on it today, and the whole structure balances around that one point of stone.

To understand why the bridge exists, picture the middle of the nineteenth century. Railways were spreading fast across Britain. A line was being built along the North Wales coast towards Holyhead, the port for ferries to Ireland. Speed to Dublin mattered for mail, for trade, and for government. But the Menai Strait stood in the way. Thomas Telford's road suspension bridge already crossed it, a little way along the shore. Trains, however, needed something far stiffer and stronger.

The engineer given the problem was Robert Stephenson, son of the railway pioneer George Stephenson. The Admiralty insisted that tall sailing ships must still pass underneath. That ruled out a low arch, and it ruled out scaffolding in the channel. Stephenson's answer was bold. He proposed enormous rectangular tubes of wrought iron, and the trains would run inside them. A tube like this works as a hollow beam. Its walls and its top and bottom carry the load together.

Stephenson did not work alone. He tested his ideas with the engineer William Fairbairn and the scientist Eaton Hodgkinson. They built models and loaded them until they failed, then strengthened the weak points. The top of the tube was given a cellular structure, a row of small boxes, because it had a habit of crumpling. This careful testing was unusual for its time. In many ways, it helped shape how engineers think about iron and steel beams today.

The building itself was a piece of theatre. The largest tubes were assembled on the shore. Then they were floated out on pontoons and steered into position between the towers. After that, hydraulic presses lifted them slowly upwards, a small distance at a time. Workers packed masonry beneath each tube as it rose, so a failure would not send it crashing down. The bridge opened to trains in 1850, and for a time it was one of the great engineering marvels of the age.

Now look towards the ends of the bridge, close to the railway level. Here you can find one of the details most visitors miss. Four large lions, carved from limestone, guard the approaches, two at each end. They are the work of the sculptor John Thomas. They lie in calm, heavy poses, and they are huge — far larger than they first appear from a distance. Many drivers cross the bridge every day without ever knowing that the lions are there beneath them.

The lions have also entered local culture. There is a well-known rhyme in Welsh about them. Its words describe four fat lions without any hair, two on this side and two over there. Children in the area have recited it for generations. It is a small reminder that this bridge is not only an engineering object. For people on both shores, it is part of everyday language and part of home.

For more than a century, the iron tubes did their work. Then, in May 1970, disaster struck. A fire started inside one of the tubes, and the cause is widely reported as boys exploring with a burning torch of paper. The tubes had a protective roof coated with tar, and the flames spread quickly along it. Firefighters could not easily reach the heart of the blaze. By the end, the heat had damaged the wrought iron so badly that the original tubes could not be saved.

What followed was a careful rebuilding. Engineers kept Stephenson's stone towers, which had survived. Beneath the old line of the tubes, they added steel arches to carry the load in a new way. Rail traffic returned in the early 1970s. Then came a second idea. A road deck was built on top, above the railway, and it opened in 1980. Today it carries the main North Wales road, the A55, across the strait.

So the bridge you see now is a layered thing. The towers are Victorian stone. The arches are twentieth-century steel. The trains run on the lower level, and the cars run above. Look at the towers and you can still see the tall openings where the tubes once passed through. Those openings now frame the railway. They show the outline of the old design, even though the iron tubes themselves are gone.

From the shore, you can compare this bridge with its older neighbour. Telford's suspension bridge hangs from chains, light and graceful, built for horses and carriages. Stephenson's bridge is heavier and more rigid, built for the weight of locomotives. Together, they tell the story of how travel changed in only a few decades. Two bridges, two engineers, and one narrow stretch of water that both of them had to tame.

Before you leave, take one more look at the strait itself. Watch how the current curls around the base of the central tower on Britannia Rock. Notice the rough stone of the piers, darkened where the tide rises and falls. Listen for the next train, and for the steady sound of cars overhead. As you walk away, you will hear both levels of this bridge still at work — Stephenson's old crossing, carrying Wales forward every day.
