The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you walk on. Somewhere behind the trees a bird calls, sharp and strange, a sound that does not belong to the Channel Islands at all. Underfoot the path is soft and damp. The air smells of cut grass, wet leaves, and faintly of animals. This is Jersey Zoo, in the quiet parish of Trinity, and it was never meant to be an ordinary zoo.

To understand this place, you need to meet the man who started it. Gerald Durrell was a British naturalist and writer. Many people know him from his book My Family and Other Animals, a funny and loving account of his childhood on the Greek island of Corfu. Those years filled him with a lifelong fascination for every creature he could find, from beetles to tortoises to scorpions hiding under stones.

As an adult, Durrell travelled on animal-collecting expeditions to Africa and South America. Over time, he grew troubled by what he saw. Forests were shrinking. Species were disappearing quietly, without anyone noticing. He came to believe that zoos should not simply put animals on display. Instead, they should help save species from extinction. That idea, unusual at the time, is the reason this place exists.

Durrell opened his zoo here in 1959, on the grounds of an old manor house called Les Augrès. Look at the granite buildings around the estate. Granite is the stone of Jersey, and you see it in farmhouses and walls all over the island. Its warm, speckled surface catches the light differently through the day. The manor gave Durrell a ready-made home, gardens, and walled spaces for his animals.

A few years later, in 1963, Durrell founded a charity to support the work. Today it is called the Durrell Wildlife Conservation Trust. Here is the first surprise for many visitors: the zoo is only one part of the story. The trust works on conservation projects in other countries, often on islands, where species are especially fragile. The animals you see in Trinity are connected to forests and coastlines thousands of kilometres away.

Keep an eye out for the dodo. It appears as the trust's emblem, and the choice is deliberate. The dodo was a large, flightless bird from Mauritius, an island in the Indian Ocean. It was wiped out after people arrived. For Durrell, the dodo was a warning. It stood for every species that had vanished because nobody acted in time. Using it as a symbol was his way of saying: never again.

Mauritius became one of the trust's most important places of work. Birds such as the Mauritius kestrel and the pink pigeon fell to tiny numbers in the twentieth century. Conservationists, including teams linked to Durrell, worked to breed and protect them. Both species are still with us today. When people talk about this zoo, they often mention these birds, because they show that extinction is not always the end of the story.

As you walk the paths, notice how the enclosures are designed. Many use plants, water, and open ground rather than bare cages. Durrell cared about how animals behaved, not just how they looked. He wanted them to forage, climb, and live in social groups. You may find yourself waiting a while to spot an animal. That waiting is part of the experience. Patience here is often rewarded with a quiet, close moment.

Now for a second surprise, and it involves a gorilla. In 1986, a young boy fell into the gorilla enclosure here. A large male gorilla named Jambo stood over him. He stood calmly, and he seemed to keep the other gorillas away. The moment was filmed, and the footage travelled around the world. Many people who saw it changed how they thought about gorillas. A creature often shown as a monster had behaved with gentleness.

Durrell wrote about life at the zoo in his own books, with the same humour he brought to his childhood stories. He described escaped animals, difficult guests, and the endless work of keeping a zoo running on very little money. Reading those stories after a visit adds a new layer. You begin to picture the early years here, when the trees were younger and everything felt like an experiment.

The zoo also became a place of learning. Over the decades, the trust has trained conservationists from many countries. They come to study animal care, breeding, and field methods, then return home to protect wildlife in their own regions. This may be the most lasting part of Durrell's work. It does not show up in any single enclosure. It lives on in people working in forests and on islands far from Jersey.

Gerald Durrell died in 1995. His wife, Lee Durrell, a zoologist herself, continued to play an important role in the trust's work. The organisation has changed its name and its look over the years, but the central idea has stayed the same. Each animal here is a reminder that a species can be saved, if people care enough and act early enough.

Take a moment before you leave. Look up at the trees moving above the paths. Listen again for those calls that do not belong to Jersey. Notice the granite walls, the children pressing close to the glass, the keepers walking past with buckets. All of it began with one man who loved animals and refused to accept their loss. As you walk back toward the gate, the dodo is still watching... a quiet reminder of what this place is for.
