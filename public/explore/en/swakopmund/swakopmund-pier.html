The guide

The story

Wooden boards knock under your feet. Between the gaps, green water rises and falls. Every few seconds a swell rolls in, lifts against the steel legs below you, and breaks toward the beach. Walking the Swakopmund pier means walking out over the open Atlantic, on a structure the sea has tried to claim for more than a century.

Swakopmund has no natural harbor. That single fact shaped the town's whole early history. Ships had to anchor offshore in the swell, and people and cargo came in through the surf in small boats. Landings were slow, wet and sometimes deadly. A young port town needed something better.

The first answer was the Mole, a stone harbor wall begun in 1899 a short walk north of here. It failed quickly. The current along this coast carries sand, and within a few years the sheltered basin had silted up. The town needed a structure the sand could not choke. The answer was to build straight out over the waves.

In 1905 workers completed a wooden jetty on this spot. Cranes lifted goods from boats below. Passengers climbed up from the swell onto solid planks. For a young town at the edge of a desert, the jetty was a lifeline. Almost everything Swakopmund used came over water, and much of it crossed these few hundred meters of timber. Old photographs show the deck crowded with trolleys, ropes and workers in heavy coats, with fog hiding the ships completely.

The sea pushed back. Waves strained the structure, and marine borers, small creatures that eat wood, tunneled through the piles. Within a few years the wooden jetty was weakening under its own work. A stronger answer was needed, and this time it would be steel.

In 1911 construction began on an iron jetty beside the wooden one. The plans were ambitious, a long, strong arm reaching far into deeper water. Riveted steel legs went down into the seabed, and the deck crept outward season by season. You are standing on that ambition now.

Then history interrupted. In 1914 the First World War reached even this far coast, and construction stopped. The steel jetty never grew to its planned length. After the war, shipping moved to the deep water port at Walvis Bay, down the coast. The jetty's working life ended almost before it began.

For decades afterward, the structure simply endured. Anglers cast lines from its deck. Couples walked out in the evenings. Salt and rust worked on the steel year after year, and at times the jetty became unsafe and was closed. A town landmark was slowly dissolving into the sea air.

Local people refused to let it go. A campaign raised money, and in the 2000s the jetty was restored, with a solid walkway and lighting. At its far end now stands a glass-walled restaurant that takes its name from the year 1905. You can eat dinner above the waves where crane hooks once swung cargo.

Today the walk out is the attraction itself. Go at sunset if you can. The sun drops straight into the Atlantic, and the boards turn gold, then grey. Anglers stand along the rails with buckets and patient faces. Cormorants and gulls ride the wind beside you. Sometimes dolphins pass through the swell beyond the breakers.

Mornings have a different mood. Fog often sits low over the water, and the far end of the jetty fades into white. The air smells of salt and kelp. By late morning the fog burns off, and the water turns a hard, bright blue. The same walk gives you two different worlds in one day.

At the far end, stop and look west. There is nothing between you and the open ocean for a very long way. The cold Benguela Current runs past this coast, thick with life, and it makes the fog that rolls over the town at night. Under your feet, the swell that started far out at sea finally ends.

A pier is a strange kind of monument. It leads nowhere except out and back. But this one carried a town through its hungriest years, survived a war, woodworm, rust and neglect, and still stands in the surf. Walk back slowly toward the lights of Swakopmund, and feel the boards give slightly with each wave. The sea is still testing them. They are still holding.
