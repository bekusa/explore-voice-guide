The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through dry grass. Under your shoes, the ground is dusty and pale. Ahead of you, a single brick tower stands alone in a wide, flat valley. Behind it, the snow on the mountains to the south catches the light. There is no city around the tower now. And yet, a city once stood here... a capital of merchants, scholars, and kings.

This is Burana Tower, in the Chuy Valley of northern Kyrgyzstan, not far from the town of Tokmok. It is a minaret, a tower from which the call to prayer was once given. Most historians link this site with Balasagun, a capital of the Karakhanids, a Turkic dynasty that ruled much of Central Asia around a thousand years ago. The tower is usually dated to roughly the eleventh century. It is one of the few large structures from that city still standing above the ground.

The Karakhanids matter here. They were among the first Turkic dynasties to adopt Islam as a state religion. Under their rule, this valley sat on one branch of the Silk Road. Caravans moved east and west through it, carrying silk, silver, horses, and ideas. A minaret like this one did two jobs. It called the faithful to prayer. It also told every traveller crossing the plain that a city of faith and power was close.

Now look at the shape. The tower rises from a square base, then turns into a round shaft that narrows slightly as it climbs. The top looks cut off — and it is. Earthquakes over the centuries brought down the upper part. Today the tower stands at roughly half of its original height. Picture it once nearly twice as tall, a thin brick needle over the valley.

Get close to the brickwork. The builders used fired clay bricks, set in horizontal bands. Some bands are laid flat. Others are set at angles to form zigzags and diamond patterns. From a distance, these bands look like simple stripes. Up close, they become a kind of woven texture in brick. It is decoration made with no paint and no tile, only with the way each brick is turned.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The lower section of the tower looks newer and cleaner than the upper parts. That is because restorers worked on the tower during the Soviet era, in the twentieth century. They rebuilt and strengthened sections of the base. As you walk around it, try to find the line where newer brick meets older brick. Small changes in colour and in the joints between bricks show where the repair work ends.

You will also notice that the entrance is not at ground level. A metal staircase climbs the outside of the tower to a doorway well above the ground. Inside, a narrow spiral staircase winds up through the dark core. The steps are steep and worn, and the passage is tight. At the top, you come out onto a small platform with the whole valley below you. It is a short climb, but it feels like a passage between two worlds.

There is a story that locals tell about this tower. Legend has it that a khan was warned by a fortune teller that his daughter would die on her sixteenth birthday. To protect her, he built a tall tower and kept her inside it, away from all danger. On her birthday, he brought her a basket of grapes. Hidden in the fruit was a spider — or in some versions, a scorpion. It bit her, and the prophecy came true. This is folklore, not history, but you will hear it often here.

The name itself has a debated origin. Some people say that Burana comes from a local form of the word munara, which means minaret. That explanation is popular, but it is not certain. What is certain is that the name has outlived the city. Balasagun faded after the Mongol conquests and later upheavals. Its mud brick walls slowly returned to the earth, leaving low mounds in the fields around you.

Walk out across the site, and you will see a field of standing stones. These are balbals — carved stone grave markers made by Turkic peoples of the steppe, mostly before the Karakhanid period. Many show a face, a moustache, and hands holding a cup at the chest. They were collected from across the region and brought here for display. Each one once stood alone on the open grassland, often facing east toward the rising sun.

Look closely at the faces on the balbals. Each one is different. Some are worn almost smooth by wind and rain. Others still show sharp eyebrows and a belt at the waist. Scholars still debate who they show exactly — honoured ancestors, warriors, or defeated enemies. Nearby you can also find the remains of mausoleum foundations, and stones with carved inscriptions. The open-air display feels less like a museum and more like a gathering of very old faces.

This city also has a link to literature. The poet Yusuf Balasaguni, whose name means Yusuf of Balasagun, wrote a long poem in the eleventh century called Kutadgu Bilig, often translated as The Wisdom That Brings Good Fortune. It is one of the earliest major works in a Turkic language. It offers advice on how a ruler should govern with justice. Kyrgyzstan honours him today. You may even recognise his face from the national banknotes.

Before you leave, there is a small museum on the site. It shows objects found in the excavations — pottery, coins, tools, and fragments of daily life. Take a few minutes inside. After the open sky, the small rooms help you picture the people who once lived here. Then step back outside and look at the tower again. It feels different once you know what surrounded it.

Turn slowly in a full circle now. To the south, the mountains. Around you, low grassy mounds where streets and houses once stood. In front of you, the brick tower with its broken top. Listen to the wind in the grass again. As you walk back toward the road, glance over your shoulder once more. The tower will grow smaller behind you, still standing, as it has for about a thousand years.
