The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves turn over pebbles at the edge of the bay, and a breeze moves through the dry scrub on the hillside. The limestone above you is pale and rough, streaked with grey and rust. The air at the mouth of the cave feels cooler than the beach behind you. You are standing at the entrance to one of the longest records of human life anywhere in Europe.

This is Franchthi Cave, on the southern edge of the Argolid peninsula in the Peloponnese. It looks across a quiet bay toward the fishing village of Koilada. From the outside, it seems modest. A wide opening in a limestone headland, a few low trees, the glitter of the water. But inside this hollow, people came and went, lived and died, across tens of thousands of years.

How long? Archaeologists believe humans first used the cave in the Upper Palaeolithic, the later part of the Old Stone Age, possibly around forty thousand years ago. The last major occupation came in the Neolithic, the New Stone Age, ending around 3000 BCE. Think about that span. People returned to this shelter for longer than the whole of recorded history since the pyramids. Generation after generation, the same rock sheltered them.

Most of what we know comes from excavations led by the American archaeologist Thomas Jacobsen, of Indiana University, between 1967 and 1979. His team dug carefully through deep layers of soil, ash, shells and bone. Each layer held the rubbish of daily life. Burnt seeds. Stone tools. Fish bones. The team sieved huge volumes of earth. That patient work turned this cave into a reference point for scholars who study prehistoric Greece and the wider Mediterranean.

Now look out at the bay. Here is the first surprise. When people first sheltered here, this water was not here. During the last Ice Age, so much water was locked in ice sheets that the sea stood far lower than today. The coast lay several kilometres away, across a plain of grass and marsh. Hunters would have looked out from this entrance over open land. Slowly, over thousands of years, the sea rose and crept toward the cave mouth.

The people of the Old Stone Age here hunted wild animals. Red deer and wild horse appear in the early layers, along with wild asses and goats. They made tools from flint and other stone, knapping them into blades and points. Over time, as the land changed, their food changed too. Snails, shellfish and wild plants became more important. The cave shows a community adapting to a world that was slowly warming around them.

Then comes the second surprise, and it involves a black, glassy stone called obsidian. Obsidian forms from volcanic lava. In the Aegean, a major source is the island of Melos, far out across open sea. Pieces of Melian obsidian appear in layers here dating to roughly eleven thousand years ago, and perhaps earlier. That means people were crossing stretches of open water by boat, long before farming reached Greece. It is some of the earliest evidence for seafaring in the Mediterranean.

The sea also offered food. In the Mesolithic, the Middle Stone Age between the hunters and the first farmers, the people here began to catch large fish. Big tuna bones appear in these layers. Catching tuna takes skill, timing and knowledge of the seasons, when the great shoals pass along the coast. So imagine small boats setting out from this shore, and the catch hauled up to the fires inside this cave.

Franchthi also holds a quieter, more human story. Excavators found burials here, including a Mesolithic grave of a young man. Some remains show signs of careful treatment by the living. These finds remind us that this was not only a workshop or a kitchen. It was a home, and sometimes a resting place. People grieved here. They laid their dead in the ground beneath the rock, close to where they cooked and slept.

Around the eighth millennium before Christ, life changed again. Domesticated sheep and goats appear, along with cultivated wheat and barley. Pottery arrives too. These are the marks of the Neolithic, when farming spread into Europe from western Asia. At Franchthi, you can follow this shift layer by layer in one deep sequence. Archaeologists also found that settlement spread beyond the cave to the old shoreline nearby, an area they call Paralia, meaning 'by the sea'.

As you step inside, notice the scale of the main chamber. Parts of the roof have collapsed over the ages, and great fallen blocks lie on the floor. Light falls through the openings and moves across the walls during the day. The trenches of the excavations have been backfilled, so you will not see open pits. Instead, you see the space itself. Stand still for a moment, and the cave feels very large and very calm.

The walk here is part of the experience. Many visitors come along the coast from Koilada, past olive groves and low stone walls. Others cross the bay by small boat from the village harbour. Either way, you approach slowly, with the headland growing in front of you. The surroundings are simple and quiet. Fishing boats, goats on the slopes, the smell of thyme and salt. Very little here suggests a famous site, and that is part of its charm.

Before you leave, turn back to face the water one more time. Picture the open plain that once lay there, the hunters watching it, and the slow rise of the sea over thousands of years. Then look at the bay as it is now, with the village across the water and the boats at rest. The same rock that sheltered those first people is still at your back. As you walk away, listen again to the pebbles at the shoreline.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the coastal path from Koilada for the best approach.
Ask in Koilada harbour about small boat crossings.
Wear sturdy shoes for rocky ground inside the cave.
Bring water and sun protection for the exposed walk.
Visit in morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
