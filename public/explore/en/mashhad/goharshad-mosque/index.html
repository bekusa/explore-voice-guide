The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The stone underfoot is cool and smooth, polished by countless pilgrims who walked here before you. Around you, the air carries rosewater and the low murmur of prayer. Then lift your eyes. Every wall of this courtyard is dressed in glazed tile. Turquoise, cobalt, saffron yellow and white catch the light at once. You are standing in the Goharshad Mosque, in the heart of Mashhad, inside the sacred complex that surrounds the shrine of Imam Reza.

This mosque is older than most of the buildings around it. It was completed in the early fifteenth century, around 1418. That makes it six hundred years old. The shrine complex has grown and changed many times since then. New courtyards, new halls and new gates have risen on every side. But this mosque has held its place. It is one of the finest surviving works of the Timurid era, the period when the descendants of the conqueror Timur ruled much of Iran and Central Asia.

Here is the first surprise. The mosque takes its name from a woman. Goharshad was the wife of Shah Rukh, the Timurid ruler, and she was one of the great patrons of her age. She paid for this mosque herself. She also commissioned buildings in Herat, in present-day Afghanistan, where her court was based. In a time when power was written in men's names, her name is still spoken here every day, by people who come to pray.

The architect is remembered as well. Records name him as Qavam al-Din Shirazi, a master builder from Shiraz who worked for the Timurid court. His design follows the classic Persian plan. Look around the courtyard and count the great arched halls, one in the middle of each side. There are four of them. Persians call such a vaulted hall an iwan — a tall arch, open on one side, like a stage facing the courtyard.

Now turn toward the largest iwan, the one that faces the direction of Mecca. This is the heart of the mosque. Behind it rises a swollen, turquoise dome. Two slender minarets stand on either side of the arch, framing it like the posts of a giant gateway. The proportions are carefully balanced. The arch pulls your eye upward, the minarets hold it in place, and the dome finishes the view against the sky.

Look closely at the band of writing around this great arch. It is a monumental inscription in elegant Arabic script, white letters on deep blue. Here is the second surprise. The calligraphy is credited to Baysunghur, the son of Goharshad. He was a prince, but he was also a skilled calligrapher and a famous patron of books and manuscripts. So mother and son are both present in this one wall — she paid for it, and his hand designed the words.

Step closer to any wall and the tiles change. From a distance they read as one surface of colour. Up close, you see thousands of separate pieces. Some are tile mosaic, where craftsmen cut small shapes from single-coloured glazed tiles and fitted them together like a puzzle. Others are painted tiles, with several colours fired onto one square. Persian builders called this second style haft rangi, which means seven colours. Both methods appear across the courtyard.

Notice the patterns themselves. There are no human figures here, which is normal in mosque decoration. Instead you see flowing vines, flowers that never existed in any garden, and endless geometric stars. The stars are built from simple shapes that repeat and interlock. Follow one line with your eye and it rarely seems to end. Many visitors find that these patterns slow their breathing. That effect is part of the purpose — the walls invite calm and attention.

This courtyard has also seen dark days. In 1935, crowds gathered here to protest against the policies of Reza Shah, the ruler of Iran at the time. His government was pushing rapid modernisation, including new rules about how men must dress. The protest inside the mosque ended in violence when troops moved against the crowd. Many people died. For Iranians, the name Goharshad still carries the memory of that event, alongside its beauty.

The mosque has also needed care over the centuries. Earthquakes, weather and conflict have all damaged parts of the complex. Some tiles you see today are original Timurid work. Others are later repairs and careful replacements. Restorers try to match colours and patterns, but the eye can sometimes find small differences. A slightly brighter blue, or a crisper line, often marks newer work. The building is not frozen in time. It is maintained like a living place, because it is one.

And it is very much alive. Mashhad is one of the most important pilgrimage cities in the Shia Muslim world. Millions of people come each year to visit the shrine of Imam Reza, the eighth imam of Twelver Shia Islam. Many of them pray in this mosque. You may see families resting on carpets, older men reading quietly, and children running between the arches. At prayer times, the courtyard fills, and rows of worshippers stretch across the stone.

A few words about being a good guest here. This is an active holy site, so modest dress is expected for everyone. Women are required to wear a chador, the full-length cloak, inside the shrine complex, and these are often available at the entrances. Rules for visitors, especially non-Muslims, can change, and some areas may be restricted. Volunteer guides for international visitors work in the complex. Ask the staff at the gate, and follow their lead.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment in the middle of the courtyard. Let the sound settle around you — footsteps, soft voices, perhaps the call to prayer. Look once more at the great arch and its blue band of writing. Then watch how the colour of the tiles shifts as the light moves across them. As you walk out through the gates, the city returns. But that turquoise dome will stay above the rooftops behind you.
