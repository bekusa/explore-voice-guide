The guide

The story

Take a moment to feel the ground under your feet. The marble is cool and smooth. Thousands of bare and stockinged feet have polished it. Around you there is a low, steady sound. It comes from murmured prayers, soft footsteps, pigeons, and now and then a child laughing. Above the courtyard walls, a dome covered in gold catches the light. On a clear day it glows so brightly that people shade their eyes to look at it.

You are standing in the heart of Mashhad, in northeastern Iran. This central part of the city is called the Samen District. The name comes from one of the titles of the man buried here. In Arabic, the name Mashhad means a place of martyrdom. Put simply, the whole city takes its name from this one tomb. Few cities in the world are so closely tied to a single grave.

The man at the centre of it all is Ali ibn Musa al-Rida, known in Iran as Imam Reza. Shia Muslims count him as the eighth of their twelve Imams. Shia Islam sees these Imams as the rightful spiritual leaders after the Prophet Muhammad. He died in the year 818, in a small village near the old town of Tus. Shia tradition holds that he was poisoned on the orders of the caliph al-Ma'mun. For believers, that makes him a martyr.

Here is a detail most visitors never hear. Imam Reza was buried beside an earlier grave — the grave of the caliph Harun al-Rashid. Many people know that name from the tales of the Thousand and One Nights. Harun was al-Ma'mun's father, and he had died in this same area some years before. So two very different men lay side by side. Over time, the caliph's grave faded from memory, and the Imam's grave became the centre of a city.

The shrine did not appear all at once. It grew over more than a thousand years. Rulers built, raiders damaged, and later rulers rebuilt. Each dynasty added rooms, courtyards, and decoration. The Safavid dynasty, which ruled Iran from the early sixteenth century, made Shia Islam the state religion. They invested heavily here. According to a well-known account, the Safavid ruler Shah Abbas walked all the way from Isfahan to Mashhad on foot as an act of devotion.

One of the oldest and most beautiful parts of the complex is the Goharshad Mosque. It was completed in 1418. Its patron was Goharshad, the wife of the Timurid ruler Shah Rukh. The Timurids were the dynasty founded by the conqueror Timur, and they loved monumental tilework. Look for the great iwans here. An iwan is a tall vaulted hall, open on one side to the courtyard. Deep blue and turquoise tiles cover them, with white calligraphy running along the edges.

The golden dome marks the tomb chamber itself. Beneath it, the grave sits inside a metal enclosure called a zarih. A zarih is an ornate latticed cage, often of silver and gold. Pilgrims press forward to touch it, to hold its bars, to tie a thread or whisper a request. The crowd near the zarih can be dense and emotional. Some people weep. Others stand very still. For many Iranians, reaching this cage is the high point of a lifetime.

Around the tomb, the complex opens into a series of vast courtyards. In Persian, a courtyard is called a sahn. Some are centuries old, with tiled arcades and carved wooden doors. Others were added in recent decades as the number of pilgrims grew. In the old courtyard, look for the small golden pavilion over a water fountain. It is called a saqqa-khaneh, a place where water is offered freely to anyone who is thirsty.

Now listen. If you are here near sunrise or sunset, you may hear drums and horns from a high balcony. This is the naqareh khaneh, a kind of ceremonial music house. The tradition of playing music to mark the day goes back many generations. The sound is deep and slow, almost like a heartbeat. It cuts through the noise of the city outside and tells everyone that the day is turning.

The second surprise is a small object. Watch the shrine's servants, called khadems. Many of them carry long, colourful feather dusters. They do not use them only for cleaning. They also use them to gently guide crowds, tapping the air to show people where to move. Serving here is a great honour. Some families have held these roles for generations, and many people volunteer for years to do it.

The shrine is not only a place of solemn prayer. It is also a place of daily life. Families sit on carpets in the courtyards and share tea and food. Elderly pilgrims rest in quiet corners. Children chase each other between the columns. The shrine also serves free meals to pilgrims, and an invitation to eat here is treasured. Many Iranians save for years to travel here, often by long overnight journeys from distant provinces.

The complex is run by a large religious foundation called Astan Quds Razavi. Beyond the courtyards, it looks after a library and several museums. The collections include manuscripts, old Qurans, carpets, and objects given by pilgrims and rulers over the centuries. Earlier versions of the zarih are sometimes on display. These museums are a calmer way to understand the shrine, away from the crowds near the tomb.

A few practical notes will help your visit go smoothly. Women are asked to wear a chador, a full-length cloak, and these are often available to borrow at the entrances. Bags are usually checked at the gates. Non-Muslim visitors are often welcome in the courtyards, but access to the inner tomb rooms is restricted. An office for international visitors can sometimes arrange a guide. Photography rules vary, so ask before you raise your camera.

Before you move on, stand still for one more minute. Look at the gold of the dome, then at the blue tiles, then at the faces of the people around you. Some have travelled for days to be here. Some come every week. As you walk back toward the city streets, notice how the sound changes — from prayer to traffic. Notice how the dome stays visible above the rooftops, long after you have left the courtyard.
