The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in the open square and look at the long stone front ahead of you. The limestone is the colour of honey, warm even on a grey day. Low jets of water rise from the paving and fall back again, and children often run through them. Behind you, there are footsteps, café chatter, and the soft echo of voices off stone walls. This is the Grandmaster's Palace, and for more than four centuries power in Malta has lived behind this plain façade.

To understand why the palace is here, start with a siege. In 1565, a huge Ottoman force attacked Malta. The defenders were the Knights of the Order of Saint John, a religious and military order of nobles from across Catholic Europe. The siege lasted for months, and the knights held on. Afterwards, they decided to build a new fortified city on this rocky peninsula. Valletta was founded in 1566 and named after their leader, Grandmaster Jean de Valette.

A city needs a heart, and this palace became that heart. The design is usually linked to Girolamo Cassar, a Maltese military engineer who shaped much of early Valletta. The building grew over time, and later grandmasters added rooms, corridors and courtyards. From here, the head of the Order ruled the islands. It worked almost like a royal court, with ambassadors, councils and ceremonies. It was also a working office for a small but very busy state.

Now look closely at the façade. It is long and fairly plain. There is no great dome and no dramatic tower. The decoration is quiet, with rhythmic windows, solid stone, and a main doorway framed by columns. Notice the wooden balconies that project from the corners. These enclosed balconies are typical of Malta, and you will see them painted in many colours all over the city. On a palace of this rank, they feel almost domestic. That is the first surprise here: the knights showed their power through scale, not through decoration.

When the Order lost Malta at the end of the eighteenth century, the palace did not fall silent. French forces held the island for a short time. Then the British arrived, and Malta became part of their empire. British governors lived and worked inside these walls. So the rooms built for crusading knights later hosted colonial officials, receptions and military planners. The building changed its rulers several times, but it stayed the seat of authority on the island.

After Malta became independent in the twentieth century, the palace kept its central role. It became the office of the President of Malta. For many years, the Maltese parliament also met inside. That arrangement ended in 2015, when parliament moved to a new building near the city gate. Many visitors do not know this: this is not only a museum. Parts of the palace are still used for state business, so some rooms may be closed when official events take place.

If you go inside, the courtyards come first. One of them holds a bronze statue of Neptune, the Roman god of the sea. It is a fitting figure for an island that has always depended on its harbours. Trees and stone arcades soften the space. Here, the noise of the square drops away quickly. Stand still for a minute and you may hear only birds, footsteps on stone, and the quiet sounds of a working building.

In another courtyard, look up for a clock. It is an old mechanical clock, and small figures stand beside the bells. When the hour comes, these figures strike the bells. They are often described as Moorish figures, carved in the style of North African dress. It is the kind of detail that is easy to miss if you hurry past. Children love it, and adults often enjoy it too. Check the time and try to be there on the hour.

Upstairs, long corridors stretch along the building. Their floors are made of patterned marble, and painted ceilings run above them. Coats of arms appear along the walls, belonging to grandmasters and noble families. Each shield was a kind of signature, showing who paid for what. Walk slowly here. The corridors were designed for processions, for people to move in ceremony from one hall to the next. Their length gives you a sense of how important ritual was in this court.

The great hall, often called the Throne Room, holds one of the palace's most important artworks. A painted frieze runs high along the walls and shows scenes from the Great Siege of 1565. It is linked to the Italian painter Matteo Perez d'Aleccio. The scenes include ships, cannon, burning fortifications and soldiers in close combat. For the knights, this was not decoration. It was a constant reminder of the event that justified their city, their order and their rule.

Another room holds a second surprise: a famous set of tapestries made in the French Gobelins workshops in Paris. They show exotic animals, plants and people from distant lands, as Europeans of the time imagined them. There are elephants, birds and tropical fruit, all woven in wool and silk. Remember, these images hung in a council chamber on a small Mediterranean island. They show how far the Order's connections reached, and how much the knights wanted to impress their guests.

The palace also holds an armoury. It is a large collection of weapons and armour from the time of the knights. Suits of steel stand in rows, with helmets, swords and early firearms. Some pieces are richly decorated, while others are plain working gear. Together, they make the history of the siege feel physical and heavy. You can imagine the weight of the metal in the summer heat. Malta was a front line, and this equipment was used.

Back outside, look across the square at the smaller building opposite. It once served as the main guard post. Soldiers here protected the palace entrance, and the square itself was used for parades and public ceremonies. Today the space is more relaxed. People sit at café tables, tourists cross with maps, and local office workers hurry through. The water jets in the paving add movement and sound. The square shows how the old centre of power has become a shared public space.

Before you move on, turn once more to the palace front. Notice how the sun moves across the stone. In the morning, the limestone looks pale. Late in the day, it turns gold, then almost orange. Knights, governors and presidents have all looked out from these windows. Now you stand where crowds once gathered for news and ceremonies. As you walk away down the straight streets of Valletta, keep an eye on the balconies above you. The same honey-coloured stone runs through the whole city.

Practical tips

Arrive in the courtyard on the hour for the clock.
Allow separate time for the armoury collection inside.
Expect some rooms to close during official state events.
Return in late afternoon to see the façade turn gold.
Rest at a café on the square before going inside.
