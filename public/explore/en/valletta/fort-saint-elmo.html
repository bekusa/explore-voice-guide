The guide

The story

Feel the stone under your hand. It is soft, almost warm, the colour of honey or old bread. This is Maltese limestone, and it has taken the sun and salt wind of this headland for centuries. Listen, too. On one side, water slaps against rock in Marsamxett Harbour. On the other, the deeper sound of the Grand Harbour carries ferries, cranes and gulls. You are standing at the very tip of Valletta, where the land runs out.

This is Fort Saint Elmo. Its shape is a star, with sharp points that push outward toward the sea. That shape was not decoration. Each angled point let defenders fire along the face of the next wall, so no attacker could shelter at its foot. From above, the fort looks like a compass rose laid on the rock. From where you stand, it feels like a ship's bow aimed at open water.

The story begins with the Knights of Saint John. They were a religious and military order, founded to care for pilgrims, who later became a sea power. In 1530 they arrived in Malta after losing their earlier island home of Rhodes. They knew the Ottoman Empire would come for them again. In 1552 they raised a fort on this bare peninsula to guard the mouths of both harbours.

At that time, there was no city here. Imagine the hill behind you empty — scrub, rock and wind. The Knights lived across the water in the fishing towns now called the Three Cities. This small fort stood alone on the point, a lookout and a gatekeeper. Its job was simple. Stop enemy fleets from sailing into the harbours, and buy time.

In 1565 that time was tested. A huge Ottoman fleet arrived, and the event became known as the Great Siege of Malta. The Ottoman commanders decided to take Saint Elmo first. They expected it to fall in a few days. Instead, a small garrison of knights and soldiers held out for around a month, under heavy cannon fire from the slopes behind you. The fort finally fell on 23 June 1565.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Dragut, the famous Ottoman corsair and admiral, was mortally wounded during the bombardment of this fort. He was one of the most feared sailors of his age. His loss was a heavy blow to the attackers. And the weeks spent breaking Saint Elmo gave the rest of Malta time to prepare and to wait for help.

The siege is also remembered for its cruelty. Chroniclers of the time recorded that the bodies of fallen knights were sent floating across the harbour toward the Knights' base. According to these accounts, the Grand Master, Jean de Valette, answered by firing the heads of Ottoman prisoners back from his cannons. These stories come from sources written soon after, and they may be coloured by war. But they show how bitter the struggle was.

By September the Ottomans withdrew. Malta had held. Within months, the victorious Grand Master laid the first stone of a new fortified city on this very hill. It took his name — Valletta. The streets you walked to reach this point were planned as a grid, so sea breezes could move through and soldiers could move fast. Saint Elmo was rebuilt and enlarged, and it became the anchor at the city's tip.

Now look at the layers in the walls. Over the following centuries, new defences were added around the old star. Some stone is deeply pitted and grey from weather. Other blocks look fresh and pale. That contrast is real history. The fort was repaired, extended and adapted many times, and a major restoration finished in the last decade gave much of it a cleaner face.

The second great test came much later. Malta became a British colony in the nineteenth century, and the fort served the British forces. On 11 June 1940, just after Italy entered the Second World War, the first air raid on Malta struck. Bombs hit Fort Saint Elmo, and soldiers here were among the first people killed in the island's long ordeal. In July 1941, the fort's guns also helped drive off an Italian attack by small fast boats on the harbour entrance.

That is why part of the fort now holds the National War Museum. Inside, the story runs from ancient times to the twentieth century. One object stops many people. It is the George Cross, a British medal for civilian courage, which King George the Sixth awarded to the whole island of Malta in 1942. You can still see that cross on the Maltese flag today. The museum also displays a Gloster Gladiator biplane named Faith, from the early air defence of the island.

As you walk the parade grounds, notice how quiet it becomes away from the harbour noise. The thick walls block the wind. Swallows and pigeons cut across the open squares. Look for the small church inside the fort, dedicated to Saint Anne, and for the stone steps worn smooth by centuries of boots. On some weekends the fort hosts historical re-enactments, and the ground fills with drums, red tunics and the smell of black powder.

Before you leave, walk to a point on the outer walls and look back toward the land. The whole city rises behind you in pale stone, domes and church bells. To your left and right, the harbours open toward the sea. Everything you can see grew from the decision to hold this one small rock. So pause here for a moment. Feel the wind again. Then, as you walk back into the streets, notice how each one still runs down toward the water — and toward this fort.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow time for the National War Museum inside the fort.
Walk the outer ramparts for views of both harbours.
Bring water and a hat; the open squares get little shade.
Come late afternoon when the limestone turns warm gold.
Check the event calendar for weekend historical re-enactments.
