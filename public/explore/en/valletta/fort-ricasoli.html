The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes in off the open sea here, and you can hear the water break against the rocks below the walls. The stone under your hand is pale and soft, the honey-coloured limestone of Malta, worn smooth and pitted by centuries of salt spray. Across the water, Valletta rises in layers of domes and bastions. You are standing at the mouth of one of the great natural harbours of the Mediterranean, on the side most visitors never reach.

This is Fort Ricasoli. It sits on a narrow point of land at Kalkara, on the eastern side of the entrance to the Grand Harbour. Directly opposite, on the Valletta side, stands Fort Saint Elmo. Together the two forts work like a pair of jaws. Any ship that wanted to enter the harbour had to pass between them, within range of guns on both sides. Once you see that geometry, the whole purpose of this place makes sense.

The fort was built by the Knights of Saint John, the religious and military order that ruled Malta from the sixteenth century until the end of the eighteenth. Work began in 1670. The Knights had already survived the Great Siege of 1565, when an Ottoman army came close to taking the island. Fort Saint Elmo fell during that siege, and the lesson stayed with the Order. The headland where you stand was a weak point. An enemy could land guns here and fire straight into the harbour.

The name comes from a knight called Giovanni Francesco Ricasoli, an Italian member of the Order. He paid a large part of the construction cost from his own money, and in return the fort carries his name. It was a common arrangement among the Knights. Wealthy members funded walls, chapels, and fountains, and their names and coats of arms stayed on the stone long after they died. Here, the honour became the whole fortress.

The design is linked to Antonio Maurizio Valperga, a military engineer from Piedmont in northern Italy who worked for the Order. Look at the shape of the walls. They do not curve. They run in straight lines and sharp angles, with pointed bastions pushing outward. This style is called the bastioned trace, sometimes called the star fort. The angles meant that defenders could fire along the face of every wall, so no attacker could shelter at its foot. It was the most advanced military thinking of its age.

Here is something many visitors miss. Fort Ricasoli is large — by area it is often described as the biggest fort in Malta. Much of the enclosed ground is open space, not buildings. Engineers planned room for barracks, stores, and a garrison large enough to hold out on its own. The work took decades, and the fort was not fully finished until close to the end of the seventeenth century. Malta's fortifications were built slowly, wall by wall, as money allowed.

If you can reach the main gate, take a moment with it. It is the most decorated part of the fort, a baroque entrance with heavy carved stonework, columns, and ornament around the arch. Baroque is the dramatic, richly decorated style of seventeenth-century Europe. The Knights wanted their gates to impress as well as defend. An entrance like this told every visitor who held power here. The plain, angled walls around it make the carving look even bolder.

The fort's darkest chapter came after the Knights had gone. In 1798 Napoleon's French forces took Malta, and soon afterwards the British arrived and stayed. In 1807 a foreign regiment in British service, known as the Froberg Regiment, was stationed here. The soldiers, recruited from many different countries, mutinied and seized the fort. The revolt ended with a powder magazine explosion, a surrender, and executions. It remains one of the strangest episodes in Malta's military history.

Under British rule, Fort Ricasoli stayed in service for well over a century. The British adapted it for newer, heavier guns as warships changed from wooden sails to iron and steam. During the Second World War, Malta was one of the most heavily bombed places on earth. The harbour was a prime target, and this fort took its share of damage. Some of the broken and missing structures you see today date from those raids.

Now for the second surprise. Fort Ricasoli has had a long second life in cinema. Its walls and open grounds have served as film sets for several large historical productions. Parts of ancient Rome were built here for the film Gladiator. Scenes for Troy and for Agora were also filmed on this site. Directors liked the fort for the same reason the Knights did — it is a wide, enclosed space with the sea on almost every side.

Today the fort is quiet, and in many places it is fragile. Weather and salt continue to eat into the soft limestone. Some sections are in poor repair, and access to the interior is often limited or closed. Restoration has been discussed for years. So treat this place gently. Stay on safe ground, keep back from crumbling edges, and remember that a wall that looks solid may not be.

Even from outside the walls, the setting rewards you. Walk along the shore road toward the harbour mouth. You will see the long breakwater reaching into the sea, and fishing boats moving in and out of the harbour. Kalkara itself is a small waterside town with a slower pace than Valletta. Many visitors cross by boat to the Three Cities nearby and never come this far east.

Before you leave, turn and face the water one more time. Look across to Fort Saint Elmo and the walls of Valletta. Imagine a ship coming in from the open sea, with guns watching it from both sides. Then listen again — to the wind, the waves on the rocks, perhaps a gull overhead. The guns are gone. The stone is still here, holding its angles against the sea, as it has for more than three hundred years.

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, rocky ground around the fort.
Combine your visit with a walk through nearby Kalkara.
