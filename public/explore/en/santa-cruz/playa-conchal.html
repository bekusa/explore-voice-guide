Playa Conchal

The story

Bend down and pick up a handful of the beach. It does not feel like ordinary sand. Instead, you hold thousands of tiny shells. They are pink, white, and pale tan. They click softly as they fall through your fingers. Hold them up to the light, and you can see the faint colors of the small creatures that once lived inside them. This is Playa Conchal, and its name says exactly what it is.

The word conchal comes from concha, the Spanish word for shell. Over a very long time, the ocean ground up countless seashells and carried the pieces here. They gathered along the shore in place of normal grains of sand. The result is a beach made almost entirely of broken shells.

Playa Conchal lies on the northern Pacific coast of Costa Rica, in Guanacaste. It sits about thirty minutes north of the town of Tamarindo. The nearest village is Brasilito, a small fishing community just up the shore. Many visitors park in Brasilito and walk the rest of the way. The drive from town is short, but the last stretch of road is rough and unpaved.

That walk is part of the experience. From Brasilito, you follow the shoreline south for about fifteen minutes. The sand under your feet starts dark and firm. Then, as you round a point, it changes. The ground grows pale and begins to glitter. You have reached the shell beach.

Getting to Conchal takes a little effort, and that is part of why it stays calmer than some beaches. The dirt roads nearby can be rough. Many people come by boat tour from Tamarindo instead. Others walk in from Brasilito at low tide, when the sand is widest and the going is easy.

The beach lies between two rocky points. This shape helps to calm the water. On most days the sea here is gentle and clear. The color is a soft aquamarine, somewhere between green and blue. Because the seabed is made of shells and not fine sand, the water stays unusually clear. Children wade in the shallows. Families spread out under the few trees at the back of the beach. The calm, clear water makes this a fine place for a slow, easy swim.

That clear water makes Conchal a good place to snorkel. Near the rocks at each end, small fish gather. You float on the surface and look down through your mask. Sunlight reaches the bottom. You can watch the fish move among the stones without much effort. Bring your own mask and fins if you can, because there is little to rent once you arrive. Early morning is the best time, before the wind picks up and stirs the surface.

The shells themselves change as you walk. The eastern end of the beach holds the most of them. Over the years, many visitors have carried shells away as keepsakes. So the further west you go, the fewer whole shells you find. The beach slowly tells the story of its own popularity.

It is best to leave the shells where they are. They are not only pretty to look at. They are the beach itself. Each shell that leaves is one less grain in a place built entirely from them. Taking shells from protected areas is also against the rules in Costa Rica. If every visitor took just one shell, the beach would slowly vanish. The simple rule here is an old one: take only photographs, and leave only footprints.

Behind the beach stands a large gated resort property. Much of the land here is private. The beach itself, though, is public, as all beaches in Costa Rica are by law. You are free to walk, swim, and rest on the sand, as long as you reach it from the shore.

Find a spot in the shade and sit for a while. The shells hold the warmth of the sun. The water laps quietly against the shore. Light bounces off the pale ground and off the calm sea. The crowds that gather on busier beaches rarely reach this quiet end. Everything here feels bright, soft, and slow.

Bring what you need with you. There is little shade on the open beach, so a hat and water help a lot. There are few shops once you leave Brasilito. The quiet is part of the reward, but it means you should plan ahead before you arrive.

Playa Conchal is the work of the ocean and of time. No machine made this beach. The sea broke down shell after shell, over more years than we can count, and laid them here. When you let the shells fall through your hand, you are holding a small piece of that patient work.

Key facts

The name comes from concha, Spanish for shell.
The beach lies about thirty minutes north of Tamarindo.
Reached by a fifteen-minute walk from Brasilito village.
The sand is made of crushed pink and white shells.
All Costa Rican beaches are public by law.

What to look for

Pink and white shells clicking through your fingers.
Soft aquamarine water between two rocky points.
Small fish gathering near the rocks at each end.
Pale shell ground glittering in the sunlight.

Practical tips

Walk in from Brasilito along the shore at low tide.
Bring a hat, water, and strong sun protection.
Leave all shells on the beach where you find them.
Pack snorkel gear to explore the clear rocky ends.
