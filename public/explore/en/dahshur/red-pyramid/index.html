The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the wind carries fine grit across the desert floor, and it makes a soft, dry hiss against the stone. There are no traffic horns and no crowds pressing in. Ahead of you rises a wide, low mountain of rust-coloured blocks, warm in the light. When the sun moves behind a cloud, the colour shifts from copper to a dusty pink. This is the Red Pyramid of Dahshur, and it is older than the Great Pyramid of Giza.

The name comes from the stone itself. The blocks you see today are local limestone, and they carry a reddish tint from iron in the rock. But this is not how the pyramid first looked. Its builders covered it in a smooth casing of fine white limestone. Over the centuries, people stripped most of that casing away and reused it elsewhere. What remains is the inner body of the monument, exposed to the sky.

The pyramid belongs to King Sneferu, a ruler of Egypt's Fourth Dynasty, about forty-six centuries ago. Sneferu is not as well known as his son. That son was Khufu, the king who later built the Great Pyramid at Giza. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The father may have moved more stone than the son. Sneferu built several large pyramids during his reign, and the one in front of you was probably his last.

To understand why this pyramid matters, look south across the desert. You may see a second large pyramid with a strange shape. Its sides start steep, then change angle about halfway up. That is the Bent Pyramid, also built for Sneferu. Many scholars think its builders saw cracks and instability, and changed the angle to reduce the weight. The lesson they learned there shaped the building you are looking at now.

The Red Pyramid rises at a gentler angle, roughly forty-three degrees. That is close to the upper slope of the Bent Pyramid, and it gives the building its wide, calm profile. Because of this, it is often described as the first successful true pyramid in Egypt. True means smooth, straight sides from base to peak, with no steps and no change of angle. Everything that came later at Giza grew from experiments in this stretch of desert.

The scale is easy to underestimate from a distance. The pyramid stands about one hundred and five metres tall. Its base is very broad, which is why it looks squat compared with the pyramids at Giza. In height, only the two largest pyramids at Giza rise above it. Walk toward one corner and look along an edge. The line of blocks runs on and on, and your eye loses count long before it reaches the top.

Here is the second surprise. Some of the casing blocks found around the pyramid carry marks left by the work gangs. These painted notes include dates written according to the king's reign, along with team names. Egyptologists have used them to estimate how quickly the pyramid went up. They are not decorations for the gods. They are practical notes from the workers themselves, a rare trace of the ordinary people who hauled and set the stone.

Near the base, look for a smaller pointed stone set on display. This is a capstone, the small pyramid shape that once sat on top of a pyramid. The capstone shown here was found in pieces near the monument and later reassembled. Scholars still discuss whether it was ever placed on this pyramid. Either way, it lets you see, at human scale, the shape that once crowned this whole structure.

Now look up the north face. The entrance sits high above the ground, and a stairway of steps and handrails leads to it. The climb is steady but not difficult. From the doorway, a long passage slopes down into the body of the pyramid. The ceiling is low, so most adults must bend almost double. Wooden boards with small slats help your feet grip on the way down.

At the bottom, the space opens up. You enter the first of two chambers, and the ceiling rises far above you in a series of stepped stone layers. This technique is called corbelling. Each row of blocks sticks out slightly past the one below, until the two sides nearly meet at the top. It spreads the enormous weight of the stone above. Stand in the centre and look straight up. The roof seems to climb into darkness.

You will also notice the air. It is warm, still and close, and many visitors notice a sharp smell, a little like ammonia. Beyond the second chamber, a short wooden stair leads up to the burial chamber, which sits higher than the first two rooms. Its floor is rough and broken, because treasure hunters dug through it long ago. No intact royal burial was found here. Some human remains were discovered, but who they belonged to is not certain.

One reason this place feels so quiet is its recent past. For many years, Dahshur sat inside a military zone, and ordinary visitors could not come here. The area opened to tourism only in the 1990s. Even now, it receives far fewer people than Giza or Saqqara. That means you can often walk the base, climb to the entrance and stand in the chambers with very few others around you.

As you come back out into the light, pause at the top of the steps. Let your eyes adjust. Below you, the desert spreads in pale tones, and the Bent Pyramid sits on the horizon. To the east, green fields mark the edge of the Nile valley. This contrast between desert and farmland shaped where Egyptians placed their tombs, on the dry western side, toward the setting sun.

Before you leave, put your hand flat on one of the red blocks. Feel how the surface has been worn by wind and sand. Somebody shaped this stone, dragged it here and set it in place, long before Giza had its great monument. As you walk away, look back once more. Watch how the colour changes as the light moves across those broad, sloping faces.

What to look for

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a torch or phone light for the chambers.
Expect to crouch low in the descending passage.
Wear shoes with good grip for the wooden boards.
Carry water, as shade around the site is scarce.
Combine your visit with the nearby Bent Pyramid.
