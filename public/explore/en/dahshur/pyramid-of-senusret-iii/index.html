The guide

The story

The ground here crunches underfoot. It is a mix of desert gravel and broken mud brick, baked so long by the sun that it has turned the colour of dry bread. Ahead of you rises a low, slumped hill, darker than the sand around it. It does not look like a pyramid at first. It looks like something the desert has been slowly eating... and in a sense, that is exactly what has happened.

This is the pyramid of Senusret the Third, a king of Egypt's Twelfth Dynasty. That places him in the period historians call the Middle Kingdom, a time of strong central rule that came several centuries after the great pyramids of Giza. By his reign, pyramid building had changed. The kings still wanted the ancient shape, but the methods, the materials, and the ambitions had all moved on.

The biggest change is right in front of you. The core of this monument is not solid stone. It is mud brick, stacked in enormous quantities and held together by its own weight. When it was new, a smooth casing of fine white limestone covered the entire surface. From a distance, it would have looked as bright and clean as any older pyramid. The difference was hidden inside.

That casing is almost all gone now. Over many centuries, people took the good limestone for other buildings and for burning into lime. Once the protective skin was removed, wind and rare rain began to wear down the brick beneath. This is why the shape is so soft today. You are looking at the inner body of the pyramid, exposed — something its builders never meant anyone to see.

Look around you, and you will understand why kings chose Dahshur. To the west and south, the horizon carries older giants. The Red Pyramid and the Bent Pyramid were built by Sneferu, a king of the Fourth Dynasty, long before Senusret. Further south stands the dark ruin of the Black Pyramid, built by Senusret's own grandson, Amenemhat the Third. By building here, a Middle Kingdom king placed himself beside the founders of the pyramid age.

So who was Senusret the Third? He is remembered as one of the most forceful rulers of his dynasty. He led military campaigns south into Nubia, the land along the Nile beyond Egypt's southern border, and he strengthened Egypt's frontier forts there. He is also linked to a canal cut through the rocks at the First Cataract, a stretch of rapids near Aswan, so that boats could pass more easily.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Senusret's statues are unlike most royal portraits from Egypt. Earlier kings were usually shown with calm, youthful, perfect faces. Senusret's sculptors gave him heavy eyelids, deep lines, and a tired, serious mouth. Scholars still discuss what this meant. Was it realism, or a deliberate image of a king who carried the weight of the world? Either way, those faces are among the most human in Egyptian art.

Inside the pyramid, the builders tried hard to defeat tomb robbers. In older pyramids, the entrance was normally on the north face. Here, the passage was hidden elsewhere, away from the expected side. Deep below, the burial chamber was lined with hard granite and held a stone sarcophagus. Yet when archaeologists finally reached it, they found it empty. Some researchers think the king may actually have been buried at Abydos, where he also built a large tomb complex.

The great surprise of this site came in the 1890s. The French archaeologist Jacques de Morgan excavated here and explored tomb galleries belonging to royal women of the king's family. In those passages, his team found jewellery that robbers had somehow missed. There were gold pectorals, inlaid with coloured stones, and delicate crowns and bracelets. These pieces became known as the Dahshur treasure, and they are now among the finest jewels kept in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

There was another discovery that sounds almost impossible in the desert. De Morgan also found wooden boats buried near the pyramid. They were real, full-sized boats, built from planks fitted together without metal nails. Their exact purpose is still debated. They may have been used in the funeral, or they may have been symbolic vessels for the king's journey after death. Today, these Dahshur boats are kept in museums in Egypt and the United States.

The work here did not end in the nineteenth century. Later, a team from the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York returned to Dahshur and studied the whole complex in detail. They mapped the temples, the surrounding walls, and the smaller pyramids built for the royal women. Their careful work showed how much of the complex lies below the surface, invisible to the casual eye.

As you walk, notice the scattered stones near the base. Some are fragments of the fine white casing, smooth on one side. Notice, too, how the mud brick shows straw and grit when you look closely. This is the same simple material that ordinary Egyptians used for their houses. Here it was raised to royal scale, then wrapped in stone to look eternal.

Access to the inner parts of this monument is often restricted, and much of the complex is fenced or unexcavated. So the best way to experience it is slowly, from the outside. Walk far enough that you can see this pyramid and the older ones of Sneferu in the same view. Then the story of Dahshur becomes clear: two different ages of kings, using the same desert to speak to the future.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen. Out here, the city of Cairo feels far away. You may hear only wind across the gravel, a distant dog, perhaps a car on the edge of the farmland. Look again at the worn brown mound. It was once a white point of light above the Nile valley. Now it is quiet, patient, and still standing... and you are one of the few people who came to see it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning when the desert light is softer.
Bring water and sun protection for walking on gravel.
Combine with the Red and Bent Pyramids nearby.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose brick and rubble.
See the Dahshur jewellery later at Cairo's Egyptian Museum.
