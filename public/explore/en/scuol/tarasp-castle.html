The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the wind moves through the larch trees and carries the sound of cowbells from the meadows below. The air smells of cut grass and cold stone. Above you, on a rounded crag of rock, white walls rise in layers toward the sky. This is Tarasp Castle. It is one of the clearest silhouettes in the Lower Engadine, the high valley of the river Inn in the far east of Switzerland.

From the spa town of Scuol, across the valley, the castle looks almost like a painting. It is pale and compact, and it sits alone on its hill. Its position is no accident. The rock gives a wide view up and down the valley. Whoever held this crag could watch the roads, the river, and the passes that lead toward Austria and Italy. For a medieval lord, that view was a form of power.

The castle's story begins in the eleventh century. Historical records place a fortress here around the middle of that century. It was built by a local noble family who took their name from the place, the lords of Tarasp. Over the following centuries the castle changed hands many times. Bishops, counts, and dukes all wanted control of this corner of the Alps, because the valley was a crossing point between larger powers.

Here is the first surprise. For several centuries, Tarasp was not really part of Switzerland at all. The castle and its small territory belonged to the Habsburgs, the ruling house of Austria. Around it lay the lands of the Grisons, the free alpine communities that later became the Swiss canton of Graubünden. So this hill was an Austrian island inside a Swiss sea. Imagine neighbours across the valley living under a completely different ruler.

That situation ended in 1803. In that year, Napoleon reorganised Switzerland, and Tarasp finally became part of Graubünden. But political change did not bring good fortune to the building. During the nineteenth century the castle lost its purpose. It was sold, partly emptied, and left to decay. Roofs leaked. Rooms stood bare. Some of its furniture and fittings were taken away. For a time, the fortress on the hill was close to becoming a ruin.

The rescue came from an unexpected direction. Around 1900, the castle was bought by Karl August Lingner, an industrialist from Dresden in Germany. He had made his fortune with a mouthwash called Odol, which was famous across Europe at the time. Lingner fell in love with Tarasp. He spent years and a great deal of money restoring it. He wanted to turn the cold fortress into a home filled with art and old furniture.

And here is the second surprise, a sad one. The restoration took many years. Lingner died in 1916, just as the work was nearly complete. He never had the chance to live in the castle he had brought back to life. When you look at the polished rooms today, you are looking at a dream that its owner did not get to enjoy. After his death, the castle passed to the Grand Ducal family of Hesse, a German noble house.

The most recent chapter is modern. In 2016, the castle was bought by Not Vital, an internationally known artist who was born in the nearby village of Sent. A local son now owns the fortress that once belonged to distant empires. Contemporary art has found a place alongside the historic rooms. This mix of old and new gives Tarasp a character that is quite different from a traditional castle museum.

As you climb toward the gate, notice how the building is arranged. It is not one single block. It is a group of structures packed onto the summit, with walls following the shape of the rock. The white plaster shines in sunlight and turns soft grey under cloud. Small windows look out in every direction. Each layer was added at a different time, as owners repaired, expanded, and defended their hill.

Take a moment to look down at the village of Tarasp. Traditional Engadine houses sit close together. Many have thick walls, deep-set windows, and decorated façades. This decoration is called sgraffito. Craftsmen scratch patterns into a top layer of plaster to show a darker layer underneath. Below the castle there is also a small lake. On calm days it holds a reflection of the white walls, and the scene looks almost too neat to be real.

Listen to the people around you, too. In this valley, many locals speak Romansh. It is one of Switzerland's four national languages, and it comes from the Latin spoken in Roman times. Village names, signs, and house inscriptions often appear in Romansh. Hearing it here is a reminder that this valley kept its own identity, even while empires and bishops argued over the castle on the hill.

The wider landscape matters as much as the building. Scuol has long been known for its mineral springs, and visitors have come to bathe here for generations. To the south, the mountains climb toward the Swiss National Park, the oldest national park in the Alps. Forests of larch and pine cover the slopes. In autumn, the larches turn gold, and the castle stands out white against the colour.

Before you leave, turn around one more time. Look at the castle against the mountains behind it. Think of the lords who watched these roads, the Austrian officials, the empty rooms of the nineteenth century, the businessman who never moved in, and the artist from the next village. As you walk back down the path, listen again for the cowbells and the wind. The hill has stayed the same through all of it.
