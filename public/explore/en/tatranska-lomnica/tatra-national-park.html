The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere above you, water runs over granite. The air feels thinner and cooler than it did in the valley, and it smells of spruce resin and wet stone. Look up from the streets of Tatranská Lomnica and the mountains seem very close. They rise straight out of the forest, grey and sharp. This is the High Tatras, the tallest part of the Carpathian mountain chain, and you are standing at its foot.

This is Tatra National Park, often shortened to TANAP. It was established in 1949, which makes it the oldest national park in Slovakia. The park protects the Slovak side of the Tatra range. Just over the ridge to the north lies Poland, which has its own Tatra National Park. Together, the two parks form a protected area that crosses the border. UNESCO recognises it as a biosphere reserve, a place where people and nature are meant to live side by side.

The Tatras are small, and that is the first surprise for many visitors. The whole range is only a few dozen kilometres long. Yet within that short distance, peaks climb above 2,600 metres. The highest of them, Gerlachovský štít, is the highest point in the entire Carpathians. That chain curves across central and eastern Europe for well over a thousand kilometres. So the tallest summit of that huge arc stands here, in this compact block of stone.

The peak directly above Tatranská Lomnica is Lomnický štít. It is the second highest summit in Slovakia, and its pointed shape is easy to recognise from the town. For a long time, many people believed it was the highest peak in the range. Surveyors later corrected that idea. Lomnický štít still feels like the guardian of this resort, though. On clear days its upper rock face catches the first light of morning before the valley wakes.

What shaped these mountains? Mostly ice. During the ice ages, glaciers filled the valleys and ground them into wide, curved shapes. When the ice melted, it left behind bowls in the rock. Many of these bowls filled with water. Today the park holds many glacial lakes. In Slovak, a mountain lake like this is called a pleso. Some are tiny and dark. Others are broad and very clear, and they reflect the peaks like a mirror.

One of the best-known lakes near here is Skalnaté pleso. It sits high above the town, on the way toward Lomnický štít. A cable car system climbs from Tatranská Lomnica toward the lake and then continues to the summit itself. The upper section is steep and dramatic, and capacity is limited, so plan ahead. Near the lake there is also an astronomical observatory. Astronomers chose this place because the mountain air is often clear and far from city lights.

Now look at the forest around the lower slopes. You may notice that parts of it look young, open, or uneven. There is a reason. In November 2004, a violent windstorm swept down from the mountains. In a few hours it flattened huge areas of spruce forest below the High Tatras. Local people still talk about that night. Since then, the forest has been growing back, and scientists have studied how a mountain forest heals after such damage.

The recovery is not simple. Foresters, scientists, and conservation groups have debated how much to clear and how much to leave to nature. In some areas, fallen trees were removed. In others, they stayed on the ground. That debate continues, and it shows a real question for every national park. Should people repair nature, or should nature be allowed to repair itself? As you walk, you can see both answers in the landscape around you.

The park is home to animals that live nowhere else in quite the same form. The Tatra chamois is a small, sure-footed mountain goat-antelope. It moves across steep rock with ease. Alpine marmots live in burrows on the high meadows. When they sense danger, they give a sharp whistle. Brown bears also live in these forests. Sightings are not common, but they happen. Keep your distance from any wild animal, and never leave food behind.

Higher up, the trails lead to mountain huts. In Slovak these are called chaty. Some of them sit in remote valleys with no road. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Supplies for some huts are still carried up on foot by mountain porters. These porters carry heavy wooden frames on their backs, loaded with food, drinks, and gas. It is a long tradition in the Tatras. If you meet one on the trail, step aside and let them pass.

The town of Tatranská Lomnica grew as a resort in the late nineteenth and early twentieth century. Wealthy visitors came for fresh air and mountain cures. Look for the Grand Hotel Praha, a large and elegant building from that era. It shows how fashionable these mountains once were with the upper classes of central Europe. A narrow-gauge electric railway links Tatranská Lomnica with the other Tatra resorts. Its small red trains still run along the foot of the range.

If you want to understand the park before you hike, visit the museum of the national park here in town. It explains the geology, plants, animals, and people of the Tatras. Remember that the higher trails are protected very strictly. Some routes close for part of each year to protect wildlife and hikers. Weather here also changes fast. A sunny morning can become a cold, foggy afternoon. Pack layers, water, and good shoes, even for a short walk.

Before you leave, look up once more at Lomnický štít. Notice how the light moves on the rock as clouds pass. Listen for the wind in the spruce trees and the sound of water from the slopes. These mountains are old, but the forest at their feet is young, still growing after the storm. As you walk away, you are walking through that recovery. Take a slow breath of this cool mountain air. It belongs to this place.
