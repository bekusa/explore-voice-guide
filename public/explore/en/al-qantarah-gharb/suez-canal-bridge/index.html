The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere below, a ship's engine hums, low and steady, almost felt more than heard. The air smells of salt, diesel, and warm dust from the desert road. Above you, a long white deck runs across the sky, held up by fans of cables that catch the sunlight like harp strings. This is the Suez Canal Bridge, and it carries a road from Africa into Asia.

The town around you has a name that fits this place. In Arabic, Al-Qantarah simply means 'the bridge'. Gharb means 'west'. So you are standing in West Bridge, on the African bank of the canal. Across the water lies its twin, Qantara Sharq, or East Bridge, on the edge of the Sinai Peninsula. The name is old. It is much older than the steel structure above your head.

Long before any canal existed, this narrow strip of land was a crossing point. Here, travellers could pass between the Nile Valley and the lands of the eastern Mediterranean. Ancient armies, traders, and pilgrims used routes through this area. Caravans moved camels and goods along it for centuries. Historians link the area to the old military road that Egyptian pharaohs used to march toward the Levant — the coastal lands that today include Israel, Palestine, Lebanon, and Syria.

Then, in the nineteenth century, the land was cut. The Suez Canal opened in 1869. It joined the Mediterranean Sea in the north to the Red Sea in the south. Suddenly, a ship could sail from Europe to Asia without going around the whole of Africa. But the canal also created a new problem. The old land route was now divided by water. For more than a century, people crossed here mostly by ferry, and you may still see small ferries working the water today.

The twentieth century was hard on this town. The canal became one of the most fought-over waterways in the world. There was the Suez Crisis of 1956. Then came the wars between Egypt and Israel. After 1967, the canal itself became a front line, and it stayed closed to shipping for about eight years. Qantara, sitting right on the water, suffered heavy damage. The canal finally reopened in 1975, and the long work of rebuilding began.

That history gives the bridge its meaning. It was built as a permanent road link across a waterway that had divided armies. Japan helped Egypt pay for it, through a grant, and Japanese engineering firms worked on the construction alongside Egyptian teams. For this reason, many people call it the Egyptian-Japanese Friendship Bridge. You may also hear other names, including the Peace Bridge. The crossing opened to traffic in 2001.

Now look up at the shape of it. This is a cable-stayed bridge. In this design, straight steel cables run from tall towers directly down to the road deck. They do not hang from a curved main cable, as on a suspension bridge. Count the cables on one side of a tower. Notice how they spread out in a fan. Each one pulls the deck up toward the tower, and together they hold the weight in balance.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. Look closely at the top of the two main towers. The designers shaped them to suggest obelisks — the tall, four-sided stone pillars of ancient Egypt, with a pointed top. Obelisks once stood at the entrances of temples along the Nile. Here, that ancient form is made in modern concrete and steel. It is a quiet signal that this bridge belongs to Egypt.

The second surprise is the height. The road deck sits about seventy metres above the water. That is roughly as tall as a twenty-storey building. Why so high? Because the canal must stay open for the largest ships in the world. Container ships and tankers pass beneath without stopping. The approach roads on each side are very long, rising slowly from the flat desert. The whole crossing runs for nearly four kilometres.

If you are lucky, a convoy will pass while you are here. Ships move through the canal in organised groups, one after another, at a slow and careful speed. From the bank, the sight can feel strange. A ship many storeys tall seems to glide through the sand itself, because the water is hidden by the flat banks. On a hazy day, the hulls look almost like buildings sliding across the desert.

This is still a working landscape, not a park. The bridge carries highway traffic, and it is not a promenade for walkers. The canal is also important national infrastructure, so security here is real. You may see police or military posts near the water. Be respectful, keep a sensible distance, and if you want to take photos, it is wise to ask first. Locals are used to the bridge. For them, it is simply the way east.

The town itself has a modest, everyday rhythm. You will hear car horns, the call to prayer from nearby mosques, and the rattle of trucks heading toward Sinai. Fishermen sometimes work along the water's edge. Tea is poured in small glasses at roadside cafés. Qantara is not a resort. It is a crossing town, as it has been for thousands of years. The bridge did not change that role. It only made the crossing faster.

Before you leave, look once more along the line of the deck. Follow it with your eyes from the west bank to the east, from one continent to another. Watch the light move on the cables. Listen for the next ship's engine below. People have crossed at this spot for a very long time. On foot, by camel, by boat — and now, high above the water, on a road held up by steel.
