The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, far above the Arctic Circle, the air often carries very little sound. Snow, when it lies on the ground, absorbs footsteps. In summer, the light hardly fades at all. In front of you rises a steep, dark red roof, almost like a great wooden tent. Its timber walls hold the cold, and its eaves come down low, close to the ground. This is Kiruna Church, one of the best-loved buildings in Sweden.

The church opened in the early twentieth century and was consecrated in 1912. Kiruna was still a very young town then. It grew up almost overnight around one thing, iron ore. The mountains nearby hold one of the richest iron deposits in the world. Workers came from all over Sweden and beyond. They lived in rough conditions, and the town needed a centre for its community, a place that was more than a workplace.

The mining company, known today as LKAB, paid for the church. Its leader in Kiruna was Hjalmar Lundbohm, an unusual mining director. He cared about art, education and the daily lives of the workers. He wanted Kiruna to be a real town, not just a camp. He brought artists here and he supported schools. This church was part of that larger idea... a building that would give the community a sense of dignity.

The architect was Gustaf Wickman. Look at the overall shape again. The steep roof and the low walls take their form from the traditional dwellings of the Sami. The Sami are the Indigenous people of northern Scandinavia, and many Sami families have herded reindeer here for generations. A Sami hut, called a kåta, is a cone or tent of wood and fabric. Wickman turned that humble form into a large church, built in wood.

The church also mixes in other styles of its time. You can see touches of the National Romantic movement, a style that looked back to Nordic traditions and local materials. There are also hints of Art Nouveau in the decorative details, with soft, flowing lines. The result does not look like a typical Swedish country church. It has no tall white spire. Instead, the whole building feels heavy, close to the earth, and built for snow.

Now look up along the roof. Gilded bronze figures stand on the building, catching the light. They are the work of the sculptor Christian Eriksson. The figures show human beings and different sides of human nature. They are not saints or angels in the usual sense. Many visitors walk past without noticing them, especially in winter, when the sun stays low. On a clear day, though, the gold shines brightly against the dark red wood.

Here is something that surprises many visitors. The bell tower does not sit on the church itself. It stands apart as a separate structure. This was common in older Nordic church building. The separate tower also keeps the strong tent-like silhouette of the main church clean and simple. As you walk around, notice how the two buildings relate to each other. One is broad and low, while the other is narrow and upright.

If the doors are open, step inside. The interior is one large, open room under a high wooden roof. You will notice the smell of old timber, and in winter, the warmth feels very welcome after the cold outside. Look toward the front. The altarpiece was painted by Prince Eugen, a son of the Swedish king who became a respected landscape painter. Instead of a dramatic religious scene, his work brings a calm, natural mood into the church.

Now for the second great surprise. This church is not standing in its original place. Kiruna is a town on the move. Mining deep beneath the old centre caused the ground to crack and sink. So the town decided to move much of its centre several kilometres to the east. Old buildings were taken down, and some were lifted and carried. The church was one of the buildings that was carried, and it was moved in one piece.

The church made its journey in 2025. Workers placed the whole wooden building on huge, slow transport vehicles. It rolled along a wide road at walking pace over about two days. Thousands of people came out to watch. Some walked beside it. For many residents, it was an emotional event. They saw their church, where families had married and said goodbye to loved ones, travel through the landscape to a new home.

The whole move shows a tension that defines Kiruna. The mine gives the town its jobs and its reason to exist. But the mine also takes the ground from under the town's feet. The church is a symbol of both sides. The mining company built it, and the mining company also had to move it. Locals have mixed feelings about this, and you may hear different opinions if you talk with people here.

The seasons change this place completely. In midsummer, the sun does not set, and the red wood glows late into the night. In the deep winter, the sun barely rises, and the church sits in a blue twilight. On clear, dark nights, the northern lights can sometimes appear above the roof. These are moving curtains of green light in the sky. Even without them, the snow and the quiet give the church a very special atmosphere.

Before you leave, take one more slow walk around the building. Notice the texture of the painted boards, the deep shadow under the eaves, and the gold figures high above. Think about how far this wooden church has travelled, and how many people have stood where you stand now. Then listen again to the quiet of the north... and carry a little of it with you as you walk on.

Practical tips

Dress in warm layers, even on summer evenings.
Walk the full circuit around the church before entering.
Check in advance whether a service is taking place.
