The story

The stone beneath your feet is warm from the sun, worn smooth by two thousand years of footsteps. Gonio Fortress rises from the coastal plain where the Chorokhi River meets the Black Sea — a massive rectangle of Roman walls, their honey-coloured blocks stacked tight without mortar, holding fast against the salt wind and the weight of empire after empire.

Rome built this place in the first century as Apsaros, a military station guarding the eastern edge of the known world. Legionaries from across the empire stood watch here, looking out over trade routes that carried silk and spices from Persia to the Mediterranean. The walls you see now — eighteen towers, each one square and solid — date largely from later Byzantine repairs, but the Roman foundations run deep. Walk close to the base and you will see the precision of those original courses, each block cut to fit its neighbour exactly.

The fortress changed hands more than a dozen times. Byzantines held it for centuries, then came waves of Arab raids, Georgian kings, Ottoman governors, each leaving their mark in the stonework. Look for the layers: Roman limestone at the bottom, Byzantine brick repairs higher up, Ottoman-era patches in rougher rubble. The walls are a vertical archive, each course a different chapter.

Inside the perimeter, archaeologists have uncovered a Roman bathhouse with intact hypocaust floors — the underfloor heating system that kept soldiers warm through Black Sea winters. The channels are still visible, narrow clay tunnels where hot air once circulated. Nearby, the remains of a basilica show where early Christians gathered, possibly as far back as the fourth century. Some traditions claim the Apostle Matthew is buried here, though no excavation has confirmed it. The story persists among locals, a thread of faith woven into the fortress stones.

The towers offer the best perspective. Climb the stairs inside the southern gatehouse and the geometry of the place reveals itself — a perfect rectangle, three hundred metres by two hundred, aligned to the cardinal points. From up here you see the river delta spreading toward the sea, the same view Roman sentries had when they scanned for Persian ships. On clear days, the Turkish coast is a dark line on the horizon.

Gonio sits just outside Batumi, close enough that the city's lights glow against the night sky, but the fortress feels removed from the modern resort strip. The coastal highway runs past the western wall, but inside the ramparts the loudest sound is usually wind through the tower openings and the occasional cry of gulls. Locals come here in the late afternoon, walking the perimeter path, sitting in the shade of the walls.

The site museum, housed in a low building near the entrance, displays finds from decades of excavation — Roman coins, fragments of amphorae, bronze arrowheads, a stone relief of a soldier. The labels are sparse, but the objects speak clearly enough: this was a working fort, not a ceremonial showpiece. Soldiers lived here, traded here, died here. The everyday detritus of garrison life fills the cases.

Excavation continues each summer. Trenches open in the central courtyard, revealing more of the Roman street grid, foundations of barracks, the outline of a granary. Each season adds detail to the picture, though much of Gonio remains underground, waiting. The fortress you walk through is only a fraction of what once stood here.

The light changes quickly near the sea. Morning sun warms the eastern towers to gold, while late afternoon casts long shadows across the courtyard, turning the stone deep amber. The best time to visit is just before sunset, when the walls glow and the heat eases and the place feels less like a ruin and more like a presence — ancient, patient, still holding its ground.

Stand at the centre of the courtyard and turn slowly. The walls surround you on all sides, blocking the modern world, framing only sky and stone. For a moment, the centuries collapse. You are standing where Roman soldiers drilled, where Byzantine priests chanted, where merchants haggled over silk. The fortress has outlasted them all, and it will outlast you too. That is the quiet power of Gonio — not drama, just endurance.
