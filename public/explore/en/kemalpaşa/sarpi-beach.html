The guide

The story

The water here is so clear you can count the pebbles beneath it. Sarpi Beach meets the Black Sea at the very southern edge of Georgia, where the land runs out and Turkey begins. Stand at the shoreline and feel the steep green hills press close behind you, while the sea opens wide and bright in front.

This is the last stretch of Georgian coast before the border. Sarpi lies about eighteen kilometers south of Batumi, a short and pretty drive along the water. The village itself is small, just a scatter of houses that climb the steep green slopes. Below them, the narrow beach gathers most of the life, pressed between the hills and the sea.

The shore is made of smooth pebbles, not sand, and this is the reason the water looks the way it does. Without sand to cloud it, the sea stays clear and deep blue, often called the cleanest water in the whole Adjara region. Walk in slowly. The stones shift and click under your feet, the cool water climbs your legs, and then the bottom drops away beneath you into clear, open blue.

In summer the sea grows warm. The water can reach around twenty-five degrees Celsius, and it holds that warmth late into autumn, long after the crowds have gone. Swimmers stay in for a long time without feeling cold. The pebbles store the sun's heat through the day, so the beach itself stays warm under your back when you lie down to rest and dry off.

Two rocky cliffs rise at the edges of the bay. Local divers use them, and on a warm day you may see swimmers climbing the rocks to leap into the deep, clear water below. The sea drops away quickly here, so it stays cold and dark just a little way out. If you prefer to stay dry, the cliffs make a good place to sit and watch the boats and the open horizon.

Above the beach stands the building that most travelers notice first. The Sarpi border checkpoint is a modern landmark, a curving tower of glass and pale concrete that leans toward the sky. It opened in 2011. Its shape is unusual, almost like a wave frozen in mid-motion, and it marks the line between two countries.

You can walk close to the crossing and watch cars and people move between Georgia and Turkey, a steady line passing from one country to the next. On the far side, the writing on the signs changes, and the style of the buildings changes with it. Many visitors come simply to stand at this meeting point of two lands and two languages, where one country quietly becomes another.

Sarpi has its own history with the Laz people, a community whose roots run along this coast on both sides of the border. They are known for their fishing, their songs, and a language of their own. A small ethnographic museum in the village keeps their tools, clothing, and stories. It is a quiet place, easy to walk past, and worth a short stop to understand who has lived here.

Near the shore you will also find a church named for Saint Andrew the First-Called. By tradition, the apostle Andrew is said to have passed through these lands long ago. A monument and a small waterfall nearby carry his name as well, set among the green slopes above the village.

The beach changes through the day. Mornings are calm and almost empty, the best time for a slow swim in still water. By afternoon, families and travelers fill the pebbles, and small cafes along the front sell coffee, cold drinks, grilled fish, and khachapuri, the warm cheese bread of the region. The smell of the sea mixes with the smell of cooking, and the sound of talk rises along the shore.

As the sun lowers, the light turns gold and then soft pink across the water. The white border tower catches the last of it and seems to glow. The crowds slowly thin. This is when many people say Sarpi is at its best, when the heat eases, the colors deepen, and the sea goes quiet.

Sarpi is a small place at the end of a country, where the road, the green hills, and the wide sea all come together and stop. Stand here as the light fades, listen to the pebbles roll back with each retreating wave, and you understand why people linger at the very edge before turning back.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear water shoes; the pebbles are hard underfoot.
Arrive early morning for calm water and space.
Bring a passport if you approach the border.
Stay for sunset, when the crowds thin out.
