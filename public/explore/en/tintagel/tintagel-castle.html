The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Atlantic hits the black rock with a deep, hollow boom, then pulls back with a long hiss over the stones. The wind carries salt and the green smell of wet grass. Underfoot, the path is slate, worn smooth and slick in places. Gulls hang in the air at eye level. This is Tintagel Castle, on the north coast of Cornwall, and almost everything about it is steep, broken, and exposed to the weather.

Look out at the headland. Locals call it the Island, although it is not quite one. A narrow neck of land once joined it firmly to the mainland, but the sea has eaten away much of that connection over the centuries. Today the castle sits in two halves. Part of the ruin stands on the mainland cliff. The rest stands on the Island itself, across a deep gap where the waves run in below.

The walls you can see were built in the thirteenth century by Richard, Earl of Cornwall. He was the younger brother of King Henry the Third, and one of the richest men in Europe. Here is the first surprise. This was never a useful fortress. It guarded no important road, no harbour of real value, no town. Most historians think Richard built it for one reason — the story attached to this place.

That story began about a century earlier. In the 1130s, a cleric named Geoffrey of Monmouth wrote a History of the Kings of Britain. It mixed real history with a great deal of invention. Geoffrey wrote that Tintagel was the place where King Arthur was conceived. In his tale, the wizard Merlin used magic to disguise Uther Pendragon as the Duke of Cornwall. Uther entered the fortress, and Arthur was the result of that night.

Geoffrey's book was enormously popular across medieval Europe. So when Richard chose to build here, he was building on a legend. A castle at Tintagel connected him to Arthur, and to an ancient line of British kings. It was a statement of status, made in stone. In a sense, the walls around you are a kind of medieval propaganda. They are also fairly modest. Richard's castle was never large, and later owners showed little interest in it.

Now the second surprise, and perhaps the most important one. Long before Richard, long before Geoffrey, people really did live here — and some of them were wealthy. Archaeologists have found the remains of many buildings on the Island from the early medieval period, roughly the fifth to the seventh centuries. That is the time after Roman rule in Britain ended, the age when any real Arthur would have lived.

Among the finds were large quantities of broken pottery from the Mediterranean. There were pieces of storage jars that once held wine and olive oil, and fragments of fine tableware. Some of this came from as far away as the eastern Mediterranean. In fact, more of this imported pottery has been found at Tintagel than at almost any other site in Britain from that period. Whoever lived here was trading with distant ports, and eating and drinking very well.

Writing has been found too. In the late 1990s, excavators uncovered a piece of slate with Latin letters cut into it, including a personal name. More recent excavations found a stone ledge with an inscription mixing Latin words, Christian symbols, and names. None of these finds mention Arthur. But they show a literate, connected community on this rock. The legend may be fiction. The important people who lived here were not.

Now walk out onto the footbridge. It opened in 2019 and runs across the gap between the mainland and the Island. Before it existed, visitors had to climb down and back up long flights of steep steps. Look down at the middle of the bridge. There is a narrow gap running across its centre, where the two halves reach towards each other without quite touching. The designers left it there on purpose. Some people like to say it marks the line between history and legend.

On the Island, take your time among the low walls of the early houses. Many are little more than outlines in the turf, with slate foundations. Stand still and imagine the roofs, the smoke from fires, and ships waiting in the cove below. You may also find a short tunnel cut into the rock. Its purpose is still uncertain. Some think it was used for cold storage, but nobody knows for sure.

On the clifftop stands a tall bronze figure, cloaked and holding a sword, looking out to sea. It was installed in 2016. It is called Gallos, a Cornish word meaning power. Many visitors assume it is Arthur, and the artist allowed room for that idea. But the figure was intended to suggest the kings and lords connected with this place, real and imagined, rather than one named man.

If the tide is low, look down to the beach in the small cove called the Haven. At its back is a sea cave that runs right through the base of the Island. It is known as Merlin's Cave. The name comes from the nineteenth century, when the poet Alfred Tennyson wrote that Merlin found the infant Arthur in the waves here. That is pure poetry, not history. Still, the cave is real, dark, dripping, and loud with the echo of the sea.

Tennyson's poems helped bring a new wave of visitors in the Victorian age. Writers, painters, and tourists came looking for Camelot. Look back towards the mainland and you will see a large hotel on the opposite cliff, built at the end of the nineteenth century to serve them. The village behind it still lives partly on Arthur, with shops selling swords, dragons, and books of legend. In a way, Geoffrey's story is still at work here, nine centuries later.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the Island and look along the coast. The cliffs run on in grey and green layers, with white water at their feet. The wind will not stop. The ruins will keep slowly losing ground to the sea, as they have for hundreds of years. What remains is a place where a real early community, a medieval earl, and a famous legend all share the same small rock... and where the waves are still the loudest voice of all.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with strong grip for wet slate paths.
Check tide times before planning a visit to Merlin's Cave.
Come early in the day to cross the bridge quietly.
Bring a windproof layer even on sunny days.
Allow extra time for the steep climb back to the village.
