The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against stone, softly and steadily, just below the white walls. A swan drifts past the base of a tower. The air carries the faint salt of the nearby Baltic coast, mixed with damp grass and old timber. Standing here, you see a building that seems to float. Its pale walls rise straight out of a quiet lake, and their reflection shivers whenever the wind crosses the water.

This is Glücksburg Castle, in the far north of Germany, close to the Danish border. It is one of the most important Renaissance water castles in northern Europe. A water castle is simply a fortified house protected by water instead of hills or cliffs. Here the lake does the work that a moat does elsewhere. The castle stands on its own foundations in the water, and only a narrow approach connects it to dry land.

The castle dates from the late sixteenth century. It was built in the 1580s for Duke John the Younger, a prince of the Schleswig-Holstein ducal family. He was a younger son, which in noble families often meant a modest inheritance. John made the most of his share. He built farms, mills and estates across his lands, and this castle became the centre of his small but carefully managed duchy.

Here is the first surprise. The ground beneath this lake once belonged to a monastery. A community of Cistercian monks lived on this site for centuries. The Cistercians were a strict order of monks known for farming, building and quiet discipline. After the Protestant Reformation reached this region, the monastery was dissolved. Its buildings were taken down, and the stone found new use. Some of that monastic material is thought to sit inside the castle walls you are looking at.

The lake itself is also not entirely natural. It was created by damming a small stream and flooding the low ground around the building site. In other words, the duke did not find a castle on a lake. He made the lake for his castle. Look across the water now, and remember that it is a piece of sixteenth-century engineering, as deliberate as the towers themselves.

Now look at the shape of the building. The main block is almost a perfect square. In fact it is three long houses joined side by side under steep roofs. At each corner, an eight-sided tower rises above the walls. The effect is compact and solid, almost like a stone chest set into the water. There is little decoration on the outside. The white plaster, the dark roofs and the regular windows give it a calm, orderly face.

The name of the castle, and of the town, comes from a motto. Duke John chose the words "Gott gebe Glück mit Frieden," which means "May God grant fortune with peace." The German word for fortune or luck is "Glück," and "Burg" means castle. So Glücksburg is, quite literally, the castle of good fortune. For a building raised in an age of religious wars, it is a hopeful name.

The second surprise is bigger. This modest ducal seat gave its name to one of the great royal families of Europe. A later branch of the family became known as the House of Glücksburg. In the nineteenth century, one of its princes became King Christian the Ninth of Denmark. His children married into royal houses across the continent, and people called him the father-in-law of Europe. Today, the royal families of Denmark and Norway descend from this line. So did the former kings of Greece, and so did Prince Philip, the husband of Queen Elizabeth the Second.

The castle also has a direct Danish story. For a time, Danish kings used Glücksburg as a summer residence, when this region was tied to the Danish crown. King Frederick the Seventh of Denmark died here in 1863. His death triggered a crisis over who should rule Schleswig and Holstein. Within a year, war broke out between Denmark on one side and Prussia and Austria on the other. Afterwards, this whole region passed into German hands. The quiet lake in front of you sits close to the centre of that story.

Inside, the castle is now a museum, cared for by a foundation linked to the ducal family. The rooms carry the layers of several centuries. You will find portraits of family members whose faces reappear in royal galleries across Europe. Look out especially for the gilded leather wall coverings. These are panels of leather, pressed with patterns and coated so they shine like gold. They were a luxury in their day, and few such rooms survive in such good condition.

The castle chapel is another highlight. It is still used for services and family occasions, which gives it a lived-in feeling that many museum chapels lack. Pause in the doorway and notice the balance between the white walls and the carved, painted woodwork. Also notice the acoustics. Even a footstep sounds clear here. Many visitors find that this room feels the most personal in the whole building.

When you leave the castle, take time for the grounds. Paths run around the lake and through the old trees of the park. From the far bank you get the classic view: four towers, white walls and their mirror image in the water. Nearby, there is also a rose garden, laid out in later years, which is full of colour and scent in early summer. The town of Glücksburg itself is small, and the shore of the Flensburg Fjord is only a short walk away.

Before you go, turn back once more toward the water. Watch how the reflection breaks and reforms as the wind moves across the lake. More than four centuries ago, a younger son built this house and hoped for fortune with peace. The peace did not always last, but the fortune of his family spread across a whole continent. Listen again to the water against the stone... and carry that quiet sound with you as you walk on.
