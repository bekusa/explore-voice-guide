The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the stone platforms, two rivers meet. The Mosna runs along one side of the site, and the smaller Huachecsa comes down from the mountains to join it. The sound of moving water is constant here, even on a still afternoon. The air is thin and cool, because you are standing at more than three thousand metres in the Peruvian Andes. Under your feet, the ground is packed earth and grass, and all around you rise walls of grey stone, fitted in long horizontal courses.

This is Chavín de Huántar, in the Huari Province of the Ancash region. Archaeologists date the beginnings of this ceremonial centre to around 1200 BC. It was built, enlarged, and rebuilt over many centuries, and it reached its height in the first millennium before Christ. That makes it far older than the Inca, by roughly two thousand years. In 1985, UNESCO added the site to its World Heritage list. The town of Chavín, with its plaza and tiled roofs, sits just beside the ruins today.

Why build here, in a narrow valley between high ranges? Part of the answer is location. This valley sits between the Pacific coast and the Amazon lowlands, with the snow peaks of the Cordillera Blanca to the west. People, goods, and ideas moved through these mountains. Archaeologists have found materials here that came from far away, including seashells from the warm waters of the coast. Chavín was not a city of farmers so much as a place people came to, for ritual and for meaning.

The complex has two main phases that visitors usually hear about. The Old Temple is a U-shaped building, with two arms that open toward the east, toward the rising sun. In front of it lies a sunken Circular Plaza, lined with carved stone panels. Later, the builders expanded the temple to the south. Archaeologists call this larger structure the New Temple, and it faces a much bigger Square Plaza. Walk slowly between the two, and you can feel how the space was designed to guide a crowd.

Look closely at the stone panels around the Circular Plaza. They show a procession of figures. Some are jaguars, carved in profile. Others are human figures with animal features — fangs, claws, and serpents in place of hair. One figure holds a tall, ribbed stalk. Many researchers identify it as the San Pedro cactus, a plant that can cause visions. This is one of the strongest clues that ritual here involved altered states of mind.

Now the first surprise. Much of Chavín is underground. The temple platforms are full of narrow stone passages, called galleries. They are low, dark, and they turn at sharp angles. Small shafts bring in air from outside, and channels carry water beneath the floors. Inside, the light drops away quickly, and your own footsteps sound loud. For a pilgrim three thousand years ago, carrying a flickering flame, these corridors must have felt like a journey into another world.

At the heart of the Old Temple, where two galleries cross, stands the most important object at Chavín. It is the Lanzón, a tall carved block of white granite, roughly four and a half metres high. Its shape resembles a long blade, or perhaps the pointed digging stick farmers used. A fanged being with clawed hands is carved on its surface. One hand points up, and the other points down. The Lanzón still stands exactly where its makers placed it, which is rare for a sculpture this old.

Here is the second surprise. The Lanzón's top reaches through the ceiling of its chamber. Above it, in a gallery on the next level, there may have been a small opening. Some researchers suggest that a hidden priest could speak from there, so that the god appeared to talk. Others connect this to the water channels below, which can produce a deep rushing sound. These are interpretations, not certainties. But they fit the careful design of the whole building.

Sound mattered here in other ways too. In 2001, a team led by the archaeologist John Rick found around twenty decorated trumpets made from large seashells, known in the Andes as pututus. When blown, they produce a long, low call. Acoustic studies suggest that the galleries could carry and change these sounds. Imagine a crowd waiting in the plaza at night, hearing a shell trumpet echo up from inside the stone, with no visible source.

On the New Temple, look for the Black and White Portal. Two cylindrical columns flank the entrance. One side is made of dark stone and the other of pale stone, and carved birds of prey appear on them. Many scholars read this as a deliberate contrast of opposites — dark and light, perhaps female and male. Nearby, a single carved stone head remains fixed in a wall. These heads, called tenon heads, once lined the outer walls in a row, staring down at arriving visitors.

Most of the other tenon heads are now in the National Chavín Museum, a short walk away in the modern town. The museum also holds the Tello Obelisk, a carved pillar covered with caimans, plants, and other creatures. It is named after Julio César Tello, the Peruvian archaeologist who studied Chavín in the early twentieth century. Another famous carving, the Raimondi Stela, is kept in Lima. Seeing the museum after the ruins helps you picture the walls as they once looked.

The site has survived real danger. The valley is steep, and mudslides from the mountains have struck it more than once. In 1945, a large flood of mud and rock poured down the Huachecsa and buried much of the complex. Archaeologists spent years clearing it again. As you walk, notice the slopes above the river. They show how close the mountains are, and how fragile this place has always been.

Before you leave, stand again near the edge of the Square Plaza and look around. The walls are quiet now, and the carved faces are mostly gone. But the rivers still meet below you, the shafts still breathe air into the galleries, and the Lanzón still waits in the dark. Listen for the water one more time as you walk back toward the town. It is the same sound the first visitors heard here, three thousand years ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the National Chavín Museum after walking the ruins.
Bring a light jacket for the cool high-altitude air.
Wear sturdy shoes for low, uneven underground galleries.
Walk slowly and drink water to handle the altitude.
Duck carefully inside galleries where ceilings are low.
