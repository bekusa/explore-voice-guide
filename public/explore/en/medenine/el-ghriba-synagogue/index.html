The guide

The story

Take off your shoes. The floor under your bare feet is cool, even when the Djerba sun is fierce outside. Light falls through high windows onto blue paint, blue tiles, and blue arches. The air smells faintly of candle wax and old wood. For a moment the island seems far away, and you are standing inside one of the oldest Jewish sanctuaries in Africa.

You are in Erriadh, a small village in the heart of Djerba, an island off the southern coast of Tunisia. Erriadh was long known as Hara Sghira, which means the Little Quarter. It was one of two traditional Jewish neighbourhoods on the island. Around you are low whitewashed houses, palm trees, and narrow lanes. In recent years many walls in the village have filled with murals by artists from around the world.

The synagogue's name is a puzzle in itself. In Arabic, Ghriba means something like the stranger, or the solitary one. It can also suggest something wondrous or marvellous. Nobody agrees on a single meaning. That uncertainty fits the place well. This is a building where history and legend overlap, and where the two are very hard to separate.

Legend has it that the story began more than two and a half thousand years ago. According to the tradition, Jewish priests fled Jerusalem after the destruction of the First Temple. They are said to have carried a door, or a stone, from the Temple itself. Where they set it down, they built a sanctuary. Locals say that piece of the Temple is still built into the synagogue. Historians cannot confirm this, but the story has shaped how the community sees itself for centuries.

Another tale explains the name in a different way. Locals tell of a mysterious young woman who arrived alone and lived apart from the villagers. One night her small hut caught fire. When people reached her, she had died, but her body was untouched by the flames. The villagers took this as a sign of holiness. According to this popular tale, the synagogue rose on the spot where she was found.

Now look at the building itself. What you see today is not ancient. The present structure dates mostly from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, built over a much older holy site. The style mixes North African and Mediterranean traditions. Notice the rows of horseshoe and rounded arches, the painted wooden ceilings, and the glazed tiles in deep blues and greens. The colours keep the hall calm and cool.

The interior has two connected halls. Benches run along the walls, so worshippers face each other as well as the ark. The ark is the cabinet where the Torah scrolls are kept. Around it hang silver lamps and many small plaques. Some carry the names of families. Others carry dedications from visitors who came here to pray for health, for marriage, or for a child.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Beneath part of the sanctuary there is a small cavity, often described as a grotto. During the pilgrimage, women write wishes and prayers on hard-boiled eggs. They place the eggs in this space and leave them there overnight. Later, the eggs are sometimes eaten as a blessing. It is a quiet, very personal ritual, and it shows how faith here mixes with older folk customs of the region.

That pilgrimage is the heartbeat of El Ghriba. Every spring, around the Jewish holiday of Lag BaOmer, which falls a little over a month after Passover, thousands of pilgrims arrive. Some come from Tunis and Djerba. Others travel from France, Israel, and beyond, many with family roots on this island. There are songs, prayers, shared meals, and the sound of Arabic and French mixing with Hebrew.

The high point is the procession of the menara. This is a tall, tiered structure, covered in colourful scarves and decorations. Pilgrims carry it through the village streets while people sing and throw flowers. Tunisian Muslims from the area have long joined the crowds as neighbours and onlookers. For many people, this festival is a living example of coexistence on the island.

The synagogue has also known violence, and it is honest to remember it. In April 2002, a truck filled with gas exploded outside the walls in an attack claimed by al-Qaeda. Many people were killed, most of them tourists. In May 2023, during the annual pilgrimage, a gunman attacked near the site and killed pilgrims and members of the security forces. You will notice the security today. It is part of the reality of this place.

And yet the community continues. The Jewish population of Djerba is small now, but it is one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities in the Arab world. Families still pray here. Children still study Hebrew on the island. In 2023, Djerba was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, recognised for its traditional pattern of island settlement, and sites linked to its Jewish life form part of that story.

As a visitor, be respectful. Cover your head when asked, and dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Shoes come off at the entrance. Photography may be limited in certain areas, so ask before you lift your camera. Across the courtyard stands a large building once used as a hostel for pilgrims. Its arcades and upper balcony help you imagine the crowds that fill this space each spring.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the main hall. Look up at the painted ceiling, then down at the worn floor under your feet. Generations of bare feet have crossed it, carrying prayers, fears, and hopes. When you step back outside, notice the bright light again, the white walls, and the quiet lanes of Erriadh. The island has kept this small blue room for a very long time.
