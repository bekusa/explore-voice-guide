The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The stone floor is cool, smooth, and polished by bare soles over many centuries. You left your shoes at the entrance, and now you feel the temple before you see it. There is the faint sweetness of jasmine, the smoky edge of camphor, and somewhere ahead, the ringing of a small brass bell. Outside, Chennai honks and hums. In here, the sound drops away to footsteps and murmured prayer.

You are standing in the Parthasarathy Temple, in the old neighbourhood of Triplicane. The temple is dedicated to Vishnu, one of the principal gods of Hinduism, in his form as Krishna. The name Parthasarathy means 'the charioteer of Partha'. Partha is another name for Arjuna, the warrior prince of the great Indian epic, the Mahabharata. In that story, Krishna drives Arjuna's chariot into battle and gives him the spiritual teaching known as the Bhagavad Gita. This temple remembers Krishna in that humble, powerful role — not as a king, but as a driver and a guide.

The neighbourhood around you takes its name from the temple's oldest surroundings. Triplicane is an English rendering of the Tamil name Thiruvallikeni. It is usually explained as 'the sacred pond of lilies'. That pond still exists. It is the temple tank, a large stepped pool just in front of the main entrance. In the early morning, when the light is low and soft, the steps go down into still, green water, and the tower above is reflected on its surface.

This is one of the oldest temples in Chennai, and it is far older than the city itself. The core of the shrine goes back to the Pallava dynasty, a line of kings who ruled much of this coast more than a thousand years ago. Inscriptions on the temple walls are generally dated to around the eighth century. Later rulers, including the Cholas and the kings of the Vijayanagara empire, added halls, shrines, and gateways. So what you see today is not one building. It is many generations of building, layered one on another.

Before any king built here, poets sang here. The temple is one of the 108 Divya Desams. These are the sacred abodes of Vishnu praised in the hymns of the Alvars, a group of Tamil poet-saints who lived roughly between the sixth and ninth centuries. Their songs were collected into a large body of devotional poetry that is still recited today. When you hear chanting in Tamil inside these walls, you may be hearing verses that are well over a thousand years old.

Now look up at the gateway tower, the gopuram. It rises in stacked tiers, crowded with painted figures of gods, guardians, and mythical creatures. Every surface is busy. The colours are bright, often freshly repainted during periodic renovations, so the tower can look surprisingly new for something so old. Step back and let your eyes climb slowly from the base to the top. Each tier tells a little more of the sacred story.

Here is a detail that most visitors miss. The main image of Krishna inside the sanctum is unusual. He is shown with a moustache, which is rare for Krishna in temple art. And according to devotional tradition, the face of the image carries marks from arrows. Legend has it that these are wounds Krishna received while driving Arjuna's chariot in the great battle. This is a story of faith, not of documented history, but it shapes how devotees see the god here — as someone who shared the danger of the battlefield.

There is a second surprise. The image in the sanctum is not alone. Krishna stands here with members of his family, including his consort Rukmini, his brother Balarama, and others from the epic tradition. That is uncommon. And the temple also holds shrines to several other forms of Vishnu, including Rama, Narasimha the lion-man, and Ranganatha, the god reclining on a great serpent. In a single complex, you can move between several very different faces of the same deity.

You will notice that the inner sanctum is quiet and dim. The only light often comes from oil lamps. Devotees press forward, palms together, and a priest may offer a flame, holy water, or a small brass crown that is briefly touched to the head. Photography is generally not permitted in the inner shrine. Take this as a gift. It forces you to simply stand, look, and listen.

One story from the neighbourhood links the temple to modern Tamil literature. Subramania Bharati, one of the most beloved Tamil poets and a voice of the Indian independence movement, lived in Triplicane. He is remembered for visiting the temple and feeding its elephant. In 1921, the elephant struck him. He survived the injury but died later that same year. His former home nearby is now preserved as a memorial, and it is a short walk from here.

The temple is busiest during its festivals. The most important is Vaikunta Ekadasi, a sacred day in the Tamil month of Margazhi, which falls in December or January. On that day, a special gateway called the door to heaven is opened, and crowds fill the streets from before dawn. During the larger annual festival, temple chariots and decorated processional images are carried through the surrounding roads. The whole neighbourhood becomes part of the ritual.

When you step back outside, take a slow look at the streets around you. Triplicane is a dense, old quarter of narrow lanes, small shops, and traditional houses. You will see flower sellers threading jasmine into long garlands. You will see shops selling brass lamps, incense, and coconuts for offerings. Small eateries serve filter coffee and simple vegetarian meals. Much of the life of these streets is still organised around the rhythm of the temple.

And just to the east, a short walk away, is the sea. Marina Beach runs along this stretch of the Bay of Bengal. It is a striking contrast — the closed, lamp-lit world of the sanctum, and then the open sky and the wind off the water. Many visitors pair the two in a single morning. Prayer first, and then the long sand and the sound of the waves.

Before you leave, pause once more by the temple tank. Look at the steps going down into the water. Listen for the bells, the chanting, the scooters in the lane behind you. People have come to this spot for more than a thousand years, to see a god who chose to be a charioteer. As you walk back into the city, notice the jasmine still in the air — and the warm stone still under your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Remove shoes at the entrance before entering the complex.
Wear modest clothing covering shoulders and knees.
Visit early morning for softer light over the tank.
Expect large crowds during the Vaikunta Ekadasi festival.
Pair your visit with a walk to Marina Beach.
