The guide

The story

Start with your bare feet. The stone floor is cool in the shaded corners and warm where the sun has reached it. The air smells of jasmine, camphor and ghee from small brass lamps. Somewhere a bell rings, then another. Around you, people move with quiet purpose. Welcome to Kapaleeshwarar Temple, in the heart of Mylapore, one of the oldest neighbourhoods in Chennai.

This is a living temple, not a museum. It is dedicated to Shiva, one of the principal gods of Hinduism, who is worshipped here as Kapaleeshwarar. His consort, the goddess Parvati, is worshipped here as Karpagambal. Her name is often explained as the goddess of the wish-granting tree. Families come here every day. They come to pray, to mark a birthday, to bless a new vehicle, or simply to sit for a while in the shade.

The neighbourhood name carries the temple's founding story. Legend has it that Parvati once took the form of a peahen and worshipped Shiva on this ground. In Tamil, the word for peacock is mayil. Locals say that is how Mylapore got its name, the town of the peacock. Treat this as sacred story rather than recorded history. Still, the peacock appears again and again here, in carvings, paintings and devotional songs.

The temple itself also has a layered past. Tamil devotional poetry from more than a thousand years ago praises a Shiva shrine at Mylapore. Many historians believe the original temple stood closer to the shore. According to widely repeated accounts, that earlier shrine was lost after Portuguese settlers arrived on this coast in the sixteenth century. The temple you see today was rebuilt inland in later centuries. It has been expanded and repainted many times since.

Now look up at the great gateway tower. In South Indian temples, this kind of tower is called a gopuram. It rises in narrowing tiers, and every tier is crowded with figures. There are gods, goddesses, guardians, dancers, sages and animals. They are painted in strong blues, pinks, yellows and greens. These figures are not carved from stone. They are modelled in plaster over a frame, so they can be repaired and repainted over the years.

This architecture belongs to the Dravidian tradition, the temple style of South India. Its signature is the gateway, not the sanctum. In many northern Indian temples, the tallest tower sits above the inner shrine. Here it is the opposite. The highest structure is the entrance, and the sanctum is lower and darker. You pass from bright, noisy colour into a quieter, shadowed space as you move inward. Many visitors only notice this reversal once someone points it out.

Inside the walls, the temple opens into courtyards and pillared halls. The pillars carry carvings of deities and mythical creatures. Small shrines sit along the edges, each with its own image and its own lamp. You will see people walking slowly around the central shrines in a clockwise direction. This circular walk is an act of devotion. If you join it, keep to the same direction and the same calm pace as everyone else.

Look for the sacred tree inside the complex. Every major Tamil temple has a tree linked to its legend. Here it is a punnai tree. According to the temple story, Parvati in her peahen form worshipped Shiva beneath this kind of tree. Nearby, look for a small shrine that shows a peahen beside a Shiva linga, the rounded stone form in which Shiva is worshipped. It is easy to walk past, and many visitors miss it.

The temple also carries the memory of Tamil saint-poets. A group of Shaivite poets known as the Nayanars wrote hymns to Shiva across this region. One of them, Sambandar, is said to have sung about Mylapore. A well-known legend says that he brought a young woman named Poompavai back to life from her ashes here. Images of the sixty-three Nayanars appear in the temple. They are carried out in procession during the great annual festival.

That festival usually falls in the Tamil month of Panguni, around March or April. For days, the surrounding streets fill with music, drums and crowds. Deities ride out on decorated vehicles shaped like animals and mythical beings. A tall wooden chariot is pulled through the streets by hundreds of hands. The day when the sixty-three saints are paraded is one of the most crowded days of the year in Mylapore.

Step outside to the west and you reach the temple tank. It is a broad, square pool with stone steps on every side. A small pavilion stands in the centre of the water. Temple tanks like this were once central to daily life. People bathed here, collected water and held rituals on the steps. In some festivals, the deities are floated across the water on a lit raft at night.

The streets around the temple are part of the experience. These roads, which circle the complex, are lined with shops selling flowers, brass lamps, silk, music and sweets. Vendors string jasmine into long white garlands while you watch. Old houses with small balconies stand between newer buildings. Tradition also links Mylapore with Thiruvalluvar, the poet of an ancient Tamil book of ethics. A shrine to him stands not far from here.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen for the bells, the chanting, the scooters on the street outside. Look again at the painted figures on the gateway, crowded together against the sky. This place has been rebuilt, repainted and rededicated many times. What has not changed is the daily rhythm of people coming here. As you walk out, notice the jasmine on the steps, and carry a little of that quiet with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Remove your shoes at the entrance before stepping inside.
Wear clothing that covers shoulders and knees.
Come early morning for cooler stone and calmer courtyards.
Walk clockwise around shrines, matching the pace of worshippers.
Ask before photographing people, priests or inner shrines.
