The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here the wind moves through dry grass and low chaparral, and the traffic of Simi Valley sounds very far away. The air smells of warm stone and sage. Red clay roof tiles catch the sun along the long, low buildings. Beyond them, brown hills fold toward the horizon in every direction. You are standing on a hilltop in Southern California, at the presidential library of Ronald Reagan, the fortieth president of the United States.

Reagan chose this place himself. He was not a native Californian. He was born in Illinois, but he came west as a young man and built a life here. First he worked as a Hollywood actor, and later he served as governor of California. The library sits on high ground with open views across the valley. On clear days, the light out here feels wide and clean, and you can see why the setting appealed to a man who loved ranches and open country.

Look at the architecture around you. The buildings follow a Spanish mission style. They have pale walls, red tile roofs, shaded walkways and courtyards. This design echoes the old Spanish missions that dot the California coast. Those were the religious outposts built when Spain ruled this region. The style feels calm and earthbound rather than grand. The buildings stay low and wide across the ridge. They do not tower over the hill.

The library was dedicated in November 1991, and that ceremony made history of its own. Five American presidents stood together on this hilltop. Richard Nixon, Gerald Ford, Jimmy Carter, Ronald Reagan, and George H. W. Bush all attended. Never before had five presidents, past and present, gathered in one place. Picture them on a single stage in the California sun, men who had been rivals and allies over several decades of American politics.

Like other presidential libraries, this one has two roles. It is a public museum, and it is also an archive. The National Archives manages the official records of the Reagan years. These include millions of pages of documents, photographs and recordings that historians still study. A private foundation supports the museum and the wider grounds. So the galleries you walk through tell one story. The quiet archive rooms behind them hold the raw material of history.

Inside, the museum follows Reagan's life in roughly chronological order. It begins with his small-town childhood in the Midwest. Then it moves through his years in radio and film and into politics. The exhibits cover his two terms as president through the 1980s. That decade included the Cold War standoff with the Soviet Union, major tax and economic debates, and an assassination attempt early in his first term. Take your time in these rooms. Many visitors rush toward the aircraft and miss the smaller personal objects along the way.

One gallery holds a full-size replica of the Oval Office as it looked during the Reagan years. Here is a detail many people miss. Look for the jar of jelly beans. Reagan was famously fond of them, and they became a small symbol of his presidency. Then stand at the doorway and take in the room as a whole. Notice the colours, the desk, and the way the light falls through the windows. The space feels both formal and surprisingly domestic.

Now for the surprise that brings many people up this hill. A huge glass pavilion holds a real Air Force One. This is not a model. It is the actual Boeing 707 that carried several American presidents, from Richard Nixon through George W. Bush. Reagan flew on it many times during his years in office. Engineers took the aircraft apart, carried it here in pieces, and put it back together inside the building. Then the pavilion walls were completed around it.

Walk up the stairs and step inside the cabin. The space feels narrower than most people expect. You can see the president's office and the seating for staff and press. Small details show the age of the aircraft. The technology looks plain by today's standards, and the fittings feel more like a working tool than a luxury. Near the plane, look for a presidential helicopter of the type called Marine One. It stands as a reminder of how presidents travel on shorter trips.

Outside, on the grounds, stands a section of the Berlin Wall. For almost three decades, that wall divided the city of Berlin into East and West, and it became a symbol of the Cold War. In 1987, Reagan spoke near the wall and called on the Soviet leader to tear it down. Two years later, in 1989, the wall fell. Look closely at the concrete. One side carries painted colour, and the other side looks plain and grey.

That difference is worth a moment of thought. The painted face came from the western side, where people could walk up and add graffiti. The bare face came from the eastern side, where guards kept people away. So a single slab of concrete holds two different experiences of the same city. Many visitors photograph the colourful side and walk on. Try to walk around and look at both faces before you leave.

On the western edge of the grounds, with views out over the hills, you will find the memorial site. Ronald Reagan died in 2004 and was buried here, on the hilltop he had chosen. His wife, Nancy Reagan, was buried beside him in 2016. The area is quiet and simple. A curved wall carries an inscription in his own words about his belief in human goodness and the worth of every life. Visitors often lower their voices here without being asked.

Before you go, find a spot along the terrace and turn toward the valley. Notice the layers of hills, the pale haze in the distance, and the small white shapes of homes far below. Listen again for the wind in the grass. Whatever you think of the politics told inside these walls, this hilltop gives you a wide and quiet view. As you walk back toward the car park, take one last look at the red roofs against the sky.

Practical tips
