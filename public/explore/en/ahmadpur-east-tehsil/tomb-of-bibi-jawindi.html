The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves across open ground, and somewhere a bird calls from the brick. Underfoot the earth is pale and fine, the colour of river silt. Ahead of you stands a tomb that seems to have been cut in half with a knife. One side still carries its glazed blue tiles. The other side is simply... gone. You are standing in Uch Sharif, an old town in Ahmadpur East Tehsil, in the south of Pakistan's Punjab. This is the Tomb of Bibi Jawindi.

Let us begin with the town itself, because the tomb makes little sense without it. Uch is one of the oldest settlements in this part of South Asia. For centuries it was a centre of Sufi learning. Sufism is the mystical tradition within Islam, focused on the inner, personal search for God. Teachers settled here, students travelled long distances to hear them, and their families became honoured lineages. When those teachers and their relatives died, the community built shrines over their graves. Over time Uch filled with them.

Bibi Jawindi belonged to one of these honoured families. Tradition holds that she was a great-granddaughter of a famous Sufi saint of Uch, Jahaniyan Jahangasht. His name means, roughly, the one who travelled the whole world. He was a scholar and a wanderer, and his descendants carried great respect in the town. Bibi Jawindi herself is remembered as a woman of piety. Beyond that, the reliable details of her life are few. What survives most clearly is the building raised in her memory.

The tomb dates to the late fifteenth century. Local tradition and many accounts credit a patron named Dilshad, often described as a prince from Khorasan, a region of eastern Persia. Some details of that story are debated, so treat the name as tradition rather than settled record. What is certain is the ambition of the work. Someone spent a great deal of money and skill here. You can see it in the shape, the tiles, and the careful brickwork that still holds together after five centuries.

Now look at the form. The tomb is an octagon, an eight-sided building. It rises in stages, each one a little narrower than the one below. At each corner stands a slender turret, tapering as it climbs. On top sits a dome. This octagonal design was a regional style. The great tomb of Rukn-e-Alam in Multan, a city to the north, uses a similar plan. Builders in this river country clearly looked at one another's work and learned from it.

The material is fired brick. There is very little stone in this flat alluvial plain, so builders worked with what the rivers gave them. Clay was shaped, fired, and laid in patient courses. Look closely at the walls and you will see the rhythm of those bricks. In some places the surface is smooth. In others it is worn and pitted by wind and heat. The brick shows every century it has survived.

Here is the detail that stops most visitors. The colour. The outer walls were dressed in glazed tiles, mainly deep blue, turquoise, and white. Glazed tile work like this is a hallmark of the Multan region. The patterns are geometric and floral, repeated in bands and panels. Under a strong sun the blue can look almost electric against the pale sky. Where tiles have fallen away, you can see the bare brick beneath, like a page with the ink rubbed off.

So why is the building broken? The answer is water. Uch stands near the meeting point of great rivers, and those rivers do not stay in one place. In the early nineteenth century a major flood swept through. The river ate away the ground beneath the tomb, and roughly half of the structure collapsed. The surviving half was left standing, open to the air. What you see today is the result of that single event... a monument sliced in two by nature.

And that collapse gives you a rare view. In most tombs you see only the finished outside and the finished inside. Here you can read the construction itself. Notice how thick the walls are. Notice how the brick courses curve upward to carry the weight of the dome. It is almost like looking at an architect's drawing in three dimensions. The damage is a loss — but it is also a lesson in how these buildings were made.

The tomb does not stand alone. Nearby are other shrines from the same tradition. One is linked to a teacher of the saintly family. Another, smaller tomb is traditionally said to belong to the master builder who worked on Bibi Jawindi's tomb. Locals say he asked to be buried close to his finest work. Whether or not the story is exact, it is a gentle idea. Together these buildings form one of the most recognisable groups of monuments in Pakistan.

That group has gained wider attention. The monuments of Uch have been placed on UNESCO's tentative list. This is the first step toward possible World Heritage status, a recognition for places of outstanding value to all humanity. It is not yet a final listing. But it shows that experts consider these tombs important beyond the region. Conservation work has been carried out over the years to support the remaining walls, and you may notice newer brick where repairs were made.

Take a moment to walk slowly around the tomb. Change your angle every few steps. From one side it looks complete and proud. From another it looks fragile, like a stage set. The light matters too. In the early morning and late afternoon the bricks turn warm and golden, and the blue tiles glow. At midday the sun is hard, and the shadows inside the broken half become deep and dark.

You may also notice visitors who come not as tourists but as pilgrims. For many people in Uch, these shrines are living places of prayer, not museum pieces. Some may pause quietly, offer a prayer, or touch the wall. Move gently around them. Dress modestly, and if you are unsure where to walk or whether to photograph someone, a simple gesture of asking goes a long way.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Look at the line where the building ends and the open sky begins. On one side, five centuries of devotion in brick and blue glaze. On the other side, empty air, left by a river that moved. Listen again to the wind. As you walk away, glance back over your shoulder. The tomb will look different from there... smaller, quieter, and still standing.
