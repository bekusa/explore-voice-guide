The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the rainforest stretches in every direction, a dense green that seems to have no edge. Then the canopy breaks. Walls of dark rock rise straight out of the trees, flat on top and sheer on the sides. Mist clings to their rims in the early hours. From a small plane, the hum of the engine is the only human sound for many kilometres. This is the Serranía de Chiribiquete, the deep Colombian Amazon.

Most people who experience Chiribiquete do it exactly like this, from the air. The park is not open to ordinary tourism on foot. Its rules protect fragile ecosystems, ancient art, and Indigenous communities who live here by choice, away from the outside world. Visitors usually fly over the park on authorised flights. Many of these flights leave from the Guaviare region, at the northern side of this wilderness. So if you are looking out of a window right now, you are seeing the park the way it is meant to be seen by outsiders.

The formations below are called tepuis. The word comes from the Pemón people of Venezuela and means something like house of the gods. A tepui is a table mountain. It is a block of very old sandstone with steep cliffs and a flat or gently broken summit. These rocks belong to the Guiana Shield, one of the oldest geological regions on Earth. Over unimaginable spans of time, rain and rivers cut away the softer land around them. The hard tops stayed behind, like islands in a sea of forest.

That isolation matters. On top of a tepui, plants and small animals can live separated from the lowland forest below for very long periods. Scientists describe these summits as places with high levels of species found nowhere else. Some plants grow in thin, poor soil on bare rock. Down in the forest, the scene changes completely. Here jaguars, tapirs, monkeys, and hundreds of bird species share the shade. The park sits where the Amazon, the Andean foothills, and the Orinoco plains influence one another.

Now for the first surprise. Chiribiquete is enormous — it is the largest national park in Colombia. It was first declared a protected area in 1989. Then it was expanded twice, most recently in 2018, to roughly 4.3 million hectares. That is an area larger than some European countries. It reaches across the departments of Caquetá and Guaviare. And yet, almost no roads enter it. Rivers and the sky are still the main ways to see its borders.

The second surprise is hidden on the rock walls themselves. Under overhangs and along the cliffs, there are paintings in red ochre. Researchers have recorded tens of thousands of figures across many sites. They show hunters, dancers, deer, birds, and above all, the jaguar. Some of the oldest painted layers are believed to be many thousands of years old. Dating rock art is difficult, and specialists still debate exact ages. But it is clear that people have returned to these walls across a vast stretch of time.

The jaguar is the key to understanding this place. For many Indigenous peoples of the Amazon, the jaguar is more than an animal. It is linked to power, protection, and the shamans who guide communities. When UNESCO inscribed Chiribiquete on the World Heritage List in 2018, it gave the site a second name: The Maloca of the Jaguar. A maloca is a large communal house of the Amazon peoples. The name suggests the whole mountain range as a sacred home.

That inscription was special for another reason. Chiribiquete was listed as a mixed site. This means it was recognised for both its natural value and its cultural value at the same time. Very few places in the world hold that double status. Here the two cannot really be separated. The rock, the forest, the animals, and the painted images all belong to one living landscape. People did not paint on empty walls. They painted on mountains they already considered meaningful.

The park also protects people. Indigenous groups are believed to live inside its boundaries in voluntary isolation. These are communities who have chosen not to have regular contact with outsiders. Colombia's protection of the area respects that choice. This is one important reason why tourists cannot hike in freely. Contact can bring diseases that isolated communities have no defence against. So the distance between you and the forest floor is not only geography. It is also a form of respect.

Rivers draw the map down there. Dark, winding waterways move through the forest and around the bases of the tepuis. In places, water drops off the rock edges as narrow waterfalls. The colour of the rivers often looks like strong tea. This comes from plant matter dissolved from the forest floor. From above, these ribbons of water help you understand scale. A river that looks thin from the plane may be wide enough for a boat to travel for days.

Chiribiquete is not untouched by pressure. Its edges face threats from deforestation, cattle ranching, and illegal activities in the wider region. Satellite images in recent years have shown forest loss near some boundaries. This is part of why the expansions of the park mattered so much. Protecting the core means protecting the forest around it too. Conservation here depends on rangers, scientists, local communities, and Indigenous knowledge working over a very large and remote area.

If you are on the ground in Guaviare instead of in the air, the wider region still has much to offer. Around San José del Guaviare, there are rock formations, natural pools, and other painted rock sites outside the park, open to guided visits. They help visitors understand the same tradition of rock art and the same kind of landscape. Local guides know these trails well. Walking them gives you the heat, the insects, and the smell of wet earth that a flight cannot.

Now, look again at what is in front of you. Notice how the shadows move across the rock faces as clouds drift past. Notice how the green below has dozens of shades, not one. Somewhere on those walls, a red jaguar may have watched this view for thousands of years. As the plane turns, or as you walk away, carry that thought with you. Some places are protected precisely because most of us will only ever see them from a distance.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book an authorised overflight; ground access is restricted.
Ask for a window seat on the side facing the tepuis.
Fly in the morning when mist often frames the cliffs.
Use a fast shutter speed to cut plane vibration.
Visit guided rock art sites near San José del Guaviare.
