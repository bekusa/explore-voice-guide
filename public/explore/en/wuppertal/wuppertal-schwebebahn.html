The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, steel wheels roll along a steel rail, and the sound comes down as a low, rising hum. Then the car swings into view, hanging beneath the track and tilting gently as it takes a curve. Below it, the Wupper River runs brown and green between stone banks. You are standing under one of the strangest railways in the world, and the people of Wuppertal ride it to work every day.

This is the Schwebebahn, which translates roughly as the floating railway. The name is a little poetic, but it is also accurate. The cars do not sit on rails like a normal train. They hang from them, suspended beneath a long steel frame, so the whole carriage seems to drift through the air. Most of the route follows the river itself, with the track carried high above the water.

To understand why anyone would build a railway over a river, you have to picture this valley in the late nineteenth century. The Wupper valley was one of the early industrial heartlands of Germany. Textile mills, dye works and factories lined the narrow floor of the valley. Towns like Elberfeld and Barmen grew fast, and the streets became crowded. There was very little flat land left for a new transport line.

The answer was to use the one open space nobody else wanted — the river. A Cologne engineer named Eugen Langen had developed a system of suspended rail. Instead of fighting for room on the ground, the trains would travel above the water. The first section opened in 1901, and the full line was completed a few years later. More than a century on, the same basic idea still carries passengers up and down the valley.

Here is something many visitors miss. The city called Wuppertal did not exist when the Schwebebahn opened. The railway was built to link separate towns along the valley. Those towns were only merged into one city in 1929. So in a sense, the railway helped to stitch the city together before it even had a name. Today the line runs between Oberbarmen in the east and Vohwinkel in the west.

Now look up at the structure itself. The track rests on a long chain of steel supports, many of them shaped like a wide letter A, with legs planted on either bank. Rivets cover the beams in neat rows. The frame is painted, and it looks light from a distance, almost like lace. Up close, you can see how heavy the steel really is. Near the western end, the track leaves the river and runs above a street instead.

If you take a ride, pay attention to the motion. The cars swing slightly outward on the curves, a little like a pendulum. It is a soft, rocking movement, not a jolt. Through the windows you see the valley from an unusual angle — rooftops, backyards, church towers, the river directly beneath your feet. You pass close to the upper floors of buildings, and you can sometimes glimpse into offices and apartments.

One passenger made the Schwebebahn famous in a way no engineer ever planned. In the summer of 1950, a young circus elephant named Tuffi was taken on a ride as a publicity stunt. The elephant became nervous in the crowded car, broke through the side, and fell into the river below. Remarkably, Tuffi survived with only minor injuries. Locals still tell the story with affection, and you will see the elephant on postcards and souvenirs across the city.

There is a quieter chapter too. In 1999, a serious accident occurred after overnight construction work, when a piece of equipment was left on the track. A train struck it and a car fell into the river, and several people lost their lives. It was the worst day in the railway's long history. The event led to major reviews of safety, and a large renovation of the whole structure followed in the years after.

That renovation changed much of what you see today. Many of the old supports and stations were rebuilt or replaced, though the overall look was kept. In the 2010s, a new generation of cars entered service, with a modern design and bright interiors. Yet the system still carries the spirit of the original. It has been protected as a historic monument, and the city treats it as part of its identity.

For something special, watch for the Kaiserwagen, the Emperor's car. This historic carriage is linked to the trial ride taken by Emperor Wilhelm the Second around the time the line was being built. It is kept for special trips and events rather than daily service. The wood panelling and old fittings give a sense of how a journey felt in the early twentieth century, long before plastic seats and digital displays.

The railway has also found its way into art and film. Wuppertal is the home of the dance company created by the choreographer Pina Bausch, and the director Wim Wenders included the Schwebebahn in his documentary about her work. Seen on screen, the cars gliding above the water look almost unreal. Seen in person, they become something ordinary — a commuter train full of students, shoppers and office workers checking their phones.

So take a moment before you move on. Watch the next car approach and listen to the hum grow louder, then fade. Look at the river under the track, at the rivets on the steel, at the people waiting on the platform as if nothing here were unusual. For them, it is simply the way to get home. As you walk along the bank, keep glancing up. The railway follows you through the valley, quietly, the way it has for well over a hundred years.
