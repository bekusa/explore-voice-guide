The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere behind the trees, a train of riders rushes over a wooden track. The timber frame rattles and groans under the weight. Closer by, someone in a winged helmet is shouting cheerful insults at a passing Roman soldier. The air smells of sugared waffles, pine needles and warm dust. This is Parc Astérix, in the forest country north of Paris. It is a theme park built around a small Gaulish village that refused to surrender to Rome.

Before the rides, it helps to know the story behind them. Astérix was created by two Frenchmen. The writer René Goscinny and the artist Albert Uderzo first published him in 1959, in a French comics magazine called Pilote. The idea was simple. The year is 50 BCE. Julius Caesar has conquered all of Gaul, the land that later became France. Well, nearly all of it. One small village in the far northwest still holds out against the Roman legions.

The secret of that village is a magic potion. The druid Getafix brews it in a large cauldron, and one sip gives a person enormous strength for a short time. Astérix is small, clever and quick. His friend Obelix is huge, kind and always hungry. Obelix does not need the potion at all. As a baby, he fell into the cauldron, and the effect never wore off. Most French children know this detail by heart. Many adults do too.

The comics became a cultural institution in France, and later across much of Europe. Their humour works on two levels. Children enjoy the fights and the slapstick. Adults enjoy the wordplay, the puns hidden in the characters' names and the gentle jokes about national habits. Obelix has a famous line: "These Romans are crazy!" You will probably hear it, or see it printed somewhere, before your visit is over.

The park opened in 1989. It was a bold choice at the time. A few years later, a much larger American resort would open on the other side of Paris. Parc Astérix chose a different identity. It would be French, playful and proud of it. Instead of fairy-tale castles, it would offer Gaulish huts, Roman forts, Greek temples and Viking ships. And it would laugh at itself the way the comics do.

As you walk, notice how the park is divided into worlds. Each one takes a civilisation from the comics and turns it into a small landscape. There is the Gaulish village, with thatched roofs and wooden fences. There is the Roman Empire, with columns, arches and soldiers who march badly on purpose. There is Ancient Greece, the land of the Egyptians, and a cold northern corner for the Vikings. Another area travels through later periods of French history. The transitions are part of the fun.

Now for the rides. The first big surprise for many visitors is the wooden roller coaster. It is called Tonnerre de Zeus, which means "Zeus's Thunder", after the Greek king of the gods. It opened in 1997. Wooden coasters feel different from steel ones. The track flexes slightly, the cars shake, and the noise is constant. Riders often say it feels wilder than it looks. If you stand nearby, you can hear the whole structure working.

For something smoother but more intense, there is OzIris, in the Egyptian area. It opened in 2012. It is an inverted coaster, which means the train hangs below the track and your feet swing free. It takes its name from Osiris, an Egyptian god of the afterlife. More recently, in 2023, the park added Toutatis. This launched coaster takes its name from a god the Gauls swear by in the comics. It shoots riders forward instead of pulling them slowly up a hill.

Not everything here is about speed. A large part of the day happens in the theatres and open-air stages. The shows lean into the slapstick spirit of the comics. Expect falls, chases, splashes of water and actors who break the fourth wall to tease the crowd. There are also water rides that leave people soaked to the skin on hot days. And there are gentler rides built for small children, where the youngest Gauls can feel brave without being frightened.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The park sits inside real woodland, and it was designed around the trees rather than in place of them. Look up as you walk between areas. Tall trunks line many of the paths, and in summer the canopy gives welcome shade. In autumn, the leaves turn gold and copper, and the park often decorates for Halloween. The forest setting gives the place a softer feel than many theme parks. It feels a little like the woods around the village in the comics.

Another detail worth noticing is the language of the signs. The comics are famous for puns, and the park continues the tradition. Shop names, restaurant names and ride names often play on Latin, Greek or French words. If you speak French, many of them will make you smile. If you do not, ask a French-speaking visitor or a staff member to explain one. The joke is usually better than you expect. It is a small way to share the humour that made these books so loved.

Food here follows the Gaulish spirit as well. In the comics, every adventure ends with a great feast under the stars. The villagers eat roast wild boar, and the bard Cacofonix is tied to a tree so that he cannot sing. The park borrows that mood. You will find hearty dishes, sweet crêpes and plenty of places to sit. Take a moment at one of the long tables. Watch the families, the school groups and the couples sharing one plate of chips.

As the day draws on, the light changes in the trees. The noise of the coasters carries further in the cooler air, and the queues begin to shift. Before you leave, stop somewhere quiet near the Gaulish village. Look at the huts, the wooden fences and the figures of the heroes. This whole place grew from a few drawings in a magazine, more than sixty years ago. A small village that would not give in... it is still holding out, here among the trees.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the big coasters early before the queues grow.
Bring a change of clothes for the water rides.
Check the show schedule when you arrive at the gate.
Visit in autumn for the forest colours and Halloween events.
Ask a French speaker to explain the punning ride names.
