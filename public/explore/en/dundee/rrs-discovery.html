The guide

The story

Stand on the quayside and listen for a moment. Wind comes off the River Tay and moves through the rigging, a low hum with a faint creak of rope under it. The air smells of salt and tar. In front of you is a long dark hull, with three masts rising above it and a tall funnel set between them. This ship was built here in Dundee to sail to the bottom of the world, and it came home to the city that made it.

This is RRS Discovery. The letters stand for Royal Research Ship. She was launched in Dundee in 1901, built for one purpose: to carry the British National Antarctic Expedition south into the ice. That expedition ran from 1901 to 1904. It was led by a young naval officer named Robert Falcon Scott, who would later become one of the best-known names in polar history. This ship gave him his first Antarctic command.

Why Dundee? The answer is in the city's working past. For generations Dundee was a whaling port. Its ships sailed north into Arctic waters, and its yards learned how to build wooden hulls that could survive pack ice. When the expedition's organisers needed a vessel for the Antarctic, they came to the people who already knew the ice. The Dundee Shipbuilders Company took on the job, and local skill went into every plank.

Look closely at the hull. It is made of wood, and it is very thick. Layers of timber were built up so the ship could take the pressure of ice pressing in from both sides. The bow is especially heavy. It is reinforced so the ship could ride up onto the ice and break it with her own weight. Here is something many visitors miss: the hull is rounded below the waterline. When ice closed in, the shape helped the ship rise up instead of being crushed.

There is a second surprise in the design. Discovery was a science ship, and one of her jobs was to measure the Earth's magnetism. Iron and steel distort a compass needle. So the builders kept iron fittings away from the area where the magnetic instruments were used. In a shipyard used to iron bolts and steel plates, this was an unusual request. It shows that the ship was built for careful measurement as much as for survival.

Now think about the people who lived aboard. Scott's crew included men whose names later filled the history books. Ernest Shackleton sailed on this voyage as a junior officer, years before his own famous expeditions. Edward Wilson came as a doctor, naturalist and artist, and his drawings and paintings recorded the landscape. Tom Crean, a sailor from Ireland, also served on board. For many of these men, Discovery was their first contact with Antarctica.

The expedition did not go entirely to plan. In the Antarctic, the ship anchored near a place now called Hut Point, on Ross Island. The crew built a wooden hut on the shore. Then the sea ice froze around the hull, and it did not release her. Discovery stayed locked in the ice through two winters. Relief ships came south, and in the end the crew used explosives and hard labour to break the ice. Only then could the ship sail free.

During those years the men did serious work. They made sledge journeys across the ice and pushed further south than anyone had gone before. They collected rocks, studied wildlife and recorded the weather month after month. Shackleton fell ill on one of the long sledge journeys and was sent home on a relief ship. That disappointment, many historians believe, helped drive his later ambition. The hut the crew built at Hut Point still stands in Antarctica today.

After the expedition, the ship had a long and varied working life. She was sold to the Hudson's Bay Company and carried cargo across the North Atlantic to Canada. Later she returned to science. In the 1920s she carried researchers who studied whales and the ocean around them in the Southern Ocean. Around 1930 she sailed south once more on a joint British, Australian and New Zealand expedition led by the geologist Douglas Mawson.

Then came a quieter chapter. For many years Discovery was moored in London, on the River Thames, and used as a training ship for young people, including Sea Scouts. Thousands of Londoners walked past her without knowing her Antarctic story. Over time the old timber needed care, and her future was uncertain. In 1986 she made her final journey home. She came back to Dundee and settled on the riverfront, where you see her now.

Today the city carries the ship in its identity. Dundee is often called the City of Discovery, and this vessel is a large part of the reason. The ship is cared for by the Dundee Heritage Trust, and the visitor centre beside her tells the story of the expedition. Just along the waterfront is the V&A Dundee design museum. Its angular walls are a strong contrast to the curved wooden hull of the ship.

When you go aboard, notice the scale of the living spaces. The cabins are small. The wardroom, where the officers ate, has wood panelling and a long table. Below deck the light is low and the ceilings are close above your head. Picture the same rooms in total darkness during the polar winter, with frost on the inside of the walls. The men lived and worked in these rooms for months without seeing the sun.

Before you leave, step back to the quayside and look at the ship one more time. Follow the line of the masts up against the Dundee sky. Notice how the hull sits low and solid in the water, built for a kind of sea most of us will never see. As you walk away along the Tay, listen again for the wind in the rigging... the same sound the crew heard on their long voyage south.
