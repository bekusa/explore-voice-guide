The guide

The story

The ground here is rough underfoot. Broken limestone and dark basalt lie scattered through dry grass, and the wind comes down from the hills toward the Turkish border. A river moves slowly below. Somewhere a dog barks from a farm. Stand still for a moment and let your eyes adjust. What looks at first like rubble starts to form lines... walls, steps, the curve of seating cut into a hillside. You are standing inside a city that was home to people for more than a thousand years.

This is Cyrrhus, in the far northwest of Syria. Locals often call the site Nabi Huri, after a holy man whose tomb stands nearby. The city sits in the hilly country north of Aleppo, close to the modern border. It feels remote today. In the ancient world it was not remote at all. Roads from the Euphrates, from Antioch and from Anatolia passed through this valley. Armies, merchants and pilgrims all came this way.

The story begins after the death of Alexander the Great, the Macedonian king who conquered Persia. His generals divided his empire. One of them, Seleucus, founded a kingdom that stretched across Syria and beyond. Around 300 BCE, his dynasty planted a string of Greek-style cities across the region. This one took its name from Cyrrhus, a town back in Macedonia. Greek settlers gave a familiar name to an unfamiliar valley — a small act of homesickness written into the map.

When Rome took control of Syria in the first century before Christ, Cyrrhus gained a new role. Its position made it useful as a military base. For a time, a Roman legion was stationed here, watching the routes toward the Euphrates frontier. Soldiers need roads, water and supplies. So the city grew around them. Much of the stonework you see across the site belongs to these Roman centuries, when Cyrrhus was an important regional centre.

Look toward the slope of the hill. The great theatre is the heart of the site. Its rows of seats were built into the hillside, a common Roman technique that used the natural slope to carry the weight. This was one of the largest theatres in Roman Syria. Picture thousands of people sitting here, facing the stage, with the valley spread out behind it. On a quiet day, a voice carries surprisingly well across the stone.

Now notice the materials. Much of the city was built from pale limestone, but you will also find dark basalt, a volcanic stone that is heavy and very hard. The contrast between the two is part of the character of northern Syria. Basalt survives centuries of weather. Limestone softens and pits. As you walk, run your eye along the blocks. Some still carry the clean edges left by Roman masons. Others have been worn smooth by wind and rain.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Below the city, two Roman bridges cross the river. They are built of stone with rounded arches, and they are still carrying traffic. Cars and farm vehicles drive over them today, much as carts and soldiers did many centuries ago. Few ancient bridges anywhere keep doing their original job. If you can, walk down and stand on one. It is a direct, physical link between the Roman road and the modern one.

In the early Christian centuries, Cyrrhus became a bishop's seat. Its most famous bishop was Theodoret, who led the church here in the fifth century. He was a scholar and a busy, outspoken figure in the religious arguments of his time. He also wrote a book about the hermits and ascetics of the Syrian countryside — men who lived on hilltops, in huts, even on pillars. His writing is one of the reasons historians know so much about this region's spiritual life.

The city was also linked with two early Christian saints, Cosmas and Damian. They were remembered as doctors who treated the sick without asking for payment. Tradition connected their relics with Cyrrhus, and pilgrims came here to honour them. Because of this, the city was sometimes known by another name, Hagioupolis, which means the city of saints in Greek. So this was once a place of healing and pilgrimage, as well as a place of soldiers.

Not far from the ancient centre stands a tall, six-sided stone tower. It was built in the Roman period as a tomb, probably for a wealthy local family. Centuries later, it became linked with a Muslim holy figure, Nabi Huri, whose name the site still carries. A mosque grew up beside it. People have come to pray at this spot for generations. One building has served Roman mourners and Muslim pilgrims. That kind of continuity is quietly remarkable.

After the Arab conquests of the seventh century, Cyrrhus slowly faded. Trade shifted, and other towns took over its role. Earthquakes, which strike this part of the world from time to time, did their own damage. By the Middle Ages, the city had shrunk to a small settlement. Archaeologists began serious work here in the twentieth century. French teams excavated parts of the site, and later a Syrian and Lebanese mission continued the work, mapping streets, walls and buildings.

The recent past has been hard on Cyrrhus. During the Syrian conflict, this region saw fighting, and there were reports of looting and damage at the site. What you see today may differ from older photographs. Some areas may be closed or unsafe. Treat the ruins gently. Stay on worn paths, and do not climb on loose stone. Every block that stays in place helps future archaeologists read the story of the city more clearly.

Before you leave, find a high point and look back over the valley. The river still runs below the bridges. The theatre still faces the same horizon. Fields and olive trees surround the ruins, as they surely did in Roman times. Listen to the wind in the grass and the sound of a car crossing the old arches. Cyrrhus is quiet now... but it is not empty. People still drive, farm and pray here, inside the outline of an ancient city.
