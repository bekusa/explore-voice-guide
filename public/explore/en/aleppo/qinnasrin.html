The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, south-west of Aleppo, the wind moves across open farmland and dry grass. A dog barks somewhere in a village. Under your shoes the ground is not plain soil. It is full of broken pottery, chips of limestone, and pale fragments that were once part of houses. You are standing on what is left of a city that people called Qinnasrin. For many centuries it was more important than Aleppo.

Today that is hard to believe. There is no grand gate here and no single monument to photograph. There are low mounds and scattered stones, and in places you can see rock cut into tombs. The landscape is quiet and agricultural. Yet this place once named a whole province. Merchants, soldiers and governors came here, and travellers from across the Near East knew its name. So keep that contrast in mind as you walk. It explains most of what Qinnasrin means.

The story begins in the Hellenistic age. That is the period after Alexander the Great, when Greek-speaking dynasties ruled much of the Middle East. In Syria the ruling family was the Seleucids, and they founded or refounded many cities. One of them stood here. The Greeks called it Chalcis, and it was often written as Chalcis ad Belum to separate it from other towns with the same name. Later, in Arabic, the name became Qinnasrin.

Why here? Look at the land around you. This is the meeting point of the fertile plains of northern Syria and the drier country that stretches east toward the steppe. A town here could control farmland, grazing land and the roads between them. Caravans heading between the coast, the inland cities and the Euphrates could pass close by. Location was everything in the ancient world, and this hill gave a clear view over the routes.

Under Rome and then under Byzantium, which was the eastern, Greek-speaking half of the Roman Empire, Chalcis stayed on the frontier. To the east lay the Persian empires, and the border region saw repeated wars. The Byzantine historian Procopius wrote that the emperor Justinian strengthened the defences of Chalcis in the sixth century. So the walls and towers that once ringed this place were not decoration. They were a response to real danger.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The most visible remains at Qinnasrin are often not the town itself, but its dead. On the nearby slopes the soft limestone was cut into tombs. Some are simple chambers. Others have carved openings and small courts in front of them. Families buried their relatives here for generations. When you find one of these openings, look at the tool marks on the rock. Real hands made those marks, one stroke at a time.

Now move forward to the seventh century. Armies from Arabia, newly united under Islam, pushed north into Byzantine Syria. In the 630s this region fell into their hands, and Qinnasrin was part of that campaign. Early Arabic histories connect its capture with the commander Khalid ibn al-Walid. Those accounts were written down generations later, so the details of the fighting are debated. The outcome is not in doubt. Byzantine rule here ended, and a new order began.

And this is the second surprise. Under the Umayyad caliphs, the first great Islamic dynasty, ruling from Damascus, Syria was divided into military districts called junds. One of them was named after this town. It was called Jund Qinnasrin, and it covered a large part of northern Syria. For a time, Aleppo itself sat inside a province that carried Qinnasrin's name. The small town you see today once gave its name to a region.

So what happened? Slowly, the balance shifted to Aleppo. Aleppo had its great citadel hill, its water and its growing markets. Over the tenth century the region became a battleground again, as Byzantine armies returned and local Arab rulers fought to hold northern Syria. Qinnasrin suffered in these wars. Later writers describe it as ruined and reduced. Power and population moved to the bigger city nearby, and they did not come back.

Cities like this rarely vanish in a single day. More often, people leave over many years. They take good building stone with them to new houses. Fields creep over old streets. Rain flattens walls into mounds. That is the process you can still read in this landscape. The cut blocks you may notice in village walls or field boundaries could be ancient stone, used again by later builders. Nothing here was wasted.

It is also important to be honest about the present. This part of Aleppo Governorate was affected by Syria's long war, and fighting took place in the surrounding area during the 2010s. Archaeological sites across the country were damaged, and some were looted. The exact condition of every tomb and wall here is not always documented. So treat what you see with care. Do not climb on fragile stonework, and leave every fragment where you find it.

If you are here with a local guide, ask about the villages around the site. Families have farmed this land for generations. They often know which mounds hide old walls, where the tombs are, and how the landscape has changed. Their memory is part of the record too. Some stories they tell may be local tradition rather than written history. Still, it is worth listening to them, because the people who live here know the ground better than any map.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look out across the plain toward Aleppo. Picture the roads that once met here. Picture soldiers on the walls, traders resting their animals, and governors sending letters to Damascus. Then look back at the quiet fields, the stones and the open sky. Both pictures belong to the same place. As you walk back, look down once more. That pale chip of pottery near your foot may be fifteen centuries old. Qinnasrin did not disappear. It sank into the earth, slowly and quietly, and it is still here under the grass.

Practical tips

Check current security conditions before travelling to the site.
Hire a local guide who knows the tomb locations.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, stony ground.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is scarce.
Visit in early morning for cooler air and softer light.
