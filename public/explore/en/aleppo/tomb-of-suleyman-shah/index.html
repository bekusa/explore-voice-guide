The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here in the north of Aleppo Governorate, the wind moves across open farmland with almost nothing to stop it. There is dust in the air, the smell of dry earth, and a wide sky. The land is low and rolling, and the Turkish border is very close. In this quiet corner stands one of the most unusual memorials in the Middle East. It is a small tomb with an outsized story, guarded by soldiers of another country.

This is the Tomb of Suleyman Shah. According to Ottoman tradition, Suleyman Shah was the grandfather of Osman the First. Osman founded the Ottoman dynasty, which went on to rule an empire across three continents for more than six centuries. To many people in Turkey, then, this grave honours a founding ancestor. It is a symbol of where the story of their state began.

Here is the first surprise. Historians are not sure that Suleyman Shah was really Osman's grandfather. Some early Ottoman sources give his ancestors different names. Many scholars believe the family tree was shaped later, when the dynasty wanted a heroic origin story. So the man in this tomb may be a figure of legend as much as of history. That does not make the place less meaningful. It simply means that belief and memory built this site as much as documents did.

The traditional story goes like this. In the thirteenth century, Turkic tribes were moving west, pushed by the Mongol invasions from Central Asia. Legend has it that Suleyman Shah led one of these groups. While crossing the Euphrates River, he drowned. His followers buried him on the riverbank, close to a medieval fortress called Qal'at Ja'bar, further downstream from here. The tribe moved on into Anatolia, the land that is now Turkey, and the rest became Ottoman history.

For centuries, the grave sat beside the Euphrates under Ottoman rule. Then the empire collapsed after the First World War. In 1921, Turkey and France signed the Treaty of Ankara. France then controlled Syria, and the two sides needed to agree on a new border. One article in that treaty made a remarkable promise. The tomb would remain Turkish property. Turkey could guard it and fly its flag there, even though the tomb now stood inside Syria.

This is the second surprise. Because of that treaty, the tomb became a tiny piece of Turkish sovereign ground inside another country. Think about that for a moment... a grave of only a few buildings, with its own flag and its own small guard of honour. Turkish soldiers took turns serving here, far from home. The arrangement continued after Syria became independent. It became one of the strangest legal details on any map in the region.

The tomb has not stayed in one place. In the 1970s, Syria built the Tabqa Dam on the Euphrates. The dam created a large reservoir, Lake Assad, and the water was going to cover the original burial site near Qal'at Ja'bar. So in 1973, the tomb was moved upstream, to a hill near the village of Qara Qozak in Aleppo Governorate. A new memorial was built there, still Turkish ground, still guarded, now above the waterline.

The next move came in very different conditions. During the Syrian civil war, the area around Qara Qozak became dangerous, and armed groups controlled much of the land. In February 2015, the Turkish army carried out an overnight operation. Soldiers crossed into Syria, brought out the guards, and carried the remains to a new site near the village of Ashme, very close to the Turkish border. The old buildings at Qara Qozak were destroyed so that no one else could use them.

That is why the setting around you feels the way it does. This is not a city monument with cafes and souvenir stalls. It is a guarded site in a border zone. The atmosphere is closer to a military post than a museum. Flags, walls, and watchful soldiers frame the experience. If you are standing here, it is likely because you have special permission or are part of an organised visit. Respect the guards and follow every instruction they give.

Take a moment to look at the tomb itself. Like many Turkish memorial tombs, it is simple rather than grand. The idea is honour, not decoration. Notice the Turkish flag above the site, and how much it dominates the open landscape. Notice too how the tomb faces a country on one side and a border on the other. The building is small, but its position carries a great deal of political weight.

It is worth thinking about why a single grave matters so much. For Turkey, the tomb links the modern republic to the earliest days of the Ottoman family. For Syria, it has sometimes been a sensitive reminder of foreign soldiers on its soil. For historians, it shows how nations build their origin stories, and how those stories then shape treaties, borders, and even military operations. Few graves in the world have been moved by an army.

The land itself also tells part of the story. This region sits between the Euphrates valley and the plains of northern Syria. For thousands of years, armies, traders, and nomads crossed it. Turkic tribes, Arab dynasties, Crusaders, Mongols, and Ottomans all passed through these lands. The river that, according to legend, took Suleyman Shah's life still runs not far to the east. It still waters the farms that you can see across these plains.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen again. The same wind is moving over the fields. The flag is still lifting and falling above the tomb. You are looking at a grave that has been moved several times, protected by treaties, and carried across a border in the dark. Whoever truly lies here, this small site holds a large question... how a people remember where they came from. Carry that question with you as you walk away.
