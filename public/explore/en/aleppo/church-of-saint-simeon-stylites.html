The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here on the ridge, the wind moves through empty arches and carries the smell of dry thyme and warm stone. The limestone under your feet is pale, almost honey-coloured, and worn smooth by centuries of footsteps. There is no roof above you, only sky. Around you stand walls, doorways and carved columns, open to the air on every side. This is the Church of Saint Simeon Stylites, on a hilltop northwest of Aleppo.

To understand this place, start with one man and one column. Simeon was a Christian monk of the fifth century. He wanted to live apart from the world, so he did something strange. He climbed onto a stone pillar and stayed there. He prayed, fasted and preached from the top, exposed to the summer sun and the winter rain. Tradition says he lived this way for more than thirty-five years, until his death in 459.

The word stylite comes from the Greek word for pillar. Simeon was the first famous pillar saint, and many monks later copied him. As the years passed, his column was rebuilt taller and taller. Accounts suggest it reached around fifteen metres or more. The aim was to rise further from the crowds below. It did not work. The higher he climbed, the more people came to see him.

And people really did come — from very far away. Theodoret, a bishop who knew Simeon and wrote about him while the monk was still alive, described the visitors. According to his account, they came from across the Roman world, from Persia, and even from distant Spain, Gaul and Britain. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Pilgrims in Rome reportedly placed small images of Simeon at the entrances of their workshops, as protection.

After Simeon died, the pilgrims kept coming. So the empire built something worthy of them. In the late fifth century, during the reign of the Byzantine emperor Zeno, builders raised this enormous church around the saint's pillar. In its time, it ranked among the largest churches in the Christian East. Some scholars compare its scale with the great churches of Constantinople, the imperial capital that is now Istanbul.

Now look at the plan of the building. The architects did something clever. They placed the pillar at the centre, inside an eight-sided space called an octagon. Then they attached four long halls, called basilicas, to that centre. Each hall points in a different direction, so together they form a giant cross. Whichever door a pilgrim entered, the path led straight to the column.

Here is the second surprise. Stand in the octagon and look toward the eastern hall. This hall was the main place of worship, and it is slightly larger than the others. But it is not perfectly in line with the rest of the cross. It turns a little off the main axis. Nobody knows the reason for certain. Some suggest the builders aligned it with a particular point of sunrise. Others think the shape of the rock forced a compromise.

Scholars also debate the octagon itself. Did it once have a wooden roof, or did it stay open to the sky around the pillar? The evidence is not clear. Either way, imagine the effect. A pilgrim walked down a long hall of columns, out of shadow and into light, and found the saint's pillar rising in the centre.

And the pillar? Look for it at the heart of the octagon. Today it is only a rounded lump of stone, not much taller than a person. For centuries, pilgrims chipped away small pieces to take home as holy relics. Stone by stone, faith slowly wore the column down. The pillar that once lifted Simeon toward the sky is now low enough to touch.

Take a moment with the carving around you. The builders shaped local limestone with real skill. On the capitals, the decorated tops of the columns, look for acanthus leaves. This is a spiky plant that classical sculptors loved. On some capitals, the leaves curve sideways, as if a strong wind is bending them. Look too at the moulding that runs around doors and windows, sometimes in continuous bands that step up and over the openings.

The church was never alone on this hill. Monks lived here, and a baptistery stood to the south, where new Christians received baptism. Below the ridge, a village grew into a pilgrim town, with hostels, monasteries and shops. Centuries later, in the tenth century, Byzantine forces fortified the site. That is why local people still call it Qal'at Sim'an, which means the Fortress of Simeon.

This church belongs to a wider landscape. The hills of northern Syria hold hundreds of abandoned stone villages from late antiquity, often called the Dead Cities. In 2011, UNESCO listed them together as a World Heritage Site, the Ancient Villages of Northern Syria. The years of war that followed brought damage here too. In 2016, reports described an airstrike that hit the site and damaged the remaining stump of the pillar.

So stand still for a moment before you leave. Look at the four halls opening out from the centre, toward the four directions. Feel the wind come through the empty doorways. Fifteen centuries ago, crowds pressed into this space to see one man on a column. Now the stone stands quiet, and the low pillar sits in the middle. As you walk back down the ridge, turn once and look up. Notice how the walls still catch the light, pale against the open sky.
