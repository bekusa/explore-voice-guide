The guide

The story

You step into a hall as wide as an aircraft hangar, and the floor seems to drop away below you. In long earthen trenches set down in the ground stand rows upon rows of soldiers made of clay. There are hundreds of them in view at once. They all face the same way, forward, silent, as if waiting for an order that will never come. The air is cool and very still.

This is the museum of the Terracotta Army, near the town of Lintong, to the east of Xi'an. Everything you see in front of you was buried deep underground for more than two thousand years. It was not made for the living at all. It was made to guard a single man, forever, in whatever world he believed waited beyond death.

That man was Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor to bring all of China under one rule. He died in 210 before the common era. He feared death greatly, and he prepared for it on an enormous scale, ordering a tomb complex larger than any that had come before. This whole army of clay was only one part of that complex, posted to protect him in the afterlife.

Surprisingly, the figures were not first uncovered by archaeologists. In 1974, a group of local farmers were digging a well in a dry field. Their tools struck pieces of hard, baked clay. Without knowing it, they had broken through into a buried pit holding thousands of silent soldiers. Word of the strange find spread quickly, and careful, slow excavation soon began across the whole site.

There are three main pits now open to visitors, each under its own roof. The first one is by far the largest. Beneath a vast curved ceiling, columns of warriors stretch far back into the earth, line after line after line. Soldiers, officers, and horses are arranged in true battle order, exactly as a real army would have stood, ready to march forward together into a fight.

Walk slowly along the railing and study the faces one by one. No two of them are the same. One soldier has a thin mustache. Another has full, round cheeks. Some look very young, while others seem worn and older. Each face was shaped and finished by hand, and that careful human work is the reason why, even now, the silent figures feel so strangely alive.

The soldiers did not always look this plain. They were once painted in bright colors, with red, green, blue, and pink covering their clothing and their skin. But after so many centuries underground, most of that paint flaked away within a short time of being dug up, as soon as the dry air reached it. Today the figures are the soft gray-brown color of the bare clay.

Look closely and you can read the differences in rank. The common soldiers stand in simple short tunics, ready to fight on foot. The officers wear longer robes and tall caps that mark their position above the troops. The very tallest figures are the generals. Their heavier armor and their calm, upright posture set them clearly apart from the dense rows of ordinary soldiers around them.

In the other two pits you will find different units: archers kneeling and standing, cavalry with their mounts, and a smaller command group that seems to direct the rest. There are war horses too, four to a chariot, frozen forever in mid-stride. Two beautiful bronze chariots, discovered broken into many pieces and then patiently rebuilt, are displayed in a separate indoor hall nearby.

All of these pits face toward a low, green, tree-covered hill that stands a short distance away. That quiet hill is the actual tomb of the emperor himself. To this day, it has never been opened. Ancient written records describe rivers of liquid metal and hidden traps sealed deep inside it. For now, out of caution, it remains closed, unexcavated, and largely unknown.

Stop for a moment and think about the sheer scale of the work. Thousands of figures, every single one of them different, made by many hands over many long years of labor. They were then buried in total darkness, built to be seen by no living eye at all. And yet here you are, all these centuries later, looking straight into their patient faces.

When you finally walk back out of the hall, the ordinary daylight feels sharp and almost too bright. Behind you, in the cool and shadowed trenches, the clay soldiers keep up their endless watch. They have stood at their posts, patient and unmoving, for more than twenty centuries, and they will go on standing there long after you have walked away.
