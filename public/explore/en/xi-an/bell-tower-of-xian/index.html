The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Car horns, the hum of electric scooters, a bus braking somewhere behind you. Traffic flows in a slow, endless circle. And at the centre of it all, a wooden tower sits on a heavy brick base, quite still. Its green-glazed roof tiles catch the light. Its eaves curve upward at the corners like the edges of a folded paper. You are standing at the exact heart of old Xi'an.

This is the Bell Tower. It marks the point where the city's four great streets meet. North Street, South Street, East Street and West Street each run straight out from here toward the gates in the old city wall. That layout is not an accident. Chinese city planning has long valued order, symmetry and a clear centre. Here, the centre is not a palace or a temple. It is a tower built to keep time.

The story begins in the Ming dynasty, the ruling house that governed China from the fourteenth to the seventeenth century. The first Ming emperor, known as Hongwu, ordered many cities fortified and reorganised. Xi'an received a new wall and new public buildings. The Bell Tower was first raised in 1384. But here is the first surprise... it did not originally stand on this spot. It was built a short distance to the west.

About two centuries later, in the late sixteenth century, the city had grown and shifted. Its true centre was no longer where the tower stood. So officials moved it. The whole structure was taken down and rebuilt here, at the new crossroads. Records place this move in 1582. Think about that for a moment — a large timber building, carefully dismantled and raised again, simply so the city's clock would sit in the right place.

Look closely at how the building is made. The base is a square platform of grey brick. Arched tunnels pass right through it in four directions, once used by people and carts. Above the base sits the wooden tower. From outside it seems to have three layers of roof, but inside there are two main floors. Red columns, painted beams and carved window lattices fill the upper levels. At the very top, a gilded finial points at the sky.

Why a bell? In imperial Chinese cities, sound kept the rhythm of the day. A bell rang in the morning. A drum sounded in the evening. People speak of this pairing as "morning bell, evening drum." The ringing told citizens when the city gates would open, when markets could start, and when the night curfew began. Without clocks in every home, the whole city lived by what it heard from this tower.

Turn and look toward the northwest. A short walk away stands the Drum Tower, the Bell Tower's partner. It is slightly older, and it is the larger of the two. Together they form a matched pair. One opened the day, and the other closed it. Today a broad public square stretches between them, and in the evening both towers glow under their lights. Many visitors see one and forget the other... it is worth seeing both.

Now for a story you should hold more lightly. Locals tell a legend about why the tower was built at all. According to the tale, Xi'an suffered from earthquakes, and people believed a restless dragon lived beneath the ground. A great bell, placed at the city's centre, was meant to calm it and hold it down. There is no record to prove this. But it shows how ordinary people explained a building that was really about time and order.

Another story concerns the bell itself. Accounts often connect the tower with an ancient bronze bell from the Tang dynasty, a much earlier golden age when this city was called Chang'an and served as an imperial capital. That old bell is said to be kept today in the Forest of Steles Museum, near the south wall. Inside the tower, you will find a large bell that visitors can see, and sometimes short music performances on traditional bronze bells.

Here is the second surprise, and it is under your feet. You cannot simply walk across the traffic to reach the tower. Instead, you go underground. Pedestrian tunnels run beneath the roundabout and connect the surrounding corners to the tower. The city's metro also has a station here, named after the tower. So this old timekeeper now sits on top of one of the busiest transport points in modern Xi'an.

If you climb up, walk slowly around the outer balcony. From here you can look straight down each of the four main streets. On a clear day the lines of traffic and shopfronts run toward the horizon. You can feel the plan of the city. Inside, look up at the ceiling. The painted panels use deep blues, greens and reds in repeating patterns, a style common in Chinese official architecture of this period.

The neighbourhood around the tower is part of the experience. Large shopping centres and hotels stand on the corners. A few minutes to the northwest, past the Drum Tower, the narrow lanes of the Muslim Quarter begin. There, the air smells of grilled lamb, cumin and fresh flatbread. Within the old city wall, many routes start or finish at this crossroads. It is a natural place to orient yourself.

So pause here a little longer. Watch the cars circle, the lights change, the people come up from the tunnels and stop to take a photograph. For more than four hundred years on this spot, the tower has stood at the centre while the city moved around it. As you walk away down any of the four streets, glance back once. The green roof will still be there, holding the middle of Xi'an in place.
