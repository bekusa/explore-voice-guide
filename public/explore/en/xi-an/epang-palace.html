The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is not stone, and it is not brick. It is earth: pale, dense and packed hard. On a dry day it has a faint dusty smell. On a damp day it turns darker, almost the colour of strong tea. Look at any exposed edge and you may see thin horizontal bands, one stacked on another. Those bands are the oldest thing here. They are more than two thousand years old, and human hands made them.

This is the site of the Epang Palace, on the plain west of modern Xi'an, south of the Wei River. In the late third century before the common era, this plain was the heart of the Qin state. The Qin had just done something no one had done before. They had conquered the rival kingdoms and joined them into a single empire. Their ruler took a new title, the First Emperor. In Chinese, that is Qin Shi Huang.

The First Emperor is the man behind the Terracotta Army, buried east of the city. He is also linked to early stretches of the Great Wall. He standardised weights, measures, coins and writing across his new territory. Late in his reign, he wanted a palace to match the scale of what he had built. His capital at Xianyang, across the river, already held many palaces. The ancient sources say it felt too crowded. So work began here, around 212 before the common era.

Most of what we know about the plan comes from Sima Qian, the great historian of the Han dynasty. Han means the dynasty that followed the Qin. Writing about a century later, he described a front hall so large that ten thousand people could sit in it. He wrote that banners several metres tall could stand beneath its roof. He also described a covered road running south toward the hills. We cannot check every detail. But his account shaped how people imagined this place for two thousand years.

Now, the first surprise. The palace was almost certainly never finished. The First Emperor died in 210 before the common era, only two years after work began. His dynasty collapsed soon after. Rebellions spread across the empire. Within a few years, the Qin was gone. The great hall described by the historian may have been a plan more than a finished building. What survives here is mainly its base: an enormous platform of earth, raised to carry halls that perhaps never rose.

That platform shows the main building method of early China. It is called rammed earth. Workers set up wooden frames and filled them with damp soil. Then they pounded it hard with heavy tools, layer after layer, until it became almost as firm as stone. When the frames came off, a new layer went on top. The thin bands you can see are those layers. Each one is a record of many people pounding the same spot, on the same day, long ago.

The scale is hard to take in from one place. Archaeologists have traced the front hall foundation for more than a kilometre from east to west. That is longer than many modern city blocks put together. It is one of the largest rammed earth foundations known from ancient China. Walk along its edge and you will notice how flat and deliberate it is. The landscape around it rolls gently. The platform does not. Someone planned these lines with great care.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a big one. For centuries, people told the same story about this palace's end. After the fall of the Qin, a rebel general named Xiang Yu took the capital. According to the traditional account, he set the palaces on fire, and the flames burned for three months. Many people believed that this fire destroyed the Epang Palace. Poems, plays and school lessons repeated the story. It became one of the best-known images of Chinese history.

But in the early 2000s, Chinese archaeologists excavated parts of this foundation. They found very little burnt earth or ash on the platform. If a great palace had burned here, you would expect thick layers of charcoal. The finds suggest another picture. Perhaps the fire was real, but it struck the older palaces at Xianyang, across the river. Perhaps this site was still a building project when the dynasty ended. The famous fire may belong to a different place.

The story survived partly because of a poem. In the Tang dynasty, many centuries later, the poet Du Mu wrote a famous rhapsody about the Epang Palace. A rhapsody here means a long, rich piece that mixes poetry and prose. He described endless halls, winding corridors and crowds of palace women. He was not writing a guidebook. He was warning the rulers of his own time about pride and waste. Many Chinese students still learn this text at school.

That is why the name carries so much weight in China. For many people, Epang means more than an old building site. It is a symbol of ambition without limit. It also stands for the cost that ordinary people paid for that ambition. The ancient sources say huge numbers of labourers and convicts worked on the First Emperor's projects. Standing on this earth, you are standing on their work. Their names are lost, but the layers they pounded are still here.

Today, much of the site is protected as a heritage area. There is open ground, grass, paths and markers that show the outline of the old foundation. In recent decades, a modern replica palace with painted halls stood in this area for tourists. It was later taken down. So do not expect red columns and golden roofs. What you see now is quieter and more honest. It is the real foundation, wide and plain under the open sky.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind moving across the flat ground, and the distant traffic of a modern city of millions. Look again at the packed, banded earth. A ruler once imagined the greatest hall in the world rising from this spot. Perhaps it never stood complete. But the ground he raised is still here, and so are you. As you walk away, notice how the land lifts slightly under your feet. That rise is the palace.

Key facts

Excavations found little evidence of the legendary great fire.
Look closer

What to look for

Thin horizontal bands in exposed rammed earth walls.
The perfectly flat line of the raised platform.
Markers tracing the outline of the old front hall.
Colour changes in the soil between dry and damp patches.
The slight rise in ground where the foundation begins.
Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for walking on uneven earth paths.
Bring water and sun protection on the open plain.
Visit after rain to see the earth layers more clearly.
Combine with the Terracotta Army for the full Qin story.
Read Du Mu's rhapsody in translation before you arrive.
