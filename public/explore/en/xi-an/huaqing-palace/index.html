Huaqing Palace

Steam rises from the ground here, even in the cold of winter.

⏱ 6 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

Steam rises from the ground here, even in the cold of winter. The air carries a faint mineral smell, soft and warm against your face. You are standing at the foot of a green mountain, where the slope meets the flat plain. Warm water has drawn people to this exact spot for nearly three thousand years. Close your eyes for a moment, and you can almost hear it moving somewhere beneath the stone.

This is Huaqing Palace. It sits below Mount Li, in the Lintong area, about thirty kilometers east of the center of Xi'an. The hot springs here are completely natural. Water rises from deep underground at a steady, gentle warmth, the same in summer and in the depth of winter. That reliable heat is the reason everything else here was built. Without the springs, there would be no palace at all.

Rulers came to bathe here long before the great dynasties that most people remember. But the place is tied most closely to the Tang dynasty, more than a thousand years ago. Across this slope, emperors built pools, halls, gardens, and covered walkways. They came to rest and to bathe, and above all to escape the cold of the northern winter. For a season each year, the business of the empire moved here, to the warm water at the mountain's foot.

One story shapes how nearly everyone remembers this palace. In the eighth century, the emperor Xuanzong fell deeply in love with a woman named Yang Guifei. He brought her here, and they spent their winters together beside these warm pools. Poets later wrote about their love, and about how it ended in rebellion, war, and sorrow. That sad and famous tale still hangs over the gardens, soft as the steam itself.

You can still see the old bathing pools today. They are sunken into the ground and lined with smooth stone, empty now but clearly shaped for a body to rest in. One pool was kept for the emperor alone. Another, shaped like an open flower, is said to have belonged to Yang Guifei. Look closely at the worn edges. Imagine the warm water filling them in the evening, with lamplight resting gently on the surface.

Walk further up the slope, past the pools. The garden opens into quiet courtyards and long covered walkways, their wooden beams painted in faded color. A tall white statue of Yang Guifei stands near one of the pools, pale against the dark trees. The branches shade the paths and soften the light. Even with many other visitors moving around you, the sound of running water is never far away.

This palace also holds a second memory, one very different in mood. In 1936, this peaceful hillside became the scene of a sudden and dangerous political crisis. China's leader at that time, a general named Chiang Kai-shek, was staying in one of the buildings here, set against the rock of the mountain. He had come, much like the emperors before him, to a place of quiet and rest.

Two of his own commanders had lost patience with him. They wanted him to stop fighting rival Chinese forces and instead unite the country against the invading Japanese army. To force his hand, they sent soldiers to arrest him, right here at the palace, in the early hours of the morning. He fled in the dark, climbing up the rocky slope behind the buildings, but he was found and taken.

This event is now known as the Xi'an Incident. It changed the direction of the war, and of modern Chinese history, in a single night. On the hillside you can find a small pavilion that marks the place connected to his capture. Look around as you walk, and notice how the calm of the garden and the tension of that history seem to sit quietly side by side.

So this one hillside holds two stories at the same time. One is a love story from more than a thousand years ago, full of music and poetry. The other is a hard, modern night of soldiers and fear from the last century. Both of them unfolded beside the very same warm springs that you can still see gently steaming today.

Behind the palace, Mount Li rises green and steep into the sky. A cable car climbs slowly toward the upper slopes. From higher up, you can look back down over the curved rooftops, the pools, and the wide flat plain that stretches into the distance. Long ago, beacon towers stood on these peaks. Guards lit fires on them to send fast signals of approaching war across the land.

There is often a large evening show staged against the dark mountain, retelling the old love story with music, light, and dance. Even if you do not stay to watch it, you can feel why this place has inspired such performances. The mountain behind, the warm water below, and the weight of long memory all seem to gather together in this single spot.

As you leave, reach out and touch the water if you can get close to it. It is warm under your fingers, exactly as it was for the emperors who came before you. Thousands of years of visitors have stood more or less where you are standing now, drawn to this place by the same quiet heat rising endlessly out of the earth.

Key facts

Natural hot springs used by rulers for nearly three thousand years
Tied to Tang emperor Xuanzong and consort Yang Guifei, eighth century
Located below Mount Li, about thirty kilometers east of Xi'an
Site of the 1936 Xi'an Incident involving Chiang Kai-shek
Imperial winter retreat built across the foot of the mountain

What to look for

The flower-shaped sunken pool lined with carved stone
A tall white statue standing beside the warm pools
The hillside pavilion marking the 1936 arrest
Steam rising from spring water along the stone channels

Practical tips

Visit early in the day to avoid the largest tour crowds
Wear sturdy shoes if you plan to climb Mount Li
Combine your visit with the nearby Terracotta Army in Lintong
Allow at least two hours to walk the full grounds
