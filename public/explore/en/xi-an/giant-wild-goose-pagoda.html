The guide

The story

You stand in a temple courtyard, and a tall brick tower rises in front of you. Its lines are plain and square. Each story steps inward a little from the one below, so the whole shape narrows toward the sky. Pigeons settle on the eaves. The sound of chanting drifts from a nearby hall.

This is the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda, one of the oldest landmarks of Xi'an. It stands inside a Buddhist temple in the south of the city. The tower was first built in 652, during the Tang dynasty, when Xi'an, then called Chang'an, was among the largest cities on earth.

The pagoda was built for a purpose, and that purpose has a name: Xuanzang. He was a Buddhist monk who set out from China on a long journey to India. He wanted to study the faith at its source and bring its texts home. His travels lasted about sixteen years and covered thousands of miles, through deserts where men lost their way and over mountain passes deep in snow.

When Xuanzang returned, he carried hundreds of Buddhist scriptures and statues, all written in languages China could not yet read. He needed a safe place to store them and a quiet place to translate them. The pagoda was raised to hold this treasure of texts and to guard it from fire and weather. The emperor of the day gave his support to the work.

Xuanzang spent his last years here, leading a team of scholars. Together they turned the Indian scriptures into Chinese, word by word, finishing scores of volumes. This work shaped Buddhist thought across East Asia for centuries, and scholars still read some of the translations today. The journey that made it possible later inspired one of China's best-loved novels, Journey to the West.

The tower you see is not exactly the first one. The early pagoda was smaller and, by some accounts, fell into ruin. It was rebuilt and enlarged in the early eighth century. Over the years it has been repaired many times, and it has leaned slightly to one side. It now rises about sixty-four meters, in seven clear stories of gray brick.

Look at how it is made. The walls are brick, but the builders shaped them to imitate wood. You can see flat columns and bracket shapes pressed into the surface, copying the timber halls of the time. This is a tower of brick wearing the clothes of a wooden building, and it has outlasted almost every wooden hall of its age.

You may wonder about the name. Why a goose? An old story explains it, though it is legend rather than record. Monks of one order, the tale says, were short of food. A flock of wild geese flew overhead, and one bird fell from the sky to the ground before them. They took this as a sign, buried the goose with honor, and raised a pagoda on the spot. Whether or not it happened, the name has lasted.

If you climb the inner stairs, the city opens below you. Through the arched windows on each side you can look north toward the old heart of Xi'an and south toward the hills. The Tang poets came here too. They climbed these floors and wrote verses about the wide view and about passing the imperial exams, which they marked by writing their names below.

Around the base, look for two carved stones set into the wall. They hold texts written by emperors and copied by a famous calligrapher of the Tang court. For people who study Chinese writing, these carvings are valued models, kept and protected for more than a thousand years.

The temple around the pagoda is still active. Monks live and pray here. In the halls you may see worshippers lighting incense and bowing before gilded figures. The smell of sandalwood hangs in the air. The pagoda is not only a monument; it remains part of a living place of faith.

In the wider squares around the old tower, the modern city has grown up. To the south stands a large statue of Xuanzang, staff in hand, facing the road as if still traveling. To the north lie gardens, fountains, and walking paths, where families gather in the evening and water shows play after dark. The ancient pagoda stands at the center of it all.

Look up once more at the steady brick form. It was built to keep safe the words a single traveler carried home across deserts and mountains. The traveler is long gone. The tower still stands, holding his memory, while the city moves and changes around its quiet base.
