The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the Place du Peyrou and look at the stone. It is a warm, honey-coloured limestone, and in morning light it glows almost gold. Run your hand along a wall and it feels slightly rough, like fine sandpaper. Above you, the bell tower rises in stages, and swifts often cut across the sky around it. This is the Cathedral of Saint Sacerdos, the spiritual heart of Sarlat-la-Canéda for many centuries.

The building in front of you did not begin as a cathedral. It began as the church of a Benedictine abbey. Benedictines are monks who follow the sixth-century Rule of Saint Benedict, a life built on prayer, work and study. The abbey of Sarlat grew powerful during the Middle Ages, and the town grew up around it. Streets, markets and houses all gathered close to the monks. In a real sense, the town was born from this abbey.

Then, in 1317, the status of the church changed. Pope John the Twenty-Second created a new diocese here, and the abbey church became a cathedral. Sarlat now had its own bishop. That was a big step for a small town in the Périgord, the green, wooded region of south-west France. Bishops brought prestige, money and builders. They also brought ambitions for a grander church than the old abbey one.

Look first at the bell tower on the west side. Its lower part is the oldest visible section of the cathedral, a survivor from the Romanesque church. Romanesque is the style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with thick walls and rounded arches. Higher up, the tower changes character. The top is crowned by a later lantern with a rounded, bulb-like roof. So in one tower you can read several building campaigns, stacked one on top of the other.

The main body of the church tells a different story. In the early sixteenth century, a bishop from the Gontaut-Biron family began a major rebuilding. The work did not run smoothly. It stopped, started again, and stretched across generations. The cathedral was only finished in the seventeenth century. That long timeline explains a lot of what you see. Gothic ideas, with pointed arches and tall vaults, mix with the calmer, more classical taste of later builders.

Step inside, and the air changes. It is cooler, and sounds soften. Your footsteps echo on the stone floor. The nave, the long central space where the congregation sits, is tall and fairly plain. There is less decoration than you might expect from a cathedral. Many visitors find that this simplicity suits the place. The light comes in through the windows in soft bands, and the pale stone holds it gently.

Now turn and look back toward the entrance, and lift your eyes. High above the door sits the organ. Its large wooden case dates from the eighteenth century and is the work of the organ builder Jean-François Lépine. Many visitors walk straight past it, because it is behind them as they enter. If you are lucky, you may arrive during a rehearsal or a concert. Then the whole building fills with sound, and the stone seems to vibrate with it.

Walk along the side chapels slowly. Each one has its own altar and its own small world of paintings, carvings and furnishings, added over the centuries by different families and religious groups. Some details are faded, some have been restored. Take your time here. The pleasure of this cathedral is not in one great masterpiece, but in the accumulation of small, careful things left by many hands across many generations.

The cathedral did not keep its bishops forever. After the French Revolution, the religious map of France was redrawn, and the diocese of Sarlat was abolished. Its territory was joined to the diocese of Périgueux. So today the building carries the title of cathedral as a memory, a mark of its past rank. It is still a working parish church, though. Please keep your voice low if a service or a private prayer is taking place.

Now step back outside and walk around to the back of the cathedral. Here is a surprise many visitors miss. On a small rise stands a strange, cone-shaped stone tower, like a giant bullet or a sugarloaf. Locals call it the Lantern of the Dead. It dates from the twelfth century, and nobody knows its original purpose for certain. One popular tale links it to a visit by Saint Bernard, a famous medieval preacher, who is said to have blessed bread that healed the sick. That story is legend, not documented history.

Near the lantern, along the slope, you will find the old burial niches cut into the walls. In French these are called enfeus, arched recesses where important people were once laid to rest. This quiet area behind the cathedral feels very different from the square at the front. It is greener and calmer. The sound of the town drops away, and you can hear birds and the wind in the trees.

Back at the front, look directly across the square from the cathedral. The tall Renaissance house with its decorated windows is the house of Étienne de La Boétie. He was born in Sarlat in 1530 and became a writer and thinker. Today he is best remembered as the close friend of the philosopher Michel de Montaigne. So the young La Boétie grew up with this cathedral as the view from his door.

The whole old town around you was carefully protected and restored from the 1960s onward, under a new French law for historic districts. That is why the streets look so complete, with stone walls and heavy roofs of stone slabs called lauzes. Film crews come here often for exactly this reason. Before you leave, find a spot on the square and simply watch. Notice how the light moves across the bell tower, and how the golden stone shifts colour as the hours pass.
