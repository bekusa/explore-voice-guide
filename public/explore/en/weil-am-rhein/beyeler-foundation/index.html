The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches softly under your feet, and somewhere nearby the trams of Basel hum along their tracks. Then the trees close in, the noise drops away, and a long, low building appears. Its walls are a warm, rusty red stone. Its glass roof seems to float above them. It is a quiet place to arrive, and that quiet is deliberate.

Welcome to the Beyeler Foundation, known in French as the Fondation Beyeler. First, a small surprise on the map. You may have come here thinking of Weil am Rhein, the German town just across the border. The museum itself stands in Riehen, on the Swiss side. Riehen is a green village on the edge of Basel, and the German border runs only a short walk away. This corner of Europe is where three countries meet, and you can feel it here.

The story of this place begins with two people, Ernst and Hildy Beyeler. They were art dealers in Basel and ran a gallery in the old town for decades. Over a long working life, they bought and sold some of the most important paintings of the twentieth century. But they also kept certain works for themselves. Slowly, piece by piece, a private collection grew. In time it became one of the finest collections of modern art in private hands.

The Beyelers did not want those works to vanish into storage or be split up after their deaths. So they created a foundation, and they commissioned a museum. It opened to the public in 1997. To design it, they chose the Italian architect Renzo Piano. He had already helped to build the Pompidou Centre in Paris, a building of exposed pipes and bright colours. Here he did almost the opposite. He made something calm, simple, and close to the ground.

Look at the walls now. That red stone is porphyry, a hard volcanic rock, and it was brought here from Patagonia in South America. Its colour changes with the weather. In grey light it looks almost brown. In low evening sun it turns a deep rose. Piano wanted the building to sit inside the landscape, not on top of it. Notice how the long walls run parallel, like lines drawn across the park.

Now think about the roof, because it is the real heart of the design. The galleries are lit mainly by daylight. Above the rooms, layers of glass and adjustable panels filter the sun before it reaches the paintings. The light inside is soft and even. It moves slowly through the day, and on a cloudy afternoon the rooms feel cooler and more muted. You are seeing the art much as an artist might have seen it in a studio.

The museum sits in Berower Park, an old estate garden with tall trees and wide lawns. At its edge stands the Villa Berower, a historic country house that today holds a restaurant. Behind the museum, the land opens towards fields and the low hills beyond. On a clear day, the view across the meadows reaches towards the German side. Inside, large windows frame this landscape, so that nature becomes part of the exhibition.

And here is one of the quiet surprises most visitors remember. Inside, a room holds a large painting of water lilies by Claude Monet, the French Impressionist. Monet painted his lily pond again and again in his garden at Giverny. Outside the glass wall of that room, the museum has its own pond with real water lilies. You look from the painted water to the living water, and back again. The two seem to continue each other.

The permanent collection follows the great story of modern art. You may meet Cézanne and Van Gogh, the painters who opened the door to the twentieth century. You may find Picasso in several of his phases, from early experiments to bold later work. There are tall, thin bronze figures by Alberto Giacometti, the Swiss sculptor. There are deep fields of colour by Mark Rothko. The works on show rotate, so each visit can be a little different.

Here is a second surprise. Alongside these European and American masters, the Beyelers also collected art from Africa and Oceania. In some rooms, a carved figure from the Pacific may stand near a modern painting. This choice was personal and deliberate. Many early modern artists, including Picasso, studied such objects closely. Placing them side by side lets you see a conversation between very different traditions, and it invites you to compare shapes, surfaces, and gestures yourself.

The foundation is also known for its temporary exhibitions. These are often large shows devoted to a single artist or a single theme. They bring in loans from museums and private collections around the world. Because of them, the museum can feel very busy on weekends and during popular shows. On a weekday morning, though, the rooms are calmer. You can stand in front of a painting for a long time, with only the soft sound of footsteps around you.

Take a moment to notice how the visit is paced. The galleries are not a maze. They run in long, connected rooms, with benches placed where you might want to rest. The floors are pale wood, and they absorb sound, so voices stay low. Again and again, a window interrupts the walls and gives your eyes a break. Piano designed for slowness. The building asks you to look at fewer things, but to look at them properly.

When you step back outside, pause on the path for a moment. Turn and look at the red stone walls once more, and at the thin roof above them. Listen for the tram, the wind in the old trees, perhaps a bird near the lily pond. A private passion became a public gift here, built from stone, glass, and light. As you walk back towards the village, carry a little of that slowness with you.

Practical tips

Walk the park and lawns after your gallery visit.
