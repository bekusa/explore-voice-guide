The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under every other sound here, there is water. It runs over granite, under the spruce roots, and down the valley past the village of Pastra. The air is cool, even on a warm afternoon. It smells of resin and wet stone. Somewhere above you, beyond the forest line, the mountain rises into bare rock and alpine meadow. You are standing at the edge of the Rila range, and the water you hear is where its story begins.

The name itself may be about water. Many scholars believe the word Rila comes from an old Thracian root meaning something like a place of much water. The Thracians were an ancient people who lived across this part of the Balkans long before the Slavs arrived. It is an explanation, not a certainty, but it fits. These mountains feed some of the most important rivers in Bulgaria, including the Iskar, the Maritsa, and the Mesta. Rain and snowmelt that fall here travel a long way from home.

Rila National Park was created in 1992. It is the largest national park in Bulgaria, covering roughly eighty thousand hectares of high mountain country. That is a vast area of forest, ridges, lakes, and rock. The park protects the high central part of the range, the zone above the villages and roads. Pastra sits below it, in the valley of the Rila River. From here, the land climbs steadily toward the protected heights, one forest belt after another.

Those forest belts are worth noticing as you walk or drive upward. Lower down, you find beech, with its smooth grey bark and broad leaves. Higher up, the beech gives way to spruce and fir, darker and denser. Higher still, you may find Macedonian pine, a five-needled tree that grows naturally in only a small part of the Balkans. Above the trees comes dwarf pine, low and tangled, and then open grass and stone. In a single climb, you pass through several different climates.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Rila holds the highest summit in Bulgaria and in the whole Balkan Peninsula. That summit is Musala, at 2,925 metres. It lies on the eastern side of the range, far from this valley, so you will not see it from Pastra. But the same granite massif runs beneath your feet. In winter, snow can stay on the high ridges for many months. In spring, that snow becomes the loud, fast water in the river beside you.

The shape of these mountains comes from ice. During the last ice ages, glaciers covered the high parts of Rila. They carved deep bowls into the rock, which geologists call cirques. When the ice melted, many of these bowls filled with water. Today the range holds well over a hundred glacial lakes. The most visited group is the Seven Rila Lakes, a chain of lakes stepped one above another on the northwestern side of the mountain. Each lake sits at a different height.

The park is also a refuge for animals that have disappeared from much of Europe. Brown bears live in these forests, though they avoid people and are rarely seen. Wolves move through the remote valleys. On the high rocks, you may spot the Balkan chamois, a mountain goat with short hooked horns. Golden eagles hunt over the ridges. Most of the time, you will see none of them. But the forest around you is their territory, and that is worth remembering.

Inside the national park there are several strict nature reserves, where human activity is kept to a minimum. One of them, Parangalitsa, protects some of the oldest spruce forest in Europe. Some of its trees are centuries old and very tall. In 1977, long before the national park existed, Parangalitsa was recognised by UNESCO as a biosphere reserve. That means scientists saw its value decades ago. These reserves are not places for casual exploring. They are left largely alone, on purpose.

Now think about the valley you are in. The road through Pastra follows the Rila River toward one of the most important places in Bulgarian history, Rila Monastery. The monastery was founded by Saint Ivan of Rila, a hermit who withdrew into these mountains in the tenth century. It stands in its own protected area, a nature park next to the national park. For centuries, pilgrims walked this same valley. They travelled beside the same water you hear now.

Local tradition holds many stories about Saint Ivan. According to popular tales, he lived for a time in a cave in the forest above the monastery, eating plants and praying in silence. Pilgrims still climb to the cave today. Whatever the exact details, the idea is clear. For a long time, people came to these mountains looking for quiet. The forest, the cold air, and the distance from towns made Rila a place for retreat.

That quiet is still here, if you give it a chance. Step away from the road for a few minutes. Stand under the trees and let your eyes adjust to the shade. You will notice moss on the rocks, ferns along the banks, and the light breaking into small patches on the ground. The river is never silent. Its sound changes as you move closer or farther away. Few places make you so aware of moving water.

If you go higher into the park, treat the mountain with respect. Weather in Rila can change quickly. A clear morning can turn into fog, wind, or cold rain by the afternoon, even in summer. Marked trails and mountain huts help hikers move through the high country. Stay on the paths, carry layers, and take your rubbish with you. The park works because people leave it as they found it.

Before you go, look up the valley one more time. The slopes rise in shades of green, dark where the spruce grows thick, lighter where beech catches the sun. Somewhere above them, beyond what you can see, lakes lie in the old ice bowls and snow waits on the ridges. All of that connects to the water at your feet. As you walk away, keep listening. The river will follow you for a long while.

Start high-altitude hikes early to avoid afternoon weather.
