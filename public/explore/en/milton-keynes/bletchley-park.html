The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches underfoot. Geese call from the lake. A breeze moves through old trees and over low wooden huts with tarred roofs. It feels like a quiet country estate on the edge of a railway town. That was exactly the idea. During the Second World War, this calm English garden was one of the most secret places in Britain.

You are standing at Bletchley Park, on the southern side of Milton Keynes. In wartime, Britain's codebreakers worked here. Their task was to read the enemy's secret messages. The work was slow, exhausting and invisible. Most of the people here never spoke about it for decades. Many took the secret to their graves, and their families never knew.

Start with the mansion, the large red-brick house near the lake. Look closely at its face. It does not follow one style. There are mock-Tudor touches, Gothic hints, a copper dome, and bays added almost at random. Before the war, a wealthy financier named Herbert Leon owned the estate, and he kept adding to the house. Some architects have been unkind about the result. But the jumble gives the building real character, and visitors often stop to point at the odd details.

In 1938, as war with Germany looked more likely, the estate was bought for the Government Code and Cypher School. This was Britain's codebreaking organisation. The story often told is that the head of the Secret Intelligence Service, Admiral Hugh Sinclair, paid for it partly from his own pocket. Why here? The site sat beside the railway line between Oxford and Cambridge, with good links to London. It was ideal for bringing in clever people quietly.

The first arrivals had a cover story. They came as a group called "Captain Ridley's Shooting Party", as if they were friends on a country weekend. Soon the house filled up. Then the lawns filled with wooden huts, built quickly to hold more and more staff. Each hut had a number, and each number became a name for a team. Hut 6 worked on German army and air force messages. Hut 8 worked on the German navy.

The great problem was the Enigma machine. It looked like a typewriter in a wooden box. Each key press sent electricity through rotating wheels, so the letter came out scrambled. The settings changed every day. The number of possible settings was enormous. To an enemy operator, the system seemed impossible to break. And here is the first thing many visitors miss — the breakthrough did not begin in Britain.

Polish mathematicians had already broken early versions of Enigma in the 1930s. Their names were Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski. In 1939, just weeks before the war began, Poland shared its methods with Britain and France. That gift gave Bletchley a head start. Today a memorial on the site honours their work. It is worth seeking out, because their part in the story was overlooked for many years.

At Bletchley, the mathematician Alan Turing helped design an electro-mechanical machine called the Bombe, building on the Polish ideas. Gordon Welchman added a key improvement. The Bombe tested possible Enigma settings at high speed. Its drums turned with a loud, steady clatter. A working rebuild now runs on the site. If you hear it, you will understand why operators often finished their shifts with ringing ears.

Now the second surprise. Most people who worked here were women. By the end of the war, around three quarters of the staff were women, and the total staff numbered in the thousands. They ran the Bombes, sorted intercepted messages, translated, indexed and analysed. Some had been chosen for their skill with languages or puzzles. One well-known recruitment story involves a difficult newspaper crossword, set as a test for possible codebreakers.

There was another enemy cipher, even harder than Enigma. German high command used a machine known to the British as Tunny, built by the Lorenz company. To attack it, the engineer Tommy Flowers led the building of Colossus. Many historians describe it as one of the world's first programmable electronic computers. A reconstruction stands at the National Museum of Computing, which shares this estate. It is a separate museum with its own entrance.

The intelligence from all this work was called Ultra. It helped the Allies in the Battle of the Atlantic and in planning the landings in Normandy. Historians still debate how much it shortened the war. Winston Churchill is often said to have called the staff the geese that laid the golden eggs and never cackled. Whatever his exact words, the secrecy held. The public only began to learn about Bletchley in the 1970s.

After the war, the estate slowly fell into decline. Some buildings were used for training, and others stood empty. At one point there was a real risk the site would be cleared for housing. Local people and former staff campaigned to save it, and a trust was formed in the 1990s. Since then, careful restoration has brought the huts and the mansion back. Inside, you can see desks, telephones and coats laid out as if the shift had only just ended.

Before you go, pause by the lake. Look back at the long low huts and the mismatched mansion. Thousands of young people walked these paths in the dark, between night shifts, saying nothing to anyone. Listen again — the geese, the gravel, a train passing in the distance. The same sounds they heard. As you walk out, notice how ordinary it all looks. That ordinary look was the most important secret of all.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least half a day to see the huts properly.
Visit the National Museum of Computing separately on site.
Arrive by train; the station sits beside the park.
Catch the working Bombe rebuild during a scheduled demonstration.
Wear comfortable shoes for gravel paths between buildings.
