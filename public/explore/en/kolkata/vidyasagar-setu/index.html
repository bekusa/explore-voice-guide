The guide

The story

Stand near the river's edge and listen. Traffic moves high above you with a low, steady hum. Below, the brown water of the Hooghly slides past, carrying silt and the occasional drifting clump of water hyacinth. Then look up. A white fan of steel cables spreads out from a tall tower and runs down toward the road deck. It looks almost light, like threads pulled tight across the sky.

This is Vidyasagar Setu, the bridge that joins Kolkata to its twin city, Howrah, on the western bank. Many locals still call it the Second Hooghly Bridge. That name makes sense. For decades, the city relied on a single great crossing upstream, the older Howrah Bridge. As the population grew, one bridge was not enough. Traffic jams on that older crossing became part of daily life, and planners began to look downstream for a second route.

The Hooghly itself matters here. It is a channel of the Ganges, the river that many Hindus treat as sacred. It flows south through West Bengal toward the Bay of Bengal. For centuries, it carried trade, ships, and people into the heart of this region. Colonial Calcutta grew along its eastern bank because the river gave access to the sea. Any bridge here had to leave room for river traffic below, and that shaped the design you see.

Now, the name. The bridge honours Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, a nineteenth century Bengali scholar, writer, and social reformer. He is remembered for his work on education, including schooling for girls. He also campaigned for the right of Hindu widows to remarry, a deeply controversial idea in his time. Many Bengali children first learn to read the alphabet through a primer he wrote. So when you say the bridge's name, you are naming a teacher.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. This bridge took a very long time to build. Work was planned and started in the 1970s, but the crossing only opened to traffic in 1992. Funding problems, technical challenges, and the sheer difficulty of building over a wide, silty, tidal river all slowed progress. For a whole generation of Kolkata residents, the unfinished bridge was a familiar sight on the skyline.

Look again at the cables. This is a cable-stayed bridge. That is different from a suspension bridge. On a suspension bridge, the road hangs from two big main cables that run between towers. Here, each cable runs directly from a tower to the deck. The cables spread out in a fan pattern. Together they hold up the long central span, so the road can cross the main channel without supports standing in the water below.

That central span is long. It runs for roughly 457 metres between the two towers. When the bridge opened, it was among the longest cable-stayed spans in India, and it remains one of the most notable in the country. The open water under that span keeps the river channel clear. Boats, ferries, and barges can pass beneath without weaving between columns.

Now compare it with its older neighbour. If the air is clear, you may be able to picture the Howrah Bridge further upstream. That bridge is a heavy steel cantilever structure, dense with dark crossing beams. It opened in the 1940s, during the Second World War. Vidyasagar Setu comes from a different age of engineering. Where the older bridge looks muscular and dense, this one looks open and airy. The two crossings tell the story of how bridge design changed over half a century.

Here is a second surprise. Although this is a city bridge, it is not really built for walkers. It carries road traffic and is a toll crossing. For most visitors, the best experience of the bridge comes from below or from a distance, not from the deck itself. That is why the riverbank on the Kolkata side has become the favourite place to see it, especially in the evening.

That riverbank spot is Prinsep Ghat. A ghat is a set of steps leading down to a river, used for bathing, rituals, and boats. At Prinsep Ghat you will find a small memorial with white columns, built in the British colonial era. It honours James Prinsep, a nineteenth century scholar who helped decode an ancient Indian script. The memorial and the bridge sit very close together. Old colonial columns in front, modern steel cables behind... it is one of the city's most photographed pairings.

As the sun drops, the light changes the whole scene. The river turns from brown to bronze. The cables catch the low sun first, then fade into shadow. After dark, lights along the bridge switch on, and their reflections stretch across the moving water. Families walk along the bank. Couples sit on the steps. Vendors sell tea in small cups and snacks like jhal muri, a spiced puffed rice mix served in paper cones.

If you want to feel the bridge's scale, take a short boat ride from one of the ghats. From the water, the deck rises far above you. The towers look much taller than they do from land. You can see how the cables meet the deck at regular intervals, each one doing its share of the work. You will also see daily river life up close: ferries, fishing boats, and the flat green banks of Howrah on the other side.

So take a moment now, before you move on. Look at the line of cables against the sky. Listen to the steady sound of traffic crossing above the river, carrying people between two cities that the water once kept apart. As you walk away along the bank, glance back once more. The bridge will look smaller with each step, but those fanned white cables will stay visible for a long time.

What to look for

Fan-shaped white cables running from each tower to the deck.
White colonial columns of the Prinsep memorial below.
Clear open channel with no piers under the main span.
Ferries and fishing boats passing beneath the deck.
Floating water hyacinth drifting on the brown river.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Prinsep Ghat at sunset for the best riverside view.
Take a short boat ride for a view from the water.
Plan to view it from the bank, not on foot across it.
Carry small change for tea and snacks along the ghat.
Stay until dark to see the bridge lights reflected.
