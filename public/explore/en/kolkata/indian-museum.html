The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Out on the road, Kolkata is all horns, engine noise and hurried footsteps. But once you pass through the entrance, the noise softens. Your shoes meet cool stone. Ahead of you, a green courtyard opens up, framed on every side by pale colonnades. The light falls in long, even bars between the columns. This is the Indian Museum, and it has been collecting the story of the subcontinent for more than two centuries.

Locals rarely call it by its official name. In Bengali, many people call it the Jadughar, which means the house of magic. The name comes from an older time, when a visit here was the first chance many people had to see an Egyptian mummy, a meteorite or a dinosaur bone. Keep that word in mind as you walk. The museum is old and sometimes a little dusty, but the sense of wonder in that name still fits the place.

The story begins in 1814. That year, the Asiatic Society of Bengal, a learned society founded in Calcutta in the late eighteenth century, decided to create a museum. A Danish botanist named Nathaniel Wallich offered his own collections and became its first superintendent. This makes the Indian Museum the oldest museum in India, and one of the oldest museums of its kind anywhere in the world. Its first rooms were modest, inside the Society's own building nearby.

The collection grew fast, and it soon needed a proper home. The building around you was designed by the architect Walter Granville and completed in the 1870s. It faces the road the British knew as Chowringhee, now called Jawaharlal Nehru Road. Notice the style. It borrows from Italian Renaissance palaces, with arched arcades, a strong symmetry and a calm, classical rhythm. In a hot, humid city, the deep verandas also had a practical role. They keep the galleries shaded and the air moving.

Walk slowly around the courtyard before you choose a gallery. The layout is simple, and it helps you stay oriented. The galleries run in long wings around this central green space. Many visitors rush straight indoors, but the courtyard is where you can feel the building's age. Look at the weathering on the stone, the old ironwork, and the way the plaster has been patched over many decades. These marks show a building that has been in constant use for generations.

Now to one of the great treasures. Somewhere in the archaeology galleries, you will find the remains of the Bharhut stupa. A stupa is a Buddhist monument, usually a dome built over sacred relics, surrounded by a carved stone railing. The Bharhut stupa stood in central India more than two thousand years ago. In the nineteenth century, the archaeologist Alexander Cunningham identified its ruins, and large sections of its railing and a tall gateway were later brought here.

Stand close to the Bharhut carvings, and look carefully. The reddish sandstone is covered with small scenes. Many of them illustrate the Jataka tales, stories of the Buddha's earlier lives, often told through animals. You will see elephants, monkeys, deer and trees. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some panels carry short inscriptions in Brahmi, one of India's oldest scripts. Some name the story being shown. Others record the people who paid for that piece of stone.

That second point is worth a pause. It means ordinary donors, two thousand years ago, wanted to be remembered for their gift. Their names are still here, cut into the rock. On the pillars, you will also see tall carved figures of yakshas and yakshis. These are nature spirits from popular Indian belief, older than the Buddhist tradition itself. Their presence shows how early Buddhist art absorbed local gods and gave them a place at the gate.

From here, the collection opens in many directions. One gallery holds sculpture from Gandhara, an ancient region in what is now Pakistan and Afghanistan. Gandhara art mixed Indian Buddhist themes with styles that came from the Greek and Roman world. Look at the robes on these Buddha figures. The folds are deep and heavy, almost like a Roman statue. It is a quiet reminder that ideas and artists travelled along trade routes long before modern borders existed.

The museum is not only about art and religion. It was built as a museum of everything, and it still feels that way. There are galleries for geology, with rocks, minerals and meteorites that fell from the sky. There are rooms of fossils and animal skeletons. There are coins, textiles, decorative arts and paintings, including miniature paintings from the courts of later centuries. The mix can feel surprising, but that mix is the old idea of a universal museum.

And then there is the mummy. For many Kolkata families, the Egyptian mummy is the moment they remember from their first childhood visit. It is a small, still figure in a quiet room, far from the Nile. Take a moment here. Think about the long journey of this object, across seas and centuries, to a city on the Hooghly River. It is perhaps the clearest example of why people still call this building the house of magic.

When you leave, take a look at the neighbourhood around you. The museum sits on one of the city's historic avenues. Close by is Sudder Street, long popular with travellers, and the old covered market known as New Market. The open green space of the Maidan stretches not far to the west. Street vendors sell tea in small clay cups, and the smell of fried snacks drifts along the pavement. The museum and the street belong to the same story.

Before you step back into the traffic, return to the courtyard for one last look. Watch how the light moves across the columns, and listen to the voices echoing under the arcades. Families, students and travellers are walking the same corridors that visitors walked in the nineteenth century. The objects here come from thousands of years and many lands. But for a few minutes, they share one quiet space with you, in the middle of Kolkata.

Long shaded arcades framing the green inner courtyard.
The Egyptian mummy in its quiet gallery.
Before you go

Practical tips

Start with a slow walk around the central courtyard.
Head to the Bharhut gallery first while you feel fresh.
Wear light clothes; some galleries can feel warm.
Pair your visit with a walk to nearby New Market.
Allow at least two to three hours for the collection.
