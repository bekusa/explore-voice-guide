The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Buses grind their gears, conductors call out destinations, and hand-pulled carts rattle past on the tarmac. Then, five times a day, the call to prayer rises above all of it. You are at Dharmatala, in the heart of central Kolkata, and the white building in front of you is the Tipu Sultan Mosque. It does not sit in a quiet garden. It stands right on the street, shoulder to shoulder with shops and traffic, as it has for nearly two centuries.

Look up first. Above the street-level noise, the mosque is crowned by a cluster of rounded domes and slender corner minarets. The style follows the Indo-Islamic tradition, the building language that grew under the Mughal emperors, who ruled much of northern India for centuries. Pointed arches, onion-shaped domes and a broad prayer hall are its main features. In bright afternoon sun, the pale walls almost glare against the haze of the city. In the evening, lights pick out the outlines of the domes.

The name on this building belongs to one of the most famous rulers in South Indian history. Tipu Sultan ruled the Kingdom of Mysore, far to the south, in the late eighteenth century. He fought several wars against the British East India Company. People called him the Tiger of Mysore, and he used the tiger as his personal emblem on weapons, banners and even uniforms. In 1799, he died defending his capital, Seringapatam, when British forces stormed the fortress.

So why is a mosque in his name here, more than a thousand kilometres away in Bengal? The answer is a story of exile. After Tipu's death, the British moved his large family away from Mysore. They were held first at Vellore, in the south. Later, after unrest there in the early nineteenth century, the family was relocated to Calcutta, as Kolkata was then called. Calcutta was the capital of British India, and the Company wanted the princes close and under watch.

The family lived here on pensions from the Company. They had titles and some wealth, but no kingdom. One of Tipu's youngest sons, Prince Ghulam Muhammad, became a well-known figure in the city. He was known for his charity and his public works. In 1832, he built this mosque and dedicated it to the memory of his father. Think about that for a moment... a son, living in the capital of the power that defeated his father, raising a house of prayer in that father's name.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. This is not the only mosque Prince Ghulam Muhammad built in honour of Tipu Sultan. A second one stands in Tollygunge, in the south of the city, an area where many members of the exiled family settled. Descendants of Tipu Sultan still live in Kolkata today. Some of them have taken part in efforts to care for the family's religious and charitable property.

Now bring your attention back to the street. The mosque sits at one of the busiest crossroads in Kolkata. Esplanade, with its tram depot and metro station, is only a short walk away. The Maidan, the city's great open park, spreads out to the south. For generations, Dharmatala has been a place of meetings, rallies, shopping and travel. The mosque has watched trams arrive, colonial carriages disappear, and motor traffic take over.

That busy location has shaped the building's own story. For many years, shops and stalls pressed tightly against its walls, and the ground floor was partly hidden behind trade. At times, the building looked crowded and worn. Restoration work and cleaning campaigns have improved its appearance at different points. Still, the mix of commerce and worship is part of its character. You may notice small shops right at its base, selling everything from caps to perfume.

Inside, the atmosphere changes quickly. The prayer hall is a calm, open space. Worshippers leave their shoes at the entrance and wash before prayer, following Islamic practice. There are no human images inside, because traditional Islamic religious art avoids them. Instead, decoration comes from arches, geometry and the rhythm of columns. On a Friday, the main day of congregational prayer, the hall and nearby areas fill with men from offices and markets across the neighbourhood.

The second surprise is about the city around you. Kolkata is often imagined as a Hindu and colonial city of temples and British offices. But central Kolkata also has a long Muslim history. Merchants, craftsmen, scholars and exiled royal families all settled here. The family of Tipu Sultan was one of several royal households that ended up in the city under British rule. This mosque is one of the clearest visible traces of that layered past.

If you visit, remember that this is an active place of worship, not a museum. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Ask a caretaker or attendant before you enter, because access for visitors can depend on prayer times and local practice. Rules for women and non-Muslim visitors may vary, so it is best to ask politely at the door. Photography inside should also wait for permission.

From across the road, you get the best overall view. Find a safe place on the pavement opposite, wait for a gap in the traffic, and look at the full facade. Notice how the domes and minarets rise above the line of shop signs and electric wires. In one frame, you can see the city's daily trade at street level and a nineteenth-century memorial above it.

Before you walk on, take one more look at the white domes against the Kolkata sky. A defeated king never saw this place. His son built it far from home, in the capital of his rivals. Today, office workers, traders and travellers pass it every few minutes, many without a second glance. As you move back into the noise of Dharmatala, listen again... and you may hear the next call to prayer rise above the horns.
