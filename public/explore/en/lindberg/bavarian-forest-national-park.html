The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the spruce, the forest floor is soft and springy. It is a thick carpet of needles, moss, and slowly rotting wood. Somewhere ahead, water runs over granite. The air smells of resin, damp earth, and mushrooms. Light comes down in narrow shafts and lands on ferns. This is the Bavarian Forest National Park, and around Lindberg you are standing near one of its quietest edges.

This park holds a special place in German conservation. It was the first national park in Germany, founded in 1970. At that time, the idea was new and controversial. Many people here earned their living from timber, and the forest was a working forest. Setting it aside for nature, and not for logging, was a bold step. Over the following decades that step shaped how the whole country thinks about wild land.

The park later grew. In the late 1990s it expanded into the area around the Falkenstein and Rachel mountains, which includes the country near Lindberg. That is why this side of the park can feel a little different. Some slopes here were managed forest within living memory. Others are older and wilder. As you walk, you can sometimes see the change. Straight rows of planted trees slowly give way to a looser, more varied woodland.

One short phrase sums up the park's philosophy. In German it is "Natur Natur sein lassen," which means "let nature be nature." The rangers do not clear fallen trees from most of the core zones. They do not plant neat replacements after storms. Instead, the forest is allowed to die, decay, and grow back on its own schedule. For a visitor this can be surprising. A national park that looks untidy is often a sign that the idea is working.

Here is something many visitors misunderstand. In the 1990s and early 2000s, large areas of spruce were killed by the bark beetle. This is a tiny insect that lays its eggs under the bark of weakened trees. Whole hillsides turned grey. People were angry, and some called it a disaster. The park chose not to fight the beetle in its core zones. Today, young trees crowd between the grey trunks of the dead ones.

Those dead trunks are not empty. Standing dead wood and fallen logs are among the most important habitats in any forest. Beetles, fungi, mosses, and lichens live inside them. Woodpeckers hunt there and carve nesting holes. Small mammals shelter under the logs. When a giant tree finally falls and softens, seedlings often sprout directly on top of it. Foresters sometimes call these "nurse logs." Look closely and you may find a line of young spruce growing along one old trunk.

The ground under your feet is ancient. The Bavarian Forest sits on very old crystalline rock, mostly granite and gneiss. These rocks are far older than the Alps to the south. Over a vast span of time, the mountains here were worn down into rounded, gentle ridges. On some summits the rock breaks through as boulder fields. In the high valleys, the last ice age left small lakes behind, such as the dark Rachelsee below the Rachel mountain.

This forest does not stop at a fence. The park runs along the border with the Czech Republic. On the other side lies Šumava National Park, which protects the same mountain range under a different name. Together the two parks form one of the largest connected areas of protected forest in central Europe. For most of the twentieth century, this border was part of the Iron Curtain. Today, deer and lynx cross it freely, and so can hikers on marked trails.

The lynx is the animal many people hope to see, and almost nobody does. This wild cat has tufted ears and large padded feet for snow. It was wiped out in this region long ago and has returned through reintroduction and natural spread. Lynx are shy, mostly active at dusk, and move over very large territories. Other residents include red deer, roe deer, and woodland birds such as owls and woodpeckers. Patience and quiet improve your chances greatly.

If you would like a guaranteed sighting, the area near Lindberg can help. At nearby Ludwigsthal, the park runs a visitor centre called the Haus zur Wildnis, which means "House of Wilderness." Around it is a large outdoor animal enclosure. Here, native species live in big fenced areas of real forest. Wolves and lynx are among the animals you may see there. The enclosures are spacious, so the animals can still hide. Seeing one feels earned rather than staged.

The visitor centre itself is a good place to start or end a walk. It explains the park's ideas, its wildlife, and the long debate about the bark beetle. Families often use it as a base. Paths lead out from there into the surrounding woods, and some routes are easy enough for children and older walkers. Other trails climb toward the higher ridges, such as the area around the Großer Falkenstein. Those routes are steeper and need proper shoes.

The seasons change this place completely. In spring, the beech trees open pale green leaves and the streams run loud with snowmelt. Summer brings deep shade and the smell of warm resin. Autumn turns the beech and maple gold and copper against the dark spruce. Winter here is long and often snowy, and the forest goes almost silent. Weather in these mountains changes quickly, so a warm layer is useful in any month.

As you walk, notice the sound of the forest. There is rarely traffic noise here. You hear wind moving high in the crowns, the tap of a woodpecker, water over stone, and your own steps. Many visitors say the quiet takes some time to get used to. After half an hour, your ears start to pick up smaller things. A rustle in the undergrowth. A bird call you cannot name. The creak of a dead trunk leaning on a living one.

Before you move on, stop and look at the trees closest to you. Some are young and some are very old. Some are standing dead, and some are already lying in the moss. All of them are part of the same slow cycle that this park was created to protect. Nobody planned exactly what this forest will look like in fifty years. That is the point. Take a breath of the cool air, and let the forest continue without you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at Haus zur Wildnis in Ludwigsthal near Lindberg.
Visit the animal enclosure at dusk for active wildlife.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steeper Falkenstein trails.
Pack a warm layer; mountain weather changes quickly.
Walk quietly to improve your chances of seeing deer.
