The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment inside the atrium. The space is tall and pale, and the light falls from above onto walls of patterned stone. Voices rise and soften as they travel up through the open floors. Somewhere a child is asking a question about dinosaurs, and somewhere else a school group is laughing. This is Naturalis, the national natural history museum of the Netherlands, and the building around you was designed to feel a little like the inside of the Earth itself.

Look at the outside of the building as you arrive or leave. The facade is made of horizontal bands of stone, laid in stripes of different tones and textures. The pattern is meant to suggest layers of rock, the kind a geologist reads in a cliff face. Each band stands for time stacked on top of time. The design comes from the Dutch firm Neutelings Riedijk Architects, and the new museum opened to the public in 2019 after years of building work.

The story behind Naturalis is much older than the building. Its roots go back to 1820, when the Dutch state founded a national museum of natural history here in Leiden. The city was a natural home for it. Leiden has the oldest university in the Netherlands, and scholars here had studied plants, animals and minerals for centuries. Over the years, several national collections were brought together under one roof. Today they form one of the largest natural history collections in the world.

How large? The collection runs into tens of millions of objects. There are insects pinned in drawers, birds and mammals, shells, fossils, rocks, and dried plants pressed onto paper. Most of it is not on display at all. It sits in climate-controlled storage, where researchers from around the world come to study it. What you see in the galleries is only a small window into a much larger scientific library — a library made of specimens instead of books.

That is the first thing many visitors miss. Naturalis is not only a museum. It is also a research institute. Scientists here describe new species, study how life changes over time, and track how nature responds to a warming world. When you walk through the exhibitions, you are walking through the public front of a working laboratory. The questions on the gallery walls are often the same questions being asked in offices a few floors away.

Now, the star of the house. In the dinosaur hall stands Trix, a Tyrannosaurus rex. Her bones were found in Montana, in the United States, in 2013. She is one of the most complete skeletons of her kind ever discovered. Her name honours Beatrix, who was queen of the Netherlands at the time. Researchers think Trix lived to an old age for a T. rex, and her skeleton carries signs of injuries and illness that she survived during her life.

Take your time with her. Walk all the way around and look up at the skull. Notice how heavy the jaw is, and how small the arms look against the body. Then look closer at the bones themselves. Real fossil bone has a dark, mineral colour, because stone slowly replaced the original material over millions of years. Standing beneath her, you get a real sense of scale that no photograph can give.

The dinosaur hall is not only about the giants. It also places them in a living world of plants, smaller animals and changing climates. That idea runs through the whole museum. The galleries move through big themes: the history of the Earth, the variety of life today, the Ice Age, how living things attract each other, and how they die and return to the soil. Each room asks a simple question and lets the objects answer it.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a quiet one. Naturalis holds the collection of Eugène Dubois, a Dutch doctor who searched for human ancestors on the island of Java in the late nineteenth century. In 1891 he found fossils that became known as Java Man, an early member of our human family now called Homo erectus. Dubois brought his finds back to the Netherlands, and they are still here in Leiden.

Among his material was a small freshwater shell. More than a century later, researchers looked at it closely and found a zigzag line scratched into its surface. Their study, published in 2014, suggested that Homo erectus made this mark roughly half a million years ago. If that is right, it is one of the oldest known engravings in the world, made long before our own species existed. A simple pattern on a shell... and a very old question about where human creativity begins.

As you move between floors, pay attention to how the museum handles light. Some rooms are bright and open. Others are darker and more theatrical, so the objects glow against the background. Glass cases are often arranged to let you circle a specimen and see it from every side. It is worth slowing down. Many cases hold small details, a beetle's metallic shell or the texture of a feather, that are easy to pass in a hurry.

Step outside again and look around the neighbourhood. Naturalis sits a short walk from Leiden's central station, close to the university buildings and the medical campus. Nearby stands the old Pesthuis, a plague house built in the seventeenth century. By most accounts, it was finished just as the plague faded, and it was never needed for its original purpose. Over the centuries it served other uses, and today it sits beside the modern museum like a reminder of a different kind of science.

That contrast says something about Leiden itself. This is a city of canals, brick houses and narrow bridges, but also a city where people have collected, sorted and named the natural world for hundreds of years. Naturalis is the latest chapter in that habit. The stone stripes on the facade, the drawers of specimens in storage, and the tall skeleton of Trix all belong to the same patient work of understanding life.

Before you go, look once more at the layered walls of the building. Think of each band as a page in the Earth's long record. Somewhere in those layers are the dinosaurs, the early humans on Java, and the birds outside in the trees today. As you walk back towards the station or into the old town, listen for those birds. They are part of the same story you have just walked through.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see Trix before school groups fill the hall.
Walk from Leiden central station in just a few minutes.
Allow at least half a day for all the galleries.
Book tickets online ahead on weekends and school holidays.
Combine your visit with a stroll through Leiden's canals.
