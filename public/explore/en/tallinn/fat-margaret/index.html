The guide

The story

Stand close to the wall and put your palm flat against the stone. It is cool, rough, and slightly uneven under your hand. These are pale grey limestone blocks, the same local stone that built most of Tallinn. Now tip your head back. The tower curves away from you like the side of a giant barrel. It does not rise into a spire. It does not reach for the sky. It simply sits here, wide and heavy, at the northern edge of the Old Town.

This is Fat Margaret, or Paks Margareeta in Estonian. The name sounds affectionate, almost like a joke, and in a way it is. Most towers along Tallinn's walls are tall and slim, with red conical roofs. This one is different. It is short, broad, and thick-walled. It was built in the early sixteenth century, and it was built for one purpose: to face the sea and to survive cannon fire.

To understand why, picture the town five hundred years ago. Tallinn was then called Reval. It was a member of the Hanseatic League, a network of German-speaking merchant towns around the Baltic and North Seas. Ships came in loaded with salt, cloth, and wine. They left with grain, furs, and wax. All that wealth moved through the harbour just beyond this tower. A rich port is a tempting target... and the town council knew it.

By the early fifteen-hundreds, warfare had changed. Gunpowder artillery could smash the thin, tall medieval walls of earlier centuries. Military builders across Europe answered with a new shape. Towers became lower and fatter. Walls became much thicker. Rounded surfaces were better at deflecting a cannonball than flat ones. Fat Margaret is a clear example of that new thinking. Its girth is not decoration. It is armour.

The tower was also a platform for the town's own guns. Cannon openings pierced the walls on several levels, aimed out over the approach from the water. Any ship that came too close, or any force that landed on the shore, would meet fire from here. At the same time, the tower guarded the gate right beside you. That gate is the Great Coast Gate, the main entrance from the harbour into the town.

Walk a few steps toward the gate and look at the passage. For centuries, sailors, merchants, porters, and customs men passed through here. Goods came off the ships and up into the town's warehouses. If you look at the outer side of the gate, you will find a carved stone panel with coats of arms. It is one of the few decorative touches on this very practical building. The town wanted visitors from the sea to know exactly whose walls these were.

And where does the name come from? Nobody knows for certain. Locals tell several stories. One says it was named after a cannon. Another says it was named after a cook, or another real woman, of generous size. The simplest explanation may be the right one: people looked at this squat, round tower and gave it a nickname. The name stuck, and today almost nobody calls it anything else.

Here is something many visitors miss. The sea is not where it used to be. When the tower was built, the shoreline was much closer than it is now. Over the centuries, the land in this part of the Baltic has slowly risen, and the city has also filled in the coastal edge. So the harbour that this tower once watched so closely now lies some distance away. Fat Margaret still faces the water, but the water has stepped back.

As cannons grew more powerful, the tower lost its military role. It was used for storage, including gunpowder. Later, in the nineteenth century, under the Russian Empire, it became a prison. Thick walls that once kept enemies out now kept people in. That chapter ended violently. In 1917, during the upheaval of the Russian Revolution, a crowd stormed the prison and set it on fire. The tower stood as a damaged shell for years afterward.

Today the tower has a much gentler life. It houses the Estonian Maritime Museum. Inside, the round rooms hold models of ships, navigation instruments, and objects from Estonia's long relationship with the sea. The building was carefully renovated in recent years. If you go in, notice how the thickness of the walls shows in the deep window openings. Each one is almost a small room of its own.

The museum's route also leads up to the roof. From there you can look out over the port, the ferries to Helsinki and Stockholm, and the open Gulf of Finland. Turn around, and the whole Old Town opens behind you. You can see red roofs, church spires, and the hill of Toompea, where the castle and the cathedral stand. It is one of the best places to understand how close this town has always lived to the sea.

Before you leave, look for a dark sculpture near the tower. It is a broken arc of metal. This is a memorial to the passenger ferry Estonia, which sank in a storm in 1994 on its way to Stockholm. More than eight hundred people died. It remains one of the worst peacetime shipping disasters in European history. Its place here, beside a tower built to guard the town from the sea, carries quiet weight.

When you are ready, walk through the gate and into Pikk Street. The name means Long Street. It was the main route from the harbour up to the hill. Just inside, on your right, you will pass three narrow merchant houses standing side by side, often called the Three Sisters. Keep going, and the tall spire of Saint Olaf's Church rises ahead of you.

But for a moment, stay here. Listen to the gulls, the traffic from the port, the footsteps on the cobbles. Look once more at the wide grey curve of the tower. It was built to take the worst the sea could send. It held, through war, fire, and five centuries of weather. Now it simply stands at the edge of the town, watching the water, as it always has.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to the museum's roof for views over the port.
Enter the Old Town through the gate onto Pikk Street.
Visit early morning to see the walls without crowds.
Bring a windproof layer; sea breezes hit this corner hard.
