The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here on Toompea Hill, the sounds of the lower town fade away. Your shoes meet uneven cobblestones, worn smooth by centuries of carriages and boots. A white tower rises above the low rooftops, crowned with a dark spire. This is Saint Mary's Cathedral, known in Estonian as Toomkirik, the Dome Church. Before we go inside, take in the quiet. It is one of the first things visitors notice up here.

Here is a small surprise to begin with. You may look up and search for a dome... and find none. The name has nothing to do with a rounded roof. It comes from the Latin word domus, meaning house, as in the house of God. Across northern Europe, a cathedral was often simply called the dom. So the Dome Church is really the cathedral church. The name describes its status, not its shape.

This is widely considered the oldest church in Tallinn, and in mainland Estonia. Its story begins in the early thirteenth century, when Danish crusaders took control of this hill. They built a fortress here, and beside it a church. The first church was probably wooden. Within a few decades, it was replaced in stone. Even the name of the city may carry a memory of that time. Many historians link Tallinn to an old Estonian phrase meaning Danish castle or Danish town.

The building you see today grew slowly over the following centuries. Builders reshaped it in the Gothic style, a medieval approach with pointed arches and tall, vaulted ceilings. Look at the walls outside. They are thick and plain, finished in pale render over local limestone. There is little decoration on the exterior. This is a northern Gothic church, built for harsh winters and a working community, not for display. The beauty, as you will see, waits inside.

The tower above you came much later. In the late seventeenth century, a great fire swept across Toompea. It destroyed much of the upper town, and it gutted this church. The interior was lost, along with much of what generations had placed inside. The congregation rebuilt. The Baroque tower, with its layered, rounded top, was added in the late eighteenth century. So when you look up at it, you are looking at a different era from the walls below.

Now step through the doors. Let your eyes adjust to the softer light. The first thing most people notice is the walls. They are covered with large wooden coats of arms, carved and painted, hanging one beside another. There are more than a hundred of them. They belonged to noble families, mostly Baltic Germans, the landowning elite who governed this region for centuries. These are funeral heraldry, made to honour members of those families after death.

Take a closer look at a few of them. Each one is different. You will see lions, crowns, knights' helmets, swords, stars and birds. Many are framed with curling leaves and gilded details. Together they form a kind of family register on the walls. For a visitor today, it is like reading a list of the people who once ruled Toompea. The hill was their world. The lower town, with its merchants and guilds, was a separate community with its own laws.

That division is worth remembering as you walk around. For centuries, Toompea and the lower town were almost two cities. The nobility and church officials lived up here. The traders and craftspeople lived below, inside their own walls. This cathedral was the church of the hill. Down in the lower town, Saint Olaf's and Saint Nicholas's served the townspeople. The two communities did not always get along, and the gates between them were sometimes closed at night.

Now look along the floor and the side walls for the tombs. One of the most elaborate belongs to Pontus De la Gardie, a commander in Swedish service, and his wife Sofia. Sofia was a daughter of King John the Third of Sweden. Their stone monument shows the couple lying side by side. It reminds us that Sweden ruled this region in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Each ruling power left its mark inside this church.

Here is a second surprise. Two admirals connected with the Russian Empire also rest here. One is Adam Johann von Krusenstern, a Baltic German who led the first Russian voyage around the world in the early nineteenth century. The other is Samuel Greig, a Scotsman who rose to high rank in the Russian navy. Scottish, Swedish, Danish, German, Russian... the names in this church tell the story of how many powers passed across this small hill.

After the great fire, the church needed new furnishings. Look toward the front for the pulpit and the main altar. They are carved in rich Baroque style, with twisting forms, figures and gilded surfaces. They are generally credited to Christian Ackermann, a woodcarver who worked in Tallinn in that period. Compare them with the plain stone walls around them. The contrast between the simple medieval structure and the ornate carving is one of the pleasures of this interior.

If you hear music during your visit, it may come from the organ. The cathedral has a large historic organ by the German builder Wilhelm Sauer, installed in the early twentieth century. Its sound fills the narrow space very quickly. Concerts are held here from time to time. Today this is the cathedral of the Estonian Evangelical Lutheran Church. The church became Lutheran during the Reformation in the sixteenth century, and it has remained a place of regular worship ever since.

The tower is often open for visitors who want to climb it. The stairs are narrow and steep, so take your time. From the top, you can look across the red roofs of the old town toward the Baltic Sea. On a clear day, the water stretches to the horizon. Back on the ground, two viewing platforms on the edge of Toompea are only a short walk away. They offer a wide view of the lower town and its spires.

Before you leave, stand outside once more and look back at the white tower. Notice how the old walls lean slightly, how the windows sit deep in the stone. Around you, the narrow streets of Toompea run toward government buildings, embassies and the onion domes of the Orthodox cathedral nearby. As you walk away, listen again for the quiet. It is the same quiet the nobles knew, high above the town, beside their church on the hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the tower slowly; the stairs are narrow and steep.
Check the schedule for organ concerts during your stay.
Walk to the nearby Toompea viewing platforms afterwards.
Dress modestly and keep quiet during church services.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven Toompea cobblestones.
