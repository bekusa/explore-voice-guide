The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. Above the limestone walls of Toompea, a square stone tower rises against the Baltic sky. At its top, a flag moves in the wind off the sea. Blue, black and white. On a quiet morning you can hear the cloth snap and the halyard tap against the pole. That small sound is the heartbeat of this place. The tower is called Pikk Hermann, which means Tall Hermann. For Estonians it is far more than an old piece of fortification.

Let's start with where you are standing. Toompea is the limestone hill that rises above Tallinn's lower Old Town. For centuries the city was split in two. Merchants and craftsmen lived in the lower town below. The nobility, the clergy and the rulers lived up here on the hill. The two communities had separate governments and often did not get along. A castle crowned the hill, and Pikk Hermann stands at its southwestern corner, watching over the land approaches to the city.

The tower is medieval. It was built in the fourteenth century, when this region was controlled by the Livonian Order. The Order was a military brotherhood of German knights who ruled much of what is now Estonia and Latvia. They built in stone, and they built for defence. The tower was later made taller in the sixteenth century, as warfare and artillery changed. Look at the thick walls and the narrow openings. This was a lookout and a last refuge, not a decoration.

Where does the name Hermann come from? Honestly, nobody can say for certain. The tower has carried the name for a very long time, and there are several explanations. Some connect it to a common German personal name. Others link it to old local stories. Because the origin is unclear, it is better to enjoy the name as it is. Tall Hermann. Simple, slightly affectionate, like a nickname for a tall friend who has stood guard for six centuries.

Now turn your attention to the flag itself, because this is the part most visitors miss. The flag on Pikk Hermann is the most important flag in Estonia. When people say that a government holds power in this country, they often mean, quite literally, whose flag flies on this tower. Over the last hundred years, that flag has changed many times. Each change marked a turning point in the history of the nation.

The blue, black and white tricolour was first raised here in December 1918. Estonia had declared independence earlier that year, and a war for that independence was underway. During the twentieth century, the country was occupied, first by the Soviet Union, then by Nazi Germany, and then again by the Soviet Union. Different flags rose and fell on this pole. For decades, the Estonian tricolour could not fly here at all. Simply keeping one at home was dangerous.

Then came 24 February 1989. Estonia was still part of the Soviet Union, but change was in the air. On that day, the Estonian flag was raised again on Pikk Hermann. Thousands of people watched. Many cried. Full independence was restored two years later, in 1991. Today, 24 February is Estonia's Independence Day, and the date of that flag raising carries extra weight. When you look at the tower now, you are looking at the place where a nation saw its colours return.

Here is a small ritual worth knowing about. The flag is raised every morning at sunrise and lowered every evening at sunset. The national anthem plays as it goes up. Another patriotic song plays as it comes down. In midsummer, this can mean a very early start, because the Baltic sun rises long before most people wake. In midwinter, the flag climbs late and comes down in the middle of the afternoon. The rhythm of the tower follows the rhythm of the northern light.

Now look at the building attached to the tower. That bright pink facade with its neat classical lines is a much later addition to the castle. It was built in the eighteenth century, when Estonia was part of the Russian Empire. Today it houses the Riigikogu, which is the Estonian parliament. So the medieval tower and the modern state sit side by side. Knights once kept watch from these walls. Now elected lawmakers debate the country's future just a few metres away.

The contrast between the two is worth a slow look. The tower is grey, rough and heavy, made from local limestone. You can see the uneven blocks and the marks of repair. The parliament building is smooth, symmetrical and painted in a cheerful colour. One was built to resist enemies. The other was built to impress. Together they show how this hill has been reused again and again, each new power adding a layer without erasing the one before.

For the best view of the tower, walk down into the small park at its foot. From the lower ground, you can see how the walls grow straight out of the hill. The slope below was once part of the defences, and you can still feel how steep and protective it is. In spring and summer the park fills with green, and the grey stone looks even taller above the trees. In winter, with snow on the slopes, the tower looks severe and quiet.

As you move around Toompea, notice how the tower keeps appearing between buildings. Across the square stands the Russian Orthodox cathedral with its onion domes, built during the imperial period. A short walk away, viewing platforms look out over the red roofs of the lower town and the sea beyond. From several of these spots, if you turn back, you will catch Pikk Hermann over the rooftops, its flag still moving. It works almost like a compass for the hill.

Before you leave, look up one more time. The stone has stood here through sieges, empires and occupations. The flag at the top has changed more often than the stone. Right now, it is blue, black and white. Listen for the wind in the cloth. Watch the light on the limestone. As you walk away, down the cobbled lanes towards the lower town, you might glance back over your shoulder. Most people do. The tall tower will still be there, keeping its quiet watch.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come at sunrise to see the daily flag raising.
Visit at sunset when the flag comes down with music.
Walk into the park below for the full tower view.
Combine with the nearby Toompea viewing platforms.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep cobbled streets up here.
