The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your feet. It is cobbled, uneven, and it rises. You have climbed onto a limestone hill above the rest of Tallinn, and the air up here often feels a little cooler and a little windier than in the streets below. In front of you is a long building painted a soft, rosy pink, with white trim and tall, orderly windows. It looks more like a palace than a fortress. That contrast is where the story of Toompea Castle begins.

This hill has been a place of power for a very long time. Long before the pink walls, people built wooden fortifications here, because the hill gives a clear view over the sea and the land around it. Whoever held Toompea could watch ships arrive in the harbour and see trouble coming from inland. Over the centuries, Danes, German crusading knights, Swedes and Russians all ruled from this spot. Today it belongs to Estonians themselves. It is the seat of the Riigikogu, the Estonian parliament.

Before the history, there is the legend. Estonian folklore says this hill is not natural at all. According to the old tale, it is the burial mound of Kalev, a giant hero of Estonian myth. His grieving widow, Linda, carried stones one by one to build a mound over his grave. Locals say that a stone slipped from her apron near the city, and that her tears formed a lake nearby. It is a story, not a record — but it shows how deeply this hill sits in the national imagination.

Now to the documented past. In 1219, Danish forces arrived and took control of the hill. A famous legend comes from this moment too. It says that during a battle here, a red flag with a white cross fell from the sky and gave the Danes new courage. That flag became the Dannebrog, the Danish national flag. One popular explanation even links the name Tallinn to an old Estonian phrase meaning "Danish town." Historians debate that origin, so treat it as one theory among several.

In the following centuries, the knights of the Livonian Order — a German military and religious order — turned the hill into a true stone fortress. They built thick walls and corner towers from the grey local limestone. If you walk around the castle, you can still see parts of that medieval strength. The western and northern walls look very different from the pink front. They are rough, grey and heavy, and they drop steeply toward the lower ground. This is the older castle, still standing beneath the newer one.

The tallest of those towers is Tall Hermann, or Pikk Hermann in Estonian. It rises at the southwest corner of the castle. Look up at its top and you will see a flag in three horizontal bands — blue, black and white. That is the Estonian flag. For many Estonians, the flag on this tower is the clearest sign of who governs the country. When the flag on Tall Hermann changes, history has changed.

And it has changed several times. During the Soviet period, a Soviet flag flew here. In early 1989, as the movement for independence grew, the blue, black and white flag was raised on Tall Hermann again. People still remember watching it go up. Here is a detail many visitors miss — the flag follows the sun. It is raised at sunrise and lowered at sunset, and the national anthem plays during the ceremony. If you are here at dawn or dusk, listen for it.

Now turn back to the pink front. Why does a medieval castle wear the face of an eighteenth century palace? The answer is the Russian Empire. In the eighteenth century, Estonia came under Russian rule. During the reign of Catherine the Great, part of the old castle was rebuilt to house the provincial government. Workers filled in a section of the medieval moat and added this long Baroque-style wing. Baroque was the grand European style of that era, built to impress with symmetry and calm, formal order.

So the building in front of you is really two buildings at once. The pink wing faces the square and speaks of imperial administration. The grey walls behind it carry the memory of knights and sieges. Standing here, you can almost read the shift in purpose — from a place built to defend, to a place built to govern. The colour itself surprises people. A parliament in pink is not what most visitors expect.

There is a third layer hidden from this view. In the courtyard of the castle stands the parliament hall, built in the early 1920s, soon after Estonia first became independent. It is an unusual building, designed in an expressionist style — a modern movement of that time that liked bold, angular shapes and strong colours. Inside, the main chamber has a dramatic ceiling and vivid surfaces. It is one of the rare parliament halls in Europe built in this style. Guided visits are sometimes possible, but they usually need to be booked in advance.

Now look across the square behind you. The large building with onion-shaped domes is the Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, a Russian Orthodox church built in the late nineteenth century. Its position is no accident. It was placed directly opposite the seat of government, as a clear statement of Russian imperial presence. Many Estonians had mixed feelings about it at the time. Today the cathedral and the castle face each other across the cobbles, two very different voices sharing one small square.

If you have a few extra minutes, walk around to the small garden just south of the castle, near the foot of Tall Hermann. From there, you get one of the best views of the tower and the old walls rising above you. The scale feels much greater from below. You can also continue to the viewing platforms on the northern edge of Toompea, where the red roofs of the lower Old Town spread out toward the sea. On clear days, you can see ships in the bay.

Before you go, take one last look at the castle. Notice how quiet it is, even though important decisions are made inside. Watch the flag move in the wind at the top of Tall Hermann. Feel the slope of the cobbles again as you start to walk away. You are standing on a hill that legend calls a giant's grave, and that history made a seat of power for eight centuries. Today, it belongs to a small country that chose its own flag... and keeps raising it, every morning, with the sun.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come at sunset to hear the anthem during flag lowering.
Book parliament hall tours well in advance.
Walk to the southern garden for the best tower view.
Continue north to Toompea's viewing platforms over Old Town.
Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven cobblestones.
