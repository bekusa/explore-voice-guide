The guide

The story

Start with the grass under your feet. It slopes gently downhill, toward a great curved shell of a stage. Beyond it, on a clear day, you can see the grey-blue line of the Baltic Sea. Listen for a moment. Often you will hear wind, a few gulls, and the hum of traffic from the coastal road. It is hard to believe that this quiet hillside once held one of the largest gatherings of people in Estonian history.

You are standing at the Tallinn Song Festival Grounds. Estonians call it Lauluväljak, which simply means the song field. It sits a little east of the Old Town, close to the green paths of Kadriorg Park. The medieval walls and church spires lie behind you to the west. Here the city opens out into space, sky and water. This open shape is deliberate. The grounds were made for crowds, and for sound.

To understand this place, you need to know about a tradition. Estonia held its first national song festival in 1869, in the university town of Tartu. At that time, the country was part of the Russian Empire, and the local elite spoke German. For ordinary Estonians, gathering to sing in their own language was a quiet act of pride. Choirs came from villages and towns across the land. The idea grew, and it never stopped.

Tallinn began to host the festival later in the nineteenth century, and over time it became the main home of the tradition. Today the event usually takes place about every five years. It runs together with a large dance festival, where thousands of dancers in folk costume fill a stadium. In 2003, UNESCO recognised the song and dance celebrations of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania as part of the intangible heritage of humanity. That means the heritage is not a building. It is the singing itself.

Now look at the stage in front of you. This arch was built in 1960, during the Soviet period. Its design is linked to the Estonian architect Alar Kotli, working with colleagues. The structure is a huge, open shell. It curves up and forward, like a hand cupped behind an ear. The shape reflects sound out toward the audience on the slope. Under this roof, a combined choir of many thousands of singers can stand together in tiers.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. The singers face the audience, but the hill also works as part of the instrument. The natural bowl of the land holds the sound. When the great choir sings, people at the top of the slope can still hear clearly. Sound engineers add amplification today. Even so, the basic idea is very old and very simple: a slope, a shell, and many voices at once.

During the Soviet decades, the festival continued, but under close control. Programmes had to include songs that praised the Soviet state. Still, Estonians found room for their own feelings. One song became especially important. Its title is Mu isamaa on minu arm, which means My Fatherland Is My Love. The words come from a nineteenth-century Estonian poet, Lydia Koidula. The music by the conductor Gustav Ernesaks became a kind of unofficial anthem for the nation.

Look for a seated bronze figure on the slope. This is a statue of Gustav Ernesaks. He sits looking toward the stage, as if he is still listening to the choir. For many Estonians, he represents the spirit of the festival. Children sometimes sit beside him for photographs. It is a small, human touch in a very large landscape, and it shows how much a single conductor can mean to a country.

Then came 1988. Across the Soviet Union, there was new space for public speech. In Estonia, people began to gather here in the summer evenings and sing patriotic songs, night after night. These were called the night song festivals. Later that year, a huge rally took place on these grounds. Estimates suggest that a very large share of the whole Estonian population came. People held old blue, black and white flags, which had been banned for decades.

This peaceful movement is now known as the Singing Revolution. It was one part of a longer process that led to the restoration of Estonian independence in 1991. Here is the second surprise for many visitors. There were no weapons in these gatherings, only songs, flags and very large crowds. Historians still discuss how much the singing itself mattered. For Estonians, however, the memory is clear. They sang, and the world began to listen.

Turn and look for the tall tower near the stage. This is the fire tower. During each festival, a flame is carried across the country and lit at the top of this tower. The ceremony marks the opening of the event. On normal days, the tower stands quiet. You can still imagine the moment of lighting, with tens of thousands of people below and the sea light fading behind the stage.

Outside festival years, the grounds have many lives. Large international concerts take place here in summer. In winter, the slope can fill with snow, and local families sometimes use it for sledging. On an ordinary weekday, you may share the place with joggers, dog walkers, or a school group. The space feels open and calm. That contrast is part of its power. The same grass that holds a nation can also hold a quiet picnic.

Before you leave, walk to the top of the slope and look back. You will see the white curve of the arch, the fire tower, and the water beyond. Try to picture the hill completely full, with every person singing the same words in the same moment. Then notice what is here now: the wind, the open sky, and the sound of your own steps. As you walk back toward the Old Town, listen. In this country, someone, somewhere, is usually singing.
