The guide

The story

Step off the cobbles and into the shade of the porch. The air cools at once. Outside, the stones of the Old Town are worn smooth under your feet, and voices drift from the square nearby. In here, the sound drops away. You notice the smell of old wood and wax, and the soft creak of a floorboard somewhere ahead. This is the Church of the Holy Spirit. It is small and plain from the outside, and it has watched over this corner of Tallinn for centuries.

Before you go deeper, look back at the building itself. The walls are limestone, the grey local stone that built most of Tallinn, and they are coated in white lime. The western front ends in a stepped gable. Its edges climb in square notches toward the sky, a shape you see across the trading towns of the Baltic. Above it rises a slender spire. Compared with the great churches of the city, this one keeps a modest profile. That modesty is part of its story.

The church took its present Gothic form in the fourteenth century. Gothic, in short, is the medieval style of pointed arches and tall, narrow windows. But this was never meant to be a grand cathedral. It began as a chapel tied to the Holy Spirit almshouse, a medieval refuge for the poor, the sick and the elderly. Care for the weak and worship sat side by side here, under one roof.

There is a second role that many visitors miss. The Town Hall stands only a short walk away, and this church served the town council. The merchants and magistrates who ran Tallinn came here to pray. So the building connected two very different worlds. On one side were the people who held power in the city. On the other were the people who had almost nothing. Both passed through these same doors.

Now look up at the outside wall again, if you passed it on the way in. A large carved clock hangs on the facade. Its face is richly decorated and painted in deep colours and gold. This is the oldest public clock in Tallinn. The carving dates from the late seventeenth century. For generations, people in the street below set their day by it. It is worth a second look as you leave.

Inside, let your eyes adjust. The space is intimate, almost like a large room rather than a hall. Wooden galleries run along the walls, and their panels carry painted scenes from the Bible. The colours have softened with age. They are warm reds, muted greens and faded blues. Light from the windows falls across the pews and catches the edges of carved wood. Take a moment to stand still and simply look.

Walk slowly toward the east end, and you reach the treasure of this church. It is a great winged altarpiece, finished in 1483 by Bernt Notke. Notke was one of the leading artists of the late Middle Ages in northern Europe. He worked from the city of Lübeck, a powerful trading port on the Baltic coast. The altar has painted panels and carved, gilded figures. The wings are hinged, so the altar could be opened or closed for different seasons of the church year.

Stand close and study the figures. The faces are calm and serious. The folds of the robes fall in deep, sharp lines, carved by hand from wood more than five hundred years ago. Gold leaf still glows on many surfaces. For medieval worshippers, the opening of these wings on a feast day was a moment of real drama. A closed altar became a blaze of colour and light.

Nearby stands the pulpit, and it is old too. It dates from the late sixteenth century, and it is among the oldest pulpits in Estonia. By then, the Reformation had reached Tallinn. This church became Lutheran, and the spoken sermon grew far more important than before. The pulpit was where the preacher spoke to the congregation, so its age tells you how long words have been delivered from this spot.

Here is the surprise that matters most to Estonians. This church was home to an Estonian-speaking congregation at a time when the city's ruling class mostly spoke German. Johann Koell, a pastor here, worked on an early Lutheran catechism in the Estonian language in the 1530s. A catechism is a short book of religious teaching in questions and answers. It is counted among the earliest printed texts in Estonian. Small fragments of it survived.

Later in the sixteenth century, another pastor of the Estonian congregation left his own mark. His name was Balthasar Russow. He wrote a chronicle of Livonia, the old name for the lands that became Estonia and Latvia. His book describes wars, politics and daily life in a time of great upheaval. Historians still read it today. So within these walls, the Estonian language and the written history of the region both have deep roots.

The church has also known disaster in recent memory. In 2002, a fire broke out and destroyed the spire. An old bell fell and was lost. People in Tallinn watched with real grief. Fortunately, much of the interior survived, including the altar. The spire was rebuilt, and the church returned to use. When you look up at the tower now, you are seeing a careful restoration of a shape the city refused to lose.

Before you leave, find a quiet seat in one of the pews. Listen to the building. You may hear footsteps on the wooden floor, a low voice, the door opening and closing. Notice how the light shifts across the painted galleries as clouds pass outside. This is still a working church, so speak softly and move gently. It belongs to its congregation first, and to visitors second.

When you step back into the street, turn around once more. Look for the painted clock on the wall, and the white gable against the sky. Then walk the few steps toward Town Hall Square. You are following the path that councillors, almshouse residents and Estonian worshippers walked for centuries. The cobbles under your feet are the same kind they knew. Take that thought with you, and let the noise of the Old Town slowly return.
