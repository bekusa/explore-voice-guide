The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of Freedom Square and listen. Your footsteps ring on the wide, open paving, and traffic hums along the street beside you. Then look up the slope of the hill to your left. A tall column of pale glass panels rises from the grass. In daylight it looks almost milky, like frosted ice. It holds the light more than it reflects it. This is the War of Independence Victory Column. It is one of the youngest monuments in Tallinn, and it marks one of the oldest arguments in modern Estonian memory.

The column commemorates the Estonian War of Independence, fought between 1918 and 1920. At the end of the First World War, the old Russian Empire collapsed, and Estonia declared itself an independent republic. That declaration had to be defended. For more than a year, Estonian forces fought the Red Army in the east. They also fought German volunteer troops in the south. Volunteers came from Finland and Denmark, and a British naval squadron helped in the Baltic Sea.

The war ended with the Treaty of Tartu in 1920. In that treaty, Soviet Russia recognised Estonian independence. For a small country, which had never before had its own state, this was a founding moment. Many families here still keep memories of grandfathers and great-grandfathers who served. The monument in front of you is not about a distant empire. It is about the war that made this country exist in the first place.

Now look at the shape at the top of the column. It is a large cross with flared arms. This is the Cross of Liberty, Estonia's highest decoration from that war. The state created the award during the fighting to honour bravery and service. Military men received it, and so did civilians and foreign allies. By placing this cross on the column, the designers made the whole monument into an enlarged medal. You can read it as an award given back to the whole generation that fought.

The history of this memorial is also a story of waiting. In the years between the two world wars, Estonians discussed a large monument to the War of Independence. However, those plans never became a finished memorial in the capital. Then, in 1940, the Soviet Union occupied Estonia. For about fifty years, monuments to the War of Independence were removed or destroyed across the country. Many were hidden, buried, or quietly taken apart. Remembering that war in public could be dangerous.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Under Soviet rule, this open space was known as Victory Square, but the victory it honoured was a different one. After Estonia restored its independence in 1991, the old name, Freedom Square, returned. The square slowly became a stage for the new state. Military parades, concerts, and national celebrations now take place on the paving where you stand. The column added a permanent focus to that open space.

The memorial came from an architectural competition held in the 2000s. A team of young Estonian designers won with an entry called Libertas, the Latin word for freedom. Their idea was bold. They proposed a monument made almost entirely of glass, lit from within. The column was unveiled in 2009 on the twenty-third of June. That date is Victory Day in Estonia. It marks a decisive battle of the war, fought near the town of Võnnu in 1919. In Latvia, the town is called Cēsis.

Walk a little closer and look at the surface. The column is not one solid block. It is built from many rectangular glass plates, fixed together in a grid. Each pane has a slightly cloudy finish. On a grey Baltic afternoon, the glass picks up the colour of the sky, so it can look almost blue. At sunset it may turn warm and golden. The cross does not stand out against the sky as a dark shape. Instead, it seems to glow.

This brings us to the second surprise. When it opened, the column was one of the most argued-about structures in Tallinn. Some people felt glass was too fragile for a war memorial. Others said the cross looked too modern, or that the cost was too high. Soon after the opening, some glass panels cracked or showed damage, and they needed repair. The lighting also caused technical trouble. For a while, newspapers seemed to report on the column almost as often as on the war itself.

Arguments like these are common for national monuments, especially new ones. With time, opinions have softened for many residents. Some people still find it cold or too modern. Others now see the glass as an honest choice. Freedom, after all, needs care and maintenance, just like these panels. Whatever you decide, notice how the column changes with weather and season. Stone monuments usually look the same every day. This one does not, because the glass depends on the light.

Turn around slowly and take in the surroundings. Behind the column is Harju Hill, a green slope that was once part of the city's fortifications. The old bastions and walls of Tallinn's defences ran through this area. On the far side of the square, you can see Saint John's Church, a pale, Lutheran church with a slim tower. Beyond the street lies the edge of the medieval Old Town. So the column stands where the old city, the modern city, and the state's ceremonial space all meet.

If you can, come back after dark. When the internal lights turn on, the glass column becomes a tall, soft lantern above the square. People walk past on their way into the Old Town or home from work. Some stop to take a photograph, and others hardly look up. On national holidays, the square fills with flags, soldiers, and families. On ordinary evenings, it is often quiet, with only a few skateboards and the sound of buses.

Before you leave, take one more look at the cross at the top. Think about the young men and women who fought for a country that did not yet fully exist. Then remember that, for fifty years, this memory could not be spoken freely here. Now it stands openly in glass, in the middle of the capital. As you walk away, perhaps toward Harju Hill or into the old streets, notice how often the column appears behind you, still catching the light.
