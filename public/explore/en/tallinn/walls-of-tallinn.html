The guide

The story

Put your hand on the stone. It is cool, slightly rough, and grey with a faint yellow tint where the sun has warmed it. This is limestone, quarried from the ground beneath the city. Above you, a red-tiled conical roof rises like a pointed hat. Somewhere nearby, a seagull calls from the direction of the harbour. You are standing beside one of the most complete medieval town walls left anywhere in northern Europe, and it has been standing guard here for centuries.

The walls you see today grew slowly. Building began in the thirteenth century, not long after Danish forces took this hill in 1219. Over the next three hundred years, the people of the town kept adding height, thickness, and towers. Each generation responded to new threats and new weapons. So what looks like a single wall is really a long conversation between builders. It carries the work of many hands, and many different fears.

At its fullest, the wall ran for more than two kilometres around the Lower Town. It had dozens of towers along its length. Today, nearly two kilometres still stand, with around two dozen towers. That survival rate is rare. Many European cities pulled down their walls in the nineteenth century to make room for traffic and new boulevards. Here, much of the old ring stayed in place. Walk any edge of the Old Town and you will meet it sooner or later.

To understand why this city needed such strong walls, think about trade. In the Middle Ages, Tallinn was known by its German name, Reval. It belonged to the Hanseatic League — a powerful network of merchant towns around the Baltic and North Seas. Grain, furs, salt, wax, and cloth passed through this port. Wealth attracts attention, and not always the friendly kind. The walls protected merchants, warehouses, and the town's hard-won independence.

Here is something many visitors miss. Tallinn is really two towns. Up on the limestone hill called Toompea sat the castle and the cathedral — the seat of the nobles and the church. Down below, in the Lower Town, lived the merchants and craftsmen. The two communities did not always trust each other. For a long time, there was even a wall between them, with gates that could be closed. The town wall you are seeing was built by the Lower Town, for the Lower Town.

Walk to the eastern side of the Old Town and you reach Viru Gate. Two slender towers with pointed roofs frame the entrance to Viru Street. They look like the whole gate, but they are only the outer part of a much larger gate complex that once stood here. Today, flower sellers often set up their stalls nearby, and the street beyond fills with cafés and shop signs. It is one of the most photographed spots in the city.

Now head north, toward the sea, and you find a very different tower. Fat Margaret is wide, round, and squat — built to guard the Great Coastal Gate and the road to the harbour. Its walls are very thick, and it was designed to impress sailors arriving by sea. Nobody is completely sure where the name came from. Locals say it may refer to a cook, or simply to the tower's generous girth. Today it houses the Estonian Maritime Museum.

On the southern side, near Toompea, stands a tall artillery tower called Kiek in de Kök. The name comes from Low German, the language of the old merchant class. It means roughly "peek into the kitchen." The story goes that guards at the top could look straight down into the kitchens of the houses below. Look closely at its walls and you can see cannonballs set into the stone — a reminder of the Livonian War, a sixteenth-century conflict over control of this region.

That tower leads to a second surprise. Beneath the ground nearby runs a network of passages inside the old bastions. These are later earthworks, added when cannons made tall medieval walls easier to break. During the Swedish period, in the seventeenth century, engineers built massive earth-and-stone bastions around the city. The tunnels inside them were used for moving soldiers and supplies unseen. Much later, people used them as shelters. Today you can visit them on a guided tour.

If you want to walk on the wall itself, there is a stretch where you can. On the western side of the Lower Town, a covered wooden walkway runs along the top, linking several towers. The boards creak under your feet. Narrow openings in the stone frame small views of red rooftops, church spires, and chimneys. It is a good way to feel the scale of the defences. You also see how close the houses stood to the wall.

Below that section lies Towers' Square, a long green lawn that runs along the outside of the wall. From here you get one of the clearest views of the fortifications. Several towers stand in a row, each with its own shape and roof. In spring and summer, the grass fills with people reading, picnicking, or simply lying in the sun. Children run along the paths. It is a quiet, open place, and a good spot to pause.

In 1997, the Old Town of Tallinn was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. The walls are a big part of the reason. Together with the street pattern, the merchant houses, and the churches, they show how a Hanseatic trading town was organised. Restoration work continues, and you may notice newer stone patched into older sections. Those repairs are part of the story too — each one keeps the wall standing for the next generation.

Before you move on, look again at the stone in front of you. Notice how the colour changes from block to block, and how moss gathers in the joints. Listen for footsteps on the cobbles, a bicycle bell, the low hum of the modern city just outside. The wall once divided inside from outside, safety from danger. Today people pass through its gates without a second thought. As you walk away, glance back once — and watch the towers rise above the rooftops.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the covered wall walkway on the western side.
Book a guided tour to enter the bastion tunnels.
Visit Towers' Square for the widest view of towers.
Come early morning to photograph Viru Gate without crowds.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven cobblestones and wooden steps.
