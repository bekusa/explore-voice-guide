The guide

The story

Put your hand against the base of this tower and feel how cool the stone stays, even on a warm afternoon. The limestone is grey and rough, set in thick courses that lean very slightly inward as they rise. Above you, the wall curves away like the side of a great drum. Somewhere nearby, a gull calls over the red roofs of the Lower Town. This is Kiek in de Kök, and it has been watching this hillside for more than five centuries.

Start with the name, because it is the best part. Kiek in de Kök comes from Low German, the language of the merchants who ran medieval Tallinn. It means, quite simply, peek into the kitchen. From the upper floors, the guards on duty could look straight down into the houses below. They could see through the chimneys and windows, right into the kitchens. Imagine the soldiers on a long winter watch, bored and hungry, studying other people's dinners.

The tower is not unique in carrying this cheeky name. Towns across the old Hanseatic world, the trading league of northern European ports, used it for tall watchtowers. But Tallinn's version became the best known. In the Middle Ages, the city was called Reval. It was a rich member of that league, moving grain, furs, salt and wax between east and west. Wealth like that attracted enemies, and wealth also paid for walls.

Construction of this tower began in the late fifteenth century. It was built as an artillery tower, which tells you a lot about the moment. Gunpowder was changing warfare across Europe. Old thin walls, designed against arrows and ladders, could no longer stop cannon fire. So the builders of Reval answered with mass. The walls here are several metres thick at the base. Later, in the sixteenth century, the tower was raised and strengthened again.

Now look up at the outer face of the wall and search carefully. You may spot dark, round shapes sitting in the stone. These are cannonballs. Tallinn was besieged during the Livonian War, a long struggle in the sixteenth century over control of the Baltic lands. Russian forces under Ivan the Fourth, often called Ivan the Terrible, attacked the city. The tower took hits and survived. Some of the iron balls set into the wall are said to be mementoes placed there afterwards, so treat each one as part fact, part pride.

That detail is worth a moment. Cities usually hide their scars. Tallinn chose to display them. Whether each cannonball landed where you see it today, or was set there later as a reminder, the message is the same. The walls held. For a merchant town that depended on trust and stability, that was a powerful story to tell visitors arriving at the gates.

Walk around the tower and notice how it fits into the wider defences. This is the slope of Toompea, the limestone hill where the castle and the nobility sat. The Lower Town below belonged to the merchants and craftsmen. The two communities were often rivals, with separate laws and separate walls. This tower stands right at the meeting point, guarding the approach to the hill from the south.

Today the tower is a museum of the city's fortifications. Inside, the stairs are narrow and the rooms are stacked one above another. Each floor once served a purpose in defence, with openings for guns pointing outward. The exhibitions follow the story of the town's walls and the weapons used to attack and defend them. Climbing upward, you feel how cramped life was for the gunners, working among smoke, noise and heavy powder.

Here is something many visitors miss. Beneath your feet runs a network of passages known as the Bastion Tunnels. They were dug during the Swedish period, when Sweden ruled this region in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The Swedes built large earth bastions around the old town, and the tunnels let soldiers move between them unseen. In the twentieth century, the tunnels found new uses, including as shelter for residents during wartime air raids.

The tunnels can usually be visited on a guided tour from the museum. Down there, the air is damp and still, and the temperature barely changes through the year. The walls are bare stone and packed earth. It is a striking contrast to the windy tower above. Together, the two spaces show how defence shifted over the centuries, from tall stone towers to low, thick earthworks that absorbed cannon fire.

Step back now and look at the neighbours. Close by stands the slimmer Maiden Tower, another piece of the old wall. The surrounding green slopes were once part of the bastion zone, later turned into parks. On a clear day, this corner has a calm, almost village feeling. Families walk dogs along the paths, and the sound of the city fades to a soft hum.

Think about how differently people have looked at this tower over time. For the gunners, it was a workplace and a weapon. For the families below, it was a neighbour who could see into their homes. For the Swedish engineers, it was old technology to be absorbed into newer defences. And for you, today, it is a window into how a small trading city kept itself alive in a dangerous neighbourhood.

Before you move on, take one last look at the wall from a little distance. Notice the slight lean of the stone, the small dark openings, the round shapes caught in the surface. As you walk back toward the old town, glance at the kitchen windows of the houses nearby. Somewhere above you, five centuries ago, a guard was doing exactly the same thing.
