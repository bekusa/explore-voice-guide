The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes. Somewhere nearby, a fountain splashes. Old oak and chestnut trees move in the wind coming off the Baltic. In front of you stands a palace with a warm reddish facade, white columns and tall windows. It looks almost too graceful for this northern coast. Welcome to Kadriorg Palace, a small piece of Italian-style Baroque that a Russian tsar placed at the edge of Tallinn.

First, a note on where you are. Kadriorg sits about two kilometres east of the medieval Old Town. It is not inside the old walls. The tightly packed merchant houses and church spires are behind you, to the west. Here the city opens into park, sea air and long tree-lined avenues. Many visitors walk out from the Old Town along the shore. Others take the tram. Both routes bring you into a calmer, greener Tallinn.

The story begins with war. In the early eighteenth century, Russia and Sweden fought a long struggle for control of the Baltic, known as the Great Northern War. Tallinn, then usually called Reval, came under Russian control in 1710. Tsar Peter the Great, the ruler who built Saint Petersburg, wanted a presence on this coast. He wanted a seaside residence too. So he bought land here, close to the harbour where his new navy could anchor.

Work on the palace began in 1718. Peter gave the commission to Niccolò Michetti, an architect from Italy who also worked on royal projects near Saint Petersburg. Michetti brought the language of Roman Baroque with him. Baroque is the grand, theatrical style of seventeenth and early eighteenth century Europe, full of curves, columns and decoration. Russian architects later continued the work after Michetti left. The result is a palace that feels Mediterranean in spirit, built under grey Baltic skies.

Here is the first surprise. The palace was a gift of love. Peter dedicated it to his wife, Catherine, who later ruled Russia herself as Empress Catherine the First. The name of this whole district comes from her. In Estonian, Kadriorg means Catherine's valley. So every time a local says the name of this neighbourhood, they are still saying her name, three centuries later. Look for her monogram inside the palace. Her initial appears in the decoration of the main hall.

And here is the second surprise. Peter barely used it. He died in 1725, before the palace was fully finished. Before that, he preferred a much smaller, plainer house nearby. That modest building still stands at the edge of the park and is known today as Peter the Great's House. According to popular stories, the tsar felt more comfortable in simple rooms than in a grand palace. Whatever the reason, the great house he built for his family stood mostly quiet for long periods.

Now look at the building itself. The main block rises two storeys high, with lower wings stretching out on each side. White pilasters, which are flat columns attached to the wall, divide the reddish facade into a steady rhythm. The windows are tall and evenly spaced. The roof is steep and dark. Nothing here is huge. This is not Versailles. It is a summer residence on a human scale, designed to look elegant rather than overwhelming.

Step inside, and the scale changes. The heart of the palace is the Great Hall, a room that rises through both storeys. Its walls and ceiling carry dense stucco work, which is decorative plaster shaped by hand into figures, garlands and frames. Painted panels fill the ceiling. Light pours in from windows on two levels. Many visitors consider this hall one of the finest Baroque interiors in the Baltic region. Take a moment in the centre of the room and look straight up.

The palace did not stay frozen in the age of the tsars. In the twentieth century, Estonia became an independent state. Between the two world wars, the palace served as an official residence for Estonia's head of state. Later came Soviet rule, and the building took on other uses. After Estonia regained independence in 1991, the palace went through a long restoration. Around the turn of the millennium it opened again, this time as an art museum.

Today the palace houses the Kadriorg Art Museum, part of the Art Museum of Estonia. The collection focuses on foreign art. You will find Western European and Russian paintings, along with prints, sculpture and decorative objects. Dutch and Flemish works hang near Italian and German pieces. Porcelain and furniture fill some rooms. The art fits the building well, because both the palace and its paintings come from a wider European world that once met here on the Baltic shore.

Do not leave without walking behind the palace. There you will find a formal flower garden laid out in geometric beds, with a fountain at its centre. In summer the beds fill with colour, and the garden is one of the best places to see the back facade. Beyond the palace, the park spreads out in every direction. There is the Swan Pond with its small island pavilion, quiet paths under old trees, and several other museums, including Kumu, the large modern home of Estonian art.

As you wander, notice how the people of Tallinn use this space. Families push strollers along the avenues. Runners pass by in all weather. Couples sit on benches near the water. On weekends, wedding parties often come here for photographs, posing against the palace walls or the garden fountain. Kadriorg is not only a monument. It is the city's favourite park, a place where history and ordinary daily life share the same paths.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment in front of the palace. Look at the white columns against the warm red wall. Listen again to the fountain, the gravel, the wind in the trees. Three centuries ago, a tsar chose this spot for his wife and then rarely came back. The house he left behind outlived his empire. As you walk away, look back once more, and notice how small and calm it looks among the trees.
