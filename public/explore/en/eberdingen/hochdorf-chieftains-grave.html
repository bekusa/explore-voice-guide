The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the grass on the mound, and somewhere beyond the fields a tractor hums. The ground rises gently under your feet. It looks like a soft green hill, the kind you would hardly notice from a passing car. But this hill was raised by human hands, about two and a half thousand years ago, over the body of one man.

You are standing near Hochdorf, a village that belongs to Eberdingen, in the rolling farmland west of Stuttgart. The people who built this mound were Celts of the Hallstatt culture. That name comes from a site in Austria, and it describes the early Iron Age of central Europe, roughly the eighth to fifth centuries before Christ. This burial dates to around 530 BC. Rome was still a modest city. Athens had not yet built the Parthenon.

For most of its life, this mound was almost invisible. Centuries of ploughing had flattened it into the fields. In 1968, a local amateur archaeologist named Renate Leibfried noticed stones and traces in the ground and reported them. Her careful eye changed everything. Professional excavations followed in 1978 and 1979, led by the archaeologist Jörg Biel. What they found inside made headlines across Europe.

Here is the first surprise. Most rich Iron Age graves in this region were robbed long ago, often in antiquity itself. This one was not. The wooden burial chamber had collapsed under a heavy layer of stones, and that weight sealed it. Thieves either missed it or gave up. So archaeologists found the chieftain almost exactly as his mourners had left him — every object still in place.

The man was roughly fifty years old when he died. He was tall, noticeably taller than most people of his time. He lay on a long bronze couch, a piece of furniture with no close parallel north of the Alps. The couch rests on eight small bronze figures of women. Each figure stands on a single wheel, so the whole couch could roll. It is a strange and beautiful object, and nothing quite like it has turned up anywhere else.

Gold covered him in places you might not expect. Thin sheets of gold decorated his shoes. A gold band circled his neck. Gold ornaments sat on his belt and on a small dagger. Some of these golden pieces appear to have been made quickly, perhaps for the funeral itself, because they are delicate and not built for daily wear. On his head he wore a conical hat made of birch bark. It is a humble material, and that contrast is part of its charm.

At the foot of the couch stood a massive bronze cauldron, large enough to hold around four hundred litres. It came from a Greek workshop, probably in southern Italy, where Greek colonists lived. Three bronze lions sat around its rim. And here is the second surprise. One of those lions does not match the others. It seems to be a local replacement, made north of the Alps when an original was lost or damaged. Look at the replica in the museum and you can see the difference in style.

Scientists studied the residue inside the cauldron. It once held mead, a drink of fermented honey, and the pollen traces showed many different flowers from the surrounding countryside. Along the chamber wall hung drinking horns, enough for a whole company of feasters. Most were made from the horns of aurochs, the great wild cattle of ancient Europe. One was made of iron and bands of gold. The message is clear. This man was a host, and he went to his grave ready to pour for his guests.

The chamber also held a four-wheeled wagon, its wood covered with iron fittings. On the wagon lay bronze dishes and tableware, and pieces of harness for a pair of horses. Beside the body were a quiver of arrows, fishing hooks, and a razor and nail clippers. These small personal things are easy to overlook. Yet they turn a powerful ruler into a person who fished, groomed himself, and cared about how he looked.

The walls and furniture were draped in textiles. Most of the fabric decayed, but enough survived for researchers to study the weaving and the dyes. Fur and woven cloth covered the bronze couch. Even flowers and herbs seem to have been part of the burial. Imagine the chamber as it was on the day it closed — dim, colourful, full of the smell of wood, wool, and honey.

Who was he? Nobody knows his name. Archaeologists believe he belonged to the ruling elite of this area, a society that traded across the Alps for Greek and Etruscan goods. Some scholars connect the region's power to the hill of Hohenasperg, which you can sometimes see on the horizon. That link is a well-known theory rather than a proven fact. What is certain is that this landscape was once a place of wealth, trade, and serious political influence.

After the excavation, the mound was rebuilt to something close to its original form. The original treasures are now kept in the Landesmuseum Württemberg in Stuttgart. But nearby, the Celtic Museum in Hochdorf holds a full-scale reconstruction of the burial chamber, with faithful replicas of the couch, the cauldron, and the wagon. Next to it, a reconstructed Iron Age farmstead shows how ordinary people lived in the shadow of rulers like this one.

Before you leave, stand still on this rise for a moment. Look out at the fields, the hedges, the roofs of Hochdorf. People farmed this same soil when the chieftain was alive. Under your feet, where the chamber once lay, there is now only earth and grass. As you walk away, notice how gently the mound slopes back into the land — and how easily, for centuries, everyone simply walked past it.
