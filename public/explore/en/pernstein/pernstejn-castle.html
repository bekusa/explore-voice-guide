The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Up here it moves around corners of stone, through narrow passages, across wooden walkways that creak under your weight. Then look down. The rock falls away beneath the walls, and below you the forested hills of the Bohemian-Moravian Highlands roll on in every direction. The air smells of pine and old masonry. You are standing on one of the best preserved castles in Central Europe.

This is Pernštejn Castle, in the Czech region of Moravia, above the small village of Nedvědice. It sits on a rocky outcrop above a meeting of streams and river valleys. From a distance it looks less like one building and more like a cluster: towers, gables and walls pressed close together and climbing the rock. That cluster is the result of centuries of building, each generation adding new layers on top of the last.

The story begins in the thirteenth century. A noble family, originally linked to the nearby lands of Medlov, built a fortress here and took its name. They became the lords of Pernštejn. Over the next three hundred years, that name grew into one of the most powerful in the Czech lands. Members of the family served kings, held high offices, and owned estates far beyond these hills.

There is a legend about how the family got its coat of arms. According to the popular tale, a humble charcoal burner named Věnava lived in these forests. He is said to have caught a wild aurochs, an enormous extinct species of wild cattle, and led it to the royal court by a ring through its nose. As a reward, he received noble rank. Look for the black aurochs head with a ring in its nose. It is the family emblem, and you will find it carved and painted around the castle.

Pernštejn is often called the marble castle. That name comes from the stone used in its finest details. Door frames, window surrounds and portals were cut from a local, marble-like stone quarried in the area. It is pale, with soft streaks and veins of grey. As you walk through, run your eyes along the edges of the doorways. That polished, cool-looking stone was a sign of wealth and taste.

The castle reached its height of power and splendour around the turn of the sixteenth century. This was the age of Vilém of Pernštejn, one of the leading noblemen of his time, a skilled politician and a careful manager of land. Under the family, the medieval fortress was expanded and modernised. Late Gothic builders, and later Renaissance craftsmen, added new halls, decorated rooms and stronger defences.

That is the first surprise of this place. Many castles in Europe were rebuilt in later centuries in the heavy Baroque style, or turned into comfortable mansions. Pernštejn mostly escaped that. Its outline still carries the shape of a late medieval and early Renaissance stronghold. When you see the towers and the defensive walls from the path below, you are looking at something close to what a visitor saw around 1500.

The second surprise is the defensive design itself. The approach is long and indirect. Visitors, and any attackers in the past, had to pass through a series of gates, walls and courtyards before they reached the heart of the castle. Each layer could be defended on its own. Notice how the path turns and narrows. Notice how often you are watched from above by a window, a loophole or a tower.

The castle is often described as never taken by force. During the Thirty Years' War, the great seventeenth-century conflict that swept across Central Europe, Swedish troops moved through Moravia. Tradition holds that Pernštejn withstood their attempt to capture it. Standing on these walls, with the steep rock below, it is easy to understand why an army would hesitate.

Power did not last forever, though. The family spent heavily, and debts grew. Toward the end of the sixteenth century, the lords of Pernštejn sold their ancestral seat. The castle passed through other noble owners in the following centuries. In modern times it became the property of the state, and today it is protected as a national cultural monument of the Czech Republic.

There is one more legend you may hear at the entrance. Near the castle stands an old linden tree, the national tree of the Czech lands. Locals say that the castle will stand for as long as this tree lives. It is folklore, not history, but it shows how closely people here tie the castle to the land around it. Take a moment to look at the tree before you go inside.

Inside, guided tours lead through a sequence of rooms with very different moods. Some are bare and cold, with thick walls and small windows. Others carry painted ceilings, old furniture, hunting trophies and portraits of former owners. You climb stairs, cross narrow passages and step out onto walkways with sudden views across the valley. The building feels alive with corners and turns, never one simple floor plan.

Now come back to where you are standing. Feel the uneven stone under your feet, worn smooth by centuries of boots and shoes. Look up at the towers against the sky, and down at the green slopes that once fed and protected this household. As you leave, turn around once more on the path below. From there, the whole castle rises from the rock, a stone crown the forest has carried for over seven hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for steep paths and uneven stone floors.
Interiors are visited on guided tours, so plan ahead.
Bring a light jacket, wind on the walls can be cold.
Walk back down the path for the best full castle view.
Allow extra time for the long climb from the village.
