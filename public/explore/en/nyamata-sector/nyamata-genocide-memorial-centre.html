The guide

The story

Before you step inside, notice the light. It does not pour in evenly. It comes through in small, sharp points, scattered across the roof and the upper walls. Each of those points is a hole. Many were made by bullets, and some by shrapnel from grenades. Stand still for a moment and let your eyes adjust. The air is cool and quiet. It smells of old fabric and dust. This is the former Catholic church of Nyamata, and today it is one of the most important memorials to the genocide against the Tutsi in Rwanda.

Nyamata sits in the Bugesera District, roughly thirty kilometres south of Kigali. The road here runs through low hills, banana groves, and red earth. On an ordinary day, the town feels calm. Motorbike taxis wait at corners, and children walk home from school in uniform. That calm is part of what makes this place so hard to absorb. The church looks like many rural churches across the country. It is simple, built of brick, with a modest bell structure. Nothing on the outside prepares you for what happened within these walls.

To understand Nyamata, it helps to know a little about Bugesera itself. In the decades before 1994, this region was dry, hot, and sparsely settled. Historians note that many Tutsi families were relocated here in the years around independence, pushed out of other parts of the country. Bugesera became a place where Tutsi lived in large numbers, under pressure and often under threat. There were outbreaks of violence long before the genocide. In 1992, a wave of killings struck this area, a grim warning of what was coming.

One person tried to make the world listen to that warning. Antonia Locatelli was an Italian lay volunteer who had worked in Rwanda for many years. In 1992, she spoke to international media about the killings in Bugesera. Shortly afterwards, she was murdered here in Nyamata. Her grave lies on the grounds of the memorial. Many visitors walk past it without knowing her story. Pause there if you can. Her death shows that the danger was visible, and that some people paid with their lives for naming it.

The genocide began in April 1994, after the president's plane was shot down over Kigali. Within days, the killing spread across the country. In Nyamata, thousands of Tutsi fled to this church. In earlier waves of violence, churches had often been respected as places of refuge. People believed they would be safe here. They brought mattresses, cooking pots, identity cards, and small children. They locked the iron gates behind them. The number commonly cited is about ten thousand people killed in and around this church.

Look now at the entrance. The metal door carries damage from the attack. According to survivor accounts, the attackers used grenades to force their way in. Soldiers and militia, known as the Interahamwe, then killed those inside with guns, grenades, and machetes. The holes above you date from those days. The walls were never repaired or repainted to hide them. That choice was deliberate. The building is kept as evidence. It is meant to show, not to explain away.

The pews are covered with clothing. Thousands of garments lie folded and heaped along the benches. They belonged to the people who died here. You will see the faded colours of wraps and dresses, small shirts, and worn shoes. Up close, the fabric is brown with dust and age. Many stains are darker. Most visitors expect bones or photographs at a memorial. Few expect the clothes to affect them so strongly. A sleeve or a collar is a very human object. It belonged to a body, and to a person with a name.

Near the front stands the altar. The cloth that covers it still carries marks of blood. Personal items lie close by. You may see rosaries, identity cards, and coins. The identity cards matter. Since the colonial period, Rwandan cards had recorded ethnicity. In 1994, that single word on a card could decide whether a person lived or died. Seeing one here, in a church, connects the paperwork of the state directly to the violence. The surviving cards show how the killing was organised, not random.

Below the church is a small crypt. A short set of steps leads down. Inside, you will find a coffin, along with shelves of skulls and bones. A guide will usually explain who rests here, and why. Some skulls show the marks of the weapons that killed them. Take your time on the stairs. The space is narrow and the air is still. Many visitors choose to go down in silence, and that is entirely acceptable. You are welcome to step back outside if you need a moment.

Behind the church are the mass graves. These are long underground vaults, reached by steps, with remains placed in coffins and on shelves. The graves hold the remains of more than forty-five thousand victims from Nyamata and the surrounding area. They were not all killed inside the church. Many died in nearby fields, swamps, and homes during those weeks. Families continue to bring remains here as they are found. The graves are therefore not finished. Burials still take place, especially during the annual commemoration.

That commemoration is called Kwibuka, a Kinyarwanda word meaning to remember. It begins each year on the seventh of April and continues for one hundred days, matching the length of the genocide. During this period, Rwandans gather at memorials across the country. Survivors give testimony, names are read, and candles are lit. Nyamata is one of the central sites for these gatherings. In 2023, this memorial was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, together with three other genocide memorial sites in Rwanda.

The church is no longer used for worship. It has become a place for testimony and learning. Guides here are often from the local community, and some are survivors themselves, or relatives of victims. Listen carefully to them. They can tell you which family came from which hill, and what happened in a particular corner of the building. Their voices carry the details that no plaque can hold. Remember too that Rwandans live with this history every day. Their reconciliation work is ongoing, practical, and difficult.

As you leave, look back at the building. Look at the brick, the iron gate, the roof full of small points of light. Then look out at the town of Nyamata around you. People are buying vegetables, fixing bicycles, and talking on their phones. Life continues here, beside the graves, not in spite of them. Walk slowly back to the road. Notice the red earth under your feet, and the hills on the horizon. The silence of this place stays with many people long after they go.
