The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead, a roller coaster climbs, and the slow clack of its chain carries across the trees. Then comes a rush of wind and a wave of distant screams. Nearer, you might hear an accordion, a fountain, or the hiss of a steam engine. The air smells of waffles, roasted almonds, and cut grass. This is Europa-Park, in the small village of Rust, in the far south-west of Germany.

Rust sits on the flat plain of the Upper Rhine. The river and the French border are only a short drive to the west. To the east, the dark ridges of the Black Forest rise on a clear day. Before the park arrived, this was a quiet farming village. Today it holds one of the largest theme parks in Europe. That contrast is worth keeping in mind as you walk. Just outside the fences, life still moves at a village pace.

The story of the park begins with a family business, not with a theme park. The Mack family of Waldkirch, a town in the Black Forest, built wagons and carriages from the late eighteenth century. Over generations, the workshop moved into fairground rides and showmen's wagons. It became Mack Rides, a company that now builds attractions for parks around the world. So many of the rides around you were designed and built by the same family that owns the ground you stand on.

Europa-Park opened in 1975. Franz Mack and his son Roland Mack wanted a place to show their rides to buyers, and also a place for families to visit. They chose a park beside an old castle estate in Rust. The first season was modest by today's standards. Here is a detail many visitors miss — the park was, in part, a working showroom. Engineers could test new ideas here before selling them abroad.

That castle still stands at the heart of the grounds. It is Balthasar Castle, an old moated residence with pale walls and steep roofs. Its origins reach back several centuries, long before any coaster. The surrounding parkland, with its mature trees and water, set the gentle tone of the early park. Look for the castle as you move between areas. It gives the whole place an anchor in real history, among buildings that were designed to look old.

The big idea of Europa-Park is simple. Each area represents a country or region of Europe. You can walk from Italy to France, from Greece to Scandinavia, in a few minutes. The architecture changes as you go. Painted Italian facades give way to Dutch gables, Swiss chalets, and Portuguese tiles. Restaurants follow the theme too. The idea grew stronger in the years after European borders opened, and many visitors read it as a small, cheerful picture of a shared continent.

Now look up. In the French area, a giant mirrored sphere catches the light. Inside is a coaster that runs largely in darkness. Not far away, the tall steel hill of Silver Star rises above the trees. It opened in 2002 and remains one of the tallest coasters in Europe. When you stand beneath it, the scale becomes physical. You feel the rumble in your chest as the trains drop past.

The park keeps building. In the Icelandic area, Blue Fire launches riders forward at speed, instead of using a slow climb. Close by, Wodan is a wooden coaster with a rough, rattling energy that steel rides do not have. In 2024 the park opened Voltron Nevera, a launched coaster in the Croatian area. Each new ride shows the latest work of the Mack engineers. In a sense, the park is still a showroom, just a much larger one.

Here is a second surprise. Not everything at Europa-Park is about speed. Some of the most loved rides are slow boat trips and dark rides. Families drift past animated scenes, painted backdrops, and soft music. In the Dutch area, a pirate-themed boat ride takes visitors through a harbour town. That ride had to be rebuilt after a large fire in 2018 damaged part of the park. The reopening was an emotional moment for many regular visitors.

The park also has a mascot you will see everywhere. He is a cheerful mouse called Ed Euromaus. Children wave at him, and he appears on signs, on merchandise, and at shows. Around the grounds, you will also find live entertainment. There are ice shows, street performers, and seasonal events. In winter, the park often changes its look with lights, markets, and festive decoration. In autumn, pumpkins and Halloween scenes fill the paths.

Europa-Park is more than a day out for many guests. The resort has several themed hotels, each tied to a part of Europe, from a Spanish castle to a Portuguese monastery style. In 2019 the family opened Rulantica, a large indoor water park next to the main park. Its theme draws on Nordic myths and the sea. Together, these places turn Rust into a destination where people stay for several days, not just a few hours.

It helps to notice the people around you. You will hear German, French, Swiss German, Dutch, and English in the same queue. Many visitors come from France and Switzerland, since both borders are close. Some families have come every year for decades, and parents now bring children who ride the same coasters they once rode. The crowd itself becomes part of the European theme — a mix of languages sharing the same benches and the same ice cream.

Take a quiet moment before you move on. Find a bench by the water near the castle, or in one of the garden corners between areas. Watch how the light falls on the painted facades. Listen to the layers of sound — the coaster chains, the music, the splash of a fountain. All of this stands on old farmland beside a small Rhine village. As you walk to your next ride, notice the castle roof above the trees, and the quiet fields waiting just beyond the gates.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the biggest coasters early before the queues grow.
Plan for two days to include Rulantica or a hotel stay.
Rest near Balthasar Castle when the crowds feel heavy.
Check the show schedule when you arrive in the morning.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks between areas.
