The guide

The story

Start with the walls. Run your hand along the dark stone and you will feel it is rough, pitted, and warm from the sun. Above you, white colonial buildings with steep, tiled roofs stand on clipped green lawns. Somewhere beyond the outer wall, traffic hums along the Makassar waterfront. The air carries salt from the strait. For a moment the fort feels very quiet, almost private, even though a busy port city surrounds it on every side.

You are standing inside Fort Rotterdam, one of the best preserved colonial fortresses in Indonesia. But the name you see on the signs is not its first name. Long before any Dutch ship arrived, this ground belonged to the Sultanate of Gowa. Gowa was a powerful kingdom of the Makassarese people, the seafaring community of southern Sulawesi. Its rulers built a fort here in the sixteenth century, and they called it Ujung Pandang. That name still lives on in the city today.

The first fort was very different from what you see now. Historians generally describe it as a structure of packed earth, a defensive work facing the sea. Later, in the early seventeenth century, Gowa rebuilt the walls in stone. Some of that stone is the dark, porous rock you touched at the start. This was a time when Makassar was one of the great trading ports of the eastern archipelago. Ships came here for spices, rice, and the trade routes that ran through the islands.

That trade is the reason for the conflict that changed this place. The Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials as the VOC, wanted control of the spice trade. Makassar stood in its way as an open port where many nations traded freely. In the 1660s, the company went to war with Gowa, with help from rival local rulers. The fighting was long and hard. It ended with the Treaty of Bongaya in 1667, and Gowa lost much of its power.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The Dutch commander in that war was Cornelis Speelman. After the victory, the fort passed to the company, and it was renamed Rotterdam, after the Dutch city where Speelman was born. So the name over the gate is a souvenir of a military victory. Much of the fort was rebuilt in the Dutch style. The neat rows of buildings around you date from that colonial period.

Now look at the shape of the whole complex. You can see it best on a map or from the top of the walls. Locals have long compared the fort to a turtle. The square body is the shell, the bastions at the corners are the legs, and a projecting section forms the head, pointing towards the sea. Because of this, the fort has a local nickname, the Turtle Fort. People say the shape suits a place that lives on both land and water.

Walk towards one of the bastions and climb up if the path is open. The view opens out over the city and, in the other direction, towards the water. In the past, ships anchored offshore and small boats carried goods to the harbour. Today you see roads, hotels, and new construction. But the line of the walls still follows the old shoreline logic. The fort faced the sea because the sea was the source of both trade and danger.

The second surprise is a story of exile. In the nineteenth century, the Dutch used Makassar as a place to hold political prisoners. The most famous was Prince Diponegoro, a Javanese noble who led a long war against Dutch rule in Java. After his capture, he was sent far from home, and he spent his final years in Makassar, where he died in 1855. Fort Rotterdam is closely linked to his confinement. Indonesians today honour him as a national hero.

Think about that for a moment as you walk the inner lawns. The same walls that once protected a sultan's port later held a man who fought against colonial power. The fort has carried several meanings over the centuries. It was a royal defence, a company headquarters, a prison, and a military post. Each group that held it left a layer behind. You can see some of these layers in the different stones and building styles.

Today several of the old buildings house the La Galigo Museum. The museum takes its name from the La Galigo, a long epic poem of the Bugis people, another major ethnic group of South Sulawesi. The epic tells stories of gods, heroes, and the creation of the world. Inside the museum you can find objects from daily life in the region, such as traditional boats, textiles, tools, and ceremonial items. It is a good place to understand the cultures that lived here before and after the Dutch.

If you visit later in the day, watch how the fort changes. The white walls of the buildings turn gold in the late light. Families arrive to sit on the grass. Students come with sketchbooks, and young couples take photographs in the arched doorways. The fort is a monument, but it is also a public space for the people of Makassar. That everyday use is part of what keeps the place alive and cared for.

When you leave, the waterfront is only a short walk away. Along the shore, the city comes out in the evening to watch the sunset over the strait. Street vendors sell grilled food and sweet snacks. Fishing boats and larger ships move slowly on the horizon. It is easy to imagine the old trading port in this scene. The boats are different now, but the sea is still the reason this city exists.

Before you go, stand still for a minute near the gate. Listen to the traffic outside and the quiet inside. Look again at the dark stone at the base of the walls and the pale colonial plaster above it. Two histories meet in that line... a Makassarese kingdom below and a Dutch company above. As you step back into the street, you carry both of them with you into the city.
