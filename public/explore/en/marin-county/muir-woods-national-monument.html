The guide

The story

Stop for a moment on the boardwalk and breathe in. The air is cool and damp, even on a warm day. It smells of wet bark, soft earth and something faintly sweet, like old wood in a closed room. Overhead, the light comes down in thin gold columns. Around you, the trunks of coast redwoods rise straight up, deep red-brown and soft as felt to the touch. This is Muir Woods, a narrow valley of ancient trees on the slopes of Mount Tamalpais, and it is one of the quietest places you can reach from a major city.

The trees around you are coast redwoods. Their scientific name is Sequoia sempervirens, which means roughly ever-living. They are the tallest living trees on Earth. Here in this valley, the tallest reach well over two hundred feet. Many of the giants along the main trail are several hundred years old. A few are more than a thousand. When you tilt your head back and try to find the tops, you often cannot. The crowns disappear into a green ceiling far above the path.

Why do they grow so well in this one valley? The answer drifts in from the Pacific Ocean. In summer, thick fog rolls over the coastal hills and settles into canyons like this one. The redwoods catch that fog in their needles. Water drips down to the forest floor, almost like a light rain. Some studies suggest the trees can also absorb moisture directly through their leaves. So on a grey morning, the fog is not hiding the forest. It is feeding it.

Listen now for running water. That is Redwood Creek, and it shaped this whole place. The creek cut the valley, and its steep walls helped protect the trees. Early loggers took most of the redwoods around the San Francisco Bay Area in the nineteenth century, to build and rebuild the growing city. This canyon was hard to reach, and much of it survived. That is the first small surprise of Muir Woods. It exists today partly because it was inconvenient.

The second reason is a person. In the early twentieth century, a local politician and businessman named William Kent and his wife, Elizabeth Thacher Kent, bought this land. A water company wanted to dam the creek and flood the valley for a reservoir. To stop that plan, the Kents donated the forest to the federal government. In 1908, President Theodore Roosevelt declared it a National Monument. It was one of the first in the country.

Roosevelt suggested naming the monument after William Kent. Kent refused. He asked instead that it carry the name of John Muir, the Scottish-born naturalist who had spent decades writing about the American wilderness and campaigning to protect it. Muir was famous for his work in places like Yosemite, high in the Sierra Nevada mountains. Kent wanted to honour that work. And so this small coastal canyon carries the name of a man whose fame was made far away, in a different landscape entirely.

As you walk, look down at the base of the larger trees. Many of them stand in circles. These are sometimes called fairy rings. When a redwood is damaged or falls, new shoots often sprout from its roots and its base. Over time, those shoots grow into a ring of trees around the space where the parent once stood. Some of the trees you see in a circle are, in a sense, one family. They may even share the same root system underground.

Now look closely at the bark. On some trunks you will see deep black scars, sometimes a hollow you could step inside. These are marks of old fires. Redwood bark is very thick and full of natural chemicals called tannins. Tannins help the tree resist fire, insects and rot. So a redwood can burn on the outside, heal over, and keep growing for centuries. The hollow trunks are not dead trees. Many of them are still alive, still adding height every year.

Further along the main trail, you reach a part of the forest called Cathedral Grove. This is a designated quiet zone. Visitors are asked to lower their voices, or to stop speaking altogether. Here is a moment from history that many people walk past without knowing. In 1945, delegates who had gathered in San Francisco to create the United Nations came to this grove. They held a memorial here for President Franklin Roosevelt, who had died just weeks earlier. A plaque near the trail marks that gathering.

Take a little time in that silence. Without voices, the forest fills with smaller sounds. Water over stones. A woodpecker somewhere high up. The soft creak of a trunk moving in the wind. Rangers say that in this valley, sound behaves differently. The tall trees and soft ground absorb a great deal of noise. That is why the city, only a short drive away, feels so distant. You are not far from traffic. It simply cannot reach you here.

The forest floor is busy, if you know where to look. Ferns spread in thick green fans along the creek. Clover-like redwood sorrel covers the ground in patches. On damp days, watch for banana slugs. They are bright yellow, sometimes as long as your hand, and they move very slowly across the path. In winter, the creek has a far bigger visitor. Coho salmon and steelhead trout swim up from the ocean to lay their eggs in the gravel. It is one of the few places near the city where they still return.

The forest also holds birds you will rarely see. Northern spotted owls nest in this area. They are shy and mostly active at night, so most visitors never notice them. Overhead, you may hear the harsh call of a Steller's jay, a dark blue bird with a black crest. Back near the entrance, look for a large cross-section of a redwood trunk. Its rings mark dates in human history. It is a simple way to feel how long one of these trees can live.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Put your hand flat against a redwood trunk. The bark feels soft and fibrous, and it is cooler than the air. Look up along the line of the trunk until it vanishes into green. Then look down at the creek, running on as it has for thousands of years. When you walk back toward the entrance, listen as the sounds of people slowly return. And notice how the light changes as the trees thin out behind you.
