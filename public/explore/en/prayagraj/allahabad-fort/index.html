The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere behind these long walls, two great rivers meet. The air near the water feels heavier here, damp and cool in the early morning, warm and dusty by noon. Under your feet, the ground slopes gently toward the banks. The fort's walls rise from that slope in long, steady lines of reddish stone, and they seem to go on further than the eye wants to follow.

This is Allahabad Fort, in the city now officially called Prayagraj. It sits at one of the most sacred places in India: the meeting point of the Ganga and the Yamuna, a confluence known as the Sangam. Hindu tradition adds a third river, the Saraswati, which flows invisibly beneath the other two. That is why pilgrims call this place the Triveni Sangam, meaning the meeting of three rivers. The fort was built to watch over that holy water.

The builder was Akbar, the Mughal emperor, who ruled much of northern India in the second half of the sixteenth century. The Mughals were a dynasty of Central Asian origin. They left behind some of the subcontinent's best-known buildings. Work on this fort began in the 1580s. Akbar also gave the surrounding settlement a new name, Illahabas, which later became Allahabad. Many historians describe this as the largest fort Akbar ever commissioned.

Why here? Think about the rivers as roads. In Akbar's time, boats carried soldiers, grain, cloth and messages along the Ganga and the Yamuna. Whoever held this point of land controlled traffic on both. The site also brought prestige. Millions of pilgrims have come to the Sangam for centuries, and a fort above it made Mughal power visible to every one of them. Strategy and symbolism stood on the same ground.

Here is the first thing most visitors do not expect. Much of the fort is still an active military site. The Indian Army uses large parts of it, so you cannot simply wander through the inner courtyards and palaces. Your experience is shaped by the outside walls, the gateways, and a small number of areas that are open to visitors. This is not a museum frozen in place. It is a working building, and it has been a working building for more than four hundred years.

The most precious object inside is much older than the fort itself. It is a tall, polished stone column known as the Ashoka Pillar. Ashoka was an emperor of the Maurya dynasty who ruled in the third century before the common era. After a violent war, he turned toward Buddhist ideas, and he had messages about moral conduct carved onto pillars across his empire. This column carries some of those messages, in an ancient script called Brahmi.

The surprise is that this one stone carries several layers of history, written by different rulers across nearly two thousand years. Centuries after Ashoka, a long poem of praise was added for Samudragupta, a king of the Gupta dynasty. That inscription is one of the most important sources historians have for his reign. Much later, an inscription linked to the Mughal emperor Jahangir was carved onto the same surface. Many scholars believe the pillar did not originally stand here, and was moved from elsewhere.

Jahangir himself has a story connected to this fort. Before he became emperor, he was Prince Salim, Akbar's eldest son. Around the turn of the seventeenth century, Salim rebelled against his father and set up his own court at Allahabad. For a time, this fort was the base of a prince who wanted the throne sooner rather than later. Father and son later reconciled, and Salim did inherit the empire. But for a few tense years, these walls held a rival court.

Now to the part of the fort that pilgrims seek out. Deep inside, there is a sacred banyan tree called the Akshayavat. The name means the tree that never perishes. In Hindu belief, it survives even the end of the world. Near it is the Patalpuri Temple, an underground shrine. For many devotees, a visit to the Sangam is not complete without paying respects here. In recent years, access to the tree has been opened more widely to pilgrims.

The tree carries old stories, and some are dark. Legend has it that in earlier centuries, some devotees jumped from the sacred tree, believing that dying here would bring release from the cycle of rebirth. A Chinese Buddhist pilgrim named Xuanzang, who travelled through India in the seventh century, wrote about a sacred tree at Prayag and the rituals around it. Whether today's tree is the same one, no one can prove. Locals will tell you, simply, that it has always been here.

After the Mughal age, control of the fort passed through other hands. In the late eighteenth century, it came under the British East India Company. The British used it as a military stronghold, and they changed parts of the interior to suit an army barracks. Some older Mughal structures were altered or lost in that period. So when you look at the fort today, you are looking at Mughal walls with layers of colonial and modern military use behind them.

The best way to feel the scale of this place is from the water. Boatmen at the Sangam take visitors out to the point where the rivers meet. From a small wooden boat, the fort's long wall runs along the bank, low and solid. You may notice that the two rivers often look different. The Ganga can appear paler and sandier, while the Yamuna often looks darker and greener. On a calm day, you can sometimes see the line where they join.

Before you leave, take one more look at the wall in front of you. Notice how the stone changes colour as the light moves across it. Listen for the bells from the riverbank, the boatmen calling, the pilgrims bathing in the cold water. Emperors, princes, soldiers and devotees have all stood near where you are standing. The fort has outlasted most of them. And the two rivers below keep meeting, exactly as they did before any wall was built.

Before you go

Practical tips

Expect most of the fort to be closed; it's an army site.
Take a boat to the Sangam to see the full wall.
Visit early morning for cooler air and softer river light.
Dress modestly if you plan to visit the Akshayavat shrine.
Check access rules before going; they change around major festivals.
