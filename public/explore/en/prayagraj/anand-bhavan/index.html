The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look around. There is the scrape of sandals on a gravel path, the call of a myna somewhere in the trees, and, beyond the gate, the steady horns of Prayagraj traffic. Then look up. A long, two-storey house sits in front of you, its pale walls lined with deep verandas and slender columns. The shade under those verandas feels cooler than the open garden. This is Anand Bhavan, and for a large part of the twentieth century it was one of the most important family homes in India.

The name itself is a good place to begin. Anand Bhavan means, roughly, the house of joy, or the abode of happiness. It was the home of the Nehru family. Motilal Nehru was a wealthy and successful lawyer in this city. His son, Jawaharlal Nehru, became the first Prime Minister of independent India. His granddaughter, Indira Gandhi, later became Prime Minister too. Three generations of one family lived, argued, planned, and grew up inside these walls.

Here is the first surprise that many visitors miss. The house in front of you is not the original Anand Bhavan. Motilal Nehru bought an older mansion nearby around the turn of the twentieth century and gave it this name. Later, he gave that first house to the Indian National Congress, the political party that led the struggle for independence. It was renamed Swaraj Bhavan, which means the house of self-rule. The family then moved into this newer building, and the name Anand Bhavan moved with them.

So you are standing beside two houses that tell one story. Swaraj Bhavan, the older home, is next door. Indira Gandhi was born there in 1917. The newer house, where you are now, became the family residence during the years when the independence movement grew stronger. If you have time, walk between the two buildings. You will feel how close together they are... a short stroll separates the place where a future Prime Minister was born and the place where she grew up.

It helps to know what this city was like in those decades. Prayagraj was long known as Allahabad, and it was a centre of law, education, and politics under British rule. Lawyers, writers, and students filled its courts and colleges. A house like this one, large and well furnished, was a sign of a wealthy professional family. But the Nehrus turned their comfort toward a cause. Over time, the family gave up much of its Western lifestyle and embraced the simple homespun cloth that became a symbol of the freedom movement.

As you go inside, notice how the house is arranged. Rooms open onto the verandas, so light and air can move through the building. This was practical design for a hot northern Indian climate. Many of the rooms are now preserved behind glass or rope barriers. You can look into studies, bedrooms, and sitting rooms that are kept much as they were. The furniture is plain and solid, with dark wood, writing desks, and shelves of books.

The books are worth a closer look. Jawaharlal Nehru was a serious reader and a prolific writer. He wrote several of his best-known works during long periods in British prisons. Some of the letters he wrote to his young daughter from prison were later published as books about world history. When you see his study here, imagine those letters travelling the other way, from a cell to this house, and being read by a girl who would one day lead the country.

This house was also a meeting place. Leaders of the independence movement came here to plan and debate. Mahatma Gandhi stayed at Anand Bhavan on his visits to the city, and a room associated with him is part of the tour. Important discussions of the Congress leadership took place under this roof. Picture the verandas full of people in white cotton, talking late into the evening, while the garden outside grew dark.

Now for the second surprise. This was not only a house of politics. It was also a family home with weddings, celebrations, and grief. Indira married Feroze Gandhi here in 1942, during a tense period of the freedom struggle. The family also knew hardship. Several of its members were arrested and jailed at different times. The house holds both kinds of memory, the joy in its name and the strain of a family that often had someone missing from the dinner table.

In 1970, Indira Gandhi gave Anand Bhavan to the Government of India. It was then opened to the public as a museum. That is why you can walk through these rooms today. Photographs, documents, and personal objects fill the display spaces. Take your time with the photographs especially. They show the family at different ages, and they help you match the faces from history books to the rooms around you.

When you step back outside, look at the gardens. The lawns and trees give the house a quiet setting, even though the city presses close on every side. On the grounds you will also find a planetarium named after Jawaharlal Nehru. It was added after the house became a museum, and it runs its own shows. It may seem unexpected next to a historic home, but Nehru was a strong supporter of science and education, so the link makes sense.

It is also worth remembering where you are in the wider city. Prayagraj is famous across India for the Sangam, the meeting point of the Ganga and Yamuna rivers, where tradition says a third, invisible river also joins them. Pilgrims come in huge numbers for religious festivals there. Anand Bhavan tells a different kind of story, about modern politics and a new nation. Together, the river and this house show two very different sides of the same city.

Before you leave, stand for a moment on the path and turn back toward the house. Look at the long line of columns and the shadows under the verandas. Listen again for the birds in the trees and the traffic beyond the gate. Many of the people who lived here are now names in textbooks. But this was, first of all, a home. As you walk out through the gate and into the street, notice how quickly the noise of the city returns... and how quiet this garden felt by comparison.

Practical tips

Allow extra time to read the photographs and documents.
Wear comfortable shoes for the gardens and gravel paths.
