The guide

The story

Listen first. A train hums somewhere beyond the houses. A bicycle bell rings on a quiet lane. Under your feet there is gravel and low grass, and a raised platform of earth marks where a great hall once stood. There are no tall walls here and no golden roofs. There is only open sky over a suburb between Kyoto and Osaka. Yet for about ten years, this ground held the capital of Japan.

This is Nagaoka-kyō, the imperial capital that history nearly forgot. Its remains lie mostly in the modern cities of Mukō and Nagaokakyō, on the western edge of the Kyoto basin. Most visitors to the region rush past on their way to the temples of Kyoto. That is a shame. The story of this short-lived city explains why Kyoto exists at all, and it is one of the strangest stories in Japanese history.

It begins with Emperor Kanmu. In 784, he moved the court away from Heijō-kyō, the capital at Nara. Historians suggest several reasons. The great Buddhist temples of Nara had grown powerful, and their influence pressed hard on the court. Kanmu also came from a new branch of the imperial line, and a new capital gave him a fresh start. Moving the seat of power was a statement — a clean break with the past.

The location was chosen with care. Look at the land around you. It is low, flat, and close to water. Not far to the south, three rivers meet: the Katsura, the Uji, and the Kizu. Together they flow toward Osaka Bay as the Yodo River. For a capital in the eighth century, this mattered a great deal. Rice, timber, and tax goods could travel by boat from distant provinces almost to the palace gates.

The new city followed a grid plan, modelled on the capitals of Tang China. Straight avenues crossed at right angles. At the northern centre stood the palace compound. Inside it was the Daigokuden, the Great Hall of State, where the emperor appeared for the most solemn ceremonies. South of it lay the Chōdōin, a set of halls where officials gathered for court business. The low platform you may be standing near today marks part of this ceremonial heart.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The city was built fast — remarkably fast. Many scholars believe that buildings were taken apart at an older palace, at Naniwa in present-day Osaka, and carried here by river for reuse. Roof tiles and timbers made the journey upstream. In other words, part of this capital may have been second-hand from the start. It was a practical solution for an emperor in a hurry.

Then, in 785, everything began to go wrong. Fujiwara no Tanetsugu, the trusted official in charge of building the city, was shot with arrows and killed. The investigation reached into the imperial family itself. Prince Sawara, the emperor's younger brother and the crown prince, was accused of involvement. He was stripped of his rank and sent into exile. On the journey, he refused food and died. He always insisted on his innocence.

What followed shaped Japanese belief for centuries. Members of Kanmu's family fell ill and died. Epidemics spread. Floods struck the low ground near the rivers. Court diviners pointed to one cause: the angry spirit of Prince Sawara. The belief in onryō, the vengeful ghosts of people who died wronged, was powerful in this period. The court offered rites and later granted the dead prince the title of emperor, hoping to calm his spirit.

In 794, Kanmu gave up on Nagaoka-kyō. He moved the court a short distance north, to a new city called Heian-kyō, the Capital of Peace and Tranquillity. That name tells you a lot about the fear behind the move. Heian-kyō grew into the city we now call Kyoto, and it stayed the imperial capital for more than a thousand years. So, in a sense, Kyoto was born from the failure of the ground beneath you.

After the court left, the abandoned city faded quickly. Buildings were dismantled once again and carried away. Farmland covered the old avenues. For a long time, Nagaoka-kyō was little more than a name in old chronicles. Some people even doubted that the city had ever been fully built. It took twentieth century archaeology to prove them wrong.

From the 1950s onward, excavations led by local researchers began to find the palace. Diggers uncovered post holes, foundation stones, drainage channels, and roof tiles. They also found mokkan — thin wooden tablets used as labels and memos. Officials wrote on them with ink, recording deliveries of goods and daily business. These small pieces of wood bring the city back to life. Real people worked here, counted rice, and sent messages across the palace.

Today, excavation is still ongoing in many places, because the old capital lies beneath homes, shops, and roads. You may see small signboards on street corners, marking where a gate or a road once stood. The palace site is now a national historic site, kept as a quiet park. Notice how the modern streets around you sometimes run straight in the old grid directions. The plan of the city has not completely disappeared.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the platform of the Great Hall. Face south, as the emperor would have faced. Imagine rows of officials in coloured robes, and the long avenue running toward distant hills. Then let the present return — the train, the houses, the wind across the grass. As you walk away, look for the small markers at your feet. Each one is a quiet reminder that a capital stood here, briefly, before it moved north to become Kyoto.
