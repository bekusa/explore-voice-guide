The guide

The story

Stand at the bus terminal in front of Kyoto Station and look up. Above the taxis and the steady line of travellers, a slim white tower rises against the sky, with a red-and-white crown at the top. In daylight it looks almost chalky, like a candle that has not yet been lit. Around you, buses hiss as their doors open. Rolling suitcases rattle over the paving stones. This is the first view of Kyoto for many visitors, and it is a modern one, not a temple roof.

That tower is Kyoto Tower. It opened in 1964, an important year for Japan. Tokyo hosted the Summer Olympics that autumn, and the first bullet train line began running between Tokyo and Osaka, with a stop right here in Kyoto. The country wanted to show the world that it had recovered from the war. A new tower beside the station, greeting passengers off those fast new trains, fitted that mood very well.

The structure stands about 131 metres tall, counting the office and shop building it sits on. It remains the tallest structure in the city. A number often repeated in Kyoto is that the height matched the city's population at the time, about 1.31 million people. Treat that as a popular explanation, not a confirmed design rule. Either way, the number is easy to remember, and locals like to tell it.

Now look closely at the shaft, and you will notice something unusual. There is no visible lattice of steel beams, and no criss-crossing girders like the frame of the Eiffel Tower. The tower is built as a monocoque, a word from aircraft and ship design. It means the outer skin carries the weight itself. Curved steel plates were welded together into a hollow cylinder, much like the hull of a ship. Shipbuilding methods helped to build this tower.

The architect was Mamoru Yamada, a leading figure of Japanese modernism. He also designed the Nippon Budokan in Tokyo, the martial arts hall built for the same 1964 Olympics. Yamada's idea for Kyoto was gentle and a little poetic. Kyoto has no sea, so he imagined a lighthouse for the city. It would be a beacon to light the old rooftops at night and guide travellers home. Many people also see a candle in the shape, a calm Buddhist image in a city of temples.

Here is the surprise that most visitors miss. When it was planned, many people in Kyoto did not want this tower. Writers, scholars, and residents argued that a modern tower would spoil a skyline shaped by wooden temples, tiled roofs, and low hills. The debate was loud and public. The tower was built anyway. But that argument helped push Kyoto, over the following decades, toward strict height limits and landscape rules that still shape what you see from the top.

Inside the base building, the tower is a busy, everyday place. The lower floors hold shops and a food hall, where office workers buy lunch, students share snacks, and travellers wait for their trains. You can smell grilled food and hot broth near the counters. A hotel occupies part of the building. Many visitors walk past it every day without ever going up. For them it is simply a landmark that means, I am almost at the station.

An elevator carries visitors up to the observation deck, which sits about one hundred metres above the ground. The ride is short. When the doors open, you enter a round room wrapped in glass, and the whole city lies around you. On a clear day you can turn in a full circle and follow the ring of green hills that surrounds Kyoto on three sides. The city sits in a basin, and from here that shape becomes very easy to understand.

This is the second surprise. From above, Kyoto looks low and flat. There are almost no skyscrapers, because the height rules keep most buildings short. The streets run in a neat grid, a plan that dates back to the eighth century, when the city was laid out as an imperial capital on a Chinese model. If you follow the straight lines with your eyes, you are looking at a street pattern more than a thousand years old, under modern roofs.

Look north and a little to the east for the large tiled roofs of temples close to the station. The deep, heavy roofs rise above the smaller houses around them. Farther away, the wooded slopes of the eastern hills hold more temples among the trees. To the south, rail lines and the long body of the station stretch out below. The deck provides telescopes for a closer view, and they help you pick out the details.

The station itself is worth a glance from up here. The present Kyoto Station building, a huge glass-and-steel structure, came much later than the tower. It brought its own round of public debate. So you are standing in one modern landmark and looking down at another. Both of them raised the same question for the city. How should a place with so much old architecture make room for new buildings? Kyoto is still answering that question today.

As evening comes, the tower changes. Lights come on along the shaft and the crown, and the white candle now glows. Lighting colours sometimes change for seasons or special events. From the streets below, people use the glowing top to find their way back to the station after dinner. In this way, Yamada's lighthouse idea still works, in a quiet and practical form. It guides tired travellers through the dark, much as he imagined.

When you come back down and step out onto the pavement, pause for a moment. Listen to the station announcements, the hum of buses, and the soft chime of crossing signals. Then look up again at the smooth white shaft above you. It is easy to dismiss it as just a tower beside a train station. But it carries a story about recovery, argument, and a city learning to live with change. Now you know where to look... and where the light will be tonight.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a clear day to see the surrounding hills.
Go up near sunset for daylight and city lights.
Use the deck telescopes to find temple roofs.
Come here first to understand Kyoto's street grid.
Eat at the base food hall while waiting for trains.
