The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel first. It shifts under your feet with a soft, dry sound, and then the path turns to stepping stones. Each stone sits slightly apart from the next. You cannot rush here. The garden sets the pace of your walk, and it has done this for close to four hundred years. Somewhere ahead, water moves against a stone bank. A breeze passes through the pines. You are in the western part of Kyoto, close to the Katsura River, and the city already feels far away.

This is Katsura Imperial Villa. It was created in the seventeenth century by an imperial prince, Prince Toshihito, the first head of a princely family called the Hachijō line. He began the work as a retreat, a place for poetry, moon-viewing, and quiet gatherings with friends. After his death, his son, Prince Toshitada, expanded the buildings and the garden. So what you see is the work of two generations. Father and son each added their own ideas, and the result still feels like one continuous thought.

Why here, on the edge of the river? The Katsura area had old literary associations. It appears in The Tale of Genji, the great eleventh-century novel of court life, often called one of the world's earliest novels. Prince Toshihito was known as a devoted reader of that book. Many writers believe that scenes and moods from the novel shaped the design of this villa. Keep that in mind as you walk. This garden was made by people who thought in poems.

Notice how the garden hides itself at the start. Near the beginning of the route, a single pine tree stands in front of the view of the pond. It blocks the water on purpose. This tree is known as the Sumiyoshi pine. Japanese garden makers call this technique concealment. You do not receive the whole picture at once. Instead, the garden gives it to you in pieces, one turn of the path at a time. The surprise is part of the design.

The heart of the garden is a large pond with an irregular shoreline. Small islands sit in the water, connected by bridges of earth, wood, and stone. This is a stroll garden. That means it is designed to be experienced while walking a loop around the water, not from a single seat. At every bend, the scene changes. A teahouse appears across the water, then disappears behind a hill. A bridge comes into view, then a lantern. The garden works like a slow film.

Look down at the paths as you move. They are not all the same. Some are made of neatly cut rectangular stones laid in straight lines. Others mix cut stone with rough natural stone. Others are only natural stones, placed loosely. These three styles follow a traditional idea of formal, semi-formal, and informal. The formal paths lead toward the main buildings. The informal ones lead toward the rustic teahouses. Your feet are reading the garden, even when your eyes are not.

On one stretch of shore, the garden imitates the sea. Flat, rounded stones cover a gentle slope down to the water, like a pebble beach. At its tip stands a small stone lantern, placed to suggest a lighthouse at the end of a cape. Nearby, a thin spit of land with stones and pines recalls Amanohashidate, a famous sandbar on the coast of northern Kyoto Prefecture. Here, the whole Japanese coastline has been folded into a few steps of garden.

Now look across to the main buildings. There are three connected halls, known as the Old Shoin, the Middle Shoin, and the New Palace. A shoin is a formal residential building with tatami mats and sliding panels. Here, the three halls do not stand in a straight line. Each one steps back diagonally from the last. People often compare this pattern to a flock of geese in flight. The arrangement lets every room open to the garden, the light, and the breeze.

At the front of the Old Shoin, a bamboo platform extends toward the pond. It was built for moon-viewing. On autumn nights, guests could sit here and watch the moon rise over the water, and see it again as a reflection on the surface. The buildings are raised on posts above the ground. This kept the floors dry when the river flooded, and it also lifts the rooms slightly, as if they float above the moss.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In 1933, the German architect Bruno Taut visited Katsura. He was a leading figure of modern architecture in Europe. He wrote about the villa with deep admiration, and he praised its simple lines, clean surfaces, and honest materials. His writing helped bring Katsura to the attention of modern architects around the world. So this seventeenth-century retreat became a reference point for twentieth-century design. Its plain grid of panels looked surprisingly modern to Western eyes.

Around the pond stand several teahouses, each with its own character. The largest is Shōkintei, whose name refers to the sound of wind in the pines. Inside, a bold checkerboard of blue and white paper covers the alcove wall and sliding doors. It looks almost like a modern painting. Another teahouse, Shōkatei, sits on a small hill like a mountain rest stop. Others face the moon or the water. Each one frames the garden in a different way.

As you walk, notice what the buildings are made of. Unpainted wood. Thatch and bark. Earthen walls. Paper screens. Bamboo. Nothing here tries to shine. The materials are left close to their natural state, and they age with the seasons. Outside the villa grounds, along the road, there is a special fence made from living bamboo, bent and woven together while the plants still grow. Even the boundary of the villa is something alive.

Today, the Imperial Household Agency cares for the villa. Visits are by guided tour along a fixed route, so you follow the path the garden makers intended. You cannot enter the buildings, but the route lets you see them from many angles. Pay attention to the gardeners if you see them at work. They sweep moss, clip pines by hand, and remove fallen needles one by one. Much of the beauty here comes from this patient, daily care.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and stand still. Look at the water and find one reflection — a lantern, a roof, a pine branch. Watch how it moves when the wind touches the pond. Then listen again to the stones under your feet. Four centuries ago, a prince who loved poetry walked a path very close to this one. As you go, notice how the garden still decides what you see next, and when.

Before you go

Practical tips

Reserve a guided tour in advance through the Imperial Household Agency.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven stepping stones and gravel.
Bring your passport or photo ID for the entrance check.
Walk slowly and pause at each bend of the path.
Visit in autumn or after rain for richer moss colours.
