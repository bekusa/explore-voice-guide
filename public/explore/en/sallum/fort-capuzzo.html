The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the plateau with nothing to stop it. Fine grit lifts from the ground and catches against low walls of pale stone. The light here is hard and white, and the horizon is almost perfectly flat in every direction. Underfoot, the earth is a crust of sand, limestone chips and scattered rubble. This is Fort Capuzzo, or what remains of it. It is a quiet place now, but for a few years in the middle of the twentieth century it was one of the most fought-over corners of North Africa.

First, a little geography, because it explains everything. Sallum sits on the Egyptian side of the border, at the foot of a long escarpment above the Mediterranean. Capuzzo lies a short distance to the west, just across the frontier in Libya, up on the high desert plateau. That position matters. Whoever held this ground could watch the coastal road and the passes that climb up from the sea. In desert warfare, a few metres of height and a reliable track were worth more than almost anything else.

The fort was built by the Italians during their colonial rule in Libya, in the first half of the twentieth century. Italy had taken Libya from the Ottoman Empire in 1911, and for years afterwards it fought a long and brutal campaign against local resistance. The most famous resistance leader was Omar Mukhtar, a Senussi religious teacher who became a guerrilla commander. Forts like this one were part of the Italian effort to control the frontier and stop fighters and supplies from crossing between Egypt and Libya.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Along this border, the Italian authorities built a long barrier of barbed wire, stretching for a great distance south from the coast into the desert. Its purpose was to cut the resistance off from support on the Egyptian side. Capuzzo stood near the northern end of that line. So before it became a Second World War battlefield, this place was already a symbol of colonial control. The walls carry both stories, one layered on top of the other.

Then came 1940. Italy entered the Second World War in June of that year, and the border between Italian Libya and British-controlled Egypt suddenly became a front line. Within days, British armoured cars raided across the frontier. Capuzzo was one of the first Italian positions they struck and captured. It was an early and surprising blow. The fort that had been built to hold the frontier was taken almost before the war in the desert had properly begun.

What followed was a pattern that defined this whole region. The fort changed hands again and again. Armies advanced across the desert, stretched their supply lines too far, and then fell back. Italian forces returned. Later, German troops under Erwin Rommel arrived to support them, and Capuzzo was drawn into some of the hardest fighting along the border. Each time the front moved, this small patch of plateau was crossed, shelled and occupied once more.

In 1941, the British launched two operations in this area, known as Brevity and Battleaxe. Both aimed to push back the German and Italian forces near the frontier. Capuzzo was among the objectives. Nearby, the steep road up from Sallum at Halfaya Pass became so deadly that British soldiers gave it a grim nickname, Hellfire Pass. Neither operation achieved its goal. Tanks burned on this open ground, and the fort's walls took yet more damage. Later that year, a larger offensive finally moved the fighting west.

Now look around you and imagine what soldiers saw. There is very little cover. No trees, no hills, just stone and sand. In summer, the heat during the day is fierce, and nights can turn surprisingly cold. Water was always scarce. Dust got into engines, food and eyes. Many accounts from the desert war describe flies, sandstorms and the constant problem of finding your way across a landscape with so few landmarks. A fort, even a ruined one, was a fixed point you could navigate by.

That is another quiet surprise of this place. Its military value was never really its walls. Against modern artillery and aircraft, stone walls offered limited protection. Its value was its location — the crossing of tracks, the view over the frontier, the access to the coast road. When you stand here, try to read the land instead of the ruins. Notice the faint lines of old tracks in the ground. Notice how far you can see. That openness is the real reason so many people fought for this spot.

The ruins today are modest. You will see low, broken walls, piles of fallen stone and the rough outline of the old enclosure. Some sections still stand at a little above head height. Others are only foundations. The stone is a pale, sandy colour that blends into the desert. In places, the surfaces show scars and chips, though it is hard to tell which marks come from war and which come from decades of wind, sun and scavenging for building material.

Be aware that this is a sensitive border zone. Access can be restricted, and the security situation along the Egyptian and Libyan frontier changes. It is not a managed heritage site with signs, paths or facilities. If you have reached it, you have likely done so with local guidance and the right permissions. Old battlefields in this region can also still hold dangerous debris from the war, so stay on clear ground and do not pick up or move any metal objects you find.

Back on the Egyptian side, Sallum keeps a quieter kind of memory. The town has a Commonwealth War Graves cemetery, where soldiers who died in the fighting along this frontier are buried. Many came from Britain, India, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and elsewhere. Visiting it after Capuzzo connects the open, empty plateau with the individual names. The desert war is often told as a story of generals and tanks. Here, it becomes a story of people, many of them very young.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and let the place settle. Feel the wind again, the same wind that has moved across this plateau for centuries, before forts, before wires, before any army arrived. Look at the line where pale stone meets pale sand, and notice how hard it is to see where one ends. In time, the desert may take these walls back completely. For now, they remain a small marker on a very large and very quiet horizon.
