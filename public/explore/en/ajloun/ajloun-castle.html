The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the wind moves steadily across the hilltop and carries the smell of pine and dry earth from the forests below. Under your feet, the path turns from soil to worn limestone, polished smooth by centuries of boots and sandals. Ahead, the walls rise in pale, honey-coloured blocks. They seem to grow straight out of the rock. This is Ajloun Castle, and before you learn a single date, notice how it sits. It is not hidden. It was built to see everything, and to be seen.

The castle stands on a summit known as Mount Auf, in the green hills of northern Jordan. On a clear day, the land falls away to the west toward the Jordan Valley. Beyond the valley, the hills of the far bank fade into haze. Locals often call the fortress Qal'at ar-Rabad, which means the castle of the hilltop or the settlement on the height. The name is simple. The position is everything. From these walls, a lookout could watch roads, valleys and villages in nearly every direction.

The fortress was built in the 1180s by Izz al-Din Usama, one of the commanders who served Saladin. Saladin was the Muslim sultan who united Egypt and Syria and fought the Crusader states, the kingdoms that European knights had founded in the region. At that time, Crusader castles stood not far away. Ajloun was part of the answer. It was a strong point that could block raids, guard the routes and hold the surrounding countryside firmly in Ayyubid hands. The Ayyubids were the dynasty that Saladin founded.

There was another reason to build here — something buried in these hills. The Ajloun region had iron ore. Iron meant weapons, tools and armour, and whoever held the mines held real power. A fortress on this summit protected that resource. It also watched the trade and pilgrim routes that linked Damascus in the north with lands further south. So this was never only a military building. It was also a guard post for an economy.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Ajloun was one link in a chain of communication. Historians describe a system of signal fires and carrier pigeons that connected fortresses across the region. A fire lit on one hilltop could be seen on the next, and the message would pass onward, summit by summit. In this way, news could travel between Damascus and Cairo far faster than any rider on a horse. Look out from the walls and imagine a flame on the horizon at night, answering this one.

As you approach the entrance, look down. A deep ditch, cut into the rock, surrounds much of the castle. This is the moat. It was dry, not filled with water. Its purpose was to keep attackers and their siege machines away from the walls. The stone that came out of this ditch was very likely used to build the walls themselves. So the defence and the building material came from the same digging. The hill was turned into its own fortress.

The castle you see today is larger than the first design. In the early thirteenth century, a governor named Aybak expanded it. He added towers and strengthened the gateway. Walk through the entrance and notice how the passage turns rather than running straight. A bent entrance was a common defensive trick. Attackers could not charge directly inside, and defenders above could strike at anyone who slowed down at the corners. Look up as you pass and you may see openings in the stone overhead.

Inside, the castle becomes a maze of vaulted halls, narrow stairways and dim corridors. The light changes quickly here. One moment you are in bright sun, the next in cool shadow under heavy stone arches. Run your hand along the walls and feel the tool marks in the blocks. Notice the narrow arrow slits. From the inside they open wide, so an archer could move and aim. From the outside they are only thin cuts, almost impossible to hit. Stand at one and look through its frame at the valley.

The castle's story did not end with the Crusades. In 1260, the Mongols swept into the region and damaged the fortress. Soon after, the Mamluks took control. The Mamluks were a dynasty of soldier-rulers based in Egypt, and they repaired the castle and kept it in use. Centuries later, in the Ottoman period, a garrison was stationed here again. Each era left its own repairs. If you look closely, you can sometimes see where newer stonework meets older courses, the joints slightly different in colour and finish.

Nature also left its mark. Strong earthquakes struck this region in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and they cracked walls and brought down parts of the structure. Some sections you walk through have been restored in modern times by Jordan's Department of Antiquities. Other parts remain broken and open to the sky. That mix is part of the experience. You can stand in a room with a complete vaulted ceiling, then step into a space where the roof is simply gone and the clouds move overhead.

There is also a local legend about this hilltop. Some people say a Christian monastery once stood here, before the castle, and that a monk gave his name to the area. It is a popular tale, and it is told with affection. But it is a story, not a firm historical record. What is certain is that people have valued this summit for a very long time. A place with this much sky and this much view rarely stays empty.

Make your way up to the higher towers if you can. The climb is uneven in places, so take your time on the steps. From the top, the whole region opens out. Olive groves cover the lower slopes in grey-green rows. Pine and oak forests darken the hills to the north. Small towns spread along the ridges. In spring, the hillsides turn green and wildflowers appear between the stones. In late afternoon, the low sun warms the limestone to a deep gold.

The town of Ajloun lies below, and its life drifts up to you. You may hear a call to prayer rising from a mosque, a car horn on the winding road, or children playing somewhere out of sight. The castle once looked down on this valley with suspicion, watching for enemies. Today it looks down on ordinary days — markets, school runs, families picnicking in the forest. The walls have not moved. Only their purpose has changed.

Before you leave, stop at the edge of one of the walls and stand still for a moment. Feel the wind again, the same wind that pushed against the faces of guards eight centuries ago. Look at the stone beside your hand, cut from this hill and stacked by people whose names are mostly lost. As you walk back down the path, turn around once. Watch how the castle settles back into the summit, pale against the sky, still holding its watch over the valley.
