The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Out on South Main Street, a trolley bell rings, and the wheels grind softly on the rails. Then you step through the door, and the street noise drops away. In its place come quiet guitar notes from a listening station somewhere ahead, a voice that sounds as if it was recorded in a small room long ago. The light is low and warm. This is the Blues Hall of Fame, and it is a place built for listening.

The Hall of Fame itself is older than this building. The Blues Foundation, a Memphis organisation that supports blues music around the world, began naming inductees in 1980. For more than three decades, though, the honour had no permanent home. The names lived on paper, in ceremonies, and in the memories of fans. Only in 2015 did the museum you are standing in open its doors. So when you walk these rooms, you are walking through an honour roll that waited a long time for walls.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The Hall of Fame does not only honour musicians. It also inducts people who worked behind the scenes, such as producers, record label owners, radio presenters, and writers. It inducts individual recordings too, both single songs and full albums. It even honours books and articles about the blues. The idea is simple. A music tradition survives because of singers and players, yes, but also because someone pressed the records, played them on the radio, and wrote the history down.

As you move through the galleries, look at the touchscreen stations. They are the heart of this museum. Each inductee has a profile, and many profiles include music you can play. Take your time here. Choose a name you know, and then choose one you have never heard. A short story on a screen, followed by a few bars of a song, often stays with you longer than any long panel of text on a wall.

The names on these walls cover a long road. You will find early singers like Bessie Smith, who filled theatres in the 1920s with a voice that needed no microphone. You will find the Mississippi Delta guitarist Robert Johnson, whose small number of recordings shaped generations of players. And you will find the electric masters from Chicago, like Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf. Both men grew up in Mississippi, and both carried the sound of the rural South north to the big city.

Why is this museum in Memphis, and not Chicago or somewhere in Mississippi? Look at a map, and the answer is clear. Memphis sits at the top of the Mississippi Delta, the flat, fertile farmland where so much of this music began. For generations, people from the Delta came to Memphis for work, for trade, and for music. Highway 61 runs south from here, straight into that farmland. In a sense, the blues came up the road and arrived at this city.

Memphis also has a special claim on the word itself. W. C. Handy, a bandleader and composer, worked here in the early twentieth century. He wrote down and published blues songs, and one of his early hits was called the Memphis Blues. Later he became known as the Father of the Blues. He did not invent the music. Ordinary people across the South were already singing it. But he helped bring it to printed sheet music, and from there to a much wider audience.

Another name you will meet here is B. B. King. As a young man, he came to Memphis from Mississippi and played on Beale Street, a few minutes from where you stand. He also worked as a radio presenter in this city. Over a long career, he became one of the best-known blues guitarists in the world. His story shows something important about Memphis. The city was not only a place where blues was played. It was a place where careers began.

Notice the objects in the cases as you walk. Museums like this one often show instruments, stage clothing, records, photographs, and posters. Look closely at the wear on them. A guitar from a working musician usually shows its life. There may be scratches near the strings, faded paint, or small repairs. Stage clothes show seams, sweat stains, and patches. These marks are honest. They remind you that the blues was a working life, often played night after night in small clubs for very little money.

Now think about where this building stands. Just across the street is the National Civil Rights Museum, built around the Lorraine Motel. That is where Martin Luther King Junior was killed in 1968. The two museums face each other, and the closeness matters. Many of the artists honoured here lived under segregation, the system of laws that kept Black and white Americans apart in the South. Their music grew in that world. It carried hardship, humour, faith, and anger, often all in the same song.

Here is a second surprise. The Blues Foundation is not only about the past. Each year it runs a large competition called the International Blues Challenge. Bands and solo players come to Memphis from across the United States and from other countries. They perform in clubs along Beale Street, and judges choose winners. The Foundation also presents the Blues Music Awards for current artists. So while this museum looks back, the organisation behind it also works to find the next generation of players.

The neighbourhood around you is worth a slow walk too. This part of South Main is an arts district, with older brick buildings, galleries, small restaurants, and painted murals on the walls. The trolley runs along the street, and the Mississippi River is only a short distance to the west. In the evening, the light turns gold on the brick. It is a good area for walking after your visit, when the music from the listening stations is still in your head.

Before you leave, go back to one profile that caught your attention. Listen to the song again, all the way through this time. Pay attention to the simple pattern of many blues songs. A line is sung, then repeated, and then answered by a third line. Often a guitar or harmonica replies between the lines, like one voice responding to another. Once you hear that pattern, you will start to notice it in rock, soul, and country music too.

When you step back out onto South Main, listen again. You will hear the trolley, cars, maybe a speaker playing music from a nearby doorway. Look across the street at the motel balcony, and then up and down this row of old brick buildings. Much of American popular music has roots in the people whose names are inside these walls. Carry one of those names with you as you walk. Somewhere in this city tonight, someone is probably playing their songs.
