The guide

The story

Feel the slope under your feet as you climb toward the gate. The cobbles are worn smooth, and the stone walls ahead are dark with damp and age. On a grey Lancashire morning, the air carries the smell of wet grass and old sandstone. Somewhere below, the River Lune runs toward the sea. Stand still for a moment. You are on Castle Hill, the high ground that people have chosen to hold for nearly two thousand years.

The story begins long before any castle. The Romans built a fort on this hilltop, and they chose it for the same reasons later rulers did. From here you can watch the river crossing and the land around it. Traces of Roman walls survive on the slope near the castle and the priory. So when you look at this site, you are looking at layers. Roman soldiers, Norman lords, medieval kings, and Victorian prisoners all stood on this same rise.

After the Norman Conquest, the new rulers raised a castle here to control the north-west. The great square keep at its heart has Norman origins, although it was altered many times over the centuries. Look at the stonework and you can see the changes. Some blocks are rough and weathered. Others are sharper and newer, added during later repairs. A castle like this was never finished. Each generation patched it, rebuilt it, and adapted it to new needs.

Now turn to the great gatehouse in front of you. Most people call it the John of Gaunt Gateway. John of Gaunt was a powerful medieval nobleman and Duke of Lancaster, the son of one king and the father of another. But here is the first surprise. The gateway was actually built after his death, in the early fifteenth century, under his son, King Henry the Fourth. The statue of John of Gaunt above the arch is even later, added in the nineteenth century.

That link to the Dukes of Lancaster still matters today. The castle belongs to the Duchy of Lancaster, an estate that has passed down with the Crown for centuries. The reigning monarch holds the title Duke of Lancaster, whether the monarch is a king or a queen. In this part of England, you may even hear an old loyal toast to the monarch as Duke of Lancaster. It is a small reminder that this building still carries living tradition, not only memory.

For most of its life, this castle was less about war and more about law. It served as a court and a prison for hundreds of years. And here is the second surprise. That role did not end in the distant past. The prison inside these walls only closed in 2011. People were held here within living memory. Even now, the Crown Court still sits inside the castle, so justice is still done on this hill.

The most famous trial held here took place in 1612. A group of people from the Pendle area of Lancashire were accused of witchcraft. They are remembered today as the Pendle Witches. The trial drew on fear, local rivalries, and the testimony of a young child. Several of the accused were found guilty and hanged. Today historians see the case as a warning about panic and injustice. For many visitors, it is the reason they come to Lancaster at all.

The castle has held other well-known prisoners too. George Fox, one of the founders of the Quakers, a Christian group known for silent worship and plain living, was imprisoned here in the seventeenth century. Catholic priests were also tried here during periods of religious persecution, and some were executed. Locals remember them as the Lancaster Martyrs. The walls around you have seen many people suffer for what they believed. That weight is part of what you feel when you walk through these courtyards.

Lancaster once had a grim nickname — the Hanging Town. The castle saw many executions over the centuries, more than most courts in England outside London. One part of the walls, often called Hanging Corner, was where public executions took place in later years. Crowds would gather on the slope below to watch. It is hard to imagine that scene now, with walkers and families moving quietly across the grass. But the place itself has not changed very much.

If you join a guided tour, you will see the Shire Hall, one of the castle's most striking rooms. It was designed in the Gothic Revival style, a later imitation of medieval architecture with pointed arches and tall lines. The walls carry hundreds of painted heraldic shields. Each one belongs to a monarch, a constable, or a high sheriff linked to Lancashire. The colours are bright against the pale stone. It feels less like a prison and more like a grand chapter house.

The tour also leads through the old courtroom and the cells. Guides often show the dock where prisoners stood, and some tell the story of branding as a punishment in earlier times. Down in the cells, the air turns cool and still. The doors are heavy and the light is low. Standing inside, even for a minute, gives you a sense of how small life became for the people kept here. Many visitors find this the most memorable part of the visit.

Before you leave, walk around to the far side of the castle. Lancaster Priory stands just beside it, an old church on the same hilltop. Between the two buildings, the paths open onto views over the river and the city. On a clear day you can see toward the hills and the coast. This is a good place to slow down. The castle looks different from every angle, and the outer walls show how much the building has grown.

So take one last look at the gateway, with its towers and its quiet statue above the arch. Listen to the sounds around you — the footsteps on the cobbles, the rooks in the trees, perhaps a church bell from the priory. This hill has been a fort, a castle, a prison, and a court. It is still a working building today. As you walk back down the slope, notice how the city of Lancaster spreads out below, still shaped by the hill above it.
