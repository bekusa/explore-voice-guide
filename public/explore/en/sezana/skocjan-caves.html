The guide

The story

Before you see anything, you hear it. A low, steady roar rises from somewhere below your feet. The air turns cool and damp, and it smells of wet limestone and moss. Your eyes adjust slowly to the dark. Then the sound takes shape. It is a river, running through the inside of the earth.

Welcome to the Škocjan Caves, a short drive from the town of Sežana in southwestern Slovenia. This is limestone country, a high plateau called the Kras. The word may sound unfamiliar, but you probably know it already. Geologists around the world borrowed this region's name for a whole type of landscape: karst. That word means land where water dissolves rock and carves out sinkholes, hidden streams and caves. In a real sense, you are standing in the place that named the science.

In 1986, UNESCO added these caves to its World Heritage List. They were among the first natural sites in this part of Europe to receive that recognition. The reason is simple to state and hard to take in. Here, a whole river disappears into the ground. It cuts one of the largest known underground canyons in Europe, and it leaves behind halls so large they hold their own weather.

That river is the Reka. Its name simply means river in Slovenian. It rises in the hills to the southeast and flows across the surface for many kilometres. Then, at the village of Škocjan, it meets the limestone and drops out of sight. Underground, it travels a long and partly unmapped route. Most of the water is believed to rise again near the Adriatic coast in Italy, where it flows out as the Timavo springs.

Most visits begin in the Silent Cave, and the name fits. This dry, older passage no longer carries the river. Instead, it holds the slow work of dripping water over very long periods. Stalactites hang from the ceiling and stalagmites rise to meet them. Some columns are thicker than a person is tall. Guides often point out formations with nicknames, like the Organ and the Giants. Listen for the drip of water. Each drop leaves behind a tiny film of calcite, the mineral that builds these shapes.

Then the path turns, and the roar comes back. You are entering the Murmuring Cave. This is the moment most visitors remember. The passage opens into an enormous canyon. Far below, the Reka runs over boulders, white and loud in the lamplight. The walls climb up into darkness, beyond where the lights can reach. People who expected a tunnel suddenly find themselves on a narrow path inside a gorge.

The path crosses the canyon on Cerkvenik Bridge. It hangs high above the water, at a height often given as about forty-five metres. Pause here if you can. Look down at the river, then up into the black ceiling. Here is a detail many visitors miss. You may notice old paths and steps cut into the rock below you. They are the remains of nineteenth-century trails, and early explorers once used them by candlelight.

Those explorers deserve a moment. Local people knew the caves for centuries, but systematic exploration began in the nineteenth century. Teams of cavers and engineers worked under very hard conditions. They pushed upstream on rafts and ropes and cut footholds into slippery walls. They often worked in near total darkness. One of the vast chambers downstream is named Martel's Hall. It honours Édouard-Alfred Martel, a French pioneer of cave science. It is counted among the largest underground chambers in Europe.

Here is the second surprise. This river does not always behave. After heavy rain, the Reka can swell far beyond its normal flow. The underground passages cannot drain the water fast enough. In major floods, the water inside the canyon has risen by dozens of metres. Look closely at the walls and you may see the marks. Layers of mud, clean bands of rock and debris lie wedged high above the river. The water has reached those heights before.

The caves also hold a long human story. Archaeologists have found traces of people in and around this area going back to prehistoric times. Some finds are believed to be offerings, left at the place where the river vanished into the underworld. It is easy to imagine why. To early people, this cave may have looked like a door between worlds. The roar, the darkness and the disappearing water would have felt like something sacred.

The experience does not end in darkness. Eventually the passage opens to daylight in a huge collapsed sinkhole. Long ago, the roof of a cave chamber fell in. It left a steep basin open to the sky. Here, the light turns green. Ferns and mosses grow on the damp walls, and birds call from the trees above. The cool cave air meets the warmer air of the surface, and the change is something you can feel on your skin.

Above you, the small village of Škocjan sits on the rim of the gorges. Its stone houses and church tower stand at the edge of a sheer drop. The village takes its name from Saint Cantianus, an early Christian martyr. The whole area is protected as a regional park. The underground river and its wetlands have also received international recognition for their importance to wildlife. Walking trails around the rim show the gorges from above, and they are worth the extra time.

So take a moment before you leave. Listen once more for the river. Even up here, on a quiet day, you may still hear it moving somewhere below the ground. Notice the grey stone underfoot, the thin soil and the twisted trees. All of it sits on top of hollow rock. As you walk back toward the road, remember that the Reka is still travelling in the dark, on its long way to the sea.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer; the cave stays cool all year.
Wear sturdy shoes with grip for wet, sloping paths.
Expect many stairs and a long walk underground.
Visits inside the caves are by guided tour only.
Follow your guide's rules on photography inside the caves.
