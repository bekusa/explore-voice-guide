The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through dry teak leaves. Somewhere a bird calls, and the sound comes back off the stone. Under your feet the path is rough sandstone, worn smooth in places by countless footsteps. Above you, enormous boulders lean together like the walls of a half-built house. These rocks have given shelter to people for a very long time. Look closely at the stone and you may see faint marks in red and white. They were made by human hands.

You are standing in the Bhimbetka rock shelters, in the Raisen district of Madhya Pradesh, in central India. The site lies in the foothills of the Vindhya range, a long chain of low hills that crosses the middle of the country. Here, wind and water have carved the sandstone into overhangs, caves and hollows. Archaeologists have recorded hundreds of these shelters spread across several hills. Many of them hold paintings. Together, they form one of the longest records of human life anywhere in South Asia.

First, the name. Locals connect Bhimbetka with Bhima, one of the five heroic brothers in the Mahabharata, the great Indian epic. According to the popular tale, the name means something like the sitting place of Bhima. The story says that the giant hero rested here during the brothers' years of exile in the forest. Nobody can prove this, of course. But the legend shows how people in this region have long seen these rocks as a special place.

For modern science, the story begins in the 1950s. The Indian archaeologist Vishnu Shridhar Wakankar was travelling through the region by train. From the window he noticed rock formations that reminded him of painted shelters he had seen in Europe. He came back to explore. What he found changed the study of prehistoric India. Excavations over the following decades uncovered stone tools and other traces of people living here across many thousands of years.

Here is the first surprise. The paintings are only part of the story. Under the floors of some shelters, archaeologists found layer after layer of human activity. The deepest layers contain stone tools from the Old Stone Age, the earliest period of human tool-making. Above them come later tools, then pottery, then signs of the historic period. In other words, people kept coming back to the same rocks, generation after generation. For an archaeologist, that unbroken sequence is as precious as any painting.

Now turn to the walls. The most common colours are red and white. Red came from minerals rich in iron, such as red ochre. White probably came from lime or similar minerals. Some paintings also use green and yellow, though these are rarer. The artists likely mixed the powder with water or plant material, then applied it with fingers, fibre brushes or chewed twigs. The colours survive best where the overhang protects them from rain and direct sun.

Look at the subjects. Many of the oldest-style paintings show animals. You may find bison, deer, boar, tigers, elephants, rhinoceros and wild cattle. Some animals are large and filled with lines or patterns. Others are small, quick outlines. Human figures often appear as simple stick shapes. They hunt with bows and spears. They gather honey. They dance in lines, holding hands. These scenes show daily life, but also community, celebration and perhaps ritual.

The paintings were not all made at once. Experts recognise several periods, based on style, colour and the way one image sits on top of another. The earliest are thought to come from the Middle Stone Age, many thousands of years ago, though exact dating of rock art is difficult. Later paintings show riders on horses and elephants, warriors with swords and shields, and processions. These belong to much more recent times. Here, one wall can hold the work of artists separated by thousands of years.

That is the second surprise. In some shelters, you can see paintings layered over older paintings. A later artist chose the same smooth patch of rock and added new figures on top. Sometimes the older image is still visible underneath, faint and ghostly. Nobody knows exactly why. Perhaps the best surfaces were limited. Perhaps certain places mattered more than others. Either way, standing here, you are looking at a conversation between artists who never met.

One famous shelter is often called the Zoo Rock. It earned the name because so many animals crowd its surface. Elephants, deer, bison and other creatures appear together, painted in different styles and periods. Take your time here. At first you may see only a few shapes. Then your eyes adjust, and more figures appear. A horn here. A tail there. A tiny hunter at the edge. The longer you look, the more the wall fills with life.

Another large shelter, sometimes called the Auditorium, is a high natural chamber with openings in several directions. Near it stands a tall rock that some researchers call the Chief's Rock. On nearby stone surfaces there are small cup-shaped hollows, known as cupules, cut into the rock by hand. Some scholars believe these cupules are extremely old, possibly among the earliest known marks of their kind. Others are more cautious. The debate continues, which is part of what makes this place so interesting.

In 2003, Bhimbetka was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The listing recognised not only the paintings, but also the living landscape around them. Villages in the surrounding area have traditions that some researchers link to the imagery on these walls. The forest itself matters too. Teak, bamboo and other trees grow across the hills. Nearby lies the Ratapani wildlife sanctuary. The setting still looks wild enough that you can imagine the animals in the paintings moving through it.

As you walk the marked trail, move slowly. The paintings are fragile, and the oils from human skin can damage them, so keep your hands off the rock. Light changes everything here. In soft morning or late afternoon light, faint colours become easier to see. In harsh midday sun, they can almost disappear. Step back sometimes, too. From a distance, you see the shape of the shelter, and you understand why people chose it.

Before you leave, stand under one of the overhangs. Feel the cooler air in the shade. Look up at the rough ceiling, darkened in places by time and weather. Then look out at the forest, the same view that sheltered families once saw. They watched rain fall from here. They told stories here. They painted what mattered to them. As you walk back down the path, listen again to the wind in the leaves... and notice the red marks you might have missed on the way in.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon for clearer faint colours.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven sandstone paths and steps.
Never touch the paintings; skin oils damage the pigment.
Carry water, as shade between shelters can be limited.
Step back from each wall to see faint figures emerge.
