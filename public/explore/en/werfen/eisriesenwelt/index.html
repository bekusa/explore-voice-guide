The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere ahead, a wooden door creaks open, and a blast of cold air pushes out of the mountain. It smells of wet rock and frost. Behind you, the summer valley is warm and green. In front of you, a dark mouth opens in a wall of pale limestone. This is Eisriesenwelt, the World of the Ice Giants, high above the town of Werfen in the Austrian state of Salzburg.

The name is not an exaggeration. This is widely described as the largest ice cave in the world. The full system runs for more than 40 kilometres through the Tennengebirge, a high limestone massif east of the Salzach valley. But here is the first surprise. Only the first part of the cave, roughly the first kilometre, holds ice. Beyond that, the passages are bare rock, dark and dry, and they are open only to cavers on scientific expeditions.

The entrance sits more than 1,600 metres above sea level. Getting here is part of the experience. Most visitors walk up a winding path, ride a cable car that climbs a steep face, and then walk again along a trail cut into the cliff. Pause on that trail if you can. Far below, the Salzach river runs through the valley, and you may see Hohenwerfen Castle on its rocky hill. The cave entrance itself is huge, a dark arch in the cliff face that you can see long before you reach it.

For most of history, local people knew this opening but kept their distance. Some believed the mountain was a gateway to the underworld. Legend has it that hunters and shepherds avoided the place, fearing what lived inside. Those stories belong to folklore, not to records. What we do know is that the cave stayed largely unexplored until the late nineteenth century, when curiosity finally became stronger than fear.

In 1879, a naturalist from Salzburg named Anton Posselt entered the cave and pushed in a few hundred metres. He published an account of what he found. Then, for decades, almost nothing happened. His report did not spark a rush of explorers. The great ice cave waited in the dark for another generation, and the real exploration began only in the early twentieth century.

That work belongs above all to Alexander von Mörk, a young caver from Salzburg. In the years before the First World War, he and his companions led expeditions deep into the mountain. They climbed ice walls, mapped passages, and found that the system was far larger than anyone had imagined. Mörk was killed in the war. His story did not end there, though. His urn was later placed inside the cave, in a great chamber that now carries his name. Your guide will likely point it out as you pass.

So why is there ice here at all, when the valley below is warm? The answer is the shape of the cave. The entrance is low, and the system rises behind it. In winter, heavy cold air sinks in through the entrance and chills the rock. In spring, meltwater trickles down through cracks in the limestone. It reaches the frozen passages and turns to ice. In summer, the cold air stays trapped, like water in a basin, so the ice survives. Year after year, layer is added to layer.

Now comes the second surprise for many visitors. There is no electric lighting along the tour. Guides hand out lamps, and the group moves through the dark with small, flickering flames. At certain points, the guide lights a strip of magnesium ribbon. For a few seconds, a white flash fills the chamber. Walls of ice appear in front of you, blue and grey and almost glassy, and then the darkness returns. It is a simple trick, and it works every time.

The formations have names, many taken from Norse mythology, the old tales of Scandinavian gods and giants. You may hear of Hymir's Castle, named for a frost giant. You may hear of Frigga's Veil, named for the wife of the god Odin, a curtain of ice that hangs from the rock. Near the start stands a great column of ice named after Anton Posselt, the man who first wrote about the cave. Each one is the work of dripping water and freezing air, not of any sculptor.

Be ready for the climb. The route inside goes up and then back down a long series of wooden and metal stairs, several hundred steps in each direction. The temperature stays around freezing, even on the hottest day of summer. Your breath will show in the lamplight. The floor can be damp. The handrails are cold under your fingers. It is a physical tour, so take it slowly, and let your eyes adjust as you go.

Listen inside, too. The cave is very quiet. You hear the shuffle of boots on the steps, the soft hiss of the lamps, and the voice of the guide echoing off the ice. Sometimes you hear a single drop of water fall. That drop is part of the same process that built everything around you. Some of this ice is very old, and it changes slowly from season to season as the climate shifts above the mountain.

When you come back out, the change is sudden. Warm air meets your face. The light seems very bright after the lamps. Many visitors stop at the entrance for a moment, just to let their eyes recover. Look back into the dark arch. It is hard to believe that so much ice lies only a short walk from a sunny mountain path, with alpine meadows and pine forest below.

As you start the walk down, look across the valley once more. The river, the castle, the rooftops of Werfen... they all sit in the warm air of the present. Behind you, inside the mountain, the cold air settles back into its basin, and the water keeps dripping and freezing in the dark. Take one last breath of that cool air from the entrance, and carry it with you down the trail.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm jacket, hat and gloves, even in summer.
Wear sturdy shoes with good grip for damp steps.
Expect several hundred stairs up, then back down.
Allow time for the uphill walk and cable car.
Photography is not allowed inside the ice cave.
