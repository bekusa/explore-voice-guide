The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the wide square in front of the palace. Feel the cobbles under your shoes, slightly uneven and worn smooth. Ahead of you, a long facade in warm, pale ochre fills your view. Rows of tall windows catch the light. On a quiet morning you can hear footsteps echo off the stone, and perhaps a bicycle bell from the town behind you. This is Schloss Esterházy, the seat of one of the most powerful noble families Central Europe has ever known.

First, a word about where you are. Eisenstadt is the capital of Burgenland, the easternmost state of Austria. But for most of this palace's life, this town was not in Austria at all. It belonged to the Kingdom of Hungary. Burgenland only became part of Austria after the First World War, in the early 1920s. So the family who built this palace were Hungarian magnates. Keep that in mind as you look around. Much of what you see here has a Hungarian story behind it.

The Esterházy family rose to great wealth and influence in the seventeenth century. They were loyal to the Habsburg rulers, and they were rewarded with land, titles, and eventually the rank of prince. At their height, the family held enormous estates across western Hungary. This palace was the centre of that world. From here, the princes managed lands, hosted guests, and showed their status to everyone who arrived in Eisenstadt.

The building did not start as a palace. A medieval castle stood on this site first, a fortified residence with defensive walls. After the Esterházy family acquired it, they rebuilt it on a grand scale in the late seventeenth century. Prince Paul Esterházy commissioned an Italian architect, Carlo Martino Carlone, to turn the old fortress into a Baroque residence. Baroque is the dramatic, ornate style of that era, designed to impress. Look at the corner towers. They still recall the shape of the old castle beneath the new skin.

Now let your eyes travel along the front of the building. Notice the rhythm of windows, pilasters, and decoration. Look closely at the row of carved busts set into the facade. These portraits in stone are part of the family's message to visitors. They link the Esterházy name to Hungarian history and to a long line of noble ancestors. In the seventeenth century, a building was not only a home. It was also a statement of who you were, and who you claimed to descend from.

Around 1800, the palace changed again. Prince Nikolaus the Second wanted a more modern look. He brought in the French architect Charles Moreau, who reshaped parts of the building in the newer Neoclassical style. Neoclassical design takes its ideas from ancient Greece and Rome — calm columns, clean lines, less ornament. The garden side of the palace shows this influence most clearly. So when you walk around the building, you are moving between two different centuries of taste.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. For about three decades, this family employed one of the greatest composers in history. Joseph Haydn entered the service of the Esterházy princes in the 1760s. He became their Kapellmeister, which means the director of the court's music. He wrote symphonies, operas, string quartets, and church music for them. Much of that work was first played for the family and their guests, in rooms very close to where you are standing.

The heart of that musical story is the great hall, now called the Haydnsaal, or Haydn Hall. It is a tall ceremonial room with painted ceilings and rich decoration. Musicians and listeners often praise its acoustics. The sound is said to be clear and warm, so that even a small ensemble fills the space. Concerts still take place here, including during the Haydn festival held in Eisenstadt. If you can attend one, you will hear this music in the kind of room it was written for.

A second surprise. Haydn did not spend all those years in Eisenstadt. Another prince of the family, Nikolaus the First, built a vast summer palace called Eszterháza, in what is now Hungary. The court spent long seasons there, and Haydn went with them. Eisenstadt remained the family's main seat, but the composer's life moved between the two. Legend has it that the musicians sometimes grew homesick during those long stays. Haydn's famous Farewell Symphony is often linked to that story, as a gentle hint to the prince.

Haydn's connection to this town did not end with his death. His remains rest today in the Bergkirche, a church a short walk up the hill from the palace. The journey of his remains there is a strange tale of its own, involving a missing skull that was only reunited with the body in the twentieth century. If you have time, walk up the street after your visit. The town, the palace, and the church form one connected story.

Behind the palace lies a large park. It was laid out in the English landscape style, which was fashionable around 1800. Instead of strict geometric flower beds, it offers winding paths, open lawns, old trees, and water. The idea was to make nature look natural, though every view was planned. Look for small garden buildings among the trees, such as the Leopoldinentempel, a little classical temple above a pond. It is a quiet place to rest after the formal rooms inside.

Today the palace belongs to a private foundation connected to the Esterházy family. Inside, exhibitions show family treasures, interiors, and the history of the region. Displays change, so what you see may differ from what friends saw before. The palace is also linked to wine, a big part of life in Burgenland. The region's vineyards stretch toward Lake Neusiedl to the east. Wine culture here is old, and the Esterházy estates have long been part of it.

Before you leave, turn around and look at the palace once more from the square. Notice how the building faces the town directly, without high walls or a long private drive. For centuries, the people of Eisenstadt lived in its shadow — working for the court, supplying it, listening to its music drift through open windows. Now the square belongs to everyone. Watch the light on the ochre stone for a moment. Then walk on into the town, perhaps humming a few bars of Haydn as you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a concert in the Haydn Hall to hear its acoustics.
Walk uphill to the Bergkirche to visit Haydn's tomb.
Save time for a slow loop through the palace park.
Check current exhibitions before you go, since displays change.
Pair your visit with a Burgenland wine tasting in town.
