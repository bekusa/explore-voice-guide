The guide

The story

The wind arrives first. It comes off the lake hot and steady, carrying fine grey dust that settles on your lips and the backs of your hands. Underfoot, the ground crunches. It is not soil so much as layers of old sediment, crumbling into pebbles and grit. The light here is hard and white for most of the day, and the lake beside you looks almost green. This is Koobi Fora, on the eastern shore of Lake Turkana, and it is one of the most important places on Earth for understanding where humans came from.

If you travelled here from Lodwar, you have crossed a real distance. Lodwar sits on the western side of the lake. Koobi Fora lies on the far eastern shore, inside Sibiloi National Park. Many visitors cross the water by boat or arrive on a small plane. The journey itself tells you something important. This is remote, dry country. That remoteness is part of the reason so much of the deep past has survived here, undisturbed and slowly exposed by wind and rain.

The lake itself has gone by several names. European explorers in the late nineteenth century called it Lake Rudolf, after an Austrian crown prince. Kenya later renamed it Lake Turkana, after the Turkana people who live along its western shores. Travellers also call it the Jade Sea, because of its colour. That green comes from algae in the water. On a calm morning, the surface can look almost solid, like polished stone.

Now look at the land around you. The low ridges and gullies are made of ancient lake and river deposits. Over millions of years, rivers carried mud and sand into the basin. Volcanoes in the region dropped layers of ash across the landscape. Each layer buried whatever lay on the surface at that time — bones, shells, stone tools. Today, erosion is slowly cutting through these layers again. In this way, the past keeps returning to the surface here.

The volcanic ash layers matter enormously. Geologists call them tuffs. Ash contains minerals that scientists can date with real precision. When a fossil is found between two dated ash layers, researchers can estimate its age with confidence. This is one reason Koobi Fora became so valuable. It is not only rich in fossils. It also gives scientists a reliable clock. The deposits here span a long stretch of time, roughly from four million to one million years ago.

Serious exploration began in the late 1960s. The Kenyan palaeontologist Richard Leakey, son of the famous fossil hunters Louis and Mary Leakey, led expeditions to this shore. He worked with a team from the National Museums of Kenya. Many of the most skilled fossil finders were Kenyan field workers. Their sharp eyes could spot a fragment of bone among thousands of similar-looking stones. Much of what the world knows from this site comes from their patient walking across these slopes.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Fossils from this area carry a catalogue code that begins with the letters K N M, E R. That stands for Kenya National Museums, East Rudolf — a reminder of the lake's older name. One of the best-known finds is a skull known simply as 1470. It was found in 1972 by a member of the team, Bernard Ngeneo. The skull had a larger brain than many scientists expected for its age.

That skull started long arguments. Was it an early member of our own genus, Homo? Did it belong to a separate species? Many researchers now place it in a species called Homo rudolfensis, named after the lake. The debate shows how this site has shaped science. Finds from Koobi Fora did not simply fill gaps. They forced researchers to rethink the human family tree. That tree now looks less like a single line and more like a branching bush.

There was a second famous debate, and it centred on dating. In the 1970s, scientists disagreed sharply about the age of one key ash layer. The disagreement mattered, because the age of several fossils depended on it. Further testing eventually settled the question, and the layer turned out to be younger than first claimed. It became a lesson for the whole field. Careful dating is as important as the fossils themselves.

Another remarkable find came from Ileret, a little to the north. Researchers discovered footprints pressed into ancient mud, around one and a half million years old. Scientists linked them to Homo erectus, a species closely related to our own ancestors. The prints show a walking style very similar to ours today. Think about that for a moment. On this same shoreline, beings with a stride like yours left marks in the wet ground beside an older lake.

Koobi Fora is not only about bones. Archaeologists have also found simple stone tools here. They are made by striking one stone against another to produce a sharp edge. Some fossil animal bones carry cut marks from those tools. These clues suggest early humans were butchering meat. Nearby in Sibiloi, there is also a petrified forest. Trees that grew here millions of years ago have turned to stone. This was once a much greener place.

Today, the area is protected. Sibiloi National Park forms part of the Lake Turkana National Parks, listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. A small museum and research base stand near the shore. Field schools still bring students here to learn how to survey, excavate and record finds. If you walk the trails, remember one rule. Never pick up or move anything. A single fragment out of place can lose its scientific meaning forever.

Before you leave, stand still and look around once more. Notice the bands of colour in the eroded slopes — pale ash, darker clay, rust-red sand. Each band is a chapter of time. Listen to the wind moving across the open ground, and the small waves on the lakeshore. Feel the heat on your skin. Our distant relatives felt this same sun beside this same water. As you walk back, watch the ground. Somewhere beneath your feet, the next discovery is still waiting.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan the crossing from Lodwar well ahead; access is remote.
Carry plenty of water; shade is scarce on the slopes.
Explore early morning or late afternoon to avoid peak heat.
Arrange a guide or ranger before walking the fossil areas.
Never pick up or move fossils or stones you find.
