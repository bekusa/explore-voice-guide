The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees of Mon Repos the traffic of Corfu Town fades into the sound of cicadas, birdsong and pine needles under your shoes. The air smells of resin and warm earth. Shade covers almost everything, and the light reaches the paths in moving patches. You are walking through a park, a royal estate, and an ancient city, all at the same time.

This green headland sits on the Kanoni peninsula, just south of the old town. Look between the tree trunks and you may catch the blue of the sea below. For most visitors, Corfu means Venetian fortresses and narrow lanes. Here the island feels different. It is quieter, older and full of shade, and every path seems to lead somewhere unexpected.

The pale villa at the heart of the estate was built in the late 1820s. At that time the Ionian Islands were a British protectorate, and Corfu was its capital. The house was made for Sir Frederick Adam, the British Lord High Commissioner. His wife, Nina Palatiano, came from a Corfiot family. Many people say that Adam built the villa as a summer retreat for her, close to the sea she knew.

Look at the building's lines. It is Neoclassical, a style that copies the calm symmetry of ancient Greek and Roman architecture. The front is simple and balanced, with columns and tall windows. Here is the first surprise. The couple did not enjoy their new home for long. Soon after the villa was finished, Adam was posted far away, to India. The house they built for rest saw very little of them.

In 1864 Britain handed the Ionian Islands to Greece. The villa then passed to the new Greek royal family, and it got the name you hear today. Mon Repos is French for 'my rest'. For decades it was the family's summer residence. Royal children played under these same trees, and the household came and went with the seasons.

Now the second surprise, and the one that brings many visitors here. On 10 June 1921, a baby boy was born inside this villa. He was Prince Philip of Greece and Denmark. Years later, the world knew him as Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, husband of Queen Elizabeth the Second. The man who spent most of his life at the heart of the British monarchy began it right here, in a quiet house on a Greek island.

His first stay on Corfu was very short. Greece was in political crisis, and his family had to leave the country soon after his first birthday. A British warship carried them away. According to a story that his family often told, the baby Philip travelled in a cot made from a wooden fruit crate. Whether every detail is exact or not, the image has stayed with the villa ever since.

Over the following decades the estate had a complicated life. The royal family's position in Greece changed several times. In the 1990s the Greek state took the property, and the former king took the case to the European courts. Today Mon Repos belongs to the public. The park is open to everyone, and the villa holds a museum about the ancient city that once stood on this land.

That ancient city is the real foundation of this place. Long before any villa, the headland was part of ancient Kerkyra, the Greek city that gave the island its name. Its harbours lay on either side of the peninsula. Merchants, sailors and priests walked here more than two thousand years ago. Inside the villa, the museum shows finds from this lost city, and the rooms themselves still carry the elegance of the royal period.

Walk deeper into the woods and the ancient world appears among the trees. On higher ground you can find the remains of a temple, which archaeologists have linked to the goddess Hera. Only foundations and scattered blocks of stone are left. Further down, towards the sea, stands a smaller Doric temple, built in the plain, sturdy style of early Greek architecture. It is easy to walk past both without noticing. Look for the cut stone between the roots.

Near that lower temple, a path leads to the Kardaki spring. Water runs from an old stone outlet into a basin. Corfiots have a well-known saying about it. Locals say that anyone who drinks from the Kardaki spring will return to Corfu one day. It is a promise, not a guarantee. Still, many visitors bend down for a sip, just in case.

Before you leave, look across the road from the main gate. There you can see the ruins of an early Christian basilica, with broken walls and open arches. It is one more layer in the same small area. Within a short walk you have seen Greek temples, a Christian church, a British governor's villa and a royal birthplace. Few places on the island hold so many chapters so close together.

So pause for a moment where you are. Notice the shade, the dry leaves, the sea light at the edge of the trees. Kings, governors and ancient priests have all stood on this ground. Each wanted something different from it. Today it simply gives what its name promises, a place to rest. As you walk back towards the gate, listen again for the cicadas. They have been singing here much longer than any of the stories you have just heard.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in the morning for cooler, quieter woodland paths.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven paths down to the temples.
Bring water, since the park walks are longer than expected.
Allow extra time to find the lower Doric temple.
Combine your visit with a walk to nearby Kanoni.
