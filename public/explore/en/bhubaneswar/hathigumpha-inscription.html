The guide

The story

Feel the rock under your feet first. It is a coarse, honey-coloured sandstone, warm in the sun and gritty where countless sandals have worn it smooth. Around you, monkeys chatter in the trees, and somewhere below, the traffic of modern Bhubaneswar hums faintly. You are on Udayagiri, the Hill of the Sunrise. Just ahead, under a broad overhang of stone, are seventeen lines of writing that have spoken for more than two thousand years.

This is the Hathigumpha inscription. The name means the Elephant Cave, and it belongs to the natural cavern whose brow carries the text. Do not expect a polished plaque. What you see is a band of weathered letters cut directly into the rough ceiling and face of the rock. Some lines are still crisp. Others have crumbled into shallow hollows. Even so, this worn surface is one of the most important written records from ancient India.

The man behind it was Kharavela, a king of Kalinga. Kalinga was the old name for this coastal region, roughly the heart of modern Odisha. Most historians place his reign around the first century before the Common Era, though some argue for a slightly earlier date. Here is the first surprise. Kharavela appears almost nowhere else in the surviving record. Without this single carved text, we would know very little about him at all.

The letters are written in Brahmi, the ancestor of many scripts used across South and Southeast Asia today. The language is a Prakrit, an everyday spoken form related to Sanskrit. If you look closely at the shapes, you may notice angles, crosses and small curves, rather than the flowing loops of modern Odia. Scholars spent much of the nineteenth century learning to read this script. James Prinsep, a British scholar in Calcutta, made one of the early attempts in the 1830s.

The inscription opens with a salute to the Jain tradition. Its first words honour the arhats, the enlightened teachers, and the siddhas, the liberated souls. Jainism is an ancient Indian religion built around non-violence and strict self-discipline. That opening tells you something about this whole hill. The small rooms carved into Udayagiri and its twin, Khandagiri across the road, were made as shelters for Jain monks. They came here to meditate, to fast, and to wait out the rainy season.

Now comes the part that makes historians lean in. The text is arranged almost like a year-by-year report of a reign. It describes the king's youth and education, his coronation as a young man, and then what he did in each year that followed. That structure is very rare for this period. Ancient Indian inscriptions often praise a ruler in general terms. This one tries to tell you what happened, and when.

According to the most common readings, Kharavela began by repairing his capital after a storm damaged its gates and walls. Later lines describe military campaigns in several directions, gifts to the people, festivals, and building works. One passage mentions a canal first opened under an earlier dynasty, the Nandas, which Kharavela extended. Because so many letters are damaged, scholars still argue over exact numbers and names. Treat every detail as a careful reading, not a certainty.

Here is the second surprise. One of the inscription's proudest moments is a homecoming. As many scholars interpret it, Kharavela marched against Magadha, the powerful kingdom to the north. He brought back a sacred Jain image that a Nanda king had once carried away from Kalinga. Think about that for a moment. This hill holds a record of a people recovering a stolen object of faith, long before the modern idea of returning cultural treasures.

There is a deeper echo here too. Kalinga is the land that the emperor Ashoka conquered in a famously bloody war, in the third century before the Common Era. Ashoka's own edicts, carved at Dhauli on the other side of Bhubaneswar, express his regret for that slaughter. Kharavela's inscription comes some generations later. Read side by side, the two records show Kalinga first as a defeated land, and then as a confident kingdom again.

The text also contains a phrase that many readers find moving. It refers to Bharadhavasa, which a number of scholars connect with Bharatavarsha, an old name for the land of India. If that reading is right, it is among the earliest known uses of the idea in stone. Not everyone agrees on its exact meaning. But it is worth pausing on the thought that a word still used in India's official name may appear on this weathered rock.

Step back and look at the cave itself. Hathigumpha is mostly a natural hollow, broad and low, not one of the neat square cells carved elsewhere on the hill. The sandstone shows bands and layers, the record of ancient sediments pressed together. Rain, heat and humidity have attacked this surface for centuries. That is why parts of the text are lost. The damage you see is not neglect alone. It is simply what time does to soft stone.

When you leave the inscription, keep walking around the hill. Nearby you will find Rani Gumpha, the Queen's Cave, a two-storey monastery with carved friezes of figures, animals and court scenes. Climb a little higher and the city opens out below, with green trees, rooftops and the distant towers of Bhubaneswar's temples. Across the road, Khandagiri rises with its own caves and a later Jain temple at the top, still visited by worshippers today.

The atmosphere here changes through the day. In the early morning the stone glows pale gold, the air is cooler, and the hill is quiet except for birds. By midday the rock radiates heat and school groups move between the caves. In the late afternoon, shadows deepen inside the hollows, and the carved letters can stand out more clearly when the light falls across them at a low angle.

Before you go, look once more at those seventeen lines. A king ordered them cut here so that his deeds would outlast him. In a way, he succeeded. His city is gone, his armies are forgotten, and the image he brought home has vanished. Yet his words are still here, above your head, in rough golden stone. Listen to the monkeys and the wind as you walk down. This hill has been a place of quiet reflection for more than two thousand years.

Practical tips

Pair the visit with Khandagiri caves across the road.
Watch your belongings around the hill's bold monkeys.
Wear shoes with good grip for uneven stone steps.
