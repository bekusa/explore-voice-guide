The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the dry scrub, and somewhere a cicada starts and stops. Under your feet the path is pale and gritty, and it crunches with each step. Around you, organ pipe cactus rises in green columns, and the sun lands hard on grey and ochre stone. This is Yagul, an old Zapotec settlement in the Tlacolula Valley of Oaxaca. Before any history, notice the climb. The city does not sit on the valley floor. It rises up a hill, as if it wanted to watch the world below.

The Zapotec are one of the great Indigenous peoples of southern Mexico, and their descendants still live in these valleys today. The name Yagul is usually explained as a Zapotec phrase meaning old tree or old stick. It is a modest name for a place like this. Many people come to the region for Monte Albán, the famous hilltop capital near Oaxaca city. Fewer visitors make it here. That is your first quiet advantage. On many days you may share these terraces with only a handful of people, and a lizard or two.

Yagul's story belongs mostly to a later chapter of Zapotec history. After the power of Monte Albán faded, political life in the valleys broke into smaller centres. Each centre had its own lords, its own alliances, and its own rivals. Archaeologists believe Yagul grew into one of these regional seats in the centuries before the Spanish arrived. People had lived on and around this hill much earlier, though. Think of the ruins as many generations of building, one layer laid over another.

Walk toward the long sunken court with sloping walls. This is the ball court, and it is often described as one of the largest in Mesoamerica. Mesoamerica means the old cultural world of Mexico and Central America. The ball game was played across that world for many centuries. Players struck a heavy rubber ball, mostly with their hips, not their hands. The game had sport in it, but also ritual and politics. Stand at one end and look down the length of the court. Imagine the echo of that ball on these stone walls.

The shape here is worth a slow look. Seen from above, the court forms a capital letter I, a long central alley with wider zones at each end. Its sloping side walls were once smooth, and probably covered with plaster. Today you see the stone core, with small rocks packed tightly between larger blocks. That is the real skeleton of the building. It shows how these walls were made... by hand, stone by stone, and without metal tools or draft animals.

Next, look for the Palace of the Six Patios. The name explains a lot. It is a complex of rooms arranged around a series of courtyards, linked by narrow passages. Walking through it can feel like entering a maze. Archaeologists generally read it as a residence for the ruling family or the elite. Notice how the doorways turn and narrow. Privacy and control seem to be built into the plan. A visitor in the past could not simply walk straight to the most important room.

Here is a detail many people miss. On some walls at Yagul you can find stone mosaic, called fretwork. Small cut stones are fitted together into stepped, geometric patterns, a little like a key shape repeated across a wall. This style is closely linked to Mitla, a larger and better known site a short drive further along the valley. Seeing fretwork here, on a smaller scale, helps you understand that Yagul and Mitla belonged to the same artistic and political world.

Yagul is also a place of tombs. Zapotec builders often placed tombs beneath patios and houses, so the dead stayed close to the living. Several tombs have been found at this site. One well known example is a triple tomb with carved stone decoration, including heads in relief near the entrance. Access to tombs can change for conservation reasons, so some may be closed when you visit. Even a closed entrance tells you something, though. Here, families buried their ancestors under the floors where life went on.

Now comes the climb. Above the palace and the ball court, a rocky hilltop rises, and people often call it the fortress. The path is steep and uneven in places, so take your time. As you climb, the reason for building here becomes clear. The view opens across the whole Tlacolula Valley. You can see fields, villages, and the blue line of distant mountains. A ruler at the top could watch roads, farms, and any group of people approaching from far away.

The second surprise is the most important one, and it reaches much further back than the palaces. In the hills around Yagul there are caves and rock shelters. Together with sites near Mitla, they form a UNESCO World Heritage landscape, listed for its prehistoric caves. Excavations in this area found very early evidence of plant domestication in the Americas, including squash and early forms of maize. In other words, part of the long story of farming in this continent can be traced to these dry hills around you.

Some of these rock shelters also hold painted images. They are faint red figures and marks, made long ago, on rock faces in the surrounding hills. They are protected and fragile, and most are not part of the main visitor path. Still, it is worth knowing they exist. The landscape you see from the hilltop is not empty scrub. It is a record of people living here for thousands of years, first as hunters and gatherers, later as farmers, and later still as builders of towns.

As you come back down, pay attention to the plants. The tall cactus columns, the low thorny shrubs, and the agave plants with their thick grey-green leaves all belong to a dry valley ecosystem. Agave matters a great deal in Oaxaca. Its hearts are roasted and distilled to make mezcal, and you will pass fields of it on the roads nearby. Many villages in the Tlacolula Valley are known for weaving and markets. The world around the ruins is still very much alive.

Before you leave, stop for a moment at the edge of a terrace. Feel the heat coming up from the stone, and the breeze moving across the slope. Look again at the ball court below, the patios beside it, and the hill above. These stones were placed by Zapotec hands, in a valley where Zapotec is still spoken. As you walk back down the path, listen to the crunch of gravel under your feet... the same sound that greeted you when you first arrived.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the morning before the valley heat builds.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep fortress path.
Carry water, since shade on the site is limited.
Pair your visit with nearby Mitla on the same day.
Bring a hat and sunscreen for exposed hilltop terraces.
