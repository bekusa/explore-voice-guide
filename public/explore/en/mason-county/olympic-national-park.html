The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees, the sound of the river never stops. It is a steady, cold roar, broken now and then by the tick of water dripping from moss. The air smells of wet cedar and old needles. The light comes down green, filtered through branches high above you. This is the Staircase corner of Olympic National Park, the southeastern doorway into one of the largest stretches of wilderness in the lower United States.

Most visitors picture this park from the other side of the peninsula. They think of the Pacific beaches, or the famous rainforest valleys in the west. But here, in Mason County, the park arrives quietly. The road climbs from Hood Canal, passes the long blue body of Lake Cushman, and then ends. Beyond the ranger station, there are no more roads. There are only trails, rivers, and mountains that run north for many kilometres.

The river beside you is the North Fork of the Skokomish. Its name comes from the Skokomish people, a Twana-speaking community whose homeland covers the southern end of Hood Canal. For thousands of years, these valleys were part of their seasonal world. People came for salmon, cedar, berries, and mountain game. The Skokomish Tribe still lives nearby, and its reservation sits close to the mouth of this river. When you hear this name, you are hearing the oldest layer of the place.

Now, the name Staircase. The story usually told goes back to an army expedition in 1890, led by Lieutenant Joseph O'Neil. His party was exploring the interior of the Olympic Mountains, which outsiders had barely mapped at the time. Somewhere along this river, a steep rock bluff blocked the way. According to the account, the men built a rough wooden staircase over it. The stairs are long gone. The name stayed.

That expedition matters for another reason. O'Neil came back convinced that these mountains should be protected. He was not the only one. In 1909, President Theodore Roosevelt created Mount Olympus National Monument. In 1938, his distant cousin, President Franklin Roosevelt, signed the law that made it a national park. So two Roosevelts, nearly thirty years apart, shaped the land you are standing on.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Theodore Roosevelt's monument was partly about one animal — the large elk of these mountains. Hunting had pushed them into decline. Today they carry his name: Roosevelt elk, the largest kind of elk in North America. Herds still move through these valleys. Usually you will find tracks or flattened grass before you see the animals themselves. If you do see them, keep your distance and let them pass.

Look up at the trees around you. The big conifers here are mostly western red cedar, Douglas fir, and western hemlock. Cedar has soft, stringy bark, often reddish, that peels in long strips. The Skokomish and other coastal peoples used that bark for clothing, baskets, and rope. The wood itself split cleanly for planks and canoes. When you touch a cedar trunk, you are touching the material that built much of life on this coast.

Notice also how much of the forest is growing on the forest. Moss covers fallen logs. Ferns sprout from branches. Young hemlocks stand in a straight line, because they all started life on one rotting trunk. Foresters call these nurse logs. Years after the old log has gone, the trees above it may still stand on stilt-like roots, with a hollow space underneath where the log once lay.

The park itself is huge. It covers close to one million acres, and most of it is official wilderness. It holds three very different worlds: glacier-capped mountains, temperate rainforest, and a long, wild Pacific coastline. In 1981, UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site. From here, the high peaks are hidden behind the ridges. But the water in this river began up there, as rain and snow on those mountains.

Here is a second surprise. The Olympic Peninsula behaves almost like an island. Ice sheets, ocean, and lowland waters cut it off for long periods. Because of that, some plants and animals here live nowhere else on Earth. Scientists point to a small marmot, a chipmunk, and several wildflowers found only in these mountains. And some familiar animals are missing. The forests never had native grizzly bears or porcupines.

The opposite story is just as interesting. Mountain goats were not native here at all. People brought them to the peninsula nearly a century ago. Over time, the goats damaged fragile alpine plants and sometimes became a danger to hikers. In recent years, the park and its partners captured many of the goats and moved them by helicopter to the Cascade Mountains, where they belong naturally. It was a careful and unusual rescue, carried out in reverse.

If you have time, the walk along the river is the best way to feel this place. A loop trail follows the North Fork past rapids and deep green pools. A suspension bridge crosses the water, and it sways a little under your feet. Even on short walks, the ground can be muddy and roots cross the path, so watch your step. The cold, clear water here moves fast, even when it looks calm.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Listen to the river again. Notice how the sound changes as you turn your head — louder toward the rapids, softer behind the trees. Look for one small thing: a single fern, a drop of water on a needle, a strip of cedar bark. The mountains are out of sight, but everything around you comes from them. Walk back slowly, and let the green light follow you to the road.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check road and weather conditions before driving up to Staircase.
Bring a park entrance pass for the Staircase entrance.
Wear waterproof shoes; river trails stay muddy most of the year.
Download offline maps; mobile signal fades near Lake Cushman.
Keep well back from elk, especially in autumn.
