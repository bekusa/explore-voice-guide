The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through the dhak trees, and somewhere above you a peacock calls out across the ruins. The path under your feet is pale dust and broken stone. Ahead, rows of roofless walls climb toward a dark ridge of the Aravalli hills, some of the oldest mountains on Earth. There are no rooftops, no doors, no voices from windows. Only stone, sky, and the small sounds of animals that now live here. This is Bhangarh, a walled town in the Alwar district of Rajasthan, and for about three centuries almost nobody has lived inside it.

Before the ghost stories, here are the facts. Bhangarh was founded in the late sixteenth century, during the reign of the Mughal emperor Akbar. Historical accounts name Raja Bhagwant Das of Amber as the builder. He made the town for his younger son, Madho Singh. Madho Singh's brother was Man Singh, one of Akbar's most powerful generals. So this was not a remote outpost. It was a family seat connected to the centre of imperial power, built in a protected valley at the edge of the hills.

Look at the shape of the place. The town sits in a bowl, with the hills curving behind it like a wall of their own. Then people added more walls. Layered stone fortifications ring the settlement, and several gates cut through them. The main entrance is often called the Hanuman Gate, after the monkey god Hanuman. A small shrine to him still stands near the entrance, and you may see fresh flowers or red paste there. Here is the first surprise. Bhangarh is a ruin, but parts of it are still holy.

As you walk in, the main path becomes a long, straight street. On both sides you see the remains of shops, built in neat stone rows. Many people know this street as the market, or bazaar. Picture it full: cloth sellers, metalworkers, grain merchants, and traders in sweets and spices. The shop fronts are low and square. Most of the upper storeys have fallen. The rhythm of the doorways is still clear, one after another, like an empty theatre where you can still count the seats.

Further along, you pass the remains of larger houses. One building is popularly called the house of the dancers. Local tradition says performers lived or performed there, but treat that name as tradition, not a documented record. What you can see for certain is the stonework. It uses rough local stone, lime mortar, and in places carefully cut blocks around doorways and niches. Run your eyes along the walls. You can often see where wooden beams once rested, because the square holes are still there.

Now turn to the temples, because they are the best-preserved buildings in Bhangarh. Several stand inside the walls, including temples dedicated to Gopinath, a form of Krishna, and to Someshwar, a form of Shiva. Their tall stone towers are carved with bands of decoration, small figures, and repeating patterns. Here is the second surprise. While the houses around them collapsed, these temples survived largely intact. Stone temples were built to last, while ordinary homes depended on timber roofs that eventually rotted and fell.

At the Someshwar temple, look for water. A small pool or tank sits close by, and trees shade the area. In the heat of a Rajasthan afternoon, this corner feels several degrees cooler. Water decided where towns could live in this dry landscape. Springs and streams from the hills fed Bhangarh, and the town needed them every day. When you stand here, you are standing near the reason the valley was chosen at all.

At the top of the slope stands the palace, set against the hill. It rises in terraces, and from below you can see its layered levels. Much of the upper structure is gone, so the building looks like a giant stone staircase with missing steps. Climb carefully. From the upper levels, the whole town spreads out below you. You can trace the bazaar street, the temple towers, and the ring of walls. This view explains Bhangarh better than any sign. You can see the full plan of a working town.

So why did it empty? Historians generally point to ordinary pressures. The town declined in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, as political power shifted and nearby rulers fought for control. Water and trade routes mattered too. When a town loses its patron and its income, people move away. But that is not the story most visitors come for. Bhangarh is best known in India as one of the country's most haunted places, and two legends explain its fall.

The first legend involves an ascetic, a holy man called Guru Balu Nath, who meditated on this spot. According to the tale, he allowed the town to be built on one condition. No house could ever cast its shadow over his retreat. When a later ruler built the palace too high, the shadow fell. The holy man's curse, the story says, destroyed the town. Locals still point out a small structure they describe as his resting place. Remember, this is folklore, not recorded history.

The second legend is darker. It tells of a beautiful princess, often named Ratnavati, and a sorcerer who wanted her. In the tale, he tried to win her with a magic potion. She saw through the trick and poured it onto a rock. The rock rolled down the hill and crushed him. Before he died, he cursed the town. Different versions change the details, but the ending is always the same. Bhangarh fell silent. These stories have spread through films, television, and the internet.

The Archaeological Survey of India, the national body that protects historic monuments, looks after the site. Near the entrance, look for its signboard. It famously tells visitors not to stay inside the walls after dark. Officially, this protects the ruins and keeps people safe in a forest area near wildlife. Many visitors read it as proof of the haunting. Either way, the sign has become one of the most photographed objects here. That says something about how legends and real rules can grow together.

While you walk, notice who lives here now. Langur monkeys, with grey fur and black faces, sit on the walls and watch you. Peacocks walk between the shops. Large banyan trees push their roots into old stone. On weekends and holidays, Indian families and groups of friends come to picnic, take photos, and test their nerves. The town is empty of residents, yet it is rarely truly quiet.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the old market street. Look at the line of empty doorways and the hills behind them. Some people come to Bhangarh for ghosts, and some come for history. Whatever brought you here, you can see the same thing: a planned town, built with care, slowly returned to the hills. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again. The wind, the birds, your own footsteps on the stones... these are Bhangarh's sounds now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early in the morning to avoid heat and crowds.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone paths and palace steps.
Carry water because shade is limited on the climb.
Keep food out of sight to avoid bold langur monkeys.
Visit on a weekday for a quieter walk through the bazaar.
