The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in the square and listen. The traffic of a small French town fades here, and what is left is the scrape of shoes on stone and perhaps the call of a jackdaw from the towers. The limestone in front of you is pale, almost cream, darkened in patches by rain and time. Two heavy towers rise above a deep western porch. This is the Cathedral of Notre-Dame of Noyon, and it is older and stranger than it first appears.

Noyon sits in the Oise valley, north of Paris, in the region that the French call Picardy. Today it feels quiet, a market town of tiled roofs and narrow streets. But for many centuries this was a place of real power. It was the seat of a bishop. The bishops of Noyon held the rank of peers of France, which meant they took part in the coronation ceremonies of French kings. A town of this size rarely carried that kind of weight.

The royal connection goes back even further. Noyon is linked with two moments that shaped the French crown. Charlemagne, the great Frankish ruler, was crowned king here in 768, long before he became emperor. More than two centuries later, in 987, Hugh Capet was crowned here too. His family, the Capetians, would rule France for hundreds of years. Neither ceremony happened in the building you see now. They took place in earlier churches on this same ground.

There is also a saint at the heart of the story. In the seventh century, the bishop of Noyon was a man called Eligius, known in French as Saint Éloi. Before he became a churchman, he was a goldsmith and an adviser at the Frankish royal court. Even today he is the patron saint of metalworkers and goldsmiths. His memory gave Noyon prestige and drew pilgrims to the town for generations.

Now look up at the building itself. The cathedral you are facing was begun in the middle of the twelfth century, after fire damaged the earlier church. That timing matters. It places Noyon among the very first generation of Gothic buildings in Europe. Gothic architecture, with its pointed arches and ribbed vaults, was just being invented in the region around Paris. Noyon was one of the places where builders tested the new ideas.

This is why the cathedral feels like two styles at once. Some parts are still solid and rounded, close to the older Romanesque tradition, with its thick walls and round arches. Other parts reach upward with pointed arches and thinner supports. Historians often call this a transitional building. You are looking at architects in the middle of changing their minds, one generation handing the work to the next.

If you walk around to the sides of the building, watch for one unusual feature. The two arms of the transept, the cross-shaped section in the middle, do not end in flat walls. They end in rounded curves, like small apses. This design is rare in northern France. Some scholars link it to influences from the Rhineland or from Flanders. It gives the building a softer outline than most Gothic cathedrals.

Inside, the most important thing to notice is the height of the walls and how they are divided. The nave wall has four levels, one stacked on another. At the bottom are the main arches. Above them is a gallery, a kind of upper corridor. Above that is a narrow band of small arches, called a triforium. At the top are the high windows. This four-level design was an experiment. Later Gothic builders mostly dropped the gallery and used three levels instead.

Look at the columns along the nave as well. They do not all match. Heavier piers alternate with lighter, slimmer columns. This rhythm, strong then light, strong then light, comes from the way the vaults above were first planned. It gives the long space a steady beat as you walk toward the altar. Many visitors pass by without noticing it. Once you see it, the pattern is hard to miss.

The cathedral has survived hard times. During the French Revolution, the bishopric of Noyon was abolished, and the church lost much of its role. Then came the First World War. Noyon stood close to the front line and changed hands more than once. The town and the cathedral suffered heavy damage, especially in 1918. Roofs and vaults were broken. Much of what you see today is the result of long and careful restoration in the decades after the war.

Around the cathedral, small pieces of the old church world still survive. Parts of the cloister remain, where the clergy once walked in quiet. Nearby stands the chapter library, a rare timber-framed building from the early sixteenth century. Its wooden beams and plaster panels look very different from the stone of the cathedral. It held the books of the canons, the priests who served here. Few buildings of this kind survive anywhere in France.

Noyon has one more famous son, though he took a very different path. John Calvin, the Protestant reformer, was born in this town in 1509. His father worked for the cathedral chapter. As a boy, Calvin grew up in the shadow of these towers. Later, in Geneva, he would lead a movement that rejected much of what this cathedral stood for. A museum dedicated to him stands in the town today.

Before you leave, step back into the square and look at the whole building once more. Notice the weight of the western towers, and then the lighter lines of the upper windows behind them. Two ideas of how to build sit side by side in this stone. As you walk back into the streets of Noyon, look at the rooftops around you. This quiet town once crowned kings, and the cathedral still stands at its centre.
