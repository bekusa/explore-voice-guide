The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind comes in off the Channel and moves through the pines. Somewhere below the bluff, waves break on a long beach of sand. Up here, the grass is cut short and even, and the gravel paths crunch softly underfoot. Ahead of you, white marble markers stand in rows so straight that they seem to shift as you walk. Each row lines up from every angle.

You are standing on a bluff above Omaha Beach, near the village of Colleville-sur-Mer in Normandy. On the morning of June 6, 1944, American soldiers landed on the sand below during the D-Day invasion. Omaha was one of five landing beaches chosen by the Allies, and it became the most costly for the Americans. Many of the men who died on that shore, and in the weeks of fighting inland that followed, now lie in this ground.

The cemetery began in the middle of the battle. On June 8, 1944, just two days after the landings, the United States First Army opened a temporary burial ground near this spot. It is often described as the first American cemetery in Europe of the Second World War. After the war, families had a choice. They could bring their loved ones home, or let them rest here in France. Thousands chose Normandy.

Here is something many visitors do not know. The land under your feet is not owned by the United States in the usual sense. France granted it to the American people for use as a permanent cemetery, without charge or taxation. Today an American government agency, the American Battle Monuments Commission, cares for it. Gardeners and staff tend the lawns, the hedges and the markers every day of the year.

Now look along the rows. More than nine thousand service members are buried here. Most markers are Latin crosses. Others are Stars of David, for Jewish soldiers. They are cut from white marble, and each one carries a name, a unit, a home state and a date of death. Walk slowly and read a few. You will see states from across the country — Texas, Ohio, New York, Oregon — gathered together on one French hillside.

Some markers carry no name at all. These are the graves of men who could not be identified. Their inscription reads: Here rests in honored glory a comrade in arms known but to God. Pause at one of these stones. It is a small moment, but many visitors say it stays with them longer than any statistic. Each of these graves belongs to someone whose family never learned exactly where he was laid to rest.

The cemetery also holds stories of families. Among the graves are brothers buried side by side. Two of the Niland brothers from New York lie here. Their family's story helped inspire the film Saving Private Ryan. Nearby is the grave of Brigadier General Theodore Roosevelt Junior, son of the American president. He landed with the first wave at Utah Beach. Beside him lies his younger brother Quentin, a pilot killed in the First World War and later reburied here.

At the eastern end of the grounds stands the memorial itself. It is a semicircular colonnade of pale stone, with a loggia at each end. In the centre rises a large bronze figure. It is called the Spirit of American Youth Rising from the Waves. A young man reaches upward, his body lifting out of the water. Around the base, an inscription speaks of the men who gave their lives on the beaches and in the fields beyond.

Inside the loggias, look for the large maps carved and coloured in stone. They show the landings and the military campaigns across northwestern Europe. Arrows trace the paths of armies and the routes of ships. Behind the memorial is the Garden of the Missing. Its curved walls carry the names of more than fifteen hundred service members whose remains were never found. Look closely. Small bronze rosettes appear beside some names. Each rosette marks a man whose remains were later recovered and identified.

From the memorial, a long reflecting pool runs west toward the graves. On a still day, it holds the sky and the colonnade in its surface. At the centre of the burial area stands a small circular chapel. Step inside if it is open. The ceiling carries a mosaic in deep colours. At the far western end of the grounds, two granite figures represent the United States and France, facing each other across the shared ground.

Now walk to the edge of the bluff. A path and viewing point look straight down onto Omaha Beach. An orientation table helps you match the shoreline to the landing sectors. Today the sand is quiet, wide and pale at low tide. It is hard to picture boats, smoke and gunfire here. That gap between what you see and what happened is part of why people come to stand in this spot.

Before or after the graves, the visitor centre is worth your time. It sits a short walk from the burial area, set low into the landscape so it does not compete with the view. Inside, exhibits tell the story of the landings through personal objects, letters, photographs and film. Many visitors find that learning about a few individual soldiers changes how they see the rows of markers outside. The names stop being a list. They become people.

If you stay until the end of the day, you may see the flag lowered. Staff fold it with care, and a bugle call called Taps — the traditional American call played at military funerals — sounds across the grass. Visitors usually stop walking and stand in silence. Even children go quiet. It is one of the most moving moments here, and it needs no explanation.

As you leave, look back one more time. Notice how the rows of white marble still line up, whichever way you turn. Listen again to the wind in the pines and the sea below the bluff. The beach is still there. So is the village, the farmland, the ordinary Norman sky. Take a slow breath, and carry the quiet with you as you walk back along the gravel path.
