The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the paving stones of Place de Neuve and listen. Trams hum along the edge of the square. Footsteps echo off pale stone. Above you rises a heavy façade of columns, arched windows and sculpted figures. On a quiet afternoon the building looks almost asleep. But on a performance night the doors open, warm light spills onto the square, and the murmur of an arriving audience fills the air. This is the Grand Théâtre de Genève, the city's opera house.

The theatre opened in 1879, late in the nineteenth century, when Geneva was growing in wealth and ambition. The city wanted a stage that could stand beside the great opera houses of Europe. Its architect, Jacques-Élysée Goss, looked closely at one model in particular, the Paris Opéra, known as the Palais Garnier. If you know that Parisian building, you may recognise the family resemblance here. It shows in the grand arches, the rich ornament and the sense of theatre that begins before you even step inside.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Part of the money behind this building came from an unusual source. A deposed German aristocrat, the Duke of Brunswick, died in Geneva in the early 1870s and left his large fortune to the city. Geneva used that inheritance for several public projects. One of them was this theatre. Another is the elaborate Brunswick Monument by the lake, his own tomb. So, in a sense, an exiled duke helped pay for Geneva's night at the opera.

Look up at the façade now. The stonework carries the language of the nineteenth century, when architects used sculpture to tell people what a building was for. Allegorical figures and decorative carving point to music, drama and the arts. Names and faces connected to the world of performance appear in the ornament. Take your time with it. From a distance the front reads as one solid mass, but closer up it breaks into many small, careful details.

Now for the moment that changed everything. In 1951, during a rehearsal of Richard Wagner's opera The Valkyrie, a fire broke out on the stage. The Valkyrie is a work full of dramatic fire scenes, which makes the story feel almost unbelievable... but it is true. The blaze destroyed the stage and the auditorium. The heavy outer walls and the front of the building survived, but the heart of the theatre was gone.

Geneva chose to rebuild. When the theatre reopened in 1962, the outside still looked like the nineteenth century. The inside, however, was a new auditorium in a modern style of its time. This is the second surprise of the building. You walk in through an old-world entrance and you arrive in a mid-twentieth-century hall. The two eras sit side by side, joined by one doorway. Few opera houses in Europe carry their history in such a visible way.

The new auditorium holds roughly 1,500 people. Its lines are cleaner and calmer than the gilded halls of the nineteenth century. Look up when you are inside. The ceiling is scattered with small points of light, and many people compare it to a night sky. When the house lights dim before a performance, that ceiling fades with them. It is a quiet, beautiful moment, and it gives the evening its first sense of anticipation.

Not everything inside is modern. The public spaces at the front of the building survived the fire, and they keep their older character. Here you find gilding, mirrors, painted surfaces and decorative plasterwork from the original theatre. During an interval, audiences gather in these rooms with a glass in hand. It is worth walking slowly through them. You are moving through the parts of the building that the flames did not reach.

In more recent years the theatre went through a long and careful renovation. It closed in the mid-2010s and reopened in 2019. The work restored the historic decoration and updated the technical machinery behind the stage. Modern opera needs complex lifts, lighting and sound, all hidden from the audience. So when you look at the building today, you see a careful balance. The old surfaces are cleaned and repaired, while new equipment works out of sight.

Today the Grand Théâtre is the largest stage for opera and dance in French-speaking Switzerland. It presents opera, and it is also home to its own ballet company. The programme mixes well-known works with newer and more experimental productions. If you are visiting Geneva for only a short time, an evening performance is one of the best ways to see the building as it was meant to be seen... full, lit, and alive with sound.

Now turn around and look at the square itself. Place de Neuve is a meeting point of Geneva's cultural life. Close by stands the Conservatoire de Musique, another fine nineteenth-century building where musicians train. Nearby is the Musée Rath, an art museum with its own row of columns. Together these buildings form a small district devoted to music and the arts. On many evenings, the sound of instruments practising drifts from open windows.

Just beyond the square lie the gates of Parc des Bastions. This green park sits below the old town, and it holds the long Reformation Wall, a monument to the leaders of the Protestant Reformation, the religious movement that shaped Geneva in the sixteenth century. On the other side, the streets climb up into the old town, with its narrow lanes and the cathedral at the top. The theatre sits at the foot of all this history, between the old city and the newer one.

Before you move on, take one more look at the façade. Notice how the stone holds the afternoon light, or how the windows glow if evening is coming. Think of the fire, the rebuilding, and the many audiences who have climbed these steps over almost one and a half centuries. Then listen again to the square around you... the trams, the voices, perhaps a distant violin. The theatre is not only a monument. It is still a working stage, waiting for its next performance.
