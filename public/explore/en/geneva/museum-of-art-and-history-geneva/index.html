The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Traffic hums along the boulevard below. Footsteps echo off pale stone. Then the heavy doors open, and the city noise drops away into a cool, quiet hall. This is the Museum of Art and History, the largest museum in Geneva. Locals simply call it the MAH. It sits on a rise just outside the old town, in the elegant Tranchées district.

Take a look at the building itself. It opened in 1910, and it was designed by the Geneva architect Marc Camoletti. The style is Beaux-Arts, a grand academic manner taught in Paris and loved by public institutions around 1900. Notice the symmetry, the carved stone, and the sense of a temple for culture. The museum was built to tell people that Geneva took its art and its past seriously.

Much of the money came from a private citizen. A Geneva banker named Charles Galland left a large bequest to the city, and it helped make this building possible. The street outside now carries his name. It is a very Genevan story. This is a city of discreet private wealth, and here that wealth was turned into something public, open to anyone who walks in.

The building is arranged around a central courtyard. Inside, the collections spread across several floors, and the range is wide. There is archaeology, fine art, and applied art, all under one roof. Think of it less as a single museum and more as a cabinet of the whole city. You can move from Egyptian tombs to Swiss landscapes in a few minutes. So don't try to see everything. Pick two or three rooms and give them time.

Start near the ground, with the deep past. The archaeology galleries hold objects from ancient Egypt, Greece, and Rome. There are also finds from Geneva itself. Long before it was a city of banks and diplomats, this was a settlement of a Celtic people called the Allobroges. Later it became a Roman town. Julius Caesar mentions Geneva in his own account of the Gallic Wars. Here is the first surprise: this lakeside city appears in one of the most famous books of the ancient world.

Look out for the carved stones. Old inscriptions and architectural fragments carry Latin letters cut nearly two thousand years ago. Some are worn smooth. Others are still sharp enough to read with your fingertip, though please only look, never touch. These pieces once stood in temples, tombs, and public buildings. Now they rest in quiet rows, and they show how much of the old city still sits just below the modern streets.

Now climb to the fine art floor, because the museum's greatest treasure waits there. It is an altarpiece by the painter Konrad Witz, dated 1444. The most famous panel shows the Miraculous Draught of Fishes, a Bible scene where Christ appears to his disciples on the water. But Witz did something remarkable. He did not paint the Sea of Galilee. He painted Lake Geneva.

Stand close and look at the background. You will see the shoreline, the fields, and the mountains beyond, painted as a real place that you can recognise today. Art historians often describe this as one of the earliest realistic landscapes in European painting. Before this, most painted backgrounds were symbolic or imagined. Here, a sacred story happens on the lake you crossed to get here. That is the second surprise, and it is a big one.

Look even more closely, and you may notice damage. The altarpiece was made for Saint Peter's Cathedral, up the hill in the old town. In the sixteenth century, Geneva became a centre of the Protestant Reformation, the movement led here by John Calvin. Reformers rejected religious images in churches, and some were destroyed. This altarpiece survived, but it carries the scars of that time. Some faces were scratched and defaced. The marks are still there, a quiet record of a city changing its faith.

Walk on through the painting galleries, and Swiss artists come forward. Look for Ferdinand Hodler, known for strong outlines, rhythm, and clear Alpine light. Look for Félix Vallotton, a Swiss painter who worked in Paris, with his cool, flat colours. And look for Jean-Étienne Liotard, an eighteenth-century Geneva artist famous for pastel portraits. Pastel is a soft, chalky colour, and in his hands skin and silk look almost real. Get close to see how delicate the surface is.

The applied art collections deserve time too. Here you find furniture, ceramics, silver, and textiles. There are also panelled period rooms, rebuilt inside the museum so you can step into the interiors of earlier centuries. Notice the wood, the low light, and the way the rooms feel lived in. Geneva was also a great centre of watchmaking and fine metalwork. Small objects here show the patience and precision that made the city's craftsmen respected across Europe.

The building has its own modern story. For years the city debated how to expand and modernise the museum. A major extension project by the French architect Jean Nouvel was proposed. In 2016, Geneva voters rejected the funding in a public vote. That is very Swiss: even the future of a museum can go to the ballot box. The debate continues, and the building still stands much as Camoletti designed it.

As you move through the halls, pay attention to the experience itself. The floors creak in places. Daylight falls from high windows. School groups whisper in front of armour and mummies. This is not a quiet shrine for experts. It is a working civic museum, where Geneva families come to see their own history next to the art of the wider world.

When you step back outside, pause on the steps. Below you lies the old town, with Saint Peter's Cathedral on the hill where the Witz altarpiece once hung. Beyond it, out of sight, is the lake that Witz painted almost six centuries ago. The mountains are still there. The water is still there. And now you carry that painted view with you as you walk back into the real one.
