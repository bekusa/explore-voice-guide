The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the wide paving of the Place des Nations. You may hear water first, low jets rising from the ground and falling back onto stone. Then look up. A wooden chair towers over you, taller than a house. It is dark brown and plain, the kind of chair you might find in any kitchen. But something is wrong with it. One of its four legs ends in a ragged, splintered stump. The chair stands anyway, as if it refuses to fall.

This is the Broken Chair, one of the most photographed sights in Geneva. It is not a monument to a king or a battle. It honours people whose names rarely appear on statues. These are the civilians who lost a leg, a foot, or their life to a landmine or a cluster bomb. The missing leg is the whole message. Once you see it, you cannot stop seeing it.

The sculpture was designed by the Swiss artist Daniel Berset. A carpenter named Louis Genève built it. The organisation behind the idea was Handicap International, a charity that works with people living with disabilities, including landmine survivors. Today the same group works under the name Humanity and Inclusion. The chair rises about twelve metres into the air. It is made from several tonnes of wood, joined with the care of traditional furniture making. The splintered leg is not damage. It was cut that way on purpose.

Here is the first surprise. The chair was never meant to stay. It went up in 1997, during a crucial moment in a global campaign. Governments were preparing to sign a treaty that would ban anti-personnel landmines. People know this agreement as the Ottawa Treaty, because the signing took place in Ottawa, the capital of Canada. The plan was simple. The chair would stand here for a few months as a reminder to diplomats. After the signing, it would come down.

It did not come down. The public grew attached to it. Visitors came to see it, and campaigners argued that the work was not finished. Many countries had still not joined the treaty. Cluster munitions, bombs that scatter many smaller explosives across a wide area, were still in use. So the chair stayed. A temporary protest slowly became a permanent part of the city. Few public artworks begin as a short-term message and end up as a landmark.

Now turn around and look behind you. Across the square stands the Palais des Nations. Today it houses the main United Nations office in Geneva. Before that, it was built for the League of Nations, the international body created after the First World War. The location of the chair is no accident. It faces the building where governments meet to negotiate. Every delegate who walks in or out can see it. The chair speaks to the diplomats as much as to the tourists.

Look along the front of the Palais and you will see a long row of flagpoles. The flags of the member states fly there side by side. From some angles, you can frame the broken chair against this line of colour. The contrast is quiet but strong. On one side is the formal world of treaties and signatures. On the other is a simple wooden object, damaged in the way human bodies are damaged by war.

Here is the second surprise. In the mid-2000s, the square went through a major renovation. The chair was taken away during the works. For a short time, this space stood without it. Then it returned, and it has stood here since. Its return showed how much the city now considered the chair part of this square. It was no longer just a campaign tool. It had become part of the identity of international Geneva.

Take a closer look at the details. Walk slowly around the base. Notice how the three whole legs end in clean, square feet. Then look at the fourth leg. The break is jagged, with long splinters pointing outward, as if the wood snapped in an explosion. The seat appears sturdy and level. The tall back rises straight upward. The artist chose a very ordinary design. That choice matters. An ordinary chair makes us think of ordinary people, sitting at home, living normal lives.

The square itself changes through the day and the year. On warm days, children run between the ground fountains and shriek when the water jumps unexpectedly. Office workers cross with coffee in their hands. Groups of students gather in front of the chair for photographs. At times, demonstrators fill the square with banners, because this is one of the main places in Geneva for peaceful protest. The chair often watches over these gatherings, a protest that never ended.

Humanity and Inclusion has long used this square for public events. Some years, people have brought pairs of shoes and piled them high near the chair. Each pair stands for a person, a symbol of the steps that many survivors can no longer take. If you visit during one of these events, you will see the square in a very different mood. On other days, the chair stands almost alone, and the message feels more personal and quiet.

It is worth thinking about the scale for a moment. Most memorials ask you to look at faces or names. This one asks you to imagine an absence. The missing leg forces your mind to complete the picture. You almost expect the chair to tip over. Yet it does not. Many visitors describe this tension as the strongest part of the experience. The sculpture is fragile and stubborn at the same time, like the people it represents.

If you have more time, the neighbourhood around you is full of related places. The International Red Cross and Red Crescent Museum lies a short walk up the hill. Its exhibitions deal with humanitarian work and the protection of people in war. The Ariana Museum, with its collection of ceramics and glass, sits nearby in a park. Guided tours of the Palais des Nations also start close to this square, though they require identification and advance planning.

Before you walk away, stand once more at a distance from the chair. Let your eye move from the splintered leg up to the top of the backrest. Listen to the fountains and the traffic of the avenue. Look at the flags behind you, moving in the wind. This chair was meant to stand here for a few months. Instead, it has stood for decades, asking the same quiet question. As you leave the square, notice how many people stop, look up, and fall silent for a moment.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning for a quieter, people-free square.
Stand far back to fit the whole chair in frame.
Pair your visit with the nearby Red Cross Museum.
Bring identification if you plan to tour the Palais.
Check the news for demonstrations planned on the square.
