The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the wind moves across a wide, flat field, and it hums against metal. Rows of aircraft stand on concrete and grass. Wings throw long shadows, and the paint on old fuselages has faded under many winters. The air smells of birch trees, damp earth, and a faint trace of old oil. You are standing among the machines that carried a country into the sky.

This is the Central Air Force Museum at Monino, a small town east of Moscow. It is one of the largest collections of aviation history anywhere in the world. Many aviation museums keep a few dozen aircraft under one roof. Here the collection spreads across an old airfield. There are well over a hundred aircraft, from early biplanes to supersonic jets, and you can see most of them in the open air.

The setting matters. Monino was a working military airfield. For decades it was also home to the Gagarin Air Force Academy, where the Soviet Union trained its senior air force officers. The academy was named after Yuri Gagarin, the first human to fly in space. The museum was founded at the airfield in the late 1950s. Many of the aircraft arrived here the most natural way. They flew in and landed on the runway themselves.

That detail explains a lot about what you see. Very few of these machines came in pieces on a truck. Pilots brought many of them in on their final flight, and they parked them here for good. When you walk between the rows, remember that most of these aircraft once took off from somewhere else. Their last touchdown was on this field.

Start inside the hangars if the weather is cold. Here the story usually begins with the early years of flight. You will see fragile frames of wood, fabric, and wire. The early aircraft look almost like large kites. Then the materials change. Wood gives way to aluminium, open cockpits close, and engines grow larger. In a short walk, you follow aviation from a few hundred metres off the ground to the edge of the stratosphere.

The Second World War section is quieter. Soviet aircraft from that war are displayed here, including fighters and ground-attack planes that fought on the Eastern Front. For many Russian families, these machines are not museum pieces. They are part of family memory. You may notice older visitors who stop for a long time in front of one particular plane. Sometimes a grandfather flew one, or repaired one, or watched one pass overhead.

Now step outside, onto the open field. This is where the scale changes. Here is the first surprise most visitors miss. One of the giants on this field is the Mil V-12, the largest helicopter ever built. It carries two huge rotors on long side wings, one on each side, instead of a single rotor on top. Only two were ever built. Stand beside its landing gear and look up. The machine is the size of a small building.

Not far away is another rare aircraft, the Sukhoi T-4. It is a long, sharp-nosed bomber prototype from the Cold War era. It was designed to fly at about three times the speed of sound. The programme was cancelled, and this airframe is the only one that survives. Its white body and needle-like nose look more like a rocket than a plane. Many people walk past it without knowing that they are looking at the last of its kind.

The passenger airliners tell a different story. Here stands a Tupolev Tu-144, the Soviet supersonic airliner. It was the Soviet answer to the Anglo-French Concorde, and it flew before Concorde did. Look at the nose. Like Concorde, it could tilt downward to give pilots a better view during landing. Look also at the small extra wings just behind the cockpit. They folded out to help the aircraft fly slowly near the ground. That feature is one way to tell it apart from its Western rival.

The Cold War bombers sit in a heavy, silent row. Their tails rise high above the grass, and some of their wingspans seem to go on and on. These aircraft were built to fly across oceans. Standing under them, you feel the strange mix of feelings in this place. There is real engineering skill here, and a great deal of human effort. There is also the memory of a long period when the world lived with the fear of war.

Here is the second surprise. Some of the most interesting objects here are not complete aircraft at all. Look for unusual experimental machines and their remains. One example is the Bartini VVA-14, a strange amphibious aircraft. It was designed to take off from water and fly very low over the sea. Its surviving parts look almost like scrap metal. Yet they show how far Soviet designers were willing to go with new ideas.

Take your time with the details. Look at the rivets, the patched panels, and the faded red stars. Many aircraft show their age honestly. The paint is chipped, and rust appears at the seams. Restoration work continues, and you may see some aircraft looking fresher than others. Open-air display is hard on metal. Each snowy winter and each wet spring leaves a mark on these machines.

The museum is large, so plan for a long visit. Wear comfortable shoes, because much of the walking is on concrete and uneven ground. In summer the open field can feel hot, with little shade between the aircraft. In winter, the wind crosses the field with nothing to stop it. Bring warm layers. On a quiet weekday, you may have whole rows of aircraft almost to yourself.

Before you leave, stop in an open space and turn slowly in a full circle. Notice the long line of tails against the sky, the birch trees at the edge of the field, and the sound of the wind against metal. These aircraft will not fly again. But they still face the runway, as if they are waiting. As you walk back toward the gate, listen... somewhere above Monino, there may still be the sound of an engine.
