The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves through the dry grass with a low, steady hiss. Somewhere below, a prairie dog gives a sharp, chirping bark. The ground under your feet is crumbly and pale, and the air smells faintly of sage. Ahead of you, the land drops away into a maze of striped hills: grey, tan, red, and black. This is the badlands of western North Dakota, and you are standing in Theodore Roosevelt National Park.

The park sits along the Little Missouri River, and it is split into three separate pieces. The South Unit lies beside the small town of Medora, the seat of Billings County. The North Unit is roughly an hour and more to the north. Between them, in a quiet bend of the river, is the Elkhorn Ranch Unit. It is the smallest piece, and it is the most personal. These three places together tell the story of one young man and one hard landscape.

That young man arrived in 1883. Theodore Roosevelt was in his mid-twenties, a New York politician with thick glasses and a wealthy family. He came west to hunt bison, which were already close to disappearing. He found few bison. He also found something he did not expect. He found that he loved this country. Before he left, he bought into a cattle ranch here. Locals were reportedly amused by the eastern dude. They did not stay amused for long.

Here is the part many visitors do not know. In February 1884, Roosevelt lost his mother and his wife on the same day, in the same house in New York. His wife had given birth only days before. In his diary he drew a large cross and wrote that the light had gone out of his life. Grieving, he returned to the badlands. He worked cattle, rode long hours, and wrote. This land was where he went to rebuild himself.

If you are in the South Unit, look for the Maltese Cross Cabin near the visitor center in Medora. It is a small log cabin, simple and solid. Roosevelt used it during his early ranching years. The cabin has been moved more than once since then, so it does not stand on its original site. Still, the rough logs and low rooms give you a real sense of how modest his life here was. There was no grand house, only work and weather.

The Elkhorn Ranch was his second, more remote ranch. Today only stone foundation blocks remain where the house once stood. There are no walls, no roof, just cottonwood trees and the river. Roosevelt later said, in effect, that he would never have become president without his years in North Dakota. When you stand at Elkhorn, that idea feels less like a slogan. The silence is the point. It is the quiet that shaped him.

Now look at the hills themselves. Those bands of colour are layers of sediment, laid down over millions of years. Rivers carried mud, sand, and ash eastward from the rising Rocky Mountains. Then the Little Missouri and its streams cut down through it all. Rain and wind keep carving it today. Much of the grey layer is a clay called bentonite. When it gets wet, it turns as slick as soap, so trails can become dangerous after rain.

Notice the black stripes in the hillsides. Those are seams of lignite, a soft brown coal. Sometimes lightning or a grass fire ignites a seam, and it can smoulder underground for years. The heat bakes the clay above it into a hard, red rock that locals call scoria. That is a second surprise of this park: much of the red you see was made by fire burning inside the earth. Look for it capping the buttes and lining the roads.

And look for life. Bison roam both the South and North Units. They vanished from this region in the late nineteenth century and were brought back in the 1950s. Elk were returned later. In the South Unit, bands of wild horses graze the open slopes, descendants of ranch and reservation horses. Prairie dog towns spread across the flats like small cities. Keep a long distance from all of them. A bison looks slow, but it can run much faster than you.

If you drive to the North Unit, the landscape changes. The canyons are deeper and greener, with juniper on the shaded slopes. One stop shows round stones sitting in the hillside, called cannonball concretions. Minerals collected around a centre underground and hardened into these balls. Erosion now exposes them like they were placed by hand. The scenic road ends high above the river at the Oxbow Overlook, where the Little Missouri makes a wide turn.

The park itself grew in stages. It was first set aside as a national memorial park in 1947, honouring Roosevelt's connection to this land. In 1978, Congress made it a full national park. That fits his legacy. As president, he protected huge areas of public land, including national forests, bird refuges, and national monuments. Many historians trace that conservation instinct back to what he saw here: bison gone, grasslands overgrazed, a fragile country under pressure.

Medora, the town at the park's edge, has its own story. It was founded in the same year Roosevelt arrived, by a French aristocrat, the Marquis de Morès. He named it after his wife. He built a meat-packing business that did not last. Today the town is small and walkable, with wooden boardwalks and false-front buildings. It is a good place to slow down before or after the drive, and to picture the rough cattle town Roosevelt knew.

Painted Canyon, along the interstate on the South Unit's eastern side, gives many travellers their first view of the badlands. Early morning and late evening are the best times to come. The low sun brings out the reds and the greys, and the shadows make the ridges look deeper. Animals are also more active in the cool hours. At midday in summer, the light goes flat and the heat can be strong, so carry plenty of water.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Feel the wind on your face. Look at the layers in the nearest hill, each one a slice of very old time. Listen for the prairie dogs, the meadowlarks, maybe the distant snort of a bison. A grieving young man once rode through this same quiet and came out stronger. As you walk back to the road, notice how small the sounds are... and how much space surrounds them.

Keep a long distance from bison and wild horses.
