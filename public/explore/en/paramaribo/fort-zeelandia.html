The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves against the riverbank, brown and slow, and somewhere a boat engine hums across the Suriname River. The air is warm and heavy. Under your feet the ground is firm, packed earth and old brick. In front of you, low walls of weathered brick rise from the grass. This is Fort Zeelandia, and it stands at the very point where the city of Paramaribo began.

The fort does not tower over you. It sits low and square-shouldered, built to hold the riverbank rather than to impress. That was the point. Ships coming upriver from the Atlantic had to pass this bend, and whoever held this ground held the gate to the colony. Many visitors expect a castle. Instead they find something closer to a fortified courtyard, with thick walls built to stop cannon fire.

The history of this spot begins before the brick. In the first half of the seventeenth century, European traders and settlers built simple wooden posts along this river. The English arrived in force in the 1650s, under Francis Willoughby, and a fort here carried his name. The Indigenous peoples of the region had lived along these rivers for a very long time before any of them came. Their story is older than every wall you see.

Then, in 1667, a fleet from the Dutch province of Zeeland sailed up the river. It was led by a commander named Abraham Crijnssen. His ships captured the English fort, and the new owners renamed it after their home province. That is why a fort in South America carries the name Zeelandia. The name means, simply, sea land, which suits a low coast where land and water meet.

Here is the first surprise. That same year, the Dutch and English signed the Treaty of Breda to end their war. Under its terms, each side kept what it held. The Dutch kept Suriname. The English kept a small Dutch trading town in North America called New Amsterdam. Today we know that town as New York. So, in a sense, this quiet riverside fort sits on one side of one of history's strangest exchanges.

Look at the shape of the walls as you walk. The fort follows the logic of European military design of its time, with angled bastions. A bastion is a pointed corner that sticks out from the wall, so defenders could fire along the walls at attackers. Corners like these leave no blind spots. The brick is mostly small and tightly laid, in reddish and yellowish tones, darkened in places by centuries of tropical rain.

The colony that grew behind these walls was built on plantations. Sugar, coffee, cotton and cacao were grown along the rivers, and the work was done by enslaved Africans. This fort protected that economy. It is important to say this plainly while you stand here. Many people resisted, and some escaped into the rainforest, where they built free communities. Their descendants, known as Maroons, are still a living part of Suriname today.

Over time, the fort's role changed. As the colony grew, the military importance of the walls faded. In later periods the buildings were used as barracks and also as a prison. The thick walls that once kept enemies out were turned to keep people in. You can feel that shift in the narrow rooms and small windows. These spaces were made for control, not for comfort.

Now the second surprise, and the hardest part of this story. In December 1982, during the years of military rule in Suriname, fifteen prominent critics of the regime were brought to this fort and killed. They included journalists, lawyers, union leaders and academics. Suriname remembers this as the December murders. For decades, families pressed for justice, and a long trial finally took place in this century. For many Surinamese, this is the most painful memory this place holds.

Today the fort houses a museum run by the Suriname Museum Foundation. The exhibits cover the country's colonial past, its many peoples, and everyday objects from earlier times. Suriname is a meeting point of Indigenous, African, Asian, Jewish and European heritage. You will hear several languages in its streets. The museum tries to hold all of these threads together in one place, inside walls that were once built for only one side of the story.

Step back outside and look along the riverfront. The fort marks the edge of the historic inner city of Paramaribo, which UNESCO listed as a World Heritage Site in 2002. Much of that old city is built from wood, painted white with dark trim. The contrast is striking. The wooden houses feel light and open to the breeze. The fort feels heavy, closed and grounded. Together they show two ways of living in this climate.

A short walk away lies Independence Square, a wide open space surrounded by government buildings, including the Presidential Palace. Behind the palace, tall royal palms stand in rows in the garden called the Palmentuin. In the late afternoon, the light comes in low across the river and turns the brick warm and golden. The heat eases a little. It is a good hour to sit near the water and simply watch the boats.

Before you go, take one more look at the river. It is wide, muddy and unhurried, the same water that carried warships, trading vessels and people in chains. The fort has watched all of it. It has been a gate, a prison, a place of grief, and now a place of memory. Run your hand along the brick if you can. It is warm from the sun. Then turn toward the white wooden streets, and carry the river with you as you walk into the city.
