The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the wide granite slabs of the Praza do Obradoiro. The stone underfoot is grey, worn smooth, and often slick with Galician rain. Around you, footsteps echo from every side of the square. Somewhere a bagpipe may be playing under an archway. Pilgrims drop their backpacks, sit down on the cold stone, and stare up at the cathedral. Now turn to face the long building on the north side of the square. This is the Hostal dos Reis Católicos.

The name means the Hostel of the Catholic Monarchs. Those monarchs were Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile, the royal couple who united much of Spain at the end of the fifteenth century. They visited Santiago as pilgrims themselves. They saw how many travellers arrived here sick, exhausted, and with nowhere to sleep. Their answer was this building, a royal hospital for pilgrims, founded around the year 1500.

It is important to understand what hospital meant at that time. It was not only a place for medicine. It was a place of shelter, food, and care for anyone on the road. People came from across Europe on foot to reach the tomb of Saint James, which lies inside the cathedral across the square. Many walked for months. Some arrived with fevers or injuries. Some never left. This building received them all.

The design is usually credited to Enrique Egas, one of the leading royal architects of his day. He worked in the style that Spanish historians call Plateresque. The word comes from the Spanish for silversmith. It describes stone carving so fine and so dense that it looks like the decoration on silver plate. Look at the main doorway in the centre of the façade. That is where you can see the style most clearly.

Walk a little closer to the portal. The carving climbs up around the door in layers. There are columns, small figures of saints, and decorative bands of leaves and shells. Look for the two round medallions with carved portraits. They show Ferdinand and Isabella, the founders, keeping watch over the entrance to their own gift to the pilgrims. Above them, the royal coats of arms are cut deep into the granite.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. The rest of the façade is surprisingly plain. Long rows of windows run along a calm, flat wall. All the energy of the building gathers in that single doorway. It works almost like a frame around a painting. Your eye has nowhere else to go. Then look up along the roofline. Small carved figures and water spouts project from the top edge of the wall.

Now think about the setting. This square holds several different centuries side by side. The cathedral fills the east side with its towering Baroque façade, the dramatic, richly ornamented style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The long neoclassical town hall, the Pazo de Raxoi, closes the west side. The old college of San Xerome sits on the south side. The hostal brings the early Renaissance to this mix. Together they show how Santiago grew around one tomb.

Inside, the old hospital was organised around four courtyards, or cloisters. The plan followed a cross shape, with the chapel at its heart. The idea was practical as well as spiritual. Sick pilgrims in the wards could hear the Mass being said without leaving their beds. Light and air reached every wing from the open courtyards. For its time, this was a carefully considered design for caring for large numbers of people.

Here is the second surprise. The building is still receiving travellers today. In the 1950s, it was converted into a parador, one of the network of state-run hotels in historic Spanish buildings. Because it has welcomed guests almost without pause for about five centuries, it is often described as one of the oldest hotels in the world. The guests now arrive by car and taxi, not only on foot. But the purpose has not changed very much.

The link with the pilgrims has also survived in a small way. There is a long-standing tradition of the hostal offering a meal to a limited number of pilgrims who have completed the Way of Saint James. The details have changed over the years, so it is best to ask locally if you have walked the route. Even so, the custom carries the old spirit of the building into the present.

If you go inside, the courtyards are the quiet heart of the place. Water, stone, and green plants replace the noise of the square. The granite here is the same grey stone that builds most of old Santiago. In wet weather it turns darker, almost silver. Moss grows in the joints. The rain in Galicia is famous, and it has shaped the look of the whole city. Every surface you see has weathered centuries of it.

Take a moment to notice the people around you on the square. You will probably see pilgrims in boots, holding a scallop shell, which is the traditional symbol of the Way of Saint James. Some are crying. Some are laughing. Some just lie flat on the stone and look at the sky. Five hundred years ago, people like them crossed this same ground and knocked on that carved door.

Before you move on, step back to the centre of the square once more. Look at the long, calm wall of the hostal and that one rich doorway. Listen to the footsteps, the voices, perhaps the bagpipes under the arches. Feel the cool stone under your feet. When you walk away, you leave by the same paths that tired pilgrims followed toward a bed and a warm meal here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stand at the square's centre to see all four façades.
Visit after rain when the granite turns dark silver.
Bring a light rain jacket; Galician showers arrive fast.
Ask at reception whether courtyards are open to visitors.
Pilgrims should ask locally about the traditional pilgrim meal.
