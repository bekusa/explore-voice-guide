The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the heat coming off the cliff behind you. The rock face is pale and warm, and the air smells of dust and dry grass. In front of you rises a square stone tower. It is plain, solid, and quiet. Its walls are pale limestone, and its surface is set with dark rectangles that look like windows. Look closer, though... none of them open. This is Ka'ba-ye Zartosht, the Cube of Zoroaster, and nobody is completely sure what it was for.

You are at Naqsh-e Rustam, a short distance from the ruins of ancient Istakhr and not far from Persepolis. This valley was sacred ground for more than one Persian dynasty. The Achaemenids came first. They ruled one of the largest empires of the ancient world, from roughly the sixth to the fourth century before Christ. Later came the Sasanians, a Persian dynasty of the third to the seventh century after Christ. Istakhr was their ancestral home. Both dynasties left their mark on this same stretch of cliff.

The tower itself belongs to the Achaemenid period. Most scholars place it in the fifth century before Christ, though the exact date is still debated. It stands about twelve metres tall. The builders used large blocks of carefully cut limestone, fitted together without mortar. Iron clamps once held some blocks in place. The dark rectangles are made of a darker stone, set into the pale walls. From a distance, they make the tower look like a house with many windows. It is a trick of design, not a real opening.

Here is the first surprise. The name you hear today has nothing to do with the builders. Ka'ba-ye Zartosht means the Cube of Zoroaster. Zoroaster was the prophet of the ancient Persian religion called Zoroastrianism. But this name came much later, from local people who saw a mysterious cube near royal tombs and gave it a sacred-sounding title. The word Ka'ba simply means cube. The Achaemenid name for the building, if it had one, has not survived.

So what was it? Scholars have proposed several ideas. Some think it held a sacred fire. Others suggest it stored royal objects, or important religious texts. Another theory says it was a tomb for a member of the royal family. Some researchers have even studied its walls for links to the solar calendar. None of these ideas is fully proven. A very similar tower once stood at Pasargadae, the earlier Achaemenid capital, but only one wall of it remains. That ruined twin suggests these towers had a shared purpose.

Now look at the single doorway, high on one face of the tower. A stone staircase leads up to it. The room inside is small and dark, with thick walls and a heavy stone ceiling. There was once a heavy door here, and the stone frame shows where it turned. Whatever was kept inside, the builders wanted it protected. A high entrance, thick walls, and one narrow opening... this was not a building for crowds. It was a building for something precious.

Notice also that the tower sits lower than the ground around you. Over many centuries, soil and debris built up around its base. Excavations in the twentieth century cleared this earth away and exposed the lower walls again. That is why you now look slightly down into a wide pit. The original ground level of the Achaemenid site lay much deeper than the path you walked in on. In a sense, you are standing on centuries of dust.

Turn around and face the cliff. Cut high into the rock are four great tombs, each shaped like a cross. These are the tombs of Achaemenid kings. One of them belongs to Darius the First, and an inscription carved there identifies him. The others are generally linked to his successors, including his son Xerxes. Each tomb front copies the face of a palace, with columns carved into the stone. The tower and the tombs face each other across this open ground. That placement was very likely deliberate.

Here is the second surprise, and it is on the tower itself. Walk to the lower walls and look for lines of writing. Around 260 years after Christ, the Sasanian king Shapur the First had a long inscription carved here. It is written in three languages: Middle Persian, Parthian, and Greek. Shapur used it to record his wars against Rome. He describes defeating Roman armies and capturing the emperor Valerian. Historians treat this text as one of the most important sources for this period.

Think about what that means. Shapur did not build a new monument for his victory text. He chose an old Achaemenid tower, already centuries old in his own day. By carving his words here, beside the tombs of ancient kings, he linked his new dynasty to the great Persian past. It was a political message in stone. Later, a powerful Zoroastrian priest named Kartir added his own inscription to the tower too. He described his religious work and his rise in the Sasanian court.

Below the royal tombs, you can also see large Sasanian rock reliefs. One famous scene shows Shapur on horseback, with Roman figures before him in submission. Another shows the founder of the Sasanian dynasty, Ardashir the First, receiving a ring of kingship from a god. These carvings speak to the tower and its inscriptions. Together they turn this valley into an open-air record of power. Achaemenid tombs above, Sasanian victories below, and the quiet cube in the middle.

As you walk around the tower, pay attention to the light. In the morning, the cliff and the stone glow soft and golden. By midday, the shadows shorten and the dark false windows stand out sharply against the pale walls. Late in the afternoon, the tombs fall into shade while the tower still catches warm light. The changes are slow, but they are worth waiting for. The site is open and exposed, so the sun shapes everything you see here.

Before you leave, stand back from the tower once more. Look at its simple square shape against the cliff and the sky. Look at the false windows that never let in light, and the single high door. Kings, priests, and travellers have stood where you are standing now. Each of them wondered, as you might, what this building really held. As you walk back toward the road, listen to the wind across the open ground... and let the question stay with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning for softer light on the cliff.
Bring water and a hat; the site has little shade.
Walk the full circuit to read every tower face.
Combine your visit with Persepolis on the same day.
Use a zoom lens to capture the high tomb carvings.
