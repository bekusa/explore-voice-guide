The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the heat coming off the rock. The cliff in front of you is a pale, sun-baked wall of limestone, and the air here is dry enough to taste. Somewhere a bird calls, and the sound bounces back off the stone. Then you lift your eyes, and you see it, a giant cross cut into the living rock, high above the ground, out of reach.

This is the Tomb of Darius the Great, one of the most powerful kings of the ancient world. The site is called Naqsh-e Rostam, and it sits close to the ruins of Istakhr, a city that was important in this valley for centuries. Persepolis, the great ceremonial capital of the Persian kings, is only a short drive away. Many people visit the palaces first and then come here, and that order makes sense. At Persepolis you see how these kings lived. Here, you see how they chose to be remembered.

Darius ruled the Achaemenid Empire, the first Persian empire, from 522 to 486 BC. At its height, his realm stretched from the edges of India to Egypt and into southeastern Europe. He reorganised the empire into provinces, built roads, and started much of the building work at Persepolis. He also left behind long inscriptions in his own words. That habit matters here, because it is the reason we can say with confidence that this tomb is his.

Look at the facade carefully. It is shaped like a cross, and it is divided into three levels. The lowest part is plain and smooth. The middle part is carved to look like the front of a palace, with four columns and a doorway in the centre. The top of each column shows the front halves of two kneeling bulls, back to back. You can see the same type of column top among the ruins of Persepolis. The tomb is, in a sense, a palace front made permanent in stone.

Now raise your eyes to the top level. Here the king stands on a stepped platform. He faces a fire altar and raises one hand. Above the scene floats a winged figure in a ring. Many scholars link this figure with Ahura Mazda, the supreme god Darius praised in his inscriptions. Fire held deep religious meaning in ancient Iran, and that meaning still lives on in the Zoroastrian faith today. The whole scene is quiet and formal. There is no battle and no enemy. There is only a king at prayer.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Look below the king's platform. It is held up by two rows of small figures, their arms raised above their heads. They are not slaves. They stand for the many peoples of the empire, and each one wears different clothing, hair, or headgear. Inscriptions name them. Together they carry their king, and the message is clear. This empire was made of many nations, and all of them supported the throne.

Those inscriptions deserve a closer look, even if you can only see them from a distance. They are carved in three languages: Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian. Elamite was the old language of this region before the Persians, and Babylonian was used across Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. One text lists the lands Darius ruled. Another reads almost like a personal statement. In it, the king describes himself as a just ruler, a skilled horseman and archer, and a man who keeps his temper under control.

Now turn and count the crosses along the cliff. There are four royal tombs here, all cut in the same style. Darius's tomb is the only one with inscriptions that name its owner. The others are usually linked with later kings of his family, such as his son Xerxes, but scholars do not agree on every attribution. That is the second surprise of this place. Of these four great tombs, only one actually tells us who was buried inside.

Behind that dark doorway, the rock opens into chambers with stone burial spaces. They were emptied long ago, and no royal body has been found here. Visitors usually cannot climb up to the entrance today, and the height alone shows why. Imagine the work. Stone cutters had to hang from the cliff or build scaffolding, and they carved down from the top. One wrong strike on the rock could not be undone.

Lower down, close to ground level, you will see carvings of a very different style. These are Sasanian reliefs, made by a later Persian dynasty several centuries after Darius. The Sasanian kings chose this cliff on purpose, to link themselves with the old rulers. The most famous panel shows the Sasanian king Shapur the First on horseback. A Roman emperor kneels before him. Rome lost that war, and Persia carved the moment into stone, right beneath the tombs of its ancestors.

The name of the site comes from much later. Naqsh-e Rostam means roughly the picture of Rostam. Rostam is a great hero of Persian legend, made famous in the Shahnameh, the national epic poem written around a thousand years ago. Over time, local people forgot who the kings in these carvings really were. According to popular belief, the giant mounted figures must be Rostam himself. The real kings were forgotten, but the hero's name stayed on the cliff.

Before you leave, turn around and look at the square stone tower that stands in front of the cliff. It is called the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht, which means the Cube of Zoroaster. Its purpose is still debated. Some people think it was a fire temple. Others think it was a tomb or a storehouse for sacred objects. Later, the Sasanians carved a long inscription on its walls. The tower is a good reminder that experts still have open questions about this site.

Take a final look up at the cross of stone. The king is still there at his altar, with his peoples holding up his platform, as they have for more than two thousand years. The palace he ruled from is in ruins, and the city of Istakhr is mostly reduced to fields and low mounds. But this carved cliff face still carries his own words. As you walk away, listen to your footsteps on the gravel, and notice how quickly the cliff becomes a pale line against the hills again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Bring a hat, sunscreen, and plenty of water.
Combine this visit with nearby Persepolis on the same day.
Use binoculars or a zoom lens to read the carvings.
Walk to the stone tower opposite for a full cliff view.
