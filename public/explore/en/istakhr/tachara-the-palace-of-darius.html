The guide

The story

Run your eyes along the stone doorframe in front of you. The surface is dark grey, almost black, and polished so finely that it still holds a dull shine. On a clear morning the light slides across it like water. Visitors have called this building the Hall of Mirrors for generations, and when you stand close you can see why. Faces and sky once reflected in these walls. Some faint trace of that sheen is still here, after roughly two and a half thousand years.

You are standing in Tachara, the private palace of Darius the Great, on the stone terrace of Persepolis. The ruins of the old city of Istakhr lie a few kilometres to the north, across the same wide plain. Istakhr grew into an important town in later centuries. This terrace, though, belongs to an earlier world. It is the ceremonial heart of the Achaemenid Empire, the Persian dynasty that ruled from the Aegean coast to the edges of India.

Darius came to the throne in the late sixth century before Christ. He chose this spot at the foot of a mountain the locals now call Kuh-e Rahmat, the Mountain of Mercy. Builders levelled a vast platform of rock and began raising halls on top of it. Tachara was one of the first. It is also one of the smallest palaces on the terrace. That size is part of its character. This was not a hall for crowds. It was a more personal space for the king himself.

Look at how the palace faces. Its main entrance opens toward the south, toward the sun. Most of the grand halls nearby face other directions. Scholars still discuss why. A southern face would have caught warmth in winter and gentle light through much of the year. Whatever the reason, the effect today is simple. In the late afternoon the reliefs on the southern side glow with a warm colour, and their carved edges become sharp and clear.

Now look carefully at the doorways. Carved into the stone jambs are figures of the king. In one scene he walks forward with attendants behind him. One servant holds a parasol over his head. Another carries a fly whisk and a small cloth. In other doorways the king grapples with a lion or a fantastic beast, stabbing it with a short blade. Historians call this figure the royal hero. It is not a hunting scene. It is a statement that the king holds back chaos and keeps order in the world.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look for the small blocks of writing carved above and beside the figures. These are royal inscriptions, written in three languages. Old Persian, the language of the kings. Elamite, the old administrative language of this region. And Babylonian, used across Mesopotamia. One short text announces that Darius, the great king, built this palace. An empire of many peoples needed many languages, and the king made sure each one could read his claim.

Darius did not see the palace finished. His son Xerxes completed the work, and Xerxes also left his own inscriptions here. Later kings added their own touches as well. The stairways leading up to the palace carry rows of carved servants. They climb the steps carrying lambs, covered bowls, wineskins and other food. Picture them as the staff of a royal banquet, frozen mid-step, forever bringing the next course to the king's table.

Take a moment to notice what is missing. The doorways and window frames are stone, but the walls between them were mostly mudbrick. The roof rested on wooden beams and columns. In 330 BC, Alexander of Macedon took Persepolis, and the terrace burned. The timber and much of the brick disappeared. The stone frames survived. That is why Tachara looks the way it does today, a forest of empty doorways standing open to the sky.

The second surprise is written right on these stones. After the fall of the Achaemenids, travellers kept coming here. Some left their mark. In later centuries, princes and officials of the Sasanian dynasty, the last Persian empire before Islam, carved short texts into this palace. Rulers and visitors of the Islamic period added inscriptions in Arabic script too. So this one small building carries layers of writing from many ages, like pages added to the same book.

The layers continue into much more recent times. European travellers of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries also carved their names into the walls of Persepolis, some with dates beside them. Today we see this as damage, and visitors must never add to it. Yet these marks also tell a story. They show how this terrace drew people across centuries, all of them wanting to leave proof that they had stood where you are standing now.

The stone itself deserves attention. It was cut from the mountain behind you, a dense limestone that takes a high polish. Craftsmen smoothed it with abrasives until it gleamed. Look closely at the carved figures and you will see the fine work in beards, in the folds of robes, in the curled tails of beasts. The details are small, crisp and patient. Imagine the sound of chisels on this terrace, many seasons of tapping and scraping, under the same hard sun.

Persepolis was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979, and conservation work here never really stops. You may see supports, protective covers or fenced areas. They exist because sun, wind and winter frost wear the stone a little every year. Keep to the paths and barriers. The polished surfaces are especially fragile, and oils from hands slowly dull them. The kindest thing a visitor can do is look closely and touch nothing.

Before you leave, turn around and look up at the hillside. Carved into the mountain are royal tombs, high above the terrace. From up there, Tachara becomes a small rectangle of doorways among the larger halls. Down here, it feels intimate, almost quiet. That contrast is worth holding for a moment. The palace of a man who ruled a vast empire is a building you can walk through in a few minutes.

So stand in one of these doorways a little longer. Feel the cool shade of the stone on one side and the open light on the other. Listen to the wind moving across the terrace, and to the voices of other travellers drifting between the columns. As you walk on, glance back once at that dark, polished stone. It caught the light for Darius. It is catching the light for you now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit late afternoon to see the south reliefs in warm light.
Bring a hat and water, since shade on the terrace is scarce.
Climb toward the hillside tombs for an overview of the palace.
Keep hands off the polished stone to protect its surface.
Combine your visit with the Istakhr ruins a short drive north.
