The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, a bell rings once and the sound rolls down through the pines. Stone steps run up into the mist, worn smooth and slightly hollow in the middle by centuries of feet. The air is cool and damp, and it carries a faint smell of incense. You are standing in the Wudang Mountains, in northwestern Hubei Province, a short journey from the city of Shiyan. For many Chinese people, this is one of the most sacred places in Taoism.

Taoism is one of China's oldest spiritual traditions. At its heart is the Tao, often translated as "the Way": the natural order that flows through everything. Taoists seek harmony with nature rather than control over it. So it makes sense that their holy places are mountains. Peaks were seen as places where heaven and earth come close together. Wudang has drawn hermits, monks and pilgrims for well over a thousand years, long before most of the buildings around you existed.

The god most closely linked to these mountains is Zhenwu, the Perfected Warrior. He is a guardian of the north, and in older traditions he appears as a turtle wrapped by a snake. According to legend, Zhenwu spent many years practising on these slopes before he rose to heaven as an immortal. Pilgrims climb in his footsteps. As you walk, watch for his image in the halls: a calm figure, often barefoot, with long loose hair.

Here is the first surprise. Much of what you see is not the slow work of humble hermits. It is the result of one enormous imperial project. In the early fifteenth century, the Yongle Emperor of the Ming dynasty ordered a vast building campaign across these mountains. The same emperor also moved the capital to Beijing and built the Forbidden City there. Historical accounts describe a workforce in the hundreds of thousands, cutting stone and hauling timber across the slopes for more than a decade.

Why would an emperor spend so much here? Yongle had taken the throne by force, from his own nephew. He claimed that Zhenwu had protected him during that struggle. Honouring the northern warrior god on a grand scale was an act of gratitude. It was also a clear political message: heaven was on his side. The result was a network of palaces, temples and pavilions, connected by paved pilgrim paths, spread over a huge stretch of the mountains.

The planners did something careful. They did not flatten the land to suit the buildings. Instead, they placed each hall to follow the ridges, cliffs and valleys. Walls curve with the slope. Roofs sit tucked into hollows in the rock. This was a Taoist idea in stone and timber: build with nature, not against it. In 1994, UNESCO listed the ancient building complex here as a World Heritage Site, partly for this close fit between architecture and landscape.

One of the most loved halls is the Purple Heaven Palace. It climbs the hillside in a series of terraces, with red walls, green-glazed roof tiles and broad stone stairways. Stand at the bottom and look up. Each level frames the next, so the climb itself becomes part of the ceremony. You may see Taoist priests in dark blue robes, hair tied up in a topknot, crossing the courtyards. This is a working religious place, not only a monument.

Further along, the Nanyan Palace, or South Cliff Palace, takes a bolder approach. Its halls cling to the face of a sheer cliff. The most famous detail here is a narrow stone beam carved like a dragon's head, pushing out over the drop, with a small incense burner on its tip. Locals say that pilgrims once crawled out along it to offer incense. It was so dangerous that the practice was later forbidden. Today you look at it from behind a railing.

Now the second surprise. The summit of the highest peak, the Heavenly Pillar Peak, carries a Golden Hall made of cast bronze and covered in gold. It was completed in the early fifteenth century. The pieces were cast elsewhere and carried up the mountain, then joined on the peak. The hall is small, but it shines in sunlight like a jewel. Around the summit runs a stone wall known as the Purple Forbidden City, an echo of the palace in Beijing.

The Golden Hall has a local legend of its own. During thunderstorms, people say, lightning strikes around the hall and great balls of fire roll across its roof. Afterwards, the bronze looks brighter, as if it had been polished. Locals describe this as heaven cleaning the hall. Whatever the science behind it, the story tells you how people see this summit: as a place where the sky reaches down and touches the earth.

Many visitors know Wudang for another reason: martial arts. Wudang styles stress softness, breath and inner energy rather than raw strength. A famous legend credits a Taoist monk named Zhang Sanfeng with creating tai chi here, after he watched a fight between a bird and a snake. Historians treat this story as folklore, not record. Still, martial arts schools around the mountain keep the tradition alive. In early mornings, you may see students moving slowly together in the courtyards.

As you climb, notice the small things. Moss grows in the joints of the steps. Red ribbons and padlocks hang from railings, left by pilgrims with wishes and prayers. Smoke from thick incense sticks drifts across the courtyards. Old cypress trees stand beside the gates, some propped up with supports. Turtles and snakes appear in carvings, a quiet nod to Zhenwu. The higher you go, the thinner the crowds and the louder the wind becomes.

Weather changes the whole experience here. On clear days, ridge after ridge folds away into blue distance. On misty days, the halls appear and disappear like ships in fog, and the sounds of bells and voices seem very close. Both are true faces of this mountain. Taoists would say that neither one is better. The mountain is simply being what it is, and the visitor's task is to notice it.

So pause for a moment where you stand. Feel the stone under your feet, cool and uneven. Listen for the bell, the wind in the pines, the soft steps of someone else climbing. Six centuries ago, workers carried these stones up the same slopes, and pilgrims came long before them. As you move on, take the next stairway slowly. On Wudang, the way up has always mattered as much as the summit.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes with good grip for steep stone steps.
Bring a warm layer since the summit is often windy.
Arrive early to watch martial arts practice in courtyards.
Walk quietly in halls where priests hold ceremonies.
Allow a full day to visit several palaces properly.
