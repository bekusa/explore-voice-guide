The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, a langur lands on a branch, and the dry sal leaves shiver and drop. The air smells of dust, wood smoke, and something sweet and heavy. In the right season, that sweetness comes from mahua flowers, fallen on the forest floor. The ground is red here, and soft in places. Every footprint stays. This is Betla National Park, in the Latehar district of Jharkhand, in eastern India. And everything in this forest leaves a trace.

Betla is the heart of a much larger protected landscape, the Palamau Tiger Reserve. When India launched Project Tiger in the early 1970s, Palamau was among the first reserves chosen. That small group of founding reserves marked a turning point. For the first time, the country set aside whole forests to protect its tigers. Betla is the part most visitors reach. It is the doorway to a wild area much bigger than anything you can see from the road.

Here is the first surprise. This region holds a quiet place in the history of wildlife science. In 1932, forest officials in Palamau carried out a tiger count using pugmarks, the footprints tigers leave in soft earth. It is often described as the first tiger census of its kind anywhere in the world. Think about that as you look down at the red soil. The same ground that keeps your footprints once helped people count the animals they could not see.

Now look at the trees around you. Most of them are sal, a tall hardwood with large, broad leaves. Sal forests cover much of the Chota Nagpur Plateau, the old, rolling upland that gives Jharkhand its shape. Between the sal trunks you will see clumps of bamboo, and open grassy patches where deer like to graze. In the dry months the forest turns gold and brown. After the monsoon rains, it becomes a deep, wet green.

Water shapes everything in Betla. The North Koel River runs through this landscape, and smaller rivers such as the Auranga feed into it. In the hot months, these rivers shrink to pools and sandy beds. Animals then gather near whatever water remains. That is why the forest roads often lead past waterholes and watchtowers. If you want to see wildlife here, the simple rule is this: follow the water, and wait.

So what might you see? Elephants are the animals most people hope for, and herds do move through this forest. You may also meet the gaur, sometimes called the Indian bison. It is a massive wild cattle, dark brown, with pale lower legs that look like white socks. Spotted chital deer and the larger sambar deer are common. Wild boar root along the roadsides. Langurs watch everything from above, and they call out loudly when a predator is near.

The tiger is a different story, and an honest guide should tell it plainly. Tigers in Palamau have become very rare in recent decades. Sightings are uncommon, and most visitors will not see one. Leopards and sloth bears also live here, but they are shy and hard to find. Do not measure your visit by one animal. A fresh elephant track, a broken branch, or an alarm call from a deer can tell you just as much.

Here is a story you will hear from local guides. They say the name Betla comes from five animals: bison, elephant, tiger, leopard, and axis, the scientific name of the chital deer. Take the first letters, and you get B, E, T, L, A. It is a charming tale, and people repeat it often. But treat it as a local saying, not proven history. The real origin of the name is probably older and simpler.

People did not only live around this forest. They also ruled from it. Close to Betla stand the ruins of the Palamau forts, linked with the Chero kings who held this region for several centuries. The walls are made of stone and brick, and much of them is broken and covered with plants. Standing there, you can see why rulers chose this ground. The hills and the river gave them natural protection, and the forest gave them timber and game.

The forest is also home to people today. Communities such as the Oraon, the Munda, and the Kharwar live in villages around the park, as their families have for generations. For many of them, the forest is part of daily life. They collect mahua flowers, leaves, and firewood, according to the rules of the season. When you drive through a village near the park, look at the earthen houses and the painted walls. This is a living landscape, not an empty one.

The best way to explore Betla is slowly. Most visitors join a jeep safari early in the morning or late in the afternoon. At these hours the light is soft, and animals are more active. In the middle of the day, the heat presses down, and the forest goes quiet. Bring patience above all. A good safari is often long stretches of silence, and then a few minutes that you remember for years.

As you move through the park, use more than your eyes. Listen for the sharp bark of a deer, which can mean a predator is close. Notice how the birds suddenly go silent, and how the langurs stop eating to stare in one direction. Smell the air near the waterholes, where animals have been drinking and rolling in mud. Small signs like these turn a drive through the trees into reading the forest.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and look down at the road. Look for prints in the red dust, the round pads of an elephant, or the sharp marks of deer hooves. Then look up at the tall sal trunks and the light coming through the leaves. Almost a century ago, people walked this same ground and counted tigers by their tracks. Your footprints are here now too, beside theirs, until the next rain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book an early morning safari for softer light and wildlife.
Carry binoculars to scan waterholes and distant grassland.
Wear muted, earthy colours rather than bright clothing.
Keep voices low so you can hear alarm calls.
Pair the safari with a visit to the nearby forts.
