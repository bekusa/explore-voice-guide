The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment near the gate. Behind you, Üsküdar keeps moving. Ferry horns sound from the water, buses pull in at the square, and simit sellers call out over the traffic. In front of you, the noise drops away. Pale grey stone, a wide courtyard, and two slender minarets frame a dome that sits low and calm against the sky. This is the Yeni Valide Mosque, the New Mosque of the Queen Mother, and it has watched this busy corner of Istanbul's Asian shore for more than three hundred years.

The name tells you most of the story. A valide sultan was the mother of a reigning sultan, and in the Ottoman palace she often held real power. This mosque was built in the early eighteenth century by Sultan Ahmed the Third in honour of his mother, Gülnuş Emetullah Sultan. Construction is generally dated to the years around 1708 to 1710. Gülnuş was mother to two sultans, Mustafa the Second and Ahmed the Third. Few women in Ottoman history could say that.

Gülnuş was not born in Istanbul. Most historians agree she came from Crete, taken to the palace as a young girl. She rose to become the favourite of Sultan Mehmed the Fourth. Later, during the reigns of her sons, she held one of the highest positions in the empire. Her story moves from a captured child to a woman with authority over palace life, and great wealth of her own. This mosque was part of how her family wanted her to be remembered.

Now look into the courtyard and find her tomb. This is the detail most visitors miss. Most Ottoman royal tombs are closed buildings with a solid dome overhead. Here, the tomb is open to the air. Instead of a roof, an ornate metal grille, almost like a delicate cage, encloses the grave. Rain and sunlight fall straight through it onto the burial place inside.

Why build it this way? Locals say Gülnuş asked not to be closed off from the sky. According to the popular tale, she wanted rain to fall on her grave and light to reach her, as on the graves of ordinary people. It is a story, not a documented record, but it fits the strange openness of the place. Stand beside the grille and look up. The sky above the tomb is the same sky above the square outside.

The mosque belongs to an interesting moment in Ottoman design. Its overall shape follows the classical tradition of the sixteenth century, the age of the great architect Sinan, with a central dome, a courtyard, and a sense of order. But some of its details point forward. Within a decade, Istanbul entered the so-called Tulip Era, a period of new taste and decorative experiment. This mosque stands just at the doorway of that change.

Walk around to the street side of the complex. On the corner you will find a sebil, a small kiosk with bronze grilles. In Ottoman cities, attendants handed out free water, and sometimes sweet sherbet, to passers-by through these windows. It was an act of charity, built into the street itself. Close by stands a public fountain. Together they show that a mosque complex was never just a place of prayer. It served the neighbourhood.

That idea runs through the whole site. A royal mosque like this one often came with a group of supporting buildings, such as schools, fountains, and rooms for the community. Some parts of the original complex have changed or disappeared over the centuries, as the city grew around them. What remains still gives a clear sense of the plan. The mosque sits at the centre, and the services spread outward toward the street.

Before you enter, remember that this is an active place of worship. Shoes come off at the door, and women are asked to cover their hair. Inside, the light is soft and even. Look at the tiles and the painted decoration on the walls and under the dome. Listen for the hush of carpet underfoot. If prayer is underway, wait quietly at the back or come back a little later.

Notice how the space feels from the centre. The dome rests on arches and supports that spread its weight down into the walls. The windows rise in rows, and each row lets light fall at a different angle through the day. In the morning the room can feel cool and blue. Later, as the sun moves west over the Bosphorus, warmer light reaches deeper inside.

This mosque is not alone in Üsküdar. The district is full of royal foundations, many of them built by or for powerful women. A short walk toward the water brings you to the Mihrimah Sultan Mosque, designed by Sinan for the daughter of Suleiman the Magnificent. Further along the shore stands the small Şemsi Pasha Mosque. Seen together, they show how Ottoman royalty shaped the Asian side of the city.

Why here, on this shore? For centuries, Üsküdar was the starting point for journeys east, toward Anatolia and beyond. Pilgrims, soldiers, and merchants gathered here before setting out. A grand mosque at the edge of this gathering place was a public statement. Anyone leaving the capital, or arriving from Asia, would pass the work of the sultan's mother. Even today, thousands of commuters walk past the walls every day.

Step back out into the courtyard before you leave. Listen to how the sound of the square returns as you move toward the gate. Look once more at the open tomb, at the grille catching the light, and at the plain grey stone of the walls. Then turn toward the water. The ferries are still crossing, as boats have crossed here for centuries, carrying people between two continents, past the mosque built for a queen mother.

Practical tips

Remove shoes before entering the prayer hall.
Bring a scarf so women can cover their hair.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers.
Walk to the Mihrimah Sultan Mosque nearby afterward.
Arrive by ferry to see the Üsküdar waterfront first.
