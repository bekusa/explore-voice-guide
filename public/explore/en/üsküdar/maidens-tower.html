The guide

The story

The stone beneath your feet is worn smooth by centuries of footsteps. This slender tower rises from a tiny islet in the Bosphorus, just off the Asian shore of Istanbul, its limestone walls catching the morning light in shades of honey and cream. The current you hear lapping at the base has carried ships between Europe and Asia for thousands of years.

No one knows exactly when the tower was first built. Byzantine chronicles mention a watchtower here in the twelfth century, possibly earlier. The Ottomans rebuilt it in the seventeen-fifties after an earthquake, adding the distinctive conical roof you see today. But legends reach back further still — some say the structure stands on foundations laid by the ancient Greeks, a lighthouse marking the entrance to the Black Sea.

The Turkish name is Kız Kulesi, and it carries the weight of a tragic folktale. A sultan, so the story goes, locked his daughter in this tower after a prophecy foretold she would die from a snake bite. He surrounded her with guards and provisions, believing the isolation would keep her safe. On her eighteenth birthday, a serpent arrived hidden in a basket of fruit. The tale is pure legend, not history, but it has stuck to the stones for generations.

What the tower actually did was far more practical. It served as a customs checkpoint, a quarantine station during plague outbreaks, and a lighthouse guiding vessels through the narrow strait. Ships approaching from the Mediterranean would see its lantern burning at night, a fixed point in the swirling currents. During the Russo-Turkish wars, it doubled as a watchtower, scanning the horizon for enemy sails.

Step inside and you climb a spiral staircase worn concave by use. The walls are thick — nearly two metres at the base — built to withstand both storms and cannon fire. Small windows pierce the stone at intervals, each framing a different slice of the city: the domes of the old town to the west, the hills of Üsküdar rising behind you, the container ships queuing in the strait below.

From the top platform, the view is disorienting. You are surrounded by water, yet close enough to both shores to hear the traffic. Ferries churn past every few minutes, their wakes rocking the small boats tied up at the tower's landing. Fishermen cast lines from the rocks nearby, just as they have for centuries. The air smells of brine and diesel and grilled fish from the restaurant that now occupies the ground floor.

The tower has been many things over the years: fortress, prison, tax office, radio beacon, even a set for a James Bond film in the nineteen-nineties. Today it is a museum and café, reached by a short boat ride from either shore. The interior has been renovated several times, but the bones remain — the spiral stairs, the vaulted ceilings, the arrow slits that once framed enemy ships.

Look closely at the stonework near the waterline. You will see patches of different masonry, repairs made after earthquakes and storms. The Bosphorus is not gentle. Winter gales hammer the tower from the north, and the current runs fast enough to pull a swimmer under. Yet the structure has endured, rebuilt and repurposed, a constant in a city that has changed hands and names and empires.

What makes the tower compelling is not just its history but its position. It sits at the crossroads of continents, neither fully in Europe nor Asia, a threshold between worlds. Stand here at dusk and watch the sun drop behind the minarets of Sultanahmet. The call to prayer drifts across the water from both shores, overlapping in waves. The lights of the city begin to flicker on, thousands of them, climbing the hills in all directions.

As you leave, notice the way the tower shrinks behind you, becoming a silhouette against the water. From a distance, it looks almost fragile, a slender column rising from the strait. But up close, you felt the weight of the stone, the thickness of the walls, the way the structure grips its rocky perch. It has stood here through Byzantine emperors and Ottoman sultans, through wars and plagues and the slow grind of the centuries. It will likely stand here long after you have gone.
