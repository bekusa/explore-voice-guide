Beylerbeyi Palace

41.0417° N · 29.0403° E · Palace

The story

Stand at the water's edge and look up. White marble catches the morning light, reflecting off the Bosphorus in soft ripples. The palace rises in tiers — arched windows, delicate balustrades, a roofline that seems almost too elegant for a building this size. This is Beylerbeyi, the summer residence Ottoman sultans built when the old Topkapı Palace felt too heavy, too formal, too far from the breeze.

The palace you see today dates from the eighteen-sixties. Sultan Abdülaziz commissioned it after fire destroyed an earlier wooden pavilion on this same spot. He wanted something modern, something that could host European royalty without embarrassment. The architect, Sarkis Balyan, came from a family of Armenian builders who had served the Ottoman court for generations. Sarkis and his brother designed a palace that blended French neoclassical symmetry with Ottoman spatial flow — high ceilings, wide halls, rooms that opened onto one another in sequences meant for summer air.

Step inside and the first thing you notice is light. Enormous Bohemian crystal chandeliers hang in the central hall, each weighing over a tonne. The Ottomans imported them from what is now the Czech Republic, along with mirrors, carpets from Hereke, and furniture from Paris. Abdülaziz wanted his guests — Empress Eugénie of France, the Duke and Duchess of Windsor, the Shah of Iran — to feel they were in a palace that matched anything in Europe, but one that still felt unmistakably Istanbul.

The floors tell their own story. In the main reception rooms, rush matting covers the parquet — a practical Ottoman touch that kept the wood cool underfoot and absorbed the humidity rising off the strait. In the harem quarters upstairs, smaller rooms open onto enclosed balconies where the women of the household could watch ships pass without being seen. Privacy and view, always negotiated.

Abdülaziz used Beylerbeyi as a summer retreat and a diplomatic stage, but he never lived to enjoy it for long. By eighteen-seventy-six, palace politics had turned against him. He was deposed, confined to another palace across the water, and died under circumstances that remain disputed. Beylerbeyi passed to his successors, then to visiting dignitaries, then eventually to the Turkish Republic as a museum.

Walk through the harem and you will find something unexpected — a basement tunnel that runs from the palace down to the water. Sultans used it to board boats discreetly, away from the public gaze. The tunnel is closed now, but the entrance remains visible near the kitchens, a reminder that even in a palace this grand, escape routes mattered.

In the gardens, magnolia trees planted in the eighteen-sixties still bloom each spring. The terraces step down toward the Bosphorus in stone tiers, each level offering a slightly different angle on the strait. On summer evenings, the palace would have been lit by oil lamps, the water dark except for the lanterns of passing caiques. Today, ferries and tankers move through the same channel, their engines a low hum beneath the gulls.

One detail most visitors miss — look closely at the window frames in the main salon. The wood is painted to imitate marble, a trompe-l'oeil technique Ottoman craftsmen borrowed from European theatre design. It is a small deception, but a telling one. Beylerbeyi was always a place of performance, a palace built to be looked at as much as lived in.

Before you leave, stand on the waterfront terrace and watch the strait for a moment. The current here runs fast — the Bosphorus is not a river but a channel connecting two seas, and the water moves in layers, surface flow one direction, deep current the other. Sultans chose this site not just for the view, but for the movement, the sense that the world was passing by and they were watching from the best seat available.

As you walk back toward the entrance gate, notice the plane trees lining the road. Their bark peels in patches, white and grey, the same colors as the palace marble. In autumn, their leaves cover the cobblestones in a bronze carpet. The palace closes at sunset, and the last light catches the upper windows, turning them gold for a few minutes before the strait goes dark.
