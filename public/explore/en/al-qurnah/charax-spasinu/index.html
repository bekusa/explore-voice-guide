The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind moves across flat ground with almost nothing to stop it. Dry grass rustles. A bird calls somewhere over the marsh. The earth under your feet is pale and crumbly, and the horizon is a thin line in every direction. It does not look like much at first... but the long low ridges around you are not natural hills. They are the remains of walls. You are standing in what was once one of the great trading ports of the ancient world.

This place is Charax Spasinu. For centuries it sat near the meeting of great rivers, in the wide delta country of southern Mesopotamia, the ancient name for the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates. Today the nearest towns are Al-Qurnah and, further south, Basra. The coastline of the Gulf was closer here in antiquity than it is now. Ships could reach the city from the sea, and goods could travel inland by river. That simple fact of geography explains almost everything about why a city grew here.

The story begins with a famous name. Ancient writers say that Alexander the Great founded a city on this spot near the end of his life, in the 320s before Christ. He called it Alexandria, as he called so many of his foundations. The Roman author Pliny the Elder, writing several centuries later, says Alexander settled it with veterans from his army. Pliny is our main written source here, so it is fair to treat his details with some caution.

The first city did not last. According to Pliny, floods from the rivers destroyed it. This is easy to believe when you look at the land around you — water here is both a gift and a threat. The city was later rebuilt by Antiochus, one of the Seleucid kings. The Seleucids were the Greek-speaking dynasty that inherited much of Alexander's empire in Asia. For a while, the city carried his name instead of Alexander's.

The name we use today comes from a third founder. In the second century before Christ, a local governor named Hyspaosines took control of the city as Seleucid power weakened. Pliny calls him Spasines. The word Charax means a fortified place, or a palisade. So Charax Spasinu means, roughly, the fortress of Spasines. He built strong embankments against the floods. From here he ruled a small kingdom that later writers called Characene, or Mesene.

Now look again at those ridges in the ground. In recent years, a team linked to the British Museum, working with Iraqi colleagues, began studying this site again. They used drone photography and aerial images to map the city from above. Here is the first surprise. From the air, the outline of an entire planned city appears — streets, blocks of buildings, and enormous defensive walls. Down here at ground level, you see only bumps and shallow hollows.

The walls are the clearest feature. They were built mostly of mud brick, the most common building material in ancient Mesopotamia. Mud brick is cheap and strong, but it melts slowly back into earth when rain and wind wear it down. That is why the walls now look like long, soft hills. Walk along one and notice how straight it runs. Natural ridges do not hold a line like that. Human builders did.

Inside the walls, the city was laid out on a grid, with streets crossing at right angles. This pattern is typical of Greek and Hellenistic planning. Hellenistic refers to the period after Alexander, when Greek culture mixed with local traditions across the Near East. The grid tells us that planners, not chance, shaped this town. Imagine houses, workshops, temples and markets packed into those blocks, and the noise of a port city filling the air.

So what made Charax rich? Trade. The city stood on a route that connected the Mediterranean world with the Gulf, Arabia and India. Merchants from Palmyra, the caravan city in the Syrian desert, were active here. Inscriptions found at Palmyra mention traders who travelled to Charax and on to the sea. Spices, textiles, pearls, incense and other precious goods passed through this harbour. For a few centuries, this quiet field was a meeting point between continents.

Here is the second surprise. A Roman emperor stood near here. In 116, the emperor Trajan pushed his armies deep into Mesopotamia and reached the head of the Gulf. The historian Cassius Dio tells a story that Trajan watched a ship setting sail for India. He is said to have remarked that he would have gone there too, if he were still young. Whether the words are exact or not, the scene shows how far this place reached in the imagination of Rome.

Charax also produced thinkers. A geographer known as Isidore of Charax wrote a description of the overland route across the Parthian Empire. The Parthians were the Iranian dynasty that ruled much of this region after the Seleucids. Isidore's text survives in part, and historians still use it. It is a small reminder that this was not only a place of warehouses and ships, but also of scholars, maps and written knowledge.

The kingdom of Characene lasted for several centuries, often as a client of the Parthians. In the third century, the new Sasanian dynasty of Iran conquered the region. Over time, the rivers shifted, the coastline moved south, and the harbour lost its value. A port without access to water cannot survive. The city faded slowly, and later settlements grew elsewhere. What remained was mud brick, returning year by year to the soil.

The site is open country, not a museum with paths and signs. Visits are best arranged with local guides and the relevant authorities, and the land deserves respect. Please do not pick up pottery or dig at the ground. Every small fragment helps archaeologists date the layers. Bring water and sun protection. In summer, the heat here is fierce, so the cooler months make the experience far more comfortable.

Before you leave, turn slowly in a full circle. Look at the straight lines in the earth, the pale scatter of broken pottery, the wide sky above the marsh. Somewhere under your feet are the streets where merchants bargained and sailors waited for the wind. The city is quiet now. But as you walk back, keep watching the ground... the shapes are still there, for anyone who looks closely.
