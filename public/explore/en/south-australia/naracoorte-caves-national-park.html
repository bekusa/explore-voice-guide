The guide

The story

Stop for a moment at the mouth of the cave and feel the air change. Outside, the sun warms the gum trees and the dry grass. One step inside, the air turns cool and damp, and it smells of wet stone and old earth. Your footsteps sound softer here. Water drips somewhere in the dark. Welcome to Naracoorte Caves National Park, in the Limestone Coast region of South Australia. Above ground, it looks like quiet farming country. Below your feet sits one of the richest fossil records on the continent.

To understand this place, start with the rock. The caves are cut into limestone that formed on an ancient seabed, millions of years ago. It is made from the shells and skeletons of countless small sea creatures. Over a very long time, the sea pulled back and the land rose. Rainwater soaked into the ground and slowly dissolved the soft stone. Hollows became chambers, and chambers became caves. What you see around you is the slow work of water, drop by drop.

That same water built the decorations hanging above you. When water seeps through limestone, it carries dissolved minerals. When it drips into open air, it leaves a tiny layer of crystal behind. Stalactites hang from the ceiling. Stalagmites rise from the floor. Sometimes the two meet and form a single column. Each one grows extremely slowly. Look closely at a thin straw stalactite and you can see a drop of water at its tip — still building, right now, while you watch.

Now here is the first surprise. Many of these caves acted as natural traps. Small openings in the ground, often hidden by plants, led straight down into the darkness. Over hundreds of thousands of years, animals wandered too close and fell in. Some could not climb back out. Their bones settled on the cave floor, and sediment washed in on top of them. Layer after layer, the caves kept a record of the animals that lived in this landscape.

That record became famous in 1969. Cavers exploring Victoria Fossil Cave pushed through a narrow passage and found chambers filled with bones. Scientists soon realised the scale of what they had. The deposits held the remains of Australian megafauna — giant animals that disappeared long ago. The fossils span a very long stretch of time, and they let researchers study how animals and climate changed here over many glacial cycles. Digging and research continue in the caves today.

So who fell into these pits? Imagine a kangaroo much larger than any alive today, with a short, flat face. Picture a wombat relative the size of a small cow. And then there is the marsupial lion, Thylacoleo carnifex — a powerful predator with huge cutting teeth and strong forearms. Researchers found a giant python here too, named Wonambi naracoortensis after this very area. These were real animals, walking through country not far from where you stand.

In 1994, these caves joined the UNESCO World Heritage List. UNESCO is the United Nations body that recognises places of outstanding value to all of humanity. The listing is shared with Riversleigh, a fossil site far to the north in Queensland. Together they are known as the Australian Fossil Mammal Sites. Riversleigh shows an older chapter of the story. Naracoorte fills in a more recent one. Between them, they tell how Australia's unusual animals came to be.

The park offers several different caves, and each one feels different. Some are open for self-guided visits, with paths and lighting. Others can only be entered with a guide. Wet Cave is a good place to start on your own. Alexandra Cave is known for its delicate formations and still pools of water that mirror the ceiling. Victoria Fossil Cave lets you see the fossil chambers and learn how the bones were found. Ask at the visitor centre which tours run today.

The Wonambi Fossil Centre brings the fossils back to life. Inside, life-sized reconstructions show the megafauna in a recreated ancient forest. You can stand next to them and get a real sense of their size. It is a good stop before you go underground, because it helps you picture the animals while you look at the bones. It is also a good place to pause with children, who usually find the giant creatures hard to forget.

Here is the second surprise. The caves are not only about the past. One of them, Bat Cave, is a breeding site for the southern bent-wing bat, a species listed as critically endangered. Females gather here to give birth and raise their young in the warm, protected chambers. Because the bats are so sensitive to disturbance, visitors do not walk inside this cave. Instead, the park uses cameras that send live images to a viewing centre.

If you visit on a warm evening in the right season, you may see the bats leave the cave at dusk. They stream out into the fading light to hunt insects over the surrounding countryside. Rangers sometimes lead evening walks for this. It is a quiet, moving sight. These small animals are part of the same long story as the fossils below — creatures that have used these caves as shelter for a very long time.

Above ground, take time to notice the landscape too. The park sits among low ridges, open woodland and farmland. Kangaroos often graze at the edges of the grass in the early morning and late afternoon. Birds call from the trees. There are walking trails that let you see the cave entrances from above. Look for small dips and holes in the ground, because some of these are the same kind of openings that once trapped ancient animals.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Listen to the drip of water inside the cave, or the wind moving through the gum trees outside. The stone around you is still changing, very slowly. The bones beneath the floor are still being studied. And the bats are still returning each year. As you walk back into the daylight, look down at the ground under your feet — and remember how much may still be waiting underneath it.
