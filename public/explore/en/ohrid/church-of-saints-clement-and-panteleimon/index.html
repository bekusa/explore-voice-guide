The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. Up here on Plaošnik hill, the path is a mix of packed earth, worn stone and patches of grass. Pine needles and dry leaves crunch underfoot. Somewhere below, out of sight, the water of Lake Ohrid moves against the shore. The air carries a faint smell of resin and warm stone. In front of you, a small church of red brick and pale stone rises among low ruins. It looks almost modest. But the story of this place reaches across a whole family of languages.

This is the Church of Saints Clement and Panteleimon. The name Plaošnik is usually linked to an old word for a flat or open place, and the hill does feel like a terrace above the town. Look around you and you will see foundations, broken columns and outlines of walls cut into the grass. Many different centuries have left something here. The church is the centre of it, and the man in its name is the reason people still come.

That man is Saint Clement of Ohrid. He was a disciple of the brothers Cyril and Methodius. Those two missionaries created the Glagolitic alphabet, an early script designed to write the Slavic language. After Methodius died, his followers were driven out of Moravia, in central Europe. Clement eventually came south, to this lake, in the late ninth century. Here, with support from the Bulgarian ruler of the time, he began to teach.

On this hill, Clement founded a monastery and dedicated it to Saint Panteleimon, an early Christian healer and martyr. Around it grew what historians call the Ohrid Literary School. Thousands of students are said to have learned here. They studied reading, writing, prayer and the translation of sacred texts. Think about that for a moment. On this quiet hill, a written culture for Slavic speakers took shape, and it spread far beyond this lake.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Clement is closely linked with the Cyrillic alphabet, the script used today in Russia, Bulgaria, Serbia, North Macedonia and many other countries. Cyrillic carries the name of his teacher, Cyril, but it took shape after Cyril's death. A long tradition credits Clement and his circle with developing it here in Ohrid. Scholars still discuss exactly who did what and where. But the link between this hill and the letters on millions of screens and signs is part of local pride.

Now look at the shape of the building. Clement's original church was small, with rounded apses on three sides. This pattern is called a trefoil or triconch plan, which simply means three lobes, like a clover leaf. Walk slowly around the outside and you can follow the curves. The brickwork is laid in bands, with thin red bricks between rows of stone. This is a style common across the Byzantine world, the Greek-speaking Christian empire centred on Constantinople.

Clement died in the early tenth century. He was buried inside the church he built, and his tomb became a place of pilgrimage. For centuries, people climbed this hill to pray beside him. The monastery grew. The church was extended and changed over time, as living buildings always are. When you go inside, look for the tomb. It is the emotional heart of the whole site.

The next chapter is harder. In the Ottoman period, after the Ottoman Turks took control of this region in the late fourteenth century, the church was turned into a mosque. Clement's relics, according to tradition, were carried down into the town for safekeeping. They went to the Church of the Holy Mother of God Peribleptos, which people later began to call the Church of Saint Clement. The mosque on Plaošnik later fell into ruin. For a long time, only fragments remained on this hill.

So here is the second surprise. Much of the church in front of you is not medieval. Archaeologists excavated the site carefully during the twentieth century. They uncovered the old foundations and many earlier layers. Then, around the turn of the twenty-first century, the church was rebuilt on its original foundations. Look closely at the lower walls and you may notice older stone beneath newer work. The building is a reconstruction, but it stands exactly where Clement's church stood.

The ruins around you are older still. Before Clement arrived, an early Christian basilica stood on this hill. Its floors were covered in mosaics, with patterns of birds, plants and geometric shapes. Some of these mosaics survive under protective roofs or covers near the church. Take a few minutes to find them. The colours are soft now, but the craft is clear. Christians were worshipping here several hundred years before the Slavic alphabet existed.

Step back and think about the wider setting. Ohrid and its lake are listed by UNESCO as World Heritage, for both their nature and their culture. Above you, on the higher ground, are the walls of the fortress often called Samuel's Fortress. Below, on a rocky point over the water, is the small Church of Saint John at Kaneo. Many visitors walk a loop between all three. Plaošnik sits in the middle, both in place and in story.

If you visit early or late in the day, the hill is often quiet. You might hear birdsong in the pines, a bell from the town, or a boat engine far out on the lake. Inside the church, the light is low and the air is cool. People speak softly here, because this is an active place of worship. Dress modestly, and take a moment before you raise a camera. Many visitors light a thin candle in the sand trays.

Stand still once more. Look at the red and cream bands of the walls, the three rounded apses, the broken columns in the grass. Listen to the pine trees and the distant water. A man came here more than a thousand years ago with books and students. What he started still shapes how hundreds of millions of people write. As you walk down the path toward the lake, notice the letters on the street signs below. Some of their story begins right here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning for a quieter visit to the hill.
Cover shoulders and knees before entering the church.
Walk the loop to Kaneo and the fortress on foot.
Wear shoes with grip for the stony, uneven paths.
Keep voices low inside, as services still take place.
