The guide

The story

Put your hand flat on the nearest stone seat. It is pale, cool in the morning and warm by late afternoon, smoothed by many centuries of weather. Below you, the rows curve down toward a round stage. Above you, the roofs of Ohrid's old town climb the hill. And somewhere past those roofs lies the lake... still and wide, the same lake that people sat beside here more than two thousand years ago.

If you have come over from Struga, you have made a short trip east along the northern shore of Lake Ohrid. Struga sits where the lake drains into the Black Drin river. This theatre belongs to its neighbour, Ohrid, and it sits right inside the old town. Many visitors from Struga come for exactly this place. Here you can sit in the same seats as the town's ancient audiences.

In ancient times this town had a different name. It was called Lychnidos. Scholars often connect the name with light, possibly from the shine of the lake. Lychnidos stood on an important route. The Via Egnatia, the great Roman road that linked the Adriatic coast with Thessalonica and later Constantinople, passed through this region. Merchants, soldiers and officials moved along it. A town on such a road needed public spaces, and a theatre was one of the most important.

Archaeologists generally date the theatre to the Hellenistic period, around 200 BCE. Hellenistic means the age after Alexander the Great, when Greek language and Greek culture spread across a huge territory. A theatre in that world was a civic space as well as a place of entertainment. Plays, music, religious festivals and public gatherings all happened in spaces like this. It is often described as the only surviving Hellenistic theatre in North Macedonia.

Now look at how the builders used the land. The seating does not stand on a huge artificial structure. Instead, it rests in a natural hollow between two hills. This was a common Greek method. The slope carried the weight of the seats, and the curve of the ground helped sound travel upward. Try it yourself. If a friend stands near the stage and speaks normally, you may hear them clearly from surprisingly high up.

Here is the first surprise. For a long time, nobody in modern Ohrid knew the theatre was here. Over the centuries, earth, rubble and houses covered it. The town grew on top of it. Its remains came to light in the twentieth century, and serious excavation and restoration followed. So the space around you is very old, but its return to public life is quite recent.

Why was it buried so completely? Part of the answer is simply time. Abandoned stone buildings often disappear under soil and new construction. But locals also tell a darker story. In the Roman period, theatres across the empire were often adapted for harsher spectacles, including fights with gladiators and wild animals. According to one popular account, Christians may have been killed in this theatre during that era.

The story continues. Locals say that later Christian residents of the town remembered this place with horror and deliberately left it buried. Treat that idea as tradition rather than proven history. What we can say is simpler. The burial protected the stone, and much of the seating survived because it lay hidden from later builders. Those builders often reused old stone in new walls and houses.

The Roman changes also have a physical side. When a Greek theatre became a space for animal shows, organisers needed to protect the audience. At several sites in the Roman world, builders removed the lowest seats or raised a barrier around the orchestra. The orchestra is the flat circular area in front of the stage. As you look down, notice the clear gap and the wall between the lowest rows and the performance space.

Here is the second surprise. The theatre is not only a monument. It is a working stage again. In summer, it becomes one of the main venues of the Ohrid Summer Festival. Then concerts, drama and dance fill the old space. On those evenings, the stone rows fill with people once more. Lights shine across the orchestra, and the sound rises toward the hills, much as it would have done in the ancient town.

Take a moment to look closely at the stone. Some blocks are ancient, with worn edges and uneven surfaces. Others are newer, cut more sharply to fill the gaps. That mix is normal at restored sites. It helps visitors understand the shape of the original theatre while keeping the authentic pieces visible. Look for subtle changes in colour and texture. They often mark where old stone ends and repair begins.

Now turn and look at the setting around you. The old town of Ohrid rises in layers of white walls, dark wooden balconies and red tile roofs. Above it stands the large fortress usually linked with Tsar Samuel, who ruled from this region in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries. Nearby are medieval churches, narrow lanes and steep steps. From here, the theatre feels like one chapter in a very long story of building on this hill.

Before you go, sit for a few minutes in silence. Listen to the swallows, the footsteps on the lanes above, and perhaps a bell from one of the churches nearby. Notice the curve of the rows around you, the empty stage below, and the light on the pale stone. As you walk back toward the lake, remember that this town has collected audiences here for more than two thousand years. For a moment, you have joined them.
