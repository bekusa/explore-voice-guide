The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your feet. It is uneven, worn smooth in places, warm if the sun has been on it for an hour. Above you, the walls rise in rough grey courses, with thin bands of brick pressed between the stones. Below, the roofs of the old town step down toward the water. And then there is the lake. Lake Ohrid lies open and pale, so clear near the shore that you can sometimes see the bottom from the hill.

You are standing on the highest point of old Ohrid, on the hill that people have defended for well over two thousand years. Locals simply call it the fortress, or Samuel's Fortress, after the ruler most closely tied to it. The walls you see today are the result of many rebuildings. Each power that held this hill added its own layer. So read the stone slowly. It is less one building than a long conversation between centuries.

The story of this hill begins long before Samuel. In antiquity, the town here was called Lychnidos. It sat on an important route across the Balkans, the road the Romans later developed as the Via Egnatia, which linked the Adriatic coast with the East. A hilltop above a lake on a major road is an obvious place for walls. Archaeologists have found traces of fortifications far older than the medieval towers around you.

Now, the name. Samuel was a tsar of the First Bulgarian Empire, and he ruled at the end of the tenth century and the start of the eleventh. During his reign, Ohrid became his capital. That made this small lakeside town, for a few decades, the political centre of a large state. It also made Ohrid a direct rival of the Byzantine Empire, ruled from Constantinople. The fortress on this hill was the heart of that capital.

Samuel spent much of his reign at war with the Byzantine emperor Basil the Second. The conflict lasted for years and ended in tragedy. In 1014, Samuel's army was defeated at the Battle of Kleidion, in a mountain pass far to the east. The Byzantine chronicles tell a grim story. Basil is said to have blinded thousands of captured soldiers, leaving one man in each hundred with a single eye to lead the others home.

According to the same sources, Samuel saw his blinded army return, collapsed, and died soon afterward. Historians still argue about the numbers in that account, and some think they were exaggerated. But the outline is accepted. Within a few years of his death, his state fell, and Ohrid passed to Byzantium. The capital became a provincial town again. Yet the name of its last great ruler stayed on the hill.

Here is something many visitors miss. Most of what you see is probably not from Samuel's time at all. Researchers believe the walls were repaired and rebuilt by later Byzantine rulers, and then again during the long Ottoman period. Large sections were also restored in the early twenty-first century. So when you touch the wall, you may be touching medieval stone, Ottoman repair, or modern conservation work. Often, you are touching all three within one arm's length.

Walk along the ramparts if they are open. The walls follow the shape of the hill, turning and dropping with the ground. Square and rounded towers break the line at intervals. Look closely at the building method. Rows of rough stone alternate with flat red brick, set in thick mortar. This mixed technique is typical of Byzantine building across the region. It made walls flexible and strong, and it gives them that striped, layered look.

Now turn toward the lake. On a clear day, the far shore rises into mountains. Part of that shore belongs to Albania, so from here you are looking across an international border on the water. Lake Ohrid is one of the oldest lakes in Europe, formed over a million years ago. Scientists value it for the many species that live in it and nowhere else. The town and the lake together are protected on the UNESCO World Heritage list.

The view from here also explains why Ohrid matters beyond politics. Below the fortress, on the slope called Plaoshnik, stands the church dedicated to Saint Clement and Saint Panteleimon. In the late ninth century, Saint Clement of Ohrid taught here. He was a student of Cyril and Methodius, the brothers who created the first Slavic alphabet. His school helped spread Slavic writing and learning across the Balkans. So this hill guarded not only a state, but a centre of literacy.

Look a little further, toward the cliff above the water. You may spot the small church of Saint John at Kaneo, perched on a rocky point. It is one of the most photographed views in the country. On the way down from the fortress, you can also find the ancient theatre, built in the Hellenistic period, when Greek culture spread across this region after Alexander the Great. Its stone seats still curve into the hillside.

There is a quieter detail worth noticing too. The streets that climb up to the fortress are steep and narrow. Many old houses lean outward on their upper floors, with white walls and dark wooden beams. This design gave families more space on small plots of land. Listen as you walk. You will hear church bells, swallows in the towers, and voices drifting up from the lanes. The sounds of daily life travel a long way on this hill.

Inside the walls, the ground is largely open now. Grass and low trees cover what was once a busy enclosed space, with buildings for soldiers, officials, and stores. Very little of that inner life survives above ground. This emptiness is part of the experience. It leaves room for the wind, the light, and your own imagination. The walls hold a space, and you decide what fills it.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment by the wall facing the water. Notice how the lake changes colour as clouds pass, from silver to deep blue to green near the shore. Notice the line of the old town below, roof by roof. Many armies stood here and looked out at this same view. Now it is yours. As you walk back down the slope, let the stone guide your steps.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep cobbled climb.
Bring water, as shade on the hilltop is limited.
Visit late afternoon for softer light over the lake.
Combine the visit with the ancient theatre below.
Walk down via Plaoshnik toward Kaneo afterward.
