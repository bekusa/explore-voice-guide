The guide

The story

Stand still on the square for a moment and tilt your head back. The stone rises, and keeps rising, until the spire thins into lace against the sky. Pale sandstone, darkened in the creases by centuries of rain. Somewhere above you, pigeons settle on ledges you will never see up close. The cobbles under your feet are cool, and the whole square seems to lean slightly toward this one building. Welcome to Ulm Minster, the church with the tallest steeple on Earth.

That is the first thing to know, and it is a real record, not a local boast. The main tower reaches just over 161 metres. No church steeple anywhere in the world stands taller. Yet here is a surprise most visitors miss: this is not a cathedral. It never had a bishop. The word minster here simply means a large, important church. And the people who paid for it were not princes or prelates. They were the ordinary citizens of Ulm.

The story begins in 1377. Ulm was then a prosperous free imperial city, which meant it answered directly to the emperor rather than to a local lord. It grew rich on trade along the Danube and on fine linen and cloth. Its citizens wanted a church that matched their confidence. Until then, their parish church had stood outside the city walls. They decided to build a new one inside, and to build it on a scale that would hold the entire population at once.

The first master builders came from the Parler family, a dynasty of stonemasons whose work shaped many great churches of central Europe. Soon the project passed to Ulrich von Ensingen. He made a bold decision. He planned a single, enormous western tower, rather than the usual pair. That one choice gave the minster its unusual silhouette, and it set the goal that later generations would chase for centuries.

Then the work stopped. Over time the building showed worrying cracks, and the vaults had to be strengthened. Religious life in the city also changed. Around 1530, Ulm accepted the Protestant Reformation, and the minster became a Lutheran church, which it remains today. By the middle of the sixteenth century, construction had come to a halt. For roughly three hundred years, the great tower stood unfinished, a stump of stone above the rooftops.

The revival came in the nineteenth century, when Germans rediscovered a passion for Gothic architecture. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches, tall windows, and stone tracery that seems to climb toward heaven. Builders took up the medieval plans again in the 1840s. They worked for decades. In 1890, the spire was finally complete. So when you look up, you are seeing two eras joined together: medieval foundations and a Victorian-age summit, built to one shared vision.

Now look closely at the main western entrance. Under the porch, at the central pillar, stands a figure of Christ as the Man of Sorrows. He shows his wounds with a quiet, weary expression. It is the work of Hans Multscher, one of the finest sculptors of fifteenth-century Germany. Many people walk straight past him on their way inside. Pause here instead. The stone figure has greeted worshippers at this door for more than five hundred years.

Step inside, and the temperature drops. Sound changes too. Footsteps soften, voices sink to a murmur, and the space opens upward into the vaults. The nave is long, high, and filled with pale light. Because this was built as a church for a whole city, the scale feels civic as much as sacred. Look for the slender pillars, and notice how the side aisles are almost as grand as the centre. It feels less like a private chapel and more like a covered town square.

Walk toward the choir, the area near the high altar, and you will find one of the great treasures of the minster: the carved wooden choir stalls by Jörg Syrlin the Elder, made in the fifteenth century. Here is the second surprise. Alongside saints and prophets, the carvings include figures from the ancient pagan world. There are sibyls, the prophetesses of classical legend, and philosophers of Greece and Rome. Their faces are sharply individual, almost portraits. It shows a city proud of learning, not only faith.

Above the choir, look up at the stained glass. Some of these windows date from the late Middle Ages, and their deep blues and reds glow when the sun falls on them. Other windows are much more recent. One reason so much survives is remarkable luck. In December 1944, a heavy bombing raid destroyed most of Ulm's old town. The streets around you burned. Yet the minster stood, damaged but standing, above a field of ruins.

There is also a lighter story the locals love, and it is legend rather than history. According to the popular tale, medieval builders struggled to carry a long wooden beam through the city gate. They held it crosswise, and it would not fit. Then they saw a sparrow carrying a straw lengthwise into its nest. The lesson was obvious. Today the Ulm sparrow is a city mascot, and you may spot a small sparrow figure on the minster roof.

If your legs are willing, you can climb the tower. The route has 768 steps, winding ever narrower through the stone. The staircase tightens, the walls press close, and the wind grows stronger as you rise. The highest viewing level sits about 143 metres above the square. From there, Ulm spreads below you, with the Danube curving past. On a very clear day, locals say, you can make out the line of the Alps far to the south.

Back on the ground, turn around and look across the square. The bright white building facing the minster is the Stadthaus, a modern civic hall designed by the American architect Richard Meier. Its clean curves and glass stand in deliberate contrast to the old stone. Some residents found the pairing jarring at first. Now it frames the minster beautifully. Old and new face each other here, much as the medieval and nineteenth-century builders met in the tower itself.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the square once more. Let your eyes travel slowly up the tower, from the heavy base to the open stonework near the top. Every level is the work of hands, generation after generation, many of whom never saw it finished. The same pigeons are still circling. The same bells may ring above you. And as you walk away, look back once or twice. From almost anywhere in Ulm, the spire will still be watching.

Practical tips

Climb on a clear day for the widest views.

Stand by the Stadthaus to fit the whole spire in frame.
Keep voices low inside; services may be underway.
