The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the stone under your shoes. It is cold, uneven, and worn smooth in places by centuries of boots. Somewhere behind you, gulls call over the harbour. The air carries salt from the sea and diesel from the ferries. In front of you rises a long, pale fortress with small windows and thick walls. This is Turku Castle, and it has watched this river mouth for more than seven hundred years.

To understand why the castle stands here, look at the water. The Aura River flows through the heart of Turku and meets the sea close to this spot. In the Middle Ages, whoever held the river mouth controlled trade, ships, and the route into the town. The Swedish crown, which ruled Finland for centuries, needed a strong base on this coast. So, around the 1280s, builders began a fortified camp on what was then a small island here.

That first fortress was simple compared with what you see today. It was a defended enclosure, with walls and buildings arranged around open ground. Over the following generations, it grew taller and heavier. The builders added stone towers and closed the courtyards in. By the end of the medieval period, the core of the castle had become a dense, inward-facing block. Its job was clear — protect the crown's interests, collect taxes, and house the men who governed this land.

Here is something many visitors miss. The building in front of you is really two castles joined together. The older part, the medieval main castle, is the compact block with the thickest walls. The longer, lower wing is the bailey, a later addition from the sixteenth century. As you walk through, pay attention to the change in the stonework, the shape of the windows, and the height of the rooms. You are moving between two different ideas of what a castle should be.

That sixteenth-century shift has a name and a face. In the 1550s, Duke John, a son of the Swedish king Gustav Vasa, was made Duke of Finland. He chose Turku Castle as his home. In 1562 he married Catherine Jagiellon, a princess from Poland. She arrived from one of the most cultured royal courts in Europe. With her came fine textiles, books, servants, and new tastes in music and food.

For a few short years, this northern fortress became a Renaissance court. Renaissance here means the European revival of art and learning that spread north from Italy. The cold halls were refitted for dining, dancing, and display. Imagine candles, rich fabrics on the walls, and conversations in several languages. For a corner of Europe that many outsiders saw as remote, this was a remarkable moment. Turku briefly had a royal household that looked south and east for its style.

The moment did not last. Duke John and his brother, King Eric the Fourteenth, fell into a bitter conflict. In 1563, royal forces besieged Turku Castle, and the duke surrendered. John and Catherine were taken to Sweden as prisoners. Later, the wheel turned again. Eric lost his throne, and John became King John the Third. Tradition connects Eric himself with imprisonment at this castle. Locals still point to a room linked with his name — but treat the details as part of the castle's storytelling.

After that dramatic century, the castle slowly lost its central role. Power moved elsewhere, and the building served more practical purposes over time. It held stores, offices, and prisoners. Parts of it fell into poor repair. This is a common story for old fortresses. Once the cannon and the court move on, the walls remain, but the life inside them changes. The rooms you walk through have been used by soldiers, clerks, and inmates as often as by royalty.

The twentieth century brought the hardest blow. In 1941, during the war between Finland and the Soviet Union, incendiary bombs struck the castle. Fire spread through the roofs and interiors. Much of what had survived for centuries burned in a short time. After the war, a long and careful restoration began. It took decades. Architects and historians worked to save the old fabric while making the building safe and usable again.

That restoration is a detail worth noticing as you explore. Some rooms show rebuilt ceilings and fresh surfaces. Others keep rough, original stone, with scars and patches left visible. Look for the places where old and new meet — a clean modern beam above a ragged medieval wall, or a smooth floor laid over ancient foundations. The castle you see today is partly a medieval survivor and partly a twentieth-century act of care.

Today the castle is a museum run by the City of Turku. Inside, the route climbs and twists through narrow stairways, low doors, and sudden open halls. You will find displays on medieval daily life, the Renaissance court, and later centuries. There is also a castle church, which is still used for services and weddings. It is a quiet surprise — a place of living ritual tucked inside a fortress built for war and control.

As you move through the rooms, use your senses as much as your eyes. Notice how the temperature drops in the thick-walled lower levels. Listen to how your footsteps echo differently in the stone halls and the wooden upper floors. Touch the window recesses, where the walls are deep enough to sit in. These small physical details explain the castle better than any date. They tell you how people stayed warm, safe, and watchful here.

When you step back outside, turn and look at the castle against its surroundings. Cranes, ferry terminals, and warehouses stand nearby. This is still a working harbour, just as it was when the fortress was first raised. The castle has not been moved to a quiet park. It still sits where the river meets the sea, where ships arrive and leave every day. Its purpose has changed, but its position has not.

Before you walk away, take one last look at those small windows and heavy walls. Think of the gulls, the river, and the ferries around you. Seven centuries ago, a lookout stood somewhere above you and watched the same water. He listened for oars instead of engines. As you head back toward the city, follow the river upstream. It will carry you, as it has carried travellers for centuries, from the castle into the heart of Turku.
