The guide

The story

Stand in the piazza for a moment and look up. The front of the cathedral is striped with bands of pale marble and darker green-grey stone, like a page of ruled paper set on its end. On a bright morning the white bands catch the sun first. The darker stripes stay cool for longer. Pigeons settle on the ledges, and the sound of bicycles on the paving stones rises around you. Welcome to the Duomo of Monza, dedicated to Saint John the Baptist.

The story of this place begins long before the stripes. At the end of the sixth century, Monza belonged to the Lombards, a Germanic people who had crossed the Alps and taken control of much of northern Italy. Their queen was Theodelinda, a Catholic from Bavaria. She is remembered as the woman who helped bring her husband's pagan and Arian court closer to Rome. Around the year 600, she founded a church on this spot. Almost everything you see grew from her decision.

Monza has a legend about why the church stands exactly here. According to the popular tale, Theodelinda was riding near the river and wondering where to build. A dove appeared and spoke a single Latin word, modo, meaning here. The queen answered etiam, meaning yes. Locals say the two words joined to form Modoetia, the old Latin name of the town. It is folklore, not documented history... but the dove still appears in local art and stories.

The queen's first church has not survived in its original form. The building in front of you is mostly a rebuilding from the fourteenth century, in the Gothic style. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches and tall windows that spread across Europe from France. Look at the large round window in the centre of the façade. Look also at the small pointed spires along the roofline. The marble bands are typical of Gothic churches in Lombardy and Tuscany.

When you step inside, the light changes at once. The street noise drops away. The air feels cooler and carries a faint smell of wax and old stone. Your eyes take a few seconds to adjust. The nave, the long central hall, leads toward the altar. Its walls and vaults were decorated in later centuries, so the interior is a layering of periods. Medieval bones carry Renaissance and Baroque paint and gilding.

Now comes the first real surprise. To the left of the main altar is a side chapel dedicated to Theodelinda. Its walls are covered from floor to vault with fifteenth-century frescoes painted by the Zavattari, a family of Lombard painters. The scenes tell the queen's life like a long illustrated book. You see her courtship, her weddings, her journeys, and the founding of this church. Many visitors walk past the chapel entrance. Those who go in rarely forget it.

Look closely at the details of the frescoes. The painters filled them with gold, rich fabrics and fashionable fifteenth-century clothing. The Lombard queen from around 600 is dressed like a noblewoman of the painters' own time. The horses wear decorated harness. Courtiers stand in elegant poses. This was a form of flattery to the powerful families of that era. It also shows us what a wealthy Lombard court looked like when the walls were painted.

The chapel also guards the cathedral's most famous object, the Iron Crown of Lombardy. It is a circlet of gold panels set with gems and enamel. Inside runs a thin metal band. Tradition says that this band was hammered from a nail of the cross of Christ. That belief gave the crown its name and its sacred power. For centuries, it was used to crown kings of Italy.

Here is the second surprise. Modern scientific analysis of the crown has suggested that the inner band is not iron at all. Studies found it to be silver. So the Iron Crown may contain no iron. That does not change its importance for the people who honoured it. For them, the meaning lay in the story and the ritual, not in the metal. The crown is usually kept in a protected altar within the chapel. Seeing it up close often requires a special guided visit.

The crown's later history reaches into modern Europe. In 1805, Napoleon was crowned King of Italy in Milan Cathedral, and he used the Iron Crown for the ceremony. He placed it on his own head. Later rulers of the Habsburg empire, which held northern Italy for decades, also used it. A small object from a Lombard church became a symbol for emperors who wanted to claim an ancient right to rule Italy.

The cathedral's museum and treasury hold more of Theodelinda's world. One of the best-known pieces is a gilded silver hen with seven chicks. It is a small, charming sculpture, often linked to the queen. Its exact meaning is debated. Some see it as a symbol of the Church and its faithful, or of the queen and her people. The treasury also keeps early medieval objects such as crosses, book covers and reliquaries, which are containers for holy relics.

Back outside, look to the side of the façade at the tall brick bell tower. It was built in the late sixteenth century, long after the Gothic front. Its warm red-brown colour contrasts with the cool marble stripes. Together they show how the cathedral grew over time. No single architect finished it. Each generation added its own layer. A few streets away runs the River Lambro, and the old town hall, the Arengario, stands close by.

Before you leave, stand again in the piazza and look at the façade. You are looking at a building that began with a queen's choice more than 1,400 years ago. People have come here for prayer, coronations, weddings and quiet visits ever since. As you walk away, listen to the bells if they ring. Notice how the striped marble changes colour as the light moves. The story of this town still begins here, at the place the dove is said to have named.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ask about guided visits to see the Iron Crown up close.
Allow extra time for the museum and treasury collection.
Visit in the morning when sunlight strikes the marble façade.
Keep quiet and dress modestly during services inside.
Combine your visit with a walk to the nearby Arengario.
