The guide

The story

Stand in the open square and look up. A wall of green and cream tile fills your view, catching the North African sun. Around you, the paving stones are warm and dusty. Pigeons lift and settle. Somewhere behind you, a vendor calls out, and a horse-drawn carriage clatters past. In front of you rises one of the great ceremonial gateways of Morocco: Bab Mansur al-'Alj, the main gate into the imperial city of Meknes.

The word "bab" simply means "gate" in Arabic. The rest of the name is more surprising. "Mansur al-'Alj" refers to a man, and the word "'alj" was used for a Christian who had converted to Islam. Tradition holds that the gate's designer was exactly such a convert, a European who had come to serve the Moroccan court. So the grandest door of this Muslim capital carries the name of a man who began life in another faith.

To understand why this gate exists, you need to meet the ruler behind it. Sultan Moulay Ismail came to power in the late seventeenth century. He made Meknes his capital and spent decades turning it into an imperial city. He built palaces, granaries, stables, and long walls that still run for kilometres around the old town. He ruled for more than half a century. In Europe, he is often compared with his contemporary, Louis the Fourteenth of France — two kings who built on an enormous scale at the same time.

Moulay Ismail did not live to see this gate finished. The work is usually said to have been completed in the early 1730s, under his son, Moulay Abdallah. That small fact explains something about the place. The gate was not only a doorway. It was a statement of continuity. The new sultan finished his father's project and placed it at the front of the city, facing the people, for everyone to see.

Now look at the shape. The central opening is a tall horseshoe arch, the kind of arch that curves inward slightly at the bottom, like the shoe of a horse. This form is common across Moroccan and Andalusian architecture. Around it, the surface is covered in raised geometric patterns and interlacing lines. On either side, the arch is framed by projecting towers, or bastions. These made the gate look defensive. In practice, the decoration tells you its real purpose was display.

The colour comes from zellij. Zellij is the Moroccan craft of cutting small pieces of glazed tile by hand and fitting them together like a puzzle. Here, much of the surface uses a soft green against cream and darker tones. Green has strong associations with Islam, and it also marked royal buildings. Step closer and you will see that the tiles are not perfectly uniform. Each small piece was shaped by hand, and the light falls a little differently on every one.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look at the marble columns set into the bastions on either side. They do not quite match the rest of the gate. These columns are often said to come from Volubilis, the Roman ruins about thirty kilometres north of here. Moulay Ismail's builders reused stone from older sites across the region. So when you look at this eighteenth-century gate, you may also be looking at pieces of a Roman town, carried here and given a new life.

Raise your eyes to the very top. A long band of Arabic calligraphy runs across the upper part of the gate. Inscriptions like this were a normal feature of royal architecture. They named the patron and praised the building. The script is part of the decoration, so the writing and the patterns flow into each other. Even if you cannot read Arabic, notice how the letters are stretched and spaced to fill the band evenly, from one end to the other.

Turn around for a moment and look at the square behind you. This is Place el-Hedim. The gate was designed to face this open space, and the relationship matters. A gate like this works best when people can stand back and take it in. In the late afternoon, the square fills with families, storytellers, food sellers, and children chasing each other. The gate becomes a backdrop to ordinary evening life, as it has been for generations.

On the far side of the square, the narrow lanes of the old medina begin. The medina is the historic walled town, with its markets and houses. Behind the gate, by contrast, lay the world of the court — palaces, gardens, and the sultan's household. So this gate sat on a real dividing line. On one side was the crowded city of traders and craftsmen. On the other side was the protected space of power. The gate stood exactly between them.

The historic city of Meknes is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. That recognition covers far more than this single gate. It includes the walls, the old town, and the imperial buildings beyond. But Bab Mansur is the image most people carry away. It appears on postcards and in countless photographs. It is the place where many visitors begin their walk, and where many of them pause at the end of the day.

If you want to see the gate at its best, think about the light. In the morning, the facade can be in partial shade. Later in the day, the sun moves around and the tiles begin to glow. After dark, the gate is often lit from below, and the patterns take on a completely different character. It is worth returning at a second time of day if you can. The same surface can look like two different buildings.

Before you move on, let your eyes rest on one small section. Choose a single panel of tile, or one column, or one curve of the arch. Think of the hands that cut those pieces and set them in place. Listen to the square around you — the voices, the hooves, the wind. The gate has watched this square for nearly three centuries. As you walk away, glance back once. The green tiles will still be catching the light behind you.
