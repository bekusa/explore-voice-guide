The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your hand. The walls here are rough and uneven, a patchwork of flint and rubble bound together with mortar. Run your fingers along them and you feel small, sharp edges. Listen, too. On most days the loudest sounds are rooks in the trees and the wind moving across open Suffolk farmland. It is quiet. Yet for centuries, this ring of stone stood at the centre of power, politics and ambition in eastern England.

Welcome to Framlingham Castle. Look around you and notice something unusual. There is no great square keep in the middle, no single tower where a lord would make a last stand. Instead, the strength of this castle lies in its outer wall. A tall curtain of stone forms a loop around the inner courtyard, and thirteen towers stand along it at intervals. Each tower could cover the stretch of wall beside it. It was a forward-looking design for its age.

The story of this castle belongs, first of all, to one family: the Bigods. They were powerful Norman nobles, and they became Earls of Norfolk. In the twelfth century, the Bigods were often at odds with the crown. Hugh Bigod joined a major rebellion against King Henry the Second in the 1170s. The revolt failed. As punishment, the king ordered the earlier castle here to be taken apart and made useless. Royal anger, in other words, cleared the ground for what you see today.

The walls around you were raised by Hugh's son, Roger Bigod, near the end of the twelfth century. Roger chose not to rebuild in the old style. He invested in this curtain of towers. Look closely at the towers and you will see that many are open on the inner side. This was a deliberate choice. If an enemy captured a single tower, the defenders in the courtyard could still see inside and fire at them. The attackers would gain very little shelter.

The new walls faced a real test only a few years later. In 1216, King John was fighting his rebellious barons, and he brought his army to Framlingham. The castle was strong, but the siege was short. The garrison surrendered after a brief stand. Historians still discuss why. Perhaps the defenders felt they were outnumbered and isolated. Perhaps they simply chose to survive. Either way, the stone was never really tested in a long battle.

Now look up at the tops of the towers. You will see tall, elegant chimneys of red brick, many of them twisted or patterned. These are Tudor additions, from the sixteenth century. Here is the surprise. Most of these chimneys never carried smoke from any fire. They are decorative. They were added to make the old fortress look fashionable, rich and up to date. A medieval war machine was given a Tudor makeover, much like a new facade on an old house.

By that time, the castle had passed from the Bigods to other great families. Eventually it came to the Howards, the Dukes of Norfolk. They were among the most powerful nobles in Tudor England, and also among the most at risk. Their fortunes rose and fell with the moods of the monarch. Several of them ended up in serious trouble with the crown. If you visit Saint Michael's Church in the town below, you can see the elaborate tombs of members of this family.

The most dramatic moment in the castle's history came in 1553. King Edward the Sixth had died young. His advisers tried to place Lady Jane Grey on the throne instead of his half-sister, Mary. Mary did not accept this. She came to Framlingham and gathered her supporters here. Thousands of people rallied to her cause. From this quiet corner of Suffolk, she went on to claim the crown. Within days, she was recognised as queen. This courtyard helped to decide who would rule England.

After the excitement of the Tudors, the castle entered a much humbler life. In the seventeenth century, the estate was left to Pembroke College in Cambridge. The terms of that gift were charitable. In time, a poorhouse was built inside the walls, a place where people with no money or support could live and work. So the same courtyard that had welcomed a queen later sheltered some of the poorest people in the parish. The building you see inside the walls comes from this later chapter.

One of the best experiences here is the wall walk. You can climb up and follow the path along much of the top of the curtain wall. From up there, you understand the site far better. You can see how the walls follow the shape of the ground. You can look down into the dry ditch that once added to the defences. And you can look out over the red roofs of the town, the church tower, and the gentle fields beyond.

On the western side, look for the stretch of water called the Mere. Today it is calm and green, with reeds along the edges and water birds on the surface. It may once have formed part of the defences, and it also added to the view of the castle for anyone approaching. In the evening, the light falls low across the water. The towers and walls are reflected on the surface, and the whole scene looks softer and less warlike.

Inside the castle buildings, there is also a local museum collection and a display on the castle's long story. Take your time with the smaller objects. Everyday items, such as tools and household goods, tell you about the people who lived in this town. Kings and dukes appear in the written records. But it was ordinary farmers, servants and townsfolk who kept this place running for centuries, and their traces are here too.

Before you leave, stand in the middle of the courtyard for a moment. Turn slowly in a full circle. Count the towers if you like. Look at the mix of materials... flint, rubble, brick, and later repairs, all fitted together. These walls carry the marks of rebels, kings, a queen and the poor. As you walk back down into Framlingham, glance over your shoulder. The ring of stone still sits on its rise above the town, quiet and patient, much as it has for more than eight hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Do the wall walk for the clearest view of the layout.
Wear sturdy shoes for the narrow steps and wall path.
Visit the Mere side in the evening for soft light.
Pair your visit with Saint Michael's Church in town.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed wall top.
