The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest arrives as sound. Water drips from leaves high above you. A bird calls somewhere you cannot place. Under your boots the ground is soft and damp, a deep layer of rotting leaves and roots. The air smells green and heavy, like wet earth after rain. Mist often hangs between the trees in the early morning. It lifts slowly as the sun climbs over the ridges.

This is Bwindi Impenetrable National Park, in the far southwest of Uganda. You are standing in Kanungu District, close to the border with the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The park covers a little over three hundred square kilometres of steep hills and narrow valleys. On a map, that area looks modest. On foot, it feels endless. The slopes rise and fall again and again, and the trees close over every one of them.

The name sounds like a warning, and in a way it is. The word Bwindi is usually linked to the local Rukiga language and an idea of darkness or an impassable place. Legend has it that the name comes from a swamp that once blocked travellers, a place people feared to cross. Locals tell versions of this story with different details. Whatever its origin, the English word impenetrable fits. Some valleys here are so thick with vines and ferns that guides cut a path with machetes.

This forest is very old. Scientists believe parts of it stayed green during the dry periods of the last Ice Age, when many other African forests shrank. That long survival is one reason the forest holds so many species. It became a national park in the early 1990s. A few years later, in 1994, UNESCO listed it as a World Heritage Site. UNESCO gives that status to places judged to matter for all of humanity, not only for one country.

Now for the reason most visitors come. Bwindi is home to roughly half of all the mountain gorillas left on Earth. The rest live mainly in the volcanic mountains a short distance to the south, across three countries. Mountain gorillas are among the most endangered great apes. Their numbers fell to a very low point in the twentieth century. Careful protection, patrols, and tourism income have helped the population grow slowly again. That growth is one of the quieter conservation success stories in Africa.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The gorillas you can visit belong to habituated families. Over a period of years, researchers and rangers visited these groups almost every day. Slowly, the gorillas learned to accept calm human presence. Only a small group of visitors may join each family on any day. They spend about one hour with them, and then they walk away. This strict limit protects the animals from stress and from human illness. Gorillas can catch many of our diseases, including a simple cold.

The trek itself is part of the experience. Trackers go out early to find where the family nested the night before. Gorillas build fresh nests of bent branches every evening. Your walk might take one hour, or it might take most of the day. The paths climb steeply and turn to mud after rain. Then, suddenly, the guide raises a hand. You hear branches crack. A dark shape moves through the leaves... and a gorilla sits a few metres away, chewing a stem.

But Bwindi is far more than gorillas. More than one hundred mammal species live in this forest. They include chimpanzees, several kinds of monkeys, forest elephants, and small antelopes called duikers. Most of these animals are shy and rarely seen. Birdwatchers come from around the world for a different reason. The park holds around 350 bird species. Many of them live only in the Albertine Rift, the chain of mountains along Africa's western Rift Valley.

One of those birds is a small surprise. The African green broadbill is a tiny, bright green bird found in very few places on Earth. Bwindi is one of them. Seeing it takes patience, sharp eyes, and usually a specialist guide. Even if you never spot it, look up into the canopy. You will see sunbirds flashing colour, and you may hear turacos calling with a deep, rolling sound.

There is a human story here too, and it is not a simple one. Before the park existed, the Batwa people lived in this forest for generations. The Batwa are a forest people who hunted and gathered here. When the national park was created, they were moved out of the forest. Many Batwa communities still live on its edges today, often in difficult conditions. Some community projects near the park now let Batwa guides share their traditions, songs, and knowledge of forest plants with visitors.

The people of Kanungu District also shape life around the park. Small farms climb the hills right up to the forest edge. You will see tea plantations, banana trees, and fields of beans and potatoes. The line between farmland and forest is often sharp, like a wall of green. Gorillas sometimes cross that line to raid crops. Local programmes work with farmers to reduce these conflicts, because the park needs the support of its neighbours to survive.

Take a moment to notice the plants close to you. Giant tree ferns rise above the path, their fronds spreading like umbrellas. Moss covers fallen trunks in thick, soft layers. Vines hang from branches high overhead. Light reaches the forest floor only in small patches, so many plants here have broad, dark leaves to catch it. After rain, every surface shines with water.

So stand still for one more minute. Let the sounds return around you: the drip of water, the insects, the distant birds. Somewhere on these hills, a gorilla family is moving slowly through the undergrowth, feeding and resting, much as their ancestors did. As you walk away, look back at the dark line of trees on the ridge. The forest closes again behind you, and keeps its quiet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book gorilla trekking permits well ahead; numbers are strictly limited.
Wear waterproof boots with strong grip for muddy slopes.
Bring garden gloves for grabbing vines on steep paths.
Hire a local porter to help carry your bag.
Stay home from trekking if you have a cold.
