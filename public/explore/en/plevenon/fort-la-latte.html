The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the walls, the sea moves against the rocks with a slow, heavy rhythm. The wind comes straight off the English Channel, carrying salt and the cry of gulls. Under your feet, the path is hard and stony, and the heather and gorse along its edges smell faintly sweet in the sun. Ahead, grey towers rise from a narrow spur of rock, with water on almost every side. This is Fort-la-Latte, on the northern coast of Brittany, in the commune of Plévenon.

Before we go inside, look at where you are standing. This stretch of shoreline is called the Emerald Coast, and on a bright day you will understand why. The water below can turn green, then turquoise, then deep slate blue as clouds pass. Across the bay to the west, you may see the cliffs of Cap Fréhel and its lighthouse. The fort sits on its own headland, facing the open sea, and it was placed here for exactly that reason.

The castle did not always carry this name. Its older name is the Château de la Roche Goyon, after the Goyon family, a powerful noble line in medieval Brittany. The family later became linked with the Matignon name, which you may notice in local place names nearby. The first stronghold on this rock dates from the fourteenth century. At that time, Brittany was still an independent duchy, and its coast was a place of constant watchfulness.

Think about what a fortress here needed to do. It had to watch the sea for ships. It had to control the bay and protect the surrounding land. And it had to survive attack from both water and shore. The rock gave it natural defences on the seaward side. On the landward side, the builders cut the ground away and added walls, ditches and gates. As you approach, notice how the path narrows and turns. That was deliberate.

Now look ahead at the entrance. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The fort has two drawbridges, one after the other, crossing deep clefts in the rock. An attacker who managed to pass the first would find himself trapped in a small space, under the eyes and weapons of the defenders, facing a second gate. Walk slowly across, and look down into the gaps. The sea has carved some of these channels, and the builders simply made use of them.

The centre of the castle is the keep, the tall tower that rises above everything else. In a medieval fortress, the keep was the last refuge. If the outer walls fell, the lord and his people could withdraw here. Its walls are thick, and its windows are small and high. From the top, the view runs along the whole coastline. On a clear day, it is easy to imagine a lookout standing in the wind, scanning the horizon for sails.

The castle you see today is not exactly the medieval one. Over the centuries, it was damaged during the wars that shook Brittany, and it fell in and out of use. Later, in the age of King Louis the Fourteenth, France looked at its coasts with new concern. Enemy fleets, especially from England, were a real threat. Fortresses like this one were adapted for a new kind of war, a war of cannon and gunpowder rather than arrows and siege ladders.

During that period, the military engineer Siméon Garangeau worked on the defences here. He was a close collaborator of Vauban, the great fortress builder of Louis the Fourteenth, and he is also known for his work at nearby Saint-Malo. Under this programme, the medieval castle became a coastal battery. Gun positions were set up to cover the sea approaches, and the old knightly stronghold became a working artillery fort.

Now comes one of the most unusual things in the whole site. Look for a small, solid stone structure known as the cannonball oven. Its purpose sounds strange today. Soldiers heated iron cannonballs in it until they glowed red. Then they loaded them into cannon and fired them at wooden ships. A red-hot ball could lodge in the timber and set a vessel on fire. Few of these ovens survive in France, which makes this one a rare piece of military history.

By the nineteenth century, the fort had lost its military importance. Warships had changed, and the defences here were no longer useful. For a long time, the castle stood neglected, open to the weather, with stone falling and walls crumbling. Its revival came in the twentieth century, when private owners took it on and began a long programme of restoration. Much of what you walk through today is the result of that patient work.

Here is a second surprise. If these walls feel somehow familiar, you may have seen them on a screen. The fort was used as a filming location for the Hollywood film The Vikings, released in 1958, with Kirk Douglas and Tony Curtis. The production chose this coast for its dramatic cliffs and its raw, wild look. Standing on the ramparts, with the wind pulling at your jacket, you can see why a film crew wanted it.

The path to the fort also carries a piece of local folklore. Along the way, many visitors pass a tall standing stone. Locals call it the Finger of Gargantua, after the giant of French folk tales. According to popular legend, the giant left it here. The stone is far older than the castle, a reminder that people marked this landscape long before any lord built a tower on the rock.

Before you leave, find a quiet place on the walls and stand still for a moment. Feel the stone under your hand. It is rough, cool, and marked by lichen and salt. Look out toward Cap Fréhel, and then down at the water around the base of the rock. As you walk back along the path, turn around once more. From a distance, the fort looks small against the sea... and yet it has stood here, watching this water, for more than six centuries.
