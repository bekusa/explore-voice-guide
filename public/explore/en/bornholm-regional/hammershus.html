The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes in off the Baltic, low and steady, and it moves through empty window openings above you. Under your feet the path is hard and uneven. It is grey granite, the old bedrock of Bornholm, pushing up through thin grass. Gulls call from the cliffs below. Ahead, broken walls rise against the sky. Some are tall and sharp, others no higher than your knee. This is Hammershus, on the northwest tip of the island. For a long stretch of the Middle Ages, whoever held these walls controlled Bornholm.

Before any history, notice the position. The castle sits on a long ridge of rock, high above the sea. On one side, steep cliffs drop to the water. On the other, a deep valley cuts the ridge off from the land behind it. Nature built half the defences here. The builders only had to finish the job. From these walls, a watchman could see ships long before they reached shore. On a clear day, the coast of Sweden appears as a thin line on the horizon.

Hammershus is often described as the largest medieval castle ruin in Northern Europe. Walk its full length and that claim starts to feel physical. The walls stretch far along the ridge, enclosing courtyards, cellars, gatehouses, and the foundations of halls. This was never a single tower. It was a small fortified town of stone, with storerooms, workshops, living quarters, and a garrison. Hundreds of years of building are layered here, one century set on top of another.

The castle was most likely begun in the mid thirteenth century. Historians usually link its founding to the Archbishop of Lund. Lund is now in southern Sweden, but in that period it was part of the Danish realm, and its archbishop was the most powerful churchman in Scandinavia. He held large estates on Bornholm. He also had a long and bitter struggle with the Danish kings. A fortress on this rock gave the Church real military power. The exact founder and the exact year are still debated, so treat any single date with some caution.

Here is something many visitors miss. For much of its early life, Hammershus was not a royal castle at all. It was a bishop's stronghold, and it was used against the crown. The king and the archbishop fought over who truly ruled Bornholm. Control of the castle passed back and forth more than once. So when you look at these walls, you are looking at a building made for a quarrel between Church and king, not only for defence against foreign enemies.

Now find the tallest square tower near the heart of the ruin. This is the Mantle Tower, the core of the castle. Its lower walls are built from rough granite blocks, and you can see brick in some of the later work above. That change in material is a clue. Granite was the local stone, hard to shape but always close at hand. Brick came later, with new ideas about building. Stand at the base and look up. The walls are thick enough to hold a small room inside them.

In the sixteenth century, a surprising new owner arrived. The Danish crown pledged Bornholm to the city of Lübeck, a powerful German trading port of the Hanseatic League. The Hanseatic League was a network of merchant cities that controlled much of the trade across the Baltic and North Seas. For around half a century, Lübeck's officials ran the island from this castle. They collected taxes and managed trade. Islanders often remembered that period as a hard one. Eventually the island returned to Danish control.

The seventeenth century brought the castle's most dramatic years. After a war in 1658, Denmark had to give Bornholm to Sweden. The islanders did not accept this quietly. They rose up against the Swedish garrison, and the Swedish commander was killed in the town of Rønne. The rebels then offered the island back to the Danish king. Bornholm returned to Denmark in 1660. Locals still tell this story with real pride. It is one of the few times the island chose its own side.

Soon after, Hammershus became a prison for two famous prisoners. Leonora Christina, a daughter of King Christian the Fourth, and her husband Corfitz Ulfeldt were held here. Ulfeldt had fallen from favour and was accused of treason. The couple tried to escape, but they were caught and kept under stricter guard. Leonora Christina later spent many years imprisoned in Copenhagen, and she wrote a famous account of her suffering. Her writing is still read in Denmark today. Picture her here, looking at the same grey sea.

After these events, the castle slowly lost its purpose. Warfare had changed, and cannons made high medieval walls far less useful. In the eighteenth century, Hammershus was abandoned. Then something sad happened. People took the stone away. Builders across the island used the ruins as a quarry. Some of the stone here may now sit in farmhouses, harbours, and walls elsewhere on Bornholm. Only in the early nineteenth century did the state protect the site, and the dismantling finally stopped.

What you see today is the result of that protection, and of careful restoration since. Some walls have been stabilised so they do not collapse. Paths and steps guide you between the courtyards. Near the approach, there is a modern visitor centre. It is set low into the landscape so it does not compete with the ruin. Its long, quiet form gives a sharp contrast with the broken medieval stone. Inside, you can learn more about the castle and the lives of the people who lived here.

Take a moment to look beyond the walls. The land around Hammershus is wild and open, with heath, rock, and low trees bent by the wind. To the north lies the Hammeren headland, a knob of granite with walking trails and sea views. Old quarries nearby have filled with clear water. In spring and summer, small flowers grow in cracks between the stones of the ruin itself. The castle has become part of the landscape again. It is no longer only a building.

If you have time, walk the full circuit slowly. Go down into the cellars, where the air is cool and smells of damp stone. Climb to the higher points, where the wind is strongest. Look out through an arrow slit and imagine an archer standing in that same narrow space. Each room had a purpose once. A kitchen, a chapel, a storeroom, a guard post. Without roofs or floors, you must use your imagination to build them again.

Before you leave, turn and face the sea one more time. The light on the Baltic changes quickly here. Clouds move fast, and the water shifts from grey to silver to deep blue. Bishops, kings, merchants, soldiers, and prisoners all stood on this rock and watched the same horizon. Their walls are broken now, but the ridge, the wind, and the sea are unchanged. As you walk back along the path, listen again. The gulls are still calling below the cliffs.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven granite paths and steps.
Bring a windproof layer; the ridge is very exposed.
Visit early morning or evening for softer light.
Allow time for the Hammeren headland trails nearby.
Start at the visitor centre to understand the layout.
