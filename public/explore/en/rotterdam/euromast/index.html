The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the tower and tilt your head back. The concrete shaft rises from the green lawns of Het Park, pale and smooth, until it seems to narrow into the sky. Around you, there is the sound of wind in tall trees, the distant rumble of traffic, and now and then the low horn of a ship on the river nearby. This is the Euromast. For more than half a century, it has given Rotterdam a way to look at itself from above.

To understand why this tower exists, it helps to remember what Rotterdam lost. In May 1940, German bombing destroyed much of the old city centre. Whole streets, churches and homes disappeared in a single afternoon. After the war, the city chose not to rebuild its past brick by brick. It chose to build something new, with wide streets, modern blocks and bold engineering. The Euromast belongs to that spirit. It is a tower that looks forward rather than back.

The tower was designed by the Dutch architect Hugh Maaskant, one of the key figures of postwar Rotterdam. It was built for the Floriade of 1960, a large international horticultural exhibition held here in Het Park. Think about that for a moment... a concrete tower, created for a flower show. The organisers wanted a viewpoint that would draw crowds and give visitors a sense of the city's ambition. The exhibition closed, but the tower stayed, and it soon became part of the Rotterdam skyline.

In its first form, the Euromast was roughly one hundred metres tall. Near the top sits a wide platform that looks a little like a ship's crow's nest. That shape is no accident. Rotterdam is a port city, and the river and the harbour shape almost everything here. From the platform, the first visitors could look out over cranes, docks and the long silver line of the river. For a city still rebuilding, that view carried real meaning.

Here is the first surprise. The tall, thin needle you see above the platform was not part of the original design. It was added around 1970, and it is called the Space Tower. This extension raised the Euromast to roughly 185 metres. Locals often tell a story about a friendly rivalry with the city of Utrecht. Utrecht's medieval cathedral tower had long been the tallest structure of its kind in the Netherlands, and according to popular tale, Rotterdam was happy to finally look down on it.

The Space Tower brought something else, too. A glass cabin, known as the Euroscoop, wraps around the upper shaft. It rotates slowly as it climbs, so the whole city turns around you while you rise. The movement is gentle. You feel it more in the shifting view than in your body. Watch how the river slides past the windows, then the rooftops, then the green of the park, as if the city is circling you.

Now look down at the base again. The tower is slender, and many visitors wonder how it stays upright in the strong winds off the North Sea. The answer lies underground. A massive concrete foundation holds the structure steady. Above it, the shaft is designed to sway very slightly in high winds, which is normal for tall towers. The motion is far too small to see from here, but engineers plan for it carefully.

From the upper levels, the view stretches across a city that looks very different from most of the Netherlands. You will not see rows of gabled canal houses. Instead, you see glass towers, sharp angles and bold experiments. Look for the white cables and tall pylon of the Erasmus Bridge, which local people sometimes call the Swan. Beyond it, the skyline of the Kop van Zuid district rises on the southern bank of the river.

Turn the other way, and the port comes into view. Rotterdam has one of the largest seaports in the world, and it stretches far to the west, towards the sea. On a clear day, you can follow the river past cranes, container stacks and industrial zones. Ships move slowly along the water, and from this height they look like toys. It is a good place to understand that this city lives by trade and by the river.

Here is the second surprise, and it is right under your feet in a different sense. Not far from the tower, the Maastunnel runs beneath the river. It opened in the early 1940s and still carries cars, cyclists and pedestrians under the water. Some of its pedestrian sections have long wooden escalators. The Euromast and the tunnel together tell a story about Rotterdam: one reaches up into the sky, the other reaches down beneath the river.

The tower is not only a viewpoint. Inside, there is a restaurant where people eat while looking out over the city. There are also a small number of hotel rooms high in the shaft, which makes the Euromast one of the more unusual places to spend a night in Europe. Over the years, the tower has also hosted adventurous events, such as abseiling down its side. It is a place where people come to celebrate, to propose, or simply to sit and watch.

The park below deserves your attention too. Het Park was laid out in the nineteenth century as a green space near the river. Today it has winding paths, old trees, ponds and wide lawns. Families picnic here in summer, and runners loop around the tower in every season. Because the Euromast rises from the park rather than from a busy street, the base feels calm. It is easy to forget that a working port sits only a short walk away.

The Euromast is now protected as a national monument, which means its postwar design is treated as part of Dutch heritage. That might seem strange for a concrete tower that is not even a century old. But heritage is not only about age. It is about what a place means to people. For Rotterdam, this tower marks the years when the city chose to rise again, with confidence and a little bit of pride.

Before you leave, take one last look up at the shaft. Notice how the light changes on the concrete as clouds move across the sky. Listen again for the wind in the trees and the sound of ships on the river. As you walk away through the park, glance back now and then. You will see the tower from new angles, between branches and over rooftops... a quiet, steady marker of a city that rebuilt itself.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a clear day to see far along the port.
Ride the rotating Euroscoop for a slow circular city view.
Walk through Het Park before or after your visit.
Book ahead if you want a table at the restaurant.
Combine your visit with a walk to the Maastunnel.
