The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere ahead, a gibbon is calling, a rising, looping whoop that carries over the paths. Closer by, gravel crunches underfoot, and the air smells of wet leaves and warm straw. Water moves somewhere out of sight. This is Diergaarde Blijdorp, the Rotterdam Zoo, and it sits in a city better known for cranes, container ships and sharp modern towers. Here, inside a green pocket of the Blijdorp district, the pace slows down. Children run ahead, and the sounds of traffic slowly fade away.

The zoo is much older than this site. It began in the nineteenth century, in 1857, as a private society garden in the centre of Rotterdam. In those days a zoo was a place for the city's well-off families. They walked among the cages on Sunday afternoons, listened to music and looked at animals from distant colonies. Over the decades the city grew all around the old garden. It became cramped, hemmed in by streets and buildings, and plans were made to move it to open land on the northern edge of town.

Then history stepped in. On 14 May 1940, German bombers destroyed much of central Rotterdam. Within days the Netherlands had surrendered. The move to Blijdorp, already planned, now became urgent. Keepers moved animals across a damaged city under occupation. The new zoo opened at the end of 1940, in the first year of the war. It is a strange thought — a garden built for pleasure, opening in one of the darkest years this city has known.

The architect of the new zoo was Sybold van Ravesteyn. He was a modernist who had turned toward something more playful, with curves, ornament and a theatrical sense of space. His largest building here is the Rivièrahal. It is a long, gently curving hall with tall windows and elegant lines. Many visitors hurry past it on the way to the elephants. Pause instead. The building is now a protected monument, and it shows how the zoo was imagined in 1940 — as a place of beauty, not only of animals.

Today the zoo follows a different idea. Instead of grouping animals by type, it arranges them by part of the world. You walk from Asia to Africa, and then on toward the Americas and other regions. Plants, buildings and paths change with each zone. The goal is for you to see an animal in something closer to its own landscape. It also tells a story about how zoos have changed — from collections of curiosities toward places that focus on habitats and conservation.

In the Asian part of the park, look for the elephant area known as Taman Indah. The name comes from Indonesian and means beautiful garden. The herd has space to bathe, to dust themselves with sand and to move as a family group. Watch the youngest elephants closely. They stay near their mothers and often copy them, trying out a trunk movement or a splash of water. Nearby you will find a Chinese garden, with a pavilion, a pond and quiet planting. It is a good place to rest your feet.

The gorillas have a famous chapter in the zoo's recent past. In 2007, a male gorilla named Bokito climbed out of his enclosure. He crossed the moat and injured a woman who had visited him very often. Afterwards, researchers and keepers talked openly about eye contact. To a male gorilla, a long, direct stare can feel like a challenge. So when you stand at the glass today, try something gentle. Look, then glance away. You are a guest in his space.

Now walk toward the Oceanium. It opened in 2001 and adds a whole world of water to the zoo. Inside, the light turns blue and the sound becomes softer. Sharks glide above you as you walk through a tunnel. Rays move past like slow, flat shadows. Here is a small surprise many visitors miss. The Oceanium follows a route across the Atlantic Ocean, from coasts to deep water. It tells one continuous story about one ocean, not just a series of separate tanks.

At the touch pools and coastal displays, the Oceanium becomes more personal. Children lean over the water and look at the small creatures of rock and shore. It is a reminder that this is a port city. Rotterdam lives by the sea, by the river Maas and by the great ships that leave its harbour every day. A zoo here that looks out toward the ocean feels natural. The North Sea is not far from where you stand.

In the Amazonica greenhouse, the air suddenly becomes warm and heavy. Your glasses may fog for a moment. Butterflies drift between leaves, and sometimes they land on a sleeve or a shoulder. Stand still and let your eyes adjust. Small birds and other animals move freely through the plants, so the space feels like a forest, not an exhibit. On a grey Dutch afternoon, this humid greenhouse is one of the best places in the whole zoo to slow down.

The zoo is more than a day out. Like many modern zoos, Blijdorp takes part in international breeding programmes for threatened species. Each birth here is part of a larger plan shared between zoos in many countries. The aim is to keep healthy, varied populations of animals that are under pressure in the wild. Read the signs near the enclosures. They often explain where a species lives, why it is in danger, and what work is being done to help it.

Blijdorp also carries a quiet second story — the story of Rotterdam itself. The city lost its old heart in 1940 and chose to rebuild in a bold, modern way. The zoo was part of the same moment. It moved, began again and kept growing. Over the decades it added new halls, new water worlds and new landscapes. In a city that often looks toward the future, this garden of trees and animals connects people to the long years behind it.

Before you leave, find a bench and listen once more. The gibbons may still be calling. Water moves in a pond somewhere, and a child laughs on the path. Look up at the tall trees, many of them planted when the zoo was young and the city was in ruins. They have grown through more than eighty years of change. As you walk back toward the gates and the streets of Blijdorp, notice how quickly the city sounds return.
