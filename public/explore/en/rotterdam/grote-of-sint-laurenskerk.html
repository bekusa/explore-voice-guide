The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the paving of the square and listen. Trams ring a few streets away. Bicycles tick past. Then you look up, and a wall of pale stone and dark brick rises above you, quiet and heavy, older than everything around it. This is the Grote of Sint-Laurenskerk, the Great Church of Saint Lawrence. In a city famous for glass towers and bold modern shapes, it is the one building that carries the Middle Ages into the present.

The name needs a short explanation. Grote Kerk simply means Great Church. That title was given to the main parish church in many Dutch towns. Saint Lawrence was an early Christian martyr from Rome. Tradition says he was put to death on an iron grill, so artists often show him holding one. Rotterdam took him as the patron of its most important church, and the name has stayed for more than five centuries.

The church you see belongs to the late Gothic period. Work on it began in the middle of the fifteenth century, when Rotterdam was still a modest town of fishermen, traders and boatbuilders. Building went on for many decades. This was normal for large medieval churches. Money came in slowly, workers came and went, and each generation added its part. Look at the walls and you can see the mix of materials: brick for much of the body, and lighter natural stone for the tower and the details.

The tower deserves a long look. It is broad and solid rather than slim and pointed. Rotterdam sits on soft, wet ground close to sea level. Builders here always fought against sinking foundations. Heavy stone towers could lean or settle over time, and this one gave its builders real trouble. So when you see its strong, square form, remember that it is a product of careful engineering on very difficult soil.

Now the moment that changed everything. On the 14th of May 1940, German bombers attacked the centre of Rotterdam. Within a short time, fire spread through the old city. Thousands of buildings were destroyed, and the medieval heart of Rotterdam almost completely disappeared. The Laurenskerk burned too. Its roof fell in, and the interior was badly damaged. But the outer walls and the tower survived the fire.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Today, this church is the only significant medieval building left in central Rotterdam. Everything around you, the apartment blocks, the shops, the famous market hall nearby, was built after the war. When you look at old photographs of the city before 1940, you see narrow streets and gabled houses everywhere. Now the church stands almost alone as a witness to that lost town.

After the war, there was a real debate about what to do with the ruin. Some people thought that a burned church had no place in a new, modern city. Others saw it as a symbol of survival. In the end the decision was to restore it. The work took many years, and the church reopened in the late 1960s. So the building in front of you is both medieval and postwar at the same time. The shape is old, but much of the fabric was repaired by twentieth century hands.

Walk to the main entrance and look closely at the doors. They are made of bronze and were created by the Italian sculptor Giacomo Manzù. Their theme is war and peace. This is a fitting subject for a church that almost died in the bombing. Take time with the panels. The figures are modern and expressive, not medieval at all. That contrast between old stone and modern bronze tells the whole story of the building in a single glance.

Inside, the space is light and open. This church became Protestant after the Reformation of the sixteenth century, when the Netherlands broke with the Catholic Church. Protestant worship placed the sermon at the centre, so interiors were cleared of many statues and paintings. What remains is calm and spacious. Notice how the tall windows fill the nave with daylight, and how your footsteps echo against the stone floor.

Here is the second surprise. This church holds one of the largest organs in the Netherlands, built by the Danish firm Marcussen in the 1970s. Its great case fills the western end of the church with rows of shining pipes. If you are lucky enough to arrive during a concert or a rehearsal, stop and listen. The sound does not come from one point. It fills the whole building, and you feel it in your chest as much as you hear it.

Look also for the tombs of Dutch naval officers. Rotterdam was a seafaring city, and in the seventeenth century the Dutch fleet fought major battles at sea. Several admirals and captains from that era are honoured here with monuments. Among them is Witte de With, a well-known commander of the Dutch Republic. These memorials are carved with ships, weapons and symbols of fame. They show how closely this church was tied to the sea and to the city's trade.

When you step back outside, look for the bronze statue of Erasmus on the square. Erasmus was a great scholar of the Renaissance, and he was born in Rotterdam. The statue was made in the early seventeenth century and is often described as the oldest bronze statue in the Netherlands. Locals like to tell a playful tale: whenever the church clock strikes, Erasmus turns a page of his book. It is only a legend, of course, but watch him for a while anyway.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the square and turn slowly in a full circle. You will see glass, concrete, steel, and the curved roof of the market hall. Then your eye returns to the old brick and stone of the church. Rotterdam chose to rebuild itself as a modern city. But it also chose to keep this one voice from the past. As you walk away, listen once more for the bells above the noise of the street.
