The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. On one side you can hear traffic on the Westzeedijk, the old dyke road that runs along the river Maas. On the other side there is the quieter sound of the Museumpark, with wind in the trees and footsteps on gravel. The Kunsthal sits right between these two worlds. Its walls are a mix of materials: grey concrete, ribbed translucent panels, glass, and plain steel. Nothing here tries to look grand. The building almost looks unfinished, and that is deliberate.

The Kunsthal opened in 1992. It was designed by the Rotterdam architect Rem Koolhaas and his firm, the Office for Metropolitan Architecture, usually called OMA. At that time Koolhaas was better known for his writing and bold competition entries than for finished buildings. This was one of the first major projects that showed what his ideas looked like in concrete and steel. Architects still travel to Rotterdam to walk through it, because many later OMA buildings around the world grew from experiments made here.

The name already tells you something important. A kunsthal is an art hall, not a museum. The building has no permanent collection of its own. Instead it hosts a changing programme of temporary exhibitions, several at the same time. One room might show fashion, another photography, another design or popular culture, and another a painter with a big name. Your visit today will be different from the visit of someone who came last season, and that change is the whole idea.

Now look at how the building meets the ground. The site has a real problem. The dyke road is high, and the park is lower. Koolhaas did not hide this difference in height. He made it part of the design. A public path runs right through the building, sloping from the dyke level down toward the park. You can walk through the Kunsthal without buying a ticket and without going into a single gallery. The city's movement passes straight through the building.

That sloping path is the key to the whole design. Inside, the building works almost like a continuous spiral. Ramps lead you from one hall to the next, and the floors tilt and turn. You rarely climb a normal staircase. Instead you drift upward or downward, and every few steps the view changes. Sometimes you look into a gallery, sometimes out to the park, sometimes back to the place you just came from. The building wants you to keep moving.

Here is something many visitors miss. Pay attention to the floors under your feet as you move through. They change material from space to space. In some places you walk on concrete, in others on wood, and in others on steel grating that lets you see light below. Each surface has its own sound and its own feel. Koolhaas treated these cheap, ordinary materials with the same care that older architects gave to marble.

The auditorium is another surprise. It sits on a slope, so the floor itself becomes the seating, angled down toward the front. In one of the halls, look at the columns. They do not all stand straight up in neat rows. Some lean at angles, which can make the space feel slightly unstable, almost playful. These choices were controversial in the early 1990s. Some critics found them careless. Others saw a new freedom in architecture.

Outside again, look up at the roofline. Bright orange steel beams cross the top of the building. They stand out sharply against the grey Rotterdam sky. Big letters spell out the name, so the Kunsthal also works almost like a sign along the busy road. This mix of art building and advertising board was intentional. Koolhaas was interested in the energy of the modern city, with its traffic, signs, and crowds, rather than in quiet, closed temples of art.

To understand why the Kunsthal looks this way, think about Rotterdam itself. German bombing destroyed much of the old city centre in May 1940. After the war, Rotterdam chose to rebuild in a modern way instead of copying what was lost. Over the decades the city became a testing ground for new architecture. The Kunsthal belongs to that attitude. It does not pretend to be old, and it does not try to be polite. It is very much a Rotterdam building.

The Kunsthal also has a darker chapter. In October 2012, thieves broke in at night and stole seven paintings from a private collection that was on loan for an exhibition. The works included paintings by Picasso, Matisse, Monet, Gauguin, and Lucian Freud. The theft took only a few minutes and made headlines around the world. Most of the paintings were never recovered, and investigators believed some were destroyed. For an art hall that depends on borrowed works, it was a painful moment.

After that difficult period, the building went through a major renovation. OMA, the original architects, returned to lead the work, which was completed in 2014. They improved the climate control, the insulation, and the entrances, while keeping the spirit of the original design. It is rare for an architect to come back and update their own building after more than twenty years. The result feels close to the 1992 version, but the building is better suited to hosting valuable loans today.

When you step back into the Museumpark, look around at the neighbours. Koolhaas and OMA also worked on the layout of this park, together with the landscape architect Yves Brunier. Nearby you will find the Natural History Museum and Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen. Beside the Boijmans stands its newer art depot, a round building covered in mirrors that reflects the trees and the sky. Together these buildings make the park a kind of open-air catalogue of Dutch architecture.

If you have time, walk the full public path through the building, from the dyke down to the park. Then turn around and walk back the other way. The experience changes depending on your direction. Going down, you see the green park open up ahead. Going up, you face the city and the river. Notice how the light changes too, as it moves from bright glass sections to the softer glow behind the ribbed panels.

Before you leave, stop on the park side and look at the whole building once more. Look at the concrete, the orange beams, the glass, and the people moving up and down the ramp inside. In a moment you will hear the traffic on the dyke again, or the wind in the trees behind you. The Kunsthal was built to sit between those two sounds — between the busy city and the quiet park. You are standing exactly where it wanted you to be.

Wear comfortable shoes for the long sloping ramps.
