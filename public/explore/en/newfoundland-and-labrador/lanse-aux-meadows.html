The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across low grass. Somewhere below, small waves break on a rocky shore. The ground under your feet is soft and springy, a mix of peat, moss and short heath. There are almost no trees here, only open meadow running down to a cold, grey-blue bay. On a clear day the light is sharp and clean. On a foggy day the whole world shrinks to a few hundred metres. Either way, you are standing at the very northern tip of Newfoundland's Great Northern Peninsula.

Look out across the meadow and you will see a scatter of low, grassy ridges. They are easy to miss. They look like nothing more than bumps in the turf. But these faint outlines mark the walls of buildings raised by Norse seafarers around a thousand years ago. This is the only Norse site in North America that experts widely accept as authentic. Long before Columbus crossed the Atlantic, people from Greenland and Iceland stood on this same shore.

The story of how the site was found is a quiet surprise. For centuries, two medieval Icelandic texts, known as the Vinland sagas, described voyages west from Greenland to a land called Vinland. Many scholars treated these tales as half legend. Then, in 1960, the Norwegian explorer Helge Ingstad and his wife, the archaeologist Anne Stine Ingstad, came searching along this coast. A local man named George Decker led them to the overgrown mounds. Local families already knew these mounds were there. They simply did not know what they were.

The Ingstads led excavations here through the 1960s. Later, Parks Canada carried out more digging in the 1970s. Together, the teams uncovered the remains of eight buildings. Some were large halls with several rooms, where people slept, cooked and worked. Others were smaller huts and workshops. The buildings were made in the Norse style, with thick walls of cut sod laid over a timber frame. It was the same method used in Iceland and Greenland, where wood was scarce.

One small hut held something remarkable — a forge. The Norse here smelted iron from bog ore, a type of iron-rich deposit found in wet ground. They used it to make and repair nails, likely for their boats. Archaeologists found slag, the glassy waste left over from iron working. This is the earliest known iron production in the Americas. Think about that for a moment. A blacksmith worked by firelight on this windy shore, roughly a thousand years ago.

The finds also tell us who lived here. Excavators recovered a stone spindle whorl, a small weight used for spinning wool into thread. They found a bone needle and a bronze cloak pin. Spinning and sewing were usually women's work in Norse society, so many experts believe women were part of this group. The sagas mention one woman by name, Gudrid, who travelled to Vinland. Whether she ever stood here is not known. But the small objects suggest a community, not only a crew of sailors.

Here is a second surprise. Among the finds were butternuts, a kind of walnut. Butternut trees do not grow in Newfoundland. They grow much further south and west, around the Gulf of Saint Lawrence and beyond. So someone carried these nuts here from a warmer region. Many researchers now think this site was a base camp, a gateway. From here, small parties sailed further south to explore and gather timber, grapes and other goods, then returned to repair their ships.

For a long time, nobody could say exactly when the Norse were here. Then, in 2021, a research team published a striking result. They studied pieces of wood cut by metal tools at the site. A burst of radiation from a solar storm in the year 993 left a clear marker in tree rings around the world. By counting rings from that marker, the team showed the wood was cut in the year 1021. It is the earliest precise date for Europeans in the Americas.

The Norse did not stay long. The evidence points to a short occupation, perhaps only a few years, or a series of brief visits. The sagas speak of conflict with the people already living in this land, whom the Norse called Skraelings. It is important to remember that this was never empty country. Indigenous peoples used this coast for thousands of years before the Norse arrived, and long after they left. The Norse chapter is short. The human story here is much longer.

In 1978, the site became one of the very first places named a UNESCO World Heritage Site, a list of places judged important to all humanity. Today it is a National Historic Site managed by Parks Canada. A boardwalk and gravel paths lead from the visitor centre across the meadow. Inside the centre, you can see objects from the excavations and learn how archaeologists read the ground. Take your time there. It makes the empty meadow much easier to understand.

A short walk from the original remains, you will find reconstructed sod buildings. They are built in the Norse style, based on what was found here. Step inside and notice how dark it becomes, and how quiet. The thick turf walls block the wind almost completely. You can smell smoke and earth. Costumed interpreters often work inside, spinning, cooking or telling stories. Ask them questions. They can show you how people slept on raised platforms along the walls, close to the warmth of the hearth.

Now look past the buildings toward the water. The bay is shallow and dotted with small islands. Norse ships could be pulled up onto the beach here. In spring and early summer, icebergs sometimes drift along this coast, carried south from the Arctic. Whales may pass offshore. The weather can change in minutes, from bright sun to cold fog. Standing here, it is easy to imagine how a sailor from Greenland might have felt — far from home, at the edge of the known world.

Before you leave, walk back along the path slowly. Look again at those low ridges in the grass. They do not look like much. There are no towers, no carved stones, no grand gates. And yet this modest meadow holds the first meeting point of two worlds that had been apart for tens of thousands of years. Feel the wind on your face. Hear the waves below. The same sea that carried those ships is still moving against the shore, right in front of you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress in warm, windproof layers even in summer.
Visit the exhibits before walking out to the remains.
Ask the costumed interpreters to demonstrate Norse crafts.
Watch the coast for icebergs in spring and early summer.
Bring rain gear because fog and showers arrive quickly.
