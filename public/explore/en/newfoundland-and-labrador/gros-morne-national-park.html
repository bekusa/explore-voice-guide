The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your boots. Here the soil is rarely soft. It is gravel, broken rock and peat, with a cool wind coming in off the Gulf of Saint Lawrence. The air smells of salt and spruce, and a little of wet stone. Look around and you will see that this place does not look finished. The mountains are bare at the top. The water is dark and cold. The land seems recently scraped clean, and in a way, it was.

This is Gros Morne National Park, on the western coast of the island of Newfoundland. It was set aside as a national park in the 1970s. In 1987, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List. That honour was not mainly for its beauty, although there is plenty of that. It was for its rocks. Geologists come here to study the deep history of the planet. They come because the evidence here is unusually clear, and unusually easy to reach on foot.

First, the name. Gros Morne is French. It is usually translated as something like a large, lonely mountain, or a great gloomy one. It comes from the early French presence on this coast, when fishing crews worked these waters every summer. The name belongs to the park's highest mountain, a broad, flat-topped peak of grey rock. It is one of the tallest summits on the whole island. On a clear day, its long, bald shoulder is easy to recognise from the roads below.

Now for the first surprise. Drive south across Bonne Bay, toward the village of Woody Point, and the colour of the land changes. The green forest stops. In its place rises a wide, flat-topped massif of rusty orange and tan rock. This is the Tablelands. It looks more like a desert, or another planet, than eastern Canada. And the reason is extraordinary. This rock did not form at the surface. It came from deep inside the Earth.

The Tablelands are made largely of rock from the Earth's mantle. That is the layer that lies beneath the crust, normally many kilometres down. Hundreds of millions of years ago, as an ancient ocean closed and continents collided, a slab of ocean floor and the mantle beneath it was pushed up and onto the edge of North America. Erosion did the rest. Today you can walk across this ancient material and pick it up in your hand. Very few places on Earth make that so easy.

Why is it so bare? The mantle rock here is low in the nutrients that most plants need. It is also high in metals such as magnesium and nickel, which many plants cannot tolerate. So the forest simply stops at the edge. Only a few hardy species manage to grow. Look closely and you may find small, tough plants in the cracks. Some are carnivorous, like the pitcher plant, which is the provincial flower. They catch insects to get the nutrients the rock will not give them.

This place matters to science. In the twentieth century, the idea of plate tectonics changed how we understand the planet. The theory says that the Earth's surface is made of moving plates that collide, split and slide. Gros Morne became one of the places where geologists could see the evidence directly. Here, the inside of an old ocean is laid out in the open. A walk here is a walk across a page from the planet's own notebook.

North of the bay, the landscape changes again. The Long Range Mountains run along the park's interior. They are part of the same old chain as the Appalachians, much further south. Glaciers covered this region during the last Ice Age. As the ice moved, it carved deep valleys into the plateau. When it melted, the sea level and the land both shifted. Some of those valleys were cut off from the ocean and filled with fresh water.

The most famous of these is Western Brook Pond. Do not let the word pond fool you. It is a long, deep body of water, held between sheer cliffs that rise straight up from the surface. People often call it a landlocked fjord. Waterfalls drop from the plateau edge, thin ribbons of white against dark rock. The water itself is very pure and very low in nutrients. Reaching the boat dock takes a walk of roughly three kilometres across boardwalks and bog.

That bog is worth a slow look. The boardwalk crosses wet, open ground covered in mosses, low shrubs and small orchids in season. Ponds reflect the sky. In the distance, the mountain wall rises with the gorge cut straight into it. This is also good country for wildlife. Moose are common here. Here is another surprise: moose are not native to Newfoundland. They were brought to the island by people around the turn of the twentieth century. Now they thrive, and drivers watch for them, especially at dusk.

The coast tells yet another story. At Green Point, north of Rocky Harbour, thin layers of rock stand tilted on the shore. Geologists chose this spot as an international reference point. It marks the boundary between two ancient periods of Earth's history, the Cambrian and the Ordovician. Fossils in these layers help scientists around the world agree on timing. To the casual visitor, it looks like a striped cliff by the sea. To a geologist, it is a kind of global clock.

And people have lived here too. Indigenous peoples used this coast for thousands of years, long before Europeans arrived. Later, small fishing communities grew along the bays. Villages like Rocky Harbour, Norris Point and Woody Point still sit inside or beside the park. Their wooden houses are painted in bright reds, yellows and blues. Fishing stages and boats line the harbours. At places like Broom Point, you can learn how families once lived from cod and the sea.

So spend some time where you are now. Notice the light. On this coast it changes fast. Fog can roll in over the water and lift again within the hour. Evening sun turns the Tablelands a deeper orange. The sea at the shore is cold, even in summer. Listen for the wind on rock, the water on gravel, perhaps a raven somewhere above. Few landscapes show their age so plainly.

As you walk back toward the road or the trailhead, look down once more. The stones by your feet may have travelled a very long way, from the bottom of an ocean, or from deep inside the Earth. They have been lifted, folded and polished by ice. And here they are, under an ordinary sky, beside an ordinary path. Take your time leaving. This land has been in no hurry for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Drive slowly at dusk, when moose often cross roads.
Book the Western Brook Pond boat tour ahead in summer.
Allow time for the walk to the boat dock.
Pack waterproof layers; coastal weather changes quickly.
Visit the Tablelands late in the day for warmer light.
