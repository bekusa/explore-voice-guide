The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Trams hum along the streets behind you, and somewhere below, beyond the old city wall line, the river Aare runs green and fast. In front of you stands a pale sandstone facade with tall windows and a calm, symmetrical front. This is the Kunstmuseum Bern, the Museum of Fine Arts. It sits on Hodlerstrasse, a short walk from the main railway station, at the northern edge of Bern's old town.

The building in front of you dates from the late nineteenth century. It opened in 1879, and it is often described as the oldest art museum in Switzerland with a permanent collection. Look at the stone. Bern is built almost entirely from this greenish-grey local sandstone, and the museum belongs to the same family as the arcades and fountains of the old town. Nothing here shouts. The architecture suits the city — solid, orderly, and quietly proud.

The street name is a clue to what waits inside. Hodlerstrasse is named after Ferdinand Hodler, one of the most important Swiss painters around 1900. He grew up in Bern, and the museum holds a strong group of his works. Hodler painted mountains, lakes, and human figures in repeating, rhythmic patterns. He believed that harmony came from parallel forms — a row of trees, a line of peaks, figures standing side by side. Once you know this, you start to see it everywhere in his canvases.

Now for a surprise many visitors miss. This museum is not only about Swiss art. Its oldest treasures are Italian panel paintings from the late Middle Ages, with backgrounds of real gold leaf. Gold leaf is metal hammered thinner than paper, and it catches light in a way paint cannot. Stand to one side of these panels and move slowly. The gold shifts from dull to bright, just as it did by candlelight centuries ago.

From there, the collection moves forward through the centuries. You will find Swiss painters of everyday life, such as Albert Anker, whose village scenes show children, grandparents, and simple kitchens with great tenderness. Anker's pictures are loved across Switzerland. They show a country before industry changed it. Look at the faces. Anker painted real neighbours, not idealised models, and that honesty still feels fresh today.

The twentieth century is where the museum becomes truly international. A private collection assembled by the Bern couple Hermann and Margrit Rupf is shown here through a foundation. The Rupfs collected Cubist art early, when it was still new and strange. Cubism is the style in which painters such as Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque broke objects into angled planes, as if viewed from several sides at once. Seeing these works in a quiet Swiss museum is one of the real pleasures of Bern.

And then there is Paul Klee. Klee was born near Bern and spent much of his life connected to the city. For many years, this museum was the main home of his work in Bern. Since 2005, a separate institution, the Zentrum Paul Klee, has held the largest Klee collection, in a wave-shaped building on the eastern edge of the city. Today the two museums work together under one shared umbrella organisation. So if Klee's small, playful, music-like pictures catch your eye here, you have somewhere else to go next.

Another name worth knowing is Meret Oppenheim. She was a Surrealist artist with strong ties to Bern. Surrealism was a movement that used dreams, odd combinations, and the unconscious as sources for art. Oppenheim is famous for unexpected objects that make people laugh and feel uneasy at the same time. She left a large part of her work to this museum, so Bern is one of the best places anywhere to understand her playful and serious imagination.

Now a chapter that brought the museum into world headlines. In 2014, the German collector Cornelius Gurlitt died and left his collection to the Kunstmuseum Bern. His father had been an art dealer during the Nazi era. Some works were suspected to have been taken from their owners, many of them Jewish families, under persecution. The museum accepted the bequest, but committed to careful provenance research — that means tracing each work's ownership history — and to returning works where that was the right outcome.

This decision changed how the museum talks about itself. Exhibitions connected to the Gurlitt collection have asked difficult questions openly. Who owned this picture? How did it move from hand to hand? What does a museum owe to the past? For a visitor, it is a reminder that every frame on a wall has a story beyond the painting. Sometimes that story is quiet. Sometimes it is painful.

As you walk through, notice how the building itself changes. The older sandstone rooms have high ceilings and a formal sequence of galleries. A later extension, added in the twentieth century, has a very different, more modern character. The contrast is part of the experience. Plans to renew and replace parts of the newer wing have been discussed for some years, so details of the building may change over time.

A practical word. The collection is far larger than what is on display at any one moment, and temporary exhibitions rotate often. So the rooms you see today may not match a description you read online. That is not a disappointment — it is the nature of a living museum. Pick up a floor plan at the entrance, choose two or three rooms that interest you, and give them real time instead of rushing through everything.

When you step back outside, pause on the pavement. The sounds of the city return — the trams, the footsteps, the bells that ring across the old town every hour. Turn and look once more at the sandstone front. Behind those windows, gold-ground saints, Swiss villagers, Cubist guitars, and Klee's small dream worlds sit side by side. Then walk a few minutes toward the river, and see how much of that same stone and light you now notice in the city around you.
