The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the cobbles of the square and look up. The stone above you is a soft, greenish grey, the colour of a river under cloud. Up close it looks almost alive, with patches of fresh pale blocks set among older, darker ones. Somewhere above, pigeons shift on narrow ledges. The air smells faintly of damp sandstone. This is Bern Minster, the largest church in Switzerland, rising over the old town.

The story of this building begins in 1421. That year Bern was a confident, expanding city-state. It wanted a church to match its power. The work was given to Matthäus Ensinger, a master builder from a family of German cathedral masons. Ensinger brought the late Gothic style, the final phase of the great medieval building tradition, with its pointed arches, tall windows and lace-like stone tracery. The new church was built around an older one, so services could continue while the walls rose.

That detail matters. The Minster was never built in one heroic burst. It grew slowly, generation after generation, over more than a century of medieval work. Masons came and went. Styles shifted a little. Money ran short, then returned. If you walk around the outside, you can see this patient rhythm in the stone itself. Some sections feel crisp and ornate. Others are plainer and heavier, the work of different hands and different decades.

Now turn to the main west portal, the great doorway facing the square. This is the part that stops most visitors. Carved into the arch is the Last Judgement, the Christian vision of the end of time. Christ sits in judgement at the centre. On one side, the saved move toward heaven. On the other, the damned are dragged toward hell. The sculptor most closely linked with this portal is Erhart Küng, who worked here in the late fifteenth century.

Take your time with it. There are more than two hundred figures in this doorway, and many are small enough to miss. Look at the faces of the damned. Some are kings, some are clergy, some are ordinary townspeople — the carvers did not spare the powerful. Look too at the wise and foolish virgins on the sides of the doorway, a parable about being ready. The wise ones hold their lamps upright. The foolish ones have let theirs go dark.

Here is the first surprise. Most of the figures you see today are careful copies. The weather in Bern is hard on sandstone, and the originals were slowly wearing away. To protect them, the original sculptures were moved indoors to the Bern Historical Museum. What stands here now is a faithful replacement, painted in colours based on traces found on the old stone. Medieval churches were often brightly coloured, not the pale grey we imagine today.

The Minster also carries a sharp turn in its history. In 1528, Bern adopted the Protestant Reformation. The city broke with the Catholic Church, and the change was fierce. Statues, altars and religious images were removed from the church. Many were smashed. The building that had been planned as a Catholic church became a Reformed one, plain and focused on the spoken word. The Last Judgement portal, interestingly, was left in place.

And this leads to the second surprise. Behind the Minster lies a raised terrace called the Münsterplattform. To build it up, workers needed huge amounts of fill. Some of the broken sculpture from the Reformation went into that fill. In 1986, during work on the terrace, a large collection of these medieval stone fragments was found buried there. For centuries, people had been walking and sitting above hidden saints and angels.

Step inside if the doors are open. The interior is calmer than the portal. The walls are mostly bare, a result of the Reformation. Your eye goes upward instead, to the ribbed vaults of the ceiling. In the choir, at the eastern end, look for the painted keystones and coats of arms in the vault. Look too for the stained glass in the choir windows, some of which survived from the medieval church. And notice the carved wooden choir stalls, rich with small figures and faces.

One thing visitors often do not realise is that the famous tower is much younger than it looks. For centuries, the Minster stood with an unfinished tower. The tall, pointed spire you see today was only completed in 1893. Builders in the nineteenth century finished what the medieval masons had started. At about one hundred metres, it is the tallest church tower in Switzerland. It gives Bern its unmistakable outline from the river and the hills.

If you have the energy, the tower can be climbed. The narrow stone staircase winds up for several hundred steps, and the walls feel cool against your hand. At the viewing levels, the whole old town opens below you. You see the red-brown roofs, the arcaded streets, and the green loop of the Aare River around the city. On a clear day, the snowy peaks of the Bernese Alps line the southern horizon.

Before you leave, walk around to the Münsterplattform. It is a quiet, shaded terrace with trees, benches and a low wall high above the river. Locals come here to read, eat lunch and play chess. There is also a well-loved story about this wall. Legend has it that, in the seventeenth century, a young student fell from the edge on horseback. The horse died, but the student, so the tale goes, survived and later became a pastor.

The work on this church has never really stopped. A permanent workshop of stonemasons still cares for the building, replacing worn blocks one by one. The fresh, pale stones you may have noticed outside are their work. This is why the Minster looks slightly patched. It is not a frozen relic. It is a living building that people keep repairing, much as they have for six hundred years. The old town around it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

So, as you stand here, look once more at the doorway, at the faces of the saved and the damned. Listen for the bells, or for the soft sound of feet on the cobbles. Then look up at that green-grey tower against the sky. As you walk away down the arcaded streets, glance back now and then. You will find the spire following you, appearing between the rooftops, again and again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the portal in the morning for softer, even light.
Wear good shoes for the steep, narrow tower staircase.
Choose a clear day to see the Alps from the tower.
Rest on the Münsterplattform terrace after your visit.
Check service times before entering, as worship still happens here.
