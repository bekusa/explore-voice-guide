The guide

The story

Stand on the cobbles of Kramgasse and listen. Under the stone arcades, footsteps echo softly, and trams hum past on their rails. Then, a few minutes before the hour, people stop walking. Heads tilt upward. Phones rise. Everyone is waiting for the same small performance, high on the face of the tower in front of you. This is the Zytglogge, and for centuries it has told Bern what time it is.

The name sounds strange to most visitors, so let us start there. Zytglogge is Bernese German, the local Swiss dialect, and it simply means "time bell." It is a plain, practical name for a building that began with a plain, practical job. Before it was a clock, before it was a postcard, it was a gate. It was part of the walls that kept the young city safe.

Bern was founded at the end of the twelfth century on a narrow loop of land. The River Aare curls around it on three sides. Only the western end was open, so that was where the city needed walls. In the early thirteenth century, this tower was built as a gate in those western defences. Anyone arriving from that direction passed beneath it. Back then it was shorter and much rougher than the tower you see today.

Cities grow, and Bern grew quickly. New walls were built further west, and this gate found itself inside the town instead of on its edge. A defensive tower with no enemy to face needs a new purpose. For a time, it served as a prison. Thick walls and a single narrow entrance made it well suited to that role. It is a darker chapter that most people walking past today never imagine.

Then came disaster. In 1405, a great fire swept through Bern. Many of the city's timber houses burned, and the tower was badly damaged inside. When the people of Bern rebuilt, they did more than repair it. They gave the tower a bell, and a clock. From that point, the old gate began its long second life as the city's timekeeper. It is one reason Bern's later houses were built in stone.

Now look up at the eastern face — the side facing Kramgasse. The large clock dial is only part of the story. Below it sits an astronomical clock, a richly painted dial full of symbols. Its mechanism was built in the early sixteenth century by a clockmaker named Kaspar Brunner. It shows far more than hours. It follows the position of the sun through the zodiac, the phase of the moon, and the day of the week. In the age before printed calendars were common, this was real public information.

Here is the moment everyone gathers for. A few minutes before each hour, the figures begin to move. A golden rooster crows and flaps its wings. A jester nods his head and rings small bells. A little procession of bears circles past — the bear, of course, is the symbol of Bern. A seated figure of Chronos, the ancient Greek personification of time, turns his hourglass. Finally, high above, a golden armoured figure swings his hammer and strikes the hour on the great bell.

Watch the crowd as much as the clock. The show is gentle, and quite short. Some first-time visitors expect something grander and laugh a little at how modest it is. But that is its charm. These figures have been performing for roughly five hundred years. They were made for a city of craftsmen and merchants, not for an audience of thousands. Seeing it, you share a small ritual with generations of people from Bern.

One surprise most visitors miss: the tower once worked like a measuring point for the whole region. Distances from Bern were measured from this spot. Along old roads, stones marked the hours of walking from the city, and the counting began here, at the Zytglogge. So this tower kept time, and it also helped define space. It sat at the centre of how people understood the journey to and from Bern.

Turn and walk a few steps down Kramgasse, and you reach a house where a young patent clerk once lived. Albert Einstein rented an apartment on this street in the early 1900s. During his years in Bern, he developed his ideas about relativity, which changed how science understands time. Locals like to tell a story that he looked at this clock on his way to work and began to question how time is measured. It is a lovely tale — but treat it as a legend, not a documented fact.

The tower's outer appearance has changed several times. In the eighteenth century, it was remodelled in a more ornate style, and its painted façades have been restored over the years. The stone is local sandstone, the same grey-green stone you see on houses all through the Old Town. Look closely at the walls around the dials and you will see colour, gold, and painted decoration. Notice how bright it looks against the softer stone of the arcades.

The whole Old Town of Bern is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and this tower sits near its heart. Around you, the streets are lined with arcades — covered walkways that run for several kilometres. Fountains with painted figures stand in the middle of the streets. It is one of the best-preserved medieval town layouts in Europe. From here, you can walk east along Kramgasse toward the river, or west toward the newer quarters and the train station.

If you want to see more, guided tours sometimes take visitors inside the tower to view the clockwork itself. Inside, iron wheels, weights, and gears still drive the figures you watched from the street. The local tourist office can tell you about current tours. Inside, you can hear the mechanism working, and see how much patience and skill it took to build a machine like this by hand.

Before you move on, look up once more. The figures are still now, waiting for the next hour. The tower has watched this street as a gate, a prison, and a clock. Around you, people hurry past under the arcades, many of them checking the time on their phones. Just above them, Kaspar Brunner's old clock keeps counting the hours, as it has for five centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive five minutes before the hour to catch the figures.
Stand on Kramgasse facing the eastern side for best views.
Ask the tourist office about guided tours of the clockwork.
Walk east down Kramgasse to see arcades and painted fountains.
