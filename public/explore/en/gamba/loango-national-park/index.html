The guide

The story

Listen first. Behind you, the forest hums with insects and the call of a bird you cannot see. In front of you, the Atlantic rolls onto a wide beach of pale sand. The air smells of salt, wet leaves and something faintly sweet, like ripe fruit left in the sun. Here the rainforest does not stop politely before the sea. It runs right down to the tide line. That meeting of jungle and ocean is the heart of Loango National Park.

You are on the coast of Gabon, in Central Africa, not far from the small town of Gamba. Gamba grew up around the oil industry, and for decades this stretch of coast was quiet, remote and hard to reach. That isolation helped protect it. Roads are few, lagoons cut the land into pieces, and most visitors still arrive by boat or small plane. The wildness you feel around you is partly a gift of geography.

Loango became a national park in 2002. That year, Gabon's government created a network of thirteen national parks at once, a bold decision for a country so dependent on oil and timber. The move protected about one tenth of the national territory. Loango was one of the jewels of that network, because it holds so many different habitats side by side. Beach, savanna, mangrove, lagoon, swamp and dense forest all sit within a short walk of each other.

The name comes from history further down the coast. Loango was an old African kingdom that traded along this shoreline for centuries, long before modern borders were drawn. Its centre lay to the south, in what is now the Republic of the Congo. The park borrowed the name, and with it a reminder that people have lived, fished and traded along this coast for a very long time. The empty beach in front of you was never truly empty.

Now here is the surprise that made this place famous. Around the turn of the millennium, the conservationist Michael Fay walked across Central Africa on a long expedition through the forest. The photographer Michael Nichols documented the journey for National Geographic. Among his images were hippos wading into the Atlantic surf and forest elephants walking on the beach. The pictures travelled around the world, and Loango gained a nickname that still sticks — the land of surfing hippos.

Those hippos are real, and so are the elephants. Forest elephants are smaller than their savanna cousins, with straighter tusks and rounder ears. They move between the forest and the open grassland, and sometimes they come out onto the sand. Seeing a grey shape step out of the trees onto a white beach is strange, almost dreamlike. Keep your voice low if it happens. These animals are wild, and the guides here take their distance seriously.

Turn your attention inland for a moment. Behind the beach, the land opens into patches of grassy savanna, dotted with clumps of trees. This mosaic is unusual in a rainforest country. Forest buffalo graze in the open, often with birds riding on their backs. Red river hogs, with their bright orange coats and white tufts of hair, trot through in noisy family groups. In the early morning, mist sits low over the grass, and every animal seems to appear out of nowhere.

Water shapes almost everything here. To the north lies the broad Iguéla Lagoon, and to the south, toward Gamba, the Ndogo Lagoon spreads out among islands and mangroves. Rivers wind through the forest and drain into these lagoons. Mangrove roots rise from the mud like tangled cages. Boat trips are often the easiest way to explore, and from the water you may see crocodiles resting on the banks, or kingfishers flashing blue above the surface.

Deep in the forest live the great apes. Western lowland gorillas and chimpanzees both make their home in Loango. Scientists have studied them here for years, watching how they use the forest and how the two species share the same space. Researchers have also worked slowly to get one gorilla group used to human presence, so that small numbers of visitors can observe them. It is quiet, patient work, and it depends on keeping disturbance to a minimum.

The ocean has its own seasons of drama. During the southern winter months, humpback whales travel up this coast to breed and give birth in warmer water. From a boat, you may see a spout, a curved back, or even a whale lifting its whole body from the sea. Later in the year, on dark nights, leatherback turtles haul themselves onto these beaches to lay eggs. The leatherback is the largest living turtle, and watching one dig its nest is unforgettable.

It helps to understand how fragile all of this is. Elephants in Central Africa have faced heavy poaching pressure. Turtles lose nests to erosion, predators and human activity. Even the beach itself moves, reshaped by storms and tides each year. Park rangers, local communities and research teams work together to monitor wildlife and protect the coast. Tourism here is deliberately small in scale. The aim is for visitors to support the park, not to crowd it.

Notice, too, the small things around your feet. Tracks in the sand tell stories of the night before. A wide, round print may belong to an elephant. A deep groove dragged up from the water may mark a turtle's path. Driftwood logs, bleached silver by salt and sun, lie scattered along the shore, many of them washed down rivers from the forest. Crabs vanish into holes the moment your shadow falls across them.

If you stay into the evening, the light changes quickly near the equator. The sun drops into the sea, the sky turns orange, then deep blue, and the forest grows louder. Frogs begin to call from the swamps. Bats leave their roosts. Somewhere in the trees, a chimpanzee may give a long hooting cry that carries far across the savanna. Night here does not bring silence. It brings a different crowd of voices.

So take one more slow look around you. The line where the trees meet the sand. The waves breaking white against the shore. The tracks you may have walked past without noticing. Very few places on Earth still let a forest touch the ocean like this, with large animals free to cross between them. As you walk away, keep listening. The sounds you hear now are the same ones this coast has carried for thousands of years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange visits through an authorised lodge or licensed operator.
Go out at dawn for the best wildlife sightings.
Bring binoculars for savanna and boat-based wildlife watching.
Pack strong insect repellent and light long-sleeved clothing.
Keep a wide distance and quiet voice around elephants.
