The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind comes up off the sea and moves across this cliff, warm and dry, carrying the smell of wild thyme and dust. Below you, the water of Episkopi Bay runs from deep blue to pale green where it meets the shore. Under your feet the ground is pale limestone, worn smooth in places by many centuries of sandals and boots. This is Kourion, and for a very long time people chose to live right here, on the edge of the drop.

Kourion was once one of the city-kingdoms of ancient Cyprus. The island was never one single state in those early centuries. It was a patchwork of small kingdoms, each with its own ruler, its own temples, and its own harbour or farmland. Kourion held this high ground above the bay, with fertile land behind it and a clear view of any ship that approached. Today the site lies about a short drive west of Limassol, inside the British Sovereign Base Area, which is one reason the land around it stayed so open.

The people of Kourion told a story about where they came from. According to their own tradition, the city was founded by settlers from Argos, in the Peloponnese in mainland Greece. The Greek historian Herodotus repeats this claim. Archaeologists treat it with some caution, because founding legends were often a way for a city to claim a proud ancestry. But the story tells us something real... the people here thought of themselves as connected to the wider Greek world across the sea.

Herodotus also records a darker moment. In the early fifth century BCE, the Cypriot kingdoms rose against the Persian Empire, which ruled the island at the time. During a decisive battle, the ruler of Kourion, a man Herodotus names Stasanor, changed sides and took his forces over to the Persians. The revolt failed. So this quiet clifftop carries a story of betrayal at the centre of the island's struggle for freedom.

Now turn toward the theatre. It is the first thing most visitors see, and it is easy to understand why. The curved rows of stone seats rise in a half circle, and the stage faces straight out to the sea. A smaller theatre stood here in the Hellenistic period, the age after Alexander the Great. In Roman times it was enlarged. Later it was even adapted for more violent shows, with animals and fighters, before it fell out of use.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. Much of the seating you see is a modern restoration. The work was done in the twentieth century so the theatre could be used again. And it is used. In the warmer months, plays, concerts and dance performances still take place here in the evening. Ancient Greek drama is sometimes staged on the same spot where audiences watched it two thousand years ago, with the sun going down behind the actors.

Walk a little further and you reach the House of Eustolios. It began as a private villa, but by the early Christian period it seems to have become a public building with baths. Its floors are covered in mosaics, small cubes of coloured stone set into patterns. Look for birds, fish, and geometric borders. These were made at a time when the old Roman gods were fading and Christianity was spreading across the empire.

And here is one of the quiet surprises of Kourion. In one of the mosaics, there is a Greek inscription. It says, in effect, that this house is no longer protected by great stones and iron, but by the signs of the much-venerated Christ. It is a small line of text on a floor. Yet it marks a real change in how people thought about safety, faith, and what kept a home standing.

That question of safety was not abstract here. In the fourth century, a powerful earthquake struck the eastern Mediterranean. It caused enormous destruction in Kourion. In the area now called the Earthquake House, excavators in the 1980s found the remains of people who died when the buildings came down. Among them were a man, a woman and a child, close together, as if the adults had tried to shield the young one. It is a very human moment, held in the ground.

The city did not disappear after the earthquake. It was rebuilt, and Christianity became central to its life. A large early Christian basilica was raised here, probably in the fifth century. Today you can walk among its foundations and see standing column shafts and fragments of decorated floors. Nearby are the remains of a baptistery, where new believers were baptised. The scale tells you that Kourion was still an important place, now the seat of a bishop.

Other houses carry the names of their best mosaics. One is called the House of Achilles, after a scene from the story of the Greek hero. Another is the House of the Gladiators, where fighters are shown in the middle of combat, with their names written beside them. Many of these floors now sit under protective roofs, which keep off the sun and rain. Step into the shade and let your eyes adjust... the colours become clearer.

Beyond the main site, along the road to the west, lie two more pieces of the ancient city. One is a stadium, where athletic contests were held. The other is the Sanctuary of Apollo Hylates, which means Apollo of the woodland. It was an important religious centre long before and during Roman times. If you have the time and the energy, these places add depth to the story. They remind you that Kourion was spread across this whole landscape, not only the clifftop.

As you walk, notice how modern life still meets this ancient place. On some days paragliders launch from the nearby cliffs and drift out over the bay. Down below, cars park along the long beach, and people swim where ancient ships once came in. The fields and low scrub around you change colour with the seasons, green in winter and spring, gold and grey by late summer.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the theatre once more and look out to sea. The view in front of you is almost the same view the people of Kourion had. The same wind, the same light on the water, the same long line of the coast. Kings changed sides, gods gave way to new faith, the ground shook and the city fell and rose again. And the sea, as you see it now, simply kept moving toward the shore.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring water and a hat; shade on the site is scarce.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stones and dusty paths.
Come in late afternoon for softer light and cooler air.
Check the summer programme for evening theatre performances.
Drive west afterwards to see the Sanctuary of Apollo Hylates.
