The guide

The story

Listen first. Behind you, traffic hums along the coast road. In front of you, the sea moves against the shore. Between the two, there is a strange quiet. Pale limestone blocks sit in the dry grass, warm under the sun. Lizards slip between broken column drums. The smell of wild thyme and salt drifts up the hillside. This is Amathus, one of the oldest cities on the island of Cyprus, at the eastern edge of modern Limassol.

For much of antiquity, Cyprus was not one kingdom but several. Each was a city-kingdom with its own king, its own coins, and its own alliances. Amathus was one of them. It controlled this stretch of the southern coast for centuries. Today the hotels of Limassol rise only a short walk away. Yet here, the ground still holds the outline of an ancient royal city: its marketplace, its streets, and the hill where its temple once stood.

What makes Amathus unusual is the people who built it. Many Cypriot cities had strong links to Greek settlers. Amathus was different. Ancient writers saw its people as native Cypriots, a population older than the Greek arrivals. Here, archaeologists found inscriptions in a language now called Eteocypriot, which means true Cypriot. It is written in the old Cypriot syllabary, a script where each sign stands for a syllable. Scholars can read the sounds. But the meaning of the language is still largely unknown. Think about that for a moment... the people of this city wrote words that nobody today can fully understand.

Amathus was not isolated, though. It sat on sea routes between the Aegean, Egypt, and the coast of the Levant, the lands of modern Lebanon, Syria, and Israel. Phoenician traders and influences reached this harbour. Goods, gods, and ideas mixed here. Excavations in the tombs around the city have produced pottery, jewellery, and sculpture that show contacts in many directions. Amathus was a crossroads, even though its own language stayed apart.

Look up toward the hill. That was the acropolis, the high city. On its summit stood a sanctuary of Aphrodite, the goddess most closely tied to Cyprus. At Amathus she was worshipped alongside older local traditions, and ancient sources link the city with the cult of Adonis, the young lover of Aphrodite in Greek myth. The climb is steep in places, but the view repays it. From the top, the whole bay opens out, and you can see why a city chose this spot.

Now for one of the surprises of Amathus. On that hilltop stood two enormous stone vases, carved from single blocks of limestone. They were so large that a person could almost sit inside. In the nineteenth century, a French expedition removed one of them. It was shipped to France, and today it stands in the Louvre Museum in Paris. The second vase stayed behind. Its broken fragments are still on the acropolis. If you climb up, look for its thick curved walls and the carved handles.

Amathus also has a place in Greek legend. According to an ancient tale, retold by the writer Plutarch, the hero Theseus left Ariadne on Cyprus after a storm. In this version of the story, she was pregnant and died here in childbirth. Locals, the legend says, honoured her in a sacred grove. It is a myth, not history. But it shows how the Greeks imagined this coast: a distant, sacred shore at the edge of their world.

The city's real history has its own dramatic moments. Around 499 BC, many Greek cities rose up against the Persian Empire. Most of Cyprus joined the revolt. Amathus refused and stayed loyal to Persia. Onesilos, a prince of the city of Salamis, laid siege to it. The Greek historian Herodotus tells what happened next. Onesilos was killed in battle. The people of Amathus hung his head above their city gate. In time, bees filled the skull and made a honeycomb inside it. An oracle then told the citizens to honour him every year. That is the kind of story Herodotus loved to record.

Come back down to the flat ground near the road. This is the lower city, and its heart was the agora, the public square and marketplace. Here people traded, argued, and met officials. Most of what you see belongs to later periods, when Cyprus was part of the Hellenistic world and then the Roman Empire. Look for the rows of restored columns. They once supported a covered walkway, a stoa, that gave shade to shoppers. Nearby are the remains of baths, a fountain, and water channels cut into the stone.

Walk slowly here and watch the ground. You will see worn paving stones, steps polished by countless feet, and drains that once carried rainwater away. These are small things. But they show daily life more clearly than any temple. Somebody swept this square. Somebody repaired that drain. The city was not only kings and gods. It was ordinary people moving through these spaces every day.

Then comes another surprise, this time under the water. Amathus had a harbour built with stone breakwaters, long walls that protected ships from waves. Today, the remains of that harbour lie in the shallow sea in front of the site. The coastline and sea level have changed. On a calm, clear day, divers and snorkellers can sometimes see the large blocks resting on the seabed. From the shore, look for darker shapes under the surface.

With Christianity, the city changed again. Amathus became the seat of a bishop. Saint Tychon, still honoured in the Limassol area, is remembered as one of its early bishops. Christian basilicas were built here, and you can find traces of them across the site. But from the seventh century, Arab raids struck the coasts of Cyprus. Over time, Amathus faded, and Limassol slowly grew in importance. Much later, in the nineteenth century, many accounts say stone from the ruins was carried away as building material. That helps explain why so much of the city is missing.

As you stand here, notice how close the modern world is. Cars pass on one side. Swimmers float in the sea on the other. Hotels shine in the distance. And yet this ground once held a royal city with its own kings, its own goddess, and its own mysterious language. Excavations continue to add pieces to the picture, and many finds are now displayed in museums in Limassol and Nicosia.

Before you leave, pause by one of the columns. Rest your hand on the stone. It is warm, rough, and marked by time. Listen again to the waves, the traffic, and the insects in the dry grass. People heard the same sea here nearly three thousand years ago. As you walk back toward the road, look once more at the hill above you... and imagine the great stone vase still standing at its summit.

What to look for

Dark shapes of sunken harbour blocks in the shallows.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven climb to the acropolis.
Bring water and a hat; shade on site is very limited.
Pair your visit with Limassol's archaeological museum for the finds.
Pack a snorkel to look for the submerged harbour stones.
