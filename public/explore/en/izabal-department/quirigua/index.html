The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere in the canopy, birds call over a low, steady hum of insects. The air is thick and warm, and it smells of wet earth and cut grass. Under your feet, the lawn is soft and springy. Then you look up, and a figure of red-brown stone rises out of the green, far taller than you. Its face is calm and its arms are folded. It has stood like this for more than twelve centuries.

This is Quiriguá, an ancient Maya city in the lower Motagua River valley of eastern Guatemala. It is not one of the giant pyramid cities. You will not climb a temple here. Instead, Quiriguá is a city of carved stone monuments. Its tall stelae, the upright stone slabs the Maya carved with rulers and dates, are some of the finest ever made. In 1981, UNESCO listed the site as a World Heritage Site.

The secret of these carvings lies in the stone itself. The local sandstone is fine-grained and fairly soft when it is first cut from the ground. Over time, it hardens in the open air. Maya sculptors used this to their advantage. They could carve deep, crisp detail, and the stone then held that detail for centuries. Look closely at the edges of the glyphs, the small picture-signs of Maya writing. Many still look sharp enough to cut a finger.

The Motagua valley mattered long before these monuments rose. The river was a natural route between the highlands and the Caribbean coast. The wider valley is also known as a major source of jade, a green stone the Maya valued above gold. A city on this river could watch, and tax, the goods moving along it. For much of its early life, Quiriguá appears to have lived in the shadow of a larger neighbour. That neighbour was Copán, a great Maya city across the mountains in what is now Honduras.

Now comes the story that makes Quiriguá famous. In the eighth century, its ruler was a man known today as K'ak' Tiliw Chan Yopaat. Some older books call him Cauac Sky. Inscriptions here and at Copán record that in 738 he captured the powerful king of Copán, Uaxaclajuun Ub'aah K'awiil, and had him put to death. The smaller city had defeated its master. Scholars still debate exactly how it happened... but the result is written in stone all around you.

What did a small kingdom do with its sudden freedom and wealth? It built. In the decades after this victory, Quiriguá raised its greatest monuments. The ruler had himself carved again and again, in his finest regalia, with long texts beside him. Each stela marked the end of a period in the Maya calendar. Each one also told visitors, very plainly, who was now in charge of this valley.

Walk to the largest of them, the monument known as Stela E. It is often described as the tallest stone monument the ancient Maya ever raised. A large part of the shaft is also buried below ground to keep it upright. Stand at its base and look up at the ruler's face. Notice how small you feel. That feeling was the point. This stone was made to impress every person who walked into the plaza.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Another stela on this plaza, Stela C, carries one of the clearest records of the Maya creation date. In our calendar, that date falls in 3114 BCE. To the Maya, this was the moment the present world began. So when you read about the famous Maya "Long Count" calendar, part of that understanding comes from this field in Guatemala. A quiet lawn holds one of the keys to Maya time.

Now turn to the low, rounded boulders near the southern end of the plaza. These are the zoomorphs, a name scholars use for large stones carved into animal-like and supernatural forms. They do not look like the tall stelae. They crouch close to the ground, and they are covered on every side with tangled faces, scrolls, and figures. The most elaborate, Zoomorph P, is often called the Great Turtle. Walk all the way around it. The carving does not stop.

Beyond the plaza lies the Acropolis, a group of low platforms and buildings around a closed courtyard. This was the heart of royal and ceremonial life. Its stone walls are modest compared with the monuments, but they show how the city was laid out. The open plaza was for public display. The Acropolis was for the ruler and the court. The river once ran closer to this area, and over the centuries floods buried parts of the site in silt.

You may notice the thatched roofs standing over many of the stelae. These shelters are modern. They protect the stone from heavy tropical rain and the strong sun of the lowlands. They also change the light. In the shade, the red-brown surface turns warm and soft, and fine details are easier to see. Look at how the shadows fall across the faces as the sun moves.

The world outside the park has its own story. For much of the twentieth century, the land around Quiriguá was used for large banana plantations. When you drive in, you pass long, even rows of banana plants. The park itself sits like an island of tall forest inside this farmland. Some of the great trees here are much older than the plantations, and their roots reach far across the ground.

The outside world began to learn about Quiriguá in the nineteenth century. The English artist Frederick Catherwood came here around 1840 and made careful drawings of the monuments. Later explorers photographed the stones and made plaster casts of them. Some of those casts went to museums abroad. Yet nothing replaces standing here, in the heat, beside the original stone, with the insects loud in your ears.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the middle of the plaza. Look from one stela to the next, and see how they line up across the grass. Listen to the birds in the trees at the edge of the clearing. Over twelve centuries ago, this same open space was full of people, smoke, and music. Now it is quiet. As you walk back toward the trees, turn once more. The stone faces are still watching the valley.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid the strongest midday tropical heat.
Bring insect repellent for the humid riverside forest.
Carry plenty of water and wear a wide-brimmed hat.
Walk all the way around each zoomorph to see every carving.
Visit after rain when the shaded sandstone looks deeper red.
