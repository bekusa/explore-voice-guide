The guide

The story

Start with the climb. The path rises steeply, and the bricks under your feet are worn smooth and warm in the sun. Around you, the air smells of dry grass and, in late summer, of ripening grapes from the vineyards below. Somewhere a bell might sound from the tower above. Then you reach the top, turn around, and the whole Alazani Valley opens out at your feet. Behind it, the wall of the Greater Caucasus rises blue and white. This is Gremi.

What you see today is a church, a bell tower, and a fortified palace tower, gathered on a single hill. It looks like a quiet monastery. But this hilltop was once the heart of a royal city. For well over a century, Gremi was the capital of the Kingdom of Kakheti. Kakheti is Georgia's eastern wine country, and for a long period it was an independent kingdom of its own.

To understand why a capital stood here, think about the land. In the fifteenth century, Georgia broke apart into several smaller kingdoms and princedoms. Kakheti became one of them. Its kings needed a seat that was easy to defend and close to wealth. This hill gave them both. The slope protected the citadel. The valley below fed it with grain and wine. And trade roads ran past it, linking the Caucasus with Persia and the lands of the east.

So picture the town that once spread around the base of this hill. There were merchants' streets, houses, a market, bathhouses, and a caravanserai — a walled inn where travelling traders rested their animals and stored their goods. Archaeologists have uncovered traces of this lower town. Very little of it rises above the ground now. Most of it lies under the fields and orchards you can see from the top. The royal city was much larger than the hill alone.

The building at the centre of the hill is the Church of the Archangels, dedicated to Saint Michael and Saint Gabriel. It was built by King Levan of Kakheti, and an inscription dates it to the 1560s. Levan ruled for a long time in the sixteenth century. His reign is often remembered as a high point for the kingdom. He chose to be buried here, inside the church he built.

Look closely at the walls. They are made almost entirely of brick, laid in neat courses with wide bands of mortar. Many famous Georgian churches are built of cut stone. Here, brick gives the building a softer, warmer colour, especially in the late afternoon. The plan is a classic cross shape, crowned by a tall drum and dome. Open arcades, rows of arches, wrap around parts of the building. This gives the church a lighter look than its thick walls might suggest.

Now step inside, if the doors are open. Your eyes need a moment to adjust. Then the walls begin to fill with colour. The interior is covered in frescoes painted in the late sixteenth century. You will see saints in long robes, scenes from the Gospels, and, according to Georgian tradition, portraits of the royal family who paid for the work. Much of the paint is faded and cracked. Some faces are scratched. Yet the deep reds, ochres, and blues still hold the room together.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Those scratches and gaps are not only the work of time. The church survived a violent attack, and the damage tells part of that story. In the early seventeenth century, the Persian ruler Shah Abbas the First invaded Kakheti. His armies devastated the region. Gremi was sacked and burned in the 1610s. The royal city never recovered from that blow.

After the destruction, the kings of Kakheti moved their court elsewhere, and in time Telavi became the main royal seat. Gremi's lower town slowly vanished. The houses fell, the market emptied, and farmers worked the land again. But the church on the hill survived. It continued as a place of worship, and it is an active church today. That is why you may see candles burning and people praying quietly inside. Please move gently and keep your voice low.

Next to the church stands the bell tower, and beside it the old palace tower. The palace tower is a tall, solid structure with several floors. Inside, there is a small museum. It displays objects found during excavations of the royal town. These include pottery, weapons, coins, and everyday tools. They help you imagine life in the capital before the invasion. Ask on site whether the museum is open during your visit.

Legend has it that a secret tunnel once ran from the citadel down to the river below. It allowed the royal family to escape or bring in water during a siege. Locals tell this story often, and you may hear guides repeat it. Treat it as folklore. It is a good reminder, though, that this was a fortress first. Notice the defensive wall that circles the hilltop, and the narrow way in.

When you walk back outside, find a spot along the wall and look across the valley. On a clear day the mountains seem close enough to touch. Rows of vines run in straight lines across the plain. This is the same view the kings of Kakheti had from their palace windows. The roads have changed, and the city is gone. But the shape of the land — the river, the vineyards, the mountains — remains almost the same.

Gremi is part of a wider story too. Kakheti is one of the oldest wine regions in the world. Georgians have made wine in large clay jars buried in the ground for thousands of years. As you continue through this valley, you will pass villages and wineries where that tradition is still alive. In that sense, Gremi is not only a ruin. It is one stop on a road that people still live along today.

Before you leave, take one last look at the church. Notice how the light moves across the brick, turning it from pale gold to deep orange. Listen for the wind in the cypress trees and the swallows around the dome. As you walk down the path, imagine the streets that once filled these fields. A royal city stood here, then fell. And the church on the hill is still here.
