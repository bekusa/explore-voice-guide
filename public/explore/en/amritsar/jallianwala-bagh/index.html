The guide

The story

The lane that leads you in is narrow. Walk it slowly. Feel how the walls press close on either side, how the sounds of the Amritsar street fall away behind you. Then the space opens into a wide garden of lawns, trees and paved paths. It is quiet here, often quieter than you expect. Families stroll, schoolchildren sit in rows on the grass, and birds call from the branches. But that narrow lane is the first thing you need to understand about this place.

This is Jallianwala Bagh. The word bagh means garden. In 1919, though, it was not a garden in the way you see it now. It was an open, uneven patch of ground, enclosed on most sides by the backs of houses and high walls. There were only a few ways in and out, and the main one was the passage you have just walked through. Keep that picture in your mind as you look around.

On the 13th of April, 1919, a large crowd gathered here. It was Baisakhi, the spring harvest festival and one of the most important days in the Punjabi and Sikh calendar. Many people had come into the city to visit the Golden Temple, only a short walk from where you stand. Some came to a public meeting. Others had simply come to rest in the open ground. Men, women and children were all among them.

The mood in the city was tense. Earlier that year, the British colonial government had passed the Rowlatt Act. This law allowed people to be detained without trial, and it caused anger across India. In Amritsar, two popular local leaders, Satyapal and Saifuddin Kitchlew, had been arrested and sent away. Protests followed, and some turned violent. The authorities had banned public gatherings. Many of the people in the garden that afternoon likely did not know about the ban.

Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer arrived with a column of troops. He placed them near the main entrance, blocking the way out. Then, without a warning to disperse, he ordered them to open fire on the crowd. The shooting continued for about ten minutes. The soldiers fired until their ammunition was nearly used up. The figure most often cited is around 1,650 rounds. People ran toward the walls and the few narrow exits. Many had nowhere to go.

The number of dead is still disputed. The official British inquiry, known as the Hunter Committee, accepted a figure of 379. Indian sources, including an inquiry by the Indian National Congress, estimated that the true number was far higher — around 1,000 or more. Many hundreds more were wounded. A curfew was in force that night, so many of the injured could not be reached or helped. Whatever the exact figure, the scale of the killing shocked people far beyond Punjab.

Now walk toward the walls that still stand around the garden. Look closely at the brickwork. In several places you will see small holes in the old bricks, framed by white painted borders. These are bullet marks, preserved and labelled so visitors can find them. They are small, and easy to miss if you walk quickly. Stand in front of one for a moment. Those marks are at the height of a person.

Nearby is the Martyrs' Well. During the firing, many people jumped into this well to escape the bullets. Many of them did not survive. Today the well is enclosed, and you can look down into it through a protective structure. A plaque nearby records the bodies recovered from it. It is often the place where visitors grow quietest. Notice how people lower their voices here, without anyone asking them to.

The massacre changed the course of Indian history. The poet Rabindranath Tagore, a Nobel laureate, gave up the knighthood the British had awarded him, in protest. Mahatma Gandhi, who had earlier hoped for cooperation with British rule, moved toward the non-cooperation movement soon after. For many Indians, faith in the fairness of colonial government ended in this garden. Dyer was censured by the inquiry and removed from his command, but he was never put on trial.

Here is a story many visitors do not know. A young man named Udham Singh, who was in Amritsar around the time of the massacre, carried its memory for more than twenty years. In 1940, in London, he shot and killed Michael O'Dwyer. O'Dwyer had been the Lieutenant Governor of Punjab in 1919 and had supported Dyer's actions. Udham Singh was hanged in Britain later that year. In India, he is remembered as a martyr, and you will find his name honoured here.

At the centre of the garden rises the main memorial. It is a tall structure of red stone, shaped like a flame. It was designed by the American architect Benjamin Polk and inaugurated in 1961. Its simple, upward form is meant to stand for the flame of freedom. Nearby, an eternal flame burns as well. Walk all the way around the memorial, and look at how it rises above the low trees and the surrounding rooftops.

The site was renovated and reopened in 2021. The changes added new galleries, lighting and sculptures. The narrow entrance lane was also redesigned, with figures and images on its walls. These changes started a public debate. Some people welcomed the new spaces for learning. Others, including some historians, felt that the redesign softened the harshness of the original place. As you walk, you may want to form your own view.

Inside the galleries, you can see photographs, documents and paintings that tell the story of 1919 and the struggle that followed. Take your time with them. Read the names. Look at the faces in the old photographs. Many of the people killed here were ordinary visitors from villages around Amritsar, who had come to the city for a festival day. The galleries help turn a large number back into individual lives.

Before you leave, find a bench or a patch of shade and simply sit for a few minutes. Listen to the voices of the families around you, the footsteps on the paths, and the leaves moving in the trees. Look again at the walls, and at the few ways out. Then, as you walk back through that narrow lane toward the Golden Temple and the noise of the city, notice how short the distance really is. Notice, too, how different the street feels now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the Golden Temple, a short walk away.
Come early morning for quieter paths and softer light.
Allow time for the galleries, not only the garden.
Keep voices low near the Martyrs' Well.
Carry water and wear a hat in summer heat.
