The guide

The story

The ground here crunches. Flint and gravel scatter across a flat, pale plain, and the wind carries a dry, mineral smell. Ahead of you sits a small, squat building of honey-coloured stone, topped with low rounded vaults. From the outside, it looks modest, almost like a farm shed left behind in the desert. But step through the doorway, let your eyes adjust to the dim light, and the walls fill with colour.

This is Qusayr 'Amra, a short drive west of the oasis town of Azraq in eastern Jordan. The name means, roughly, the little castle of 'Amra. It is not really a castle at all. It is a bathhouse and a reception hall, built in the early eighth century by the Umayyads. The Umayyads were the first great dynasty of the Islamic caliphate, and they ruled a vast empire from Damascus.

Why build a bathhouse out here, so far from any city? The answer lies in the landscape. This building stands beside a seasonal riverbed, Wadi al-Butm, named after the wild terebinth trees that still grow along it. In winter and spring, rain can turn this ground green. For Umayyad princes, the desert was a place to hunt, to ride, to meet with Bedouin tribes, and to relax far from the formality of the court.

Scholars still debate exactly who built it. Most link it to the Umayyad prince al-Walid, though historians argue over whether this was the caliph al-Walid the First, or his later namesake, al-Walid the Second, who was known for his love of poetry, wine, and the desert. Either way, it was built for someone powerful, someone who wanted comfort and spectacle in the middle of the steppe.

Now look around the main hall. It has three parallel barrel vaults, and almost every surface carries paintings. You will see hunters chasing animals, musicians, dancers, craftsmen at work, and bathing figures. Here is the first surprise. Many people assume early Islamic art avoided images of people entirely. Qusayr 'Amra shows that, in a private, princely space like this, the rules were very different. The frescoes are full of human bodies, faces, and everyday life.

One painting has become especially famous among historians. It shows a row of rulers standing together. Inscriptions in Greek and Arabic identify some of them, including a Byzantine emperor, the Persian Sasanian shah, the king of Abyssinia, and Roderic, the Visigothic king of Spain. Roderic was defeated by Umayyad armies in the early eighth century. The message seems clear: these kings of the world stand as witnesses to the power of the caliph.

Keep walking, and you enter the bath rooms. They follow a plan inherited from Roman and Byzantine baths. First comes a changing room, then a warm room, then a hot room. Look at the floors and lower walls. Hot air once flowed through hollow spaces beneath the floor, warming the rooms from below. In a desert where water is precious, this was luxury of the highest order.

Now look up, in the small domed room at the end. This is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it. The ceiling of the dome is painted with the night sky. Constellations and signs of the zodiac curve across the surface. It is considered one of the oldest surviving depictions of the heavens painted on a dome anywhere in the world. Imagine lying in the warm steam and looking up at the stars, painted over twelve centuries ago.

Outside, near the building, you can see the remains of a well and a water-lifting system. An animal, probably a donkey or a camel, walked in a circle to turn a wheel. The wheel lifted water from deep underground into a tank. From there, the water flowed to the baths. It is a simple machine, but without it, none of the comfort inside would have been possible.

For centuries after the Umayyads fell, the building stood quietly in the desert. Bedouin knew it, and travellers passed by. In the late nineteenth century, the Czech explorer and scholar Alois Musil visited and described its paintings to European audiences. At first, some scholars doubted his account. Paintings of this kind, in an early Islamic building, seemed hard to believe. Later visits and photographs proved him right.

The frescoes you see today are the result of long and careful conservation. Over the decades, soot, dirt, and old layers of varnish darkened the walls. In recent years, conservators from Jordan and from international teams worked to clean the surfaces, slowly and by hand. Their work brought back colours and details that had been hidden for generations. In 1985, Qusayr 'Amra was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List.

As you move through the rooms, pay attention to the light. There are only small openings in the walls, so the interior stays dim and cool, even on a hot afternoon. Your eyes need a moment to find the details. Take your time. A figure that looks faded at first may slowly come into focus: a hand holding a bow, the curve of a dancer's arm, the long neck of a gazelle.

The building is small, and you can walk through it in a few minutes. But the paintings reward patience. Stand in the centre of the main hall and turn slowly. Every wall tells a different part of the story. Some scenes show the hunt, some show celebration, some show quiet work. Together, they give us one of the most complete pictures we have of how the early Umayyad elite lived and how they wanted to be seen.

When you step back outside, the brightness will feel sharp after the shade. Look again at the plain stone walls and the low domes. From here, nothing suggests the colour inside. Listen to the wind moving over the open ground, and look along the line of the wadi. A prince once rode out across this same land to hunt, then came back to warm water, music, and a painted sky. The desert around you has hardly changed.
