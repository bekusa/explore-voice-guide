The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here the wind comes in steady off the hills, and somewhere below you a sheep calls across the valley. The grass under your feet is short and springy, grazed close by animals. When you reach the walls, put a hand on the stone. It is grey limestone, cool and rough, and it is the same rock the castle stands on. Carreg Cennen does not just sit on its cliff. It grows straight out of it.

The name gives you a clue. In Welsh, carreg means stone or rock, and the Cennen is the small river running through the valley far below. So this is simply the rock above the Cennen. On the southern side the ground ends in a sheer limestone drop. On a clear day you look out over the western edge of the Brecon Beacons, now often called by its Welsh name, Bannau Brycheiniog. Few castles in Britain have a stronger position than this one.

People used this crag long before any castle was built here. Finds from the area and from the cave beneath your feet suggest prehistoric visitors, and there are hints of later Roman-era activity too. The details are patchy and partly uncertain. But it makes sense. A high rock with a cliff on one side and wide views on all the others has always attracted people who wanted to see trouble coming.

The first castle here was very probably Welsh. This valley lay inside Deheubarth, the old kingdom of south-west Wales. Its princes, the family of the great Lord Rhys, held power across these hills in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. They likely had a stronghold on this rock. Nothing clearly survives from it, though. The walls you see now belong to a later, harder chapter in the story.

That chapter begins with Edward the First of England and his conquest of Wales in the late thirteenth century. After the Welsh princes fell, the castle passed into English hands. Most of the stonework you are looking at dates from around that time, built under the Marcher lord John Giffard and his successors. Marcher lords were the powerful nobles who ruled the borderlands for the English crown. They built to dominate the land, and also to be seen dominating it.

Now look at how you actually entered the castle. Visitors in the Middle Ages could not simply walk up to a gate. The approach climbs through a long, defended passage called a barbican, an outer gateway system. It turns and rises along stone ramps. At points along it there were pits crossed by drawbridges. Lift the bridge, and the path behind you became a deep gap. Every step of the way, defenders above had a clear shot at anyone below.

Inside, the inner ward is smaller than many people expect. Towers stand at the corners, some still tall, some reduced to stumps. The gatehouse was the strongest part, the place where the lord could hold out if the outer defences fell. Walk the line of the walls slowly. You can still trace rooms, doorways and window openings, and the grey stone shows centuries of weather and repair.

Here is the part most visitors miss at first. In the south-east corner, a vaulted passage leads down along the very edge of the cliff. It is narrow, dark and cool, and it runs to a natural cave deep inside the rock. Nobody knows for certain why the builders took such trouble to enclose it. Perhaps it gave water. Perhaps the garrison simply wanted to stop an enemy using it as a hidden way in. Either way, a castle with a cave in its foundations is a rare thing.

The cave has its own stories. According to one popular tale, a great sleeping warrior lies somewhere inside, waiting for the day Wales needs him. Some versions name Owain Lawgoch, a fourteenth-century Welsh soldier who claimed the princely line. Others reach further back to older heroes. This is folklore, not history. But stand in that darkness for a moment, and it is easy to understand why the story has lasted.

The castle saw real violence too. In the early fifteenth century, during the great Welsh rising led by Owain Glyndŵr, the castle came under siege. Glyndŵr's forces did serious damage, and the garrison inside had a difficult time. Repairs followed. For a while the fortress went on serving the crown, still watching over the valley it had been built to control.

Its end came during the Wars of the Roses, the long struggle between the houses of Lancaster and York for the English throne. Lancastrian supporters held the castle. After the Yorkists took it in the 1460s, they decided it should never be used against them again. A large team of workmen arrived with picks and crowbars and deliberately pulled much of it apart. Historians call this slighting. Much of the ruin you see today is the result of that work.

Perhaps that is why the castle feels so honest today. It was never turned into a grand country house or rebuilt by a Victorian owner. It is simply a medieval fortress, broken on purpose and then left to the weather. The castle is cared for by Cadw, the Welsh government's historic environment service. It stands on a working farm, and you reach it on foot, climbing up from the farm buildings past fields of grazing animals.

Before you start back down, stop at the edge of the walls and look out. The valley falls away in shades of green, crossed by hedges and small stone farms. Clouds move fast over these hills, so the light changes from minute to minute. As you walk down the slope, turn around once or twice. The castle will look different every time, grey stone against sky, growing out of its rock just as it has for seven centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a torch for the dark passage into the cave.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep climb from the farm.
Pack a waterproof layer; weather changes fast on the hilltop.
Walk the barbican slowly to understand the defensive route.
Visit on a clear day for the widest valley views.
