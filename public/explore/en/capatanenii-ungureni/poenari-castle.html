The guide

The story

Listen first. Your breathing is loud after the climb, and the wind moves through the beech trees below. Under your boots the stone is uneven and worn smooth in places. Far beneath you, the Argeș River runs through a narrow green valley. You have reached the ruins of Poenari Castle, high on a cliff in the southern Carpathian Mountains of Romania. Few castles in Europe sit this close to the edge of a drop.

Getting here is part of the story. A long stairway of close to 1,500 concrete steps climbs the forested slope from the road. Most visitors stop more than once to rest. Some count the steps out loud, and others lose count halfway. That effort matters. It gives you a small, honest sense of why this ridge was chosen for a fortress. Any army that wanted this place had to climb too.

The castle is older than its most famous owner. Historians generally place its first construction around the thirteenth century. That was the early period of Wallachia, the medieval principality that later became southern Romania. The rulers of that time are linked to the Basarab family, the founding dynasty of the principality. Not far down the valley lies Curtea de Argeș, one of Wallachia's earliest seats of power. A watchtower here could guard the mountain route toward Transylvania, on the other side of the Carpathians.

Now to the name everyone comes here for. In the fifteenth century, the ruler Vlad the Third took an interest in this abandoned fortress. History knows him as Vlad the Impaler. His family name, Dracula, came from his father, who belonged to the Order of the Dragon, a society of Christian knights. Vlad repaired and strengthened Poenari and used it as one of his strongholds. Today it is the place most closely tied to the real man behind the name.

Here is the first surprise. Bran Castle, in Transylvania, is the one most tourists call Dracula's Castle. It is beautiful and easy to reach. But its connection to Vlad is weak. Poenari, by contrast, is a fortress Vlad actually rebuilt and held. The fiction of Bram Stoker went one way. The history went another. If you came to find the real Vlad, this rough ruin on the cliff is far closer to him than any polished tower.

How did Vlad rebuild it? An old story has a dark answer. According to a tale passed down in Romanian chronicles and tradition, Vlad held an Easter feast for the nobles, or boyars, whom he distrusted. He then arrested many of them and forced them to march up here and rebuild the walls with their own hands. Some say they worked until their clothes fell apart. Historians treat parts of this as legend. Still, it shows how people remembered him.

Take a moment and look at the walls themselves. They are built from brick and rough local stone, set in thick layers. In places you can see where newer repair work meets the older masonry. The fortress is small and narrow, stretched along the ridge, because the cliff leaves no room for anything wider. Towers once stood at the corners. What remains feels more like a stone ship than a palace, with a long deck and sheer sides.

The second legend is quieter and sadder. Locals say that during an Ottoman attack, Vlad's wife saw that the castle would fall. Rather than be captured, she threw herself from the walls into the river below. There is no firm historical record of this. Another tale says Vlad escaped through a secret path, helped by villagers, with his horse's shoes reversed to confuse pursuers. These are stories, not documents. But stand at the edge and you understand why they were told here.

After Vlad's time, the castle lost its importance. The routes changed, and the rulers of Wallachia had other fortresses. Over the centuries Poenari slowly fell into ruin. Then nature took a turn. In the late nineteenth century, a landslide carried part of the cliff away, and sections of the castle went down with it. That is why some walls end so suddenly. Look carefully and you can see where the building simply stops at open air.

Later restoration work in the twentieth century stabilised what remained and made the site safer to visit. Wooden walkways, railings, and the long stairway all belong to this modern era. Some visitors find a few displays inside the walls that tell the story of Vlad and his punishments. These are reconstructions for visitors, not original objects. They are part of the experience, but the real treasure is the stone itself, and the view it was built to control.

So turn around and look at that view. To the north, the valley climbs toward the high Făgăraș Mountains. A famous mountain road, the Transfăgărășan, winds up through those peaks. It was built in the 1970s and crosses the range to Transylvania. Upstream from here is the Vidraru Dam and its long reservoir. Below you, the road and river run side by side through the gorge, small as threads.

One more thing surprises many visitors. This forest is home to brown bears. Romania has one of the largest bear populations in Europe, and bears do come near the path here. Because of this, local authorities sometimes close the stairway or limit visits. Signs at the bottom usually explain the current rules. On the way down, stay on the path, keep food sealed, and keep your voice at a normal level. You share this ridge with them.

Before you leave, rest a hand on the cool brick of the wall. Feel the wind come up from the valley. People built here, fought here, suffered here, and told stories here for centuries. Now there is mostly birdsong and the sound of other climbers catching their breath. As you walk back down those many steps, watch the castle disappear behind the trees. Then look up once more from the road. It will seem impossibly high.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes and bring water for the long stair climb.
Check signs at the base for bear-related closures before climbing.
Come early in the morning to avoid crowds on the stairs.
Combine your visit with a drive up the Transfăgărășan road.
Keep food sealed and stay on the marked path.
