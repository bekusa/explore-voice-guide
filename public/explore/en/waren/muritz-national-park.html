The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere out on the water, a reed bed is moving in the wind with a dry, papery hiss. Underfoot, the path is soft sand, pale and loose, scattered with pine needles. The air smells of resin and wet earth. Ahead, the trees open onto a lake so still that the sky sits inside it. This is Müritz National Park, and the first thing it asks of you is to slow down.

The park lies just east of the town of Waren, in the state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania in northern Germany. It covers roughly three hundred and twenty square kilometres. That makes it one of the larger national parks in the country. Yet you will rarely feel crowded here. The land is a patchwork of pine forest, beech woods, bogs, meadows and water. And water is the key to understanding the whole place.

The park holds more than one hundred lakes. Some are large enough to cross by boat. Others are small, dark pools hidden among the trees. Along its western edge lies the Müritz itself, the largest lake that sits entirely within Germany's borders. Locals sometimes call it the little sea. On a windy day, with waves breaking on the shore and the far bank lost in haze, the name makes sense.

So where did all these lakes come from? The answer is ice. During the last Ice Age, huge glaciers pushed across northern Europe and covered this region. When the climate warmed, the ice retreated slowly. It left behind gravel ridges, sandy plains, and hollows where blocks of ice had melted. Those hollows filled with water. Almost every gentle hill and every lake you see today carries the shape of that retreating ice.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Somewhere within this park, a modest trickle of water becomes the start of the Havel river. The Havel flows south from these lakes, passes through Berlin, and eventually joins the Elbe. Water that begins its journey in this quiet woodland ends up, many weeks later, flowing towards the North Sea. It is a reminder that this remote corner of the country is connected to its capital.

The park is also a young one. It was established in 1990, during the final months of East Germany. In that short window, conservationists managed to secure protection for several large natural areas before reunification. Müritz was one of them. During the East German era, parts of this landscape had been closed to the public and used as hunting grounds for the state's leadership. That restricted access, ironically, helped keep some areas wild.

The park comes in two separate pieces. The larger part stretches across the eastern shore of the Müritz and the country behind it. A smaller part, known as Serrahn, lies further east, near the town of Neustrelitz. Serrahn is special. Its old beech forest has been left largely to its own devices for decades. Fallen trunks lie where they drop. Fungi, beetles and woodpeckers do the rest.

In 2011, the Serrahn beech forest became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. It joined a group of ancient and primeval beech forests spread across many European countries. Together, these woods show how beech trees spread across the continent after the Ice Age. Walking through Serrahn, you notice the smooth grey bark, the high leafy canopy, and the soft green light at ground level. It feels quieter than an ordinary forest, and it is.

Now, look up. The open skies over the park belong to some remarkable birds. The osprey, a fish-eating hawk, nests here, often on the tops of tall trees or on electricity pylons. The white-tailed eagle, Germany's largest bird of prey, hunts over the lakes. Its wings are so broad that people sometimes describe it as a flying door. If you see a huge dark shape gliding low over the water, it may well be one.

In autumn, the park becomes a stage for cranes. These tall grey birds gather here in large numbers on their journey south. At dusk, they fly in from the fields to spend the night standing in shallow water, where predators cannot easily reach them. Their calls are loud, rolling and trumpet-like. Once you have heard thousands of cranes calling at sunset, you do not forget it. Observation points near the lakes let visitors watch without disturbing them.

The human story here is quieter, but it is present. Small villages sit at the edges of the park, with brick churches, cobbled lanes and old farmhouses. Near the village of Ankershagen, the archaeologist Heinrich Schliemann spent part of his childhood. Later in life, he became famous for his excavations at the site he identified as ancient Troy. Legend has it that local stories of buried treasure first sparked his imagination. That tale is told often, but it is best enjoyed as folklore.

The best way to experience this place is slowly, and without an engine. Many visitors come by bicycle, following long, mostly flat routes through forest and along lakeshores. Others paddle canoes through chains of connected lakes and narrow channels. Viewing towers rise above the treeline in several spots. Climb one, and the landscape spreads out below you in rings of green and blue. The silence up there can be surprisingly complete.

Before you move on, take one more look around. Notice the colour of the water, which changes from steel grey to deep green as clouds pass. Notice the sand on your shoes, carried here by melting ice long ago. Listen again for the reeds, the wind in the pines, perhaps a distant bird call. This landscape is not finished. It is still being shaped by water, weather and time. And for a little while, you are part of it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Rent a bicycle in Waren to explore the flat trails.
Come at autumn dusk to watch cranes arrive.
Bring binoculars for spotting eagles and ospreys over lakes.
Pack insect repellent for summer walks near wetlands.
Keep to marked paths in protected zones.
