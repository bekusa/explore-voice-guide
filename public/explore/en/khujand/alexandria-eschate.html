The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere close by, the Syr Darya is moving. It is wide, brown and slow, and it carries cold water down from the mountains of the Fergana Valley. The air here often smells of dust and warm brick. On a clear day, the light falls flat and bright across the old earthen walls. Under your feet is ground that people have defended, abandoned and rebuilt for more than two thousand years.

You are standing near the heart of what most historians believe was Alexandria Eschate. The name is Greek. It means, roughly, Alexandria the Furthest. Alexander the Great founded it in 329 BCE, during his campaign through Central Asia. It was meant to be the edge of his world. Beyond this river lay the open steppe and the nomadic peoples the Greeks called Scythians. Today the city around you is Khujand, in northern Tajikistan.

A word of honesty before we go further. Scholars still discuss the exact location of Alexander's city. No inscription has been found here that says, in plain letters, this is Alexandria. But the river, the geography and the ancient descriptions all point strongly to Khujand. Most researchers accept the link. So as you walk, keep that small question mark in mind. It is part of the story.

In ancient times, this river had another name. The Greeks called it the Jaxartes. For Alexander's army, it was a frontier. To the south lay Sogdiana, a land of farming towns and trading cities. Sogdiana sat roughly where Tajikistan and Uzbekistan meet today. To the north lay the steppe. A city on this bank could guard the crossing, watch the nomads, and hold the valley. That logic explains why people have built here again and again.

The ancient writers left us one detail that is hard to forget. The Roman-era historian Arrian, writing centuries after Alexander, says the city wall went up in about twenty days. Another ancient author, Curtius, gives an even shorter figure. These numbers may be exaggerated. Ancient historians liked a heroic pace. Still, the image is vivid. Thousands of soldiers and settlers raised mud-brick walls at speed, while enemy riders watched from the far bank.

And those riders did not stay quiet. According to the ancient accounts, Scythian horsemen gathered on the opposite shore and shouted challenges across the water. Alexander, the story goes, crossed the river under cover of catapult fire and drove them back. It is one of the few times we hear of him fighting a battle on this northern edge. Look out across the river now, and try to picture that far bank lined with mounted archers.

The city Alexander left behind was a mix of peoples. Greek and Macedonian veterans settled here. So did local Sogdians, and probably prisoners from nearby towns. Over the following centuries, the Greek layer faded. The town did not. It became a Sogdian and later a Persian-speaking city. It grew into a stop on the trade routes that later generations would call the Silk Road. Goods, languages and religions passed through this bend in the river.

What you can see today is mostly the Khujand fortress, built on the site and rebuilt many times. Its walls and gate are largely reconstructions. They follow the line of much older defences. Inside, there is a museum of archaeology and fortification. It displays objects from different periods of the city's life. This is the best place to see the real, physical evidence of the people who lived here, layer upon layer.

Here is something many visitors miss. The most dramatic siege in Khujand's history was not Greek at all. In 1220, the armies of Genghis Khan arrived. The city's governor, a commander remembered as Timur Malik, refused to surrender. Medieval Persian chroniclers say he fortified an island in the river and fought from boats protected against fire arrows. In the end he escaped downstream. Khujand fell, but his defiance is still remembered with pride here.

The city changed names more than once. In the Soviet period, it was called Leninabad. That name lasted for most of the twentieth century. After Tajikistan became independent in 1991, the old name Khujand returned. So in one lifetime, a person here could have lived in Leninabad and then in Khujand, without ever moving house. The streets around you still show both eras. Older traditional houses sit near broad Soviet-era avenues.

The second surprise is about time itself. Many cities in Europe call themselves old. Khujand is older than most of them. It was already an important place when Alexander arrived, and it has been lived in almost continuously ever since. That makes it one of the oldest cities in Central Asia. When you touch a wall here, even a rebuilt one, you are standing on more than two thousand years of continuous use.

If you have time, walk a little further into the city. Khujand has a large covered market, the Panjshanbe Bazaar. Its name refers to Thursday, the old market day. Inside, you will hear traders calling out and see piles of dried apricots, nuts and fresh bread. Across from the bazaar stands a mosque and shrine complex. This is where daily life feels closest to the city's long history of trade.

As you move around the fortress, pay attention to the materials. Notice the mud-brick and fired brick, and the thick, sloping lines of the walls. These were practical choices in a dry land with little stone close at hand. Builders here have worked with earth for centuries. The colour of the walls is close to the colour of the ground. The fortress almost seems to rise straight out of the riverbank.

Before you leave, turn once more toward the river. The water keeps moving past the city, just as it did when Alexander's soldiers stood here. The far bank is still there. The mountains still close in on the horizon. Empires have come and gone, but the river crossing has stayed the same. As you walk away, listen for the traffic, the voices and the river behind them. Alexander's furthest city is still a living town.

Practical tips

Visit the fortress museum to see real archaeological finds.
Walk to the riverbank to picture the ancient frontier.
Come early morning when light is softer on the walls.
Combine your visit with the Panjshanbe Bazaar nearby.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground around the walls.
