The guide

The story

Listen first. Under your feet, gravel crunches with every step. Ahead, a wide courtyard of pale granite spreads out under the sky. Behind the rooftops, a rocky mountain rises, green and grey, close enough to feel like part of the wall. This is Gyeongbokgung, the first and largest royal palace of the Joseon dynasty. Seoul's traffic is only a few hundred metres away... yet here the sounds are footsteps, birds, and the soft rustle of silk.

That silk is worth a second look. Many of the people around you are wearing hanbok, the traditional Korean dress. The skirts are full and high-waisted. The jackets are short, with long ribbons tied at the chest. Some visitors are Korean, some are from far away. Most rented their outfits from small shops near the palace gates. For a few hours, the old courtyards fill again with colour, much as they once did on court days.

The palace dates to 1395. That was just a few years after a general named Yi Seong-gye founded a new kingdom and became King Taejo. He moved the capital here, to the city then called Hanyang, and ordered a royal palace. Its name is often translated as the palace greatly blessed by heaven. The new dynasty was called Joseon, and it ruled Korea for about five centuries. Gyeongbokgung was meant to be its heart.

The location was chosen with great care. Builders followed the principles of geomancy, a traditional way of reading land, water, and wind for good fortune. A mountain should protect the back. Open ground and water should lie in front. Turn around and look north. Bugaksan, the peak behind the palace, plays the role of guardian. The whole complex lines up along a single north-to-south axis, from the main gate to the private quarters.

Now look at the great hall in front of you. This is Geunjeongjeon, the throne hall. Here kings received officials, foreign envoys, and important ceremonies. The building stands on a two-level stone terrace with carved railings. Its double roof curves upward at the corners, and its painted beams show deep green, red, and blue. This painting style is called dancheong. It decorates the wood, and it also helps protect the timber from weather and insects.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. In the courtyard, two rows of small stone markers stand on each side of the central path. Each marker carries a rank. On ceremonial days, officials lined up beside the stone for their own position. Civil officials stood on one side, military officials on the other. The courtyard paving is rough rather than polished. A common explanation is practical — the uneven stone scatters sunlight and reduces glare.

Look up at the corner of the roofs. Small clay figures sit in a line along the edge, like a little parade. These figures are meant to guard the building against bad spirits and misfortune. Down at ground level, keep an eye out for stone creatures with curly manes. Many are haechi, mythical animals linked to justice and to protection from fire. For a palace built mostly of wood, fire was always the deepest fear.

That fear came true. In 1592, Japanese armies invaded Korea, and during that war the palace burned. Then something remarkable happened — or rather, nothing happened. For roughly two and a half centuries, the site stayed largely in ruins. Kings lived in other palaces across the city. Only in the 1860s did rebuilding begin, under the Heungseon Daewongun, the powerful regent and father of the young King Gojong. The project was enormous and costly, and it strained the kingdom's finances.

Walk west from the throne hall, and the mood changes. Gyeonghoeru, a large pavilion, rises from a square pond on rows of stone pillars. Kings used it for state banquets and for hosting foreign visitors. On a calm day, the whole building lies upside down in the water. This view gives the palace one of its quietest moments. Stand still for a minute, and you may see koi moving slowly near the edge.

A second surprise sits deeper in the grounds. Near the northern part of the palace, in the late 1880s, the court installed electric lights. This is often described as one of the first uses of electric lighting in Korea. Imagine it — the old timber halls, lit by humming electric bulbs, while most of the country still lived by candle and oil lamp. The palace was not only a place of tradition. It was also a place where the kingdom tested the modern world.

The late nineteenth century also brought tragedy. In 1895, Queen Min, later known as Empress Myeongseong, was murdered inside the palace grounds, at the residence called Geoncheonggung, by agents linked to Japan. Then, during the Japanese colonial period in the twentieth century, many palace buildings were torn down. A large government headquarters was built directly in front of the throne hall, blocking the view. That building was finally demolished in the mid-1990s, and restoration continues today.

So much of what you see is rebuilt — and that is part of the story. Gwanghwamun, the grand main gate facing the city, was restored and reopened in 2010. Near that gate, you can often watch the changing of the royal guard. Guards in bright uniforms carry flags and drums. Inside the grounds, two major museums tell more of the story: the National Palace Museum of Korea and the National Folk Museum of Korea.

Before you leave, find a quiet corner and look again at the mountain behind the roofs. The same peak watched over the first builders, the fires, the empty centuries, and the long work of repair. Listen for the gravel under someone's shoes, a bell from the guard ceremony, or children laughing in their hanbok. As you walk back toward Gwanghwamun, notice how the palace slowly gives way to the modern city — one gate at a time.
