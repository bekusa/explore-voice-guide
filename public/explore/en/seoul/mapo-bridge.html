The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the bridge move. It is a faint tremor underfoot, as buses and trucks roll across the deck behind you. Below, the Han River spreads wide and grey-green, and the wind comes off the water with nothing to stop it. To the north are the dense hills of Mapo. To the south, the glass towers of Yeouido catch the light.

This is Mapo Bridge, one of the many crossings that stitch Seoul together across the Han. The river is very wide here. That width explains much about the city. For centuries, the Han was both a highway and a barrier. Today, more than two dozen road and rail bridges cross it inside the capital. Each one has its own character, and this one has a story that reaches far beyond Korea.

The bridge opened in 1970, during the years when Seoul was rebuilding and expanding at enormous speed. It was first called Seoul Bridge. Later it took the name of the district on its northern bank, and it has been Mapo Bridge ever since. Its purpose was practical. It linked the old riverside neighbourhoods to Yeouido, a flat sandy island that the government was turning into a new centre of power and money.

Look south, and you are looking at the result. Yeouido was once mostly sandbanks and grassland. In the early twentieth century it became home to Korea's first airfield. Then, from around the time this bridge opened, the island was transformed. The National Assembly moved there, along with broadcasters, banks and the stock exchange. The tall golden tower you may see to the southeast is the 63 Building, long one of Seoul's best-known landmarks.

Now turn north. The name Mapo belongs to a much older story. Before the bridges, this bank was a busy river port. Boats came up the Han carrying grain, fish and salt from the west coast. Mapo became especially known for salted seafood, such as fermented shrimp, which Koreans still use in kimchi. Merchants, porters and ferrymen worked these banks. Today the port is gone, but the district still carries the name.

Here is a detail that most visitors never notice. Under your feet, below the river itself, trains are running. Seoul Subway Line 5 crosses the Han in a tunnel beneath the riverbed, between Mapo Station on the north bank and Yeouinaru Station on the south. When it opened in the 1990s, it was the first subway tunnel to pass under the Han. Most of the city's other lines cross the river on bridges, in full view.

The bridge also carries a heavier history, and it is worth telling with care. For many years, Mapo Bridge was known as a place where people in deep distress came to end their lives. This was painful for the city. Over time, it became a public question about loneliness, pressure and mental health in a fast-changing society.

In 2012, the city of Seoul and a large insurance company tried something new. They turned the railings into what they called the Bridge of Life. Sensors switched on lights as people walked past. Short messages appeared along the rail, written like a friend speaking to you. Some asked simple questions, such as whether you had eaten today, or how you were feeling. Others encouraged people to think of someone they loved. The project won major international advertising awards.

The story did not end there, and this is the second surprise. The campaign brought great attention to the bridge, and some critics argued that it drew more people here, not fewer. In the years that followed, the city changed its approach. The messages were eventually removed, and taller, harder-to-climb safety railings were installed. Emergency phones and patrols became part of the bridge too. Today the railing you see is a quieter, plainer barrier, built for protection rather than words.

That history is why a walk across this bridge can feel different from other crossings. Many people walk here simply for the view, or to reach the parks on either bank. Others come to remember. If you or someone with you is struggling, the emergency phones on the bridge connect to help, and Korea runs a national crisis line that anyone can call. There is no shame in using it. That is the message the bridge still carries, even without the lighted words.

Walk to the southern end and you reach Yeouido Hangang Park. On warm evenings, the riverbank fills with families on picnic mats, cyclists and people ordering fried chicken by delivery to eat on the grass. The long cycle paths along the Han run beneath the bridge here. In spring, the streets around the National Assembly are lined with cherry trees, and the whole island turns pale pink for a few short weeks.

At the northern end, the Mapo neighbourhood is worth a slower look. The side streets near Mapo Station are known for grilled meat restaurants, where pork and beef sizzle on charcoal at the table. The smell of smoke and garlic drifts into the street at dinner time. Office workers fill the small restaurants after work. It is one of the most ordinary, most local scenes in Seoul, and it sits just a few minutes from the river.

As evening comes, the bridge changes. The lights of Yeouido switch on across the water, and the river turns dark and smooth. Traffic keeps flowing, a steady line of red and white. The neighbouring bridges light up one after another, and you can trace the city along the river by their glow.

Before you move on, take one more look over the railing. The water below has carried boats, salt and fish. It now carries reflections of towers that did not exist when this bridge opened. Feel the small tremor of traffic again under your feet. Then walk on gently, toward whichever bank is calling you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk across at dusk to see Yeouido light up.
Start at Mapo Station and finish in Yeouido Hangang Park.
Bring a windproof layer; the bridge is very exposed.
Try a charcoal grill restaurant near Mapo Station afterwards.
Visit in spring for cherry blossoms around the National Assembly.
