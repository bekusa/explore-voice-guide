The guide

The story

Feel the slope under your feet first. The path up Namsan rises steadily through pine and oak, and the air grows a little cooler with every turn. Somewhere below, a bus changes gear. Above the trees, a slim white shaft with a round head rises into the sky. This is Namsan Seoul Tower, known today as N Seoul Tower. For many people in Seoul, it is the first thing they look for when they want to know where they are.

The name Namsan simply means South Mountain. For centuries it was the southern guardian of the old capital. When the Joseon kings built their city in the late fourteenth century, they placed the royal palaces to the north. This hill sat across the valley, facing them. A stone fortress wall once ran along its ridge, linking it to the other mountains around the city. Parts of that wall still climb these slopes today.

Now, here is a detail many visitors walk past. Near the base of the tower, look for low, rounded stone structures, like small chimneys or kilns. These are reconstructions of beacon fire stations. During the Joseon period, a chain of beacons stretched across the Korean peninsula. Signal fires were lit on mountaintops, one after another. Smoke by day and flames by night carried news of danger from the borders to the capital. Namsan was the end of that chain — the place where messages arrived for the king.

The tower itself belongs to a much later age. It was built as a broadcasting tower around the beginning of the 1970s, when television was spreading quickly across South Korea. Its job was practical. Antennas at the top sent television and radio signals across the city. For years the public could only look at it from below. It opened to visitors around 1980, and from that moment it became something else — a place people chose to come to.

In the 2000s, the tower went through a major renovation. New lighting, new restaurants and new observation spaces were added, and the name changed to N Seoul Tower. The letter N is often explained as standing for new, or for Namsan, or for nature. Whatever the meaning, the shape stayed the same. The concrete shaft still carries its antenna mast, and it is still a working broadcast tower above the heads of the tourists.

Think about the height for a moment. The tower is not the tallest structure in Seoul today. Newer skyscrapers rise higher from the ground. But the tower stands on top of a mountain. That means its observation deck sits far above most of the city. From up here, the hill does most of the work. It is a simple trick, and an old one — the same logic that put the beacon fires on this summit centuries ago.

Around the base, you will see fences, railings and even small trees covered in padlocks. There are thousands of them. Red, pink, gold, heart-shaped, painted with names and dates. Couples write their names on a lock, fix it to the railing, and leave it there as a promise. The practice grew popular in the 2000s, helped along by Korean television dramas filmed on these terraces. Some locks are bright and new. Others have rusted into one solid mass.

If you go up to the observation deck, take a slow walk around the full circle. Seoul spreads out in every direction, and it is wider than most people expect. To the south, the Han River curves through the city in a broad silver band, crossed by bridge after bridge. To the north, the older city sits in a bowl of granite mountains. The palace district lies down there, though the modern towers make it hard to pick out.

Here is a second small surprise. On the windows of the observation deck, look for the names of cities around the world printed on the glass. Each name sits in the direction of that city, along with its distance from Seoul. You can stand facing Tokyo, or Beijing, or a city on the other side of the planet. It is a quiet reminder of how this country has looked outward over the last half century.

Down on the mountain, life goes on at a gentler speed. Early in the morning, older residents walk the paths around Namsan. Some carry small radios. Others stop at outdoor exercise machines and swing their arms in the fresh air. In spring, cherry blossoms line some of the roads up the hill. In autumn, the slopes turn red and orange. The mountain is a park first, and a tourist site second.

There are several ways up. A cable car runs from the lower slopes, and many visitors take it for the short, swaying ride above the trees. Buses climb the winding road to a stop near the summit. And the paths are open to anyone willing to walk. The climb is steady rather than difficult, with stairs in places. On foot, you see the stone city wall up close, and you understand how high this hill really is.

At night, the tower changes character. Its lights come on, and the white shaft glows against the dark sky. From almost anywhere in central Seoul, you can look up and find it. Down on the terraces, the city lights spread out to the horizon. Traffic moves in long red and white lines along the river. It is one of the best places in the city to see how large Seoul has grown.

So take a moment before you leave. Look at the rounded stones of the beacon stations, where fire once carried warnings across the mountains. Then look up at the antenna, which still carries signals across the city today. Two very different ways of sending a message — from the same summit. As you walk back down through the trees, listen for the wind in the pines. The noise of the city will slowly rise to meet you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the cable car up, then walk down the forest paths.
Come near sunset to see the city by day and night.
Walk part of the old city wall along the trail.
Bring your own padlock if you want to add one.
Check air quality first; haze can hide the distant mountains.
