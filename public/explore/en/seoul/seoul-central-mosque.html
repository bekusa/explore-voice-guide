The guide

The story

Start with your legs. The road up to the mosque climbs steeply. By the time you reach the gate, you can probably feel the slope in your calves. Then look up. Two slender white minarets rise above the rooftops of Itaewon. Below them, the face of the building is pale and clean, with bands of blue tilework. Somewhere behind you, a delivery scooter rattles past a kebab shop. In front of you, the city goes quiet in a way that surprises most first-time visitors.

This is Seoul Central Mosque. It sits on a hillside in the Hannam-dong area of Yongsan District, just above the busy streets of Itaewon. It opened in 1976, and it is widely recognised as the first purpose-built mosque in Korea. For many Korean Muslims, it remains the centre of their community. The building is also the base of the Korea Muslim Federation, the main national organisation for Muslims in the country. So this is more than a place of prayer. It is a headquarters, a school, and a meeting point.

To understand why a mosque stands here at all, it helps to go back to the Korean War in the early 1950s. Turkey sent a large brigade of soldiers to fight alongside the United Nations forces. Some of those Turkish troops brought imams with them. In their spare hours, they held prayers and talked about their faith with local people. A small number of Koreans became Muslim through those meetings. That quiet beginning, in tents and temporary rooms, is where the story of this hilltop really starts.

For about two decades after the war, the young Muslim community had no permanent mosque. People gathered in rented spaces and makeshift halls. Then, in the late 1960s and early 1970s, the Korean government provided this plot of land. Muslim-majority countries contributed funds for the construction. At that time, South Korea was building closer economic ties with the Middle East. Many Korean construction firms and workers were heading to the Gulf. The mosque was, in part, a sign of that new relationship between Seoul and the wider Islamic world.

Now look closely at the entrance. Above the main doors, you can see Arabic calligraphy set against the blue tiles. The phrase there is the declaration that God is greater, a line Muslims repeat many times in every prayer. Arabic script runs from right to left, and the letters flow and loop like a single drawn line. Even if you cannot read it, you can follow the rhythm of the strokes. Calligraphy matters in Islamic art because traditional mosques avoid images of people. The written word becomes the decoration.

The architecture follows a classic mosque pattern, but at a modest scale. There is a central domed hall, a pair of minarets, and a broad terrace in front. The minarets are tall towers. Historically, a caller stood near the top and announced the five daily prayer times to the neighbourhood. Here, in a dense residential district of Seoul, the towers work mostly as a visual signal. They tell you from several streets away that this is a place of worship. Look at how they frame the dome between them.

Here is something many visitors miss. Turn around on the terrace and look back down the hill. From this height you can see a wide sweep of Yongsan rooftops, apartment towers, and on clear days the hills beyond. Most people come for the facade and never turn around. The view explains why the site was chosen. A building on a slope can be seen from below, and it can look out over the city. The mosque and the neighbourhood watch each other.

Inside, the main prayer hall is a calm, open room covered in carpet. There are no pews. Worshippers stand, bow, and kneel together in straight rows. One wall holds a small niche called the mihrab. It shows the direction of Mecca, the holy city in Saudi Arabia, which all Muslims face when they pray. There is a separate area for women, as is common in many mosques. Visitors who enter should take off their shoes, speak softly, and avoid walking in front of anyone praying.

The atmosphere changes a great deal depending on when you come. On an ordinary weekday afternoon, the courtyard can be almost empty, with only a few students or an older man sitting in the shade. On Fridays, around midday, it fills up. Friday is the most important congregational prayer of the week in Islam. Men and women arrive from across Seoul and beyond. You will hear Arabic, Korean, Urdu, Turkish, Malay, Indonesian, and English, often in the same conversation on the steps.

That mix of languages is the second surprise of this place. The congregation is not one community but many. There are Korean converts, some from the earliest post-war generation. There are migrant workers from South and Southeast Asia. There are students from Central Asia, the Middle East, and Africa. There are diplomats, business travellers, and tourists. During the holy month of Ramadan, when Muslims fast from dawn to sunset, the mosque often hosts shared evening meals. Strangers sit side by side to break the fast together.

When you walk back down the hill, pay attention to the streets around you. This area is often called Itaewon's Muslim street. You will pass halal butchers, grocery shops with dates and spices, bookshops selling copies of the Quran, and small restaurants serving Turkish, Pakistani, Indian, and Uzbek food. Halal means permitted under Islamic law, especially for food and meat. The mosque created this cluster of businesses. People came to pray, and then they needed somewhere to eat, shop, and talk afterwards.

Itaewon itself has long been one of Seoul's most international neighbourhoods. For decades, it sat close to a large United States military base, which brought foreign residents, bars, tailors, and shops. Embassies settled in nearby Hannam-dong. The mosque fits into that history of outsiders finding a home on these slopes. Yet it is also deeply Korean. Notices are posted in Korean. Local converts lead parts of community life. It is a Korean institution as much as a foreign one.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment on the terrace. Listen. You may hear the soft scrape of shoes on stone, a child's voice from the courtyard, the low hum of traffic far below. Look once more at the blue tiles and the white towers against the Seoul sky. A few soldiers' prayers in wartime tents led, a generation later, to this building on a hill. As you walk back into Itaewon, notice how often the minarets still appear between the buildings behind you.
