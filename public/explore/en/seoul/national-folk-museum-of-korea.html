The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, beyond the palace walls, traffic moves along the wide avenues of central Seoul. In front of you, the sound changes. Gravel crunches underfoot. A breeze moves through old trees. Above the treetops, a tiered building climbs toward the sky. It looks like a wooden temple pagoda grown far larger than any real temple could hold. This is the National Folk Museum of Korea. It sits inside the grounds of Gyeongbokgung, the main royal palace of the Joseon dynasty, in the Jongno District.

That location tells you something already. Gyeongbokgung was the seat of kings. Its halls held ceremonies, councils and royal family life. Yet this museum, inside the same walls, is not about kings at all. It is about everyone else. Farmers and fishermen. Grandmothers and newborn babies. Shopkeepers, shamans, potters and students. The museum is about ordinary Korean life, from birth to death and through the turning seasons of the year. A palace for the people, you might say, set right beside the palace of the throne.

Look up at the building before you go inside. It is not one historic structure. It is a careful assembly of several. The architects drew on famous Buddhist temple buildings from across Korea and combined them into one design. The tall, layered tower takes its form from Palsangjeon, the five-storey wooden pagoda hall at Beopjusa Temple. Other parts of the building follow other temple halls. The stone staircase at the front recalls the stone bridge-stairs at Bulguksa Temple near Gyeongju.

Here is the first surprise. The tower looks like wood, but it is concrete. The building was put up in the early 1970s. In that period, South Korea wanted modern public buildings that still looked unmistakably Korean. So the designers used modern materials and shaped them into traditional forms. Some people admire the result. Others found it strange, a temple made of cement. Either way, the building shows a country trying to hold its past and its future in a single shape.

The building also has a second life story. It did not begin as a folk museum. It was first built to house the National Museum of Korea, the country's main collection of art and archaeology. That collection later moved away to larger homes elsewhere in Seoul. The folk museum then moved into this building. So the halls you are about to walk through once held royal treasures and ancient relics. Today they hold rice scoops, wedding clothes and the tools of daily work.

Inside, the permanent galleries follow a simple and very human logic. One part of the museum tells the long story of how people on the Korean Peninsula have lived, from early times into the modern age. Another part follows a single year. You move through spring planting, summer heat, autumn harvest and the long cold of winter. Each season brings its own foods, festivals and tasks. Farming families measured time this way, by the moon and the fields, not by clocks.

A third part follows a single human life. It begins with birth customs, such as the rope of straw and charcoal that families once hung across the gate after a baby arrived. The rope warned visitors to stay away for a while and protect the mother and child. From there the story moves to childhood, study, marriage and work. It ends with old age and the rituals that honour the dead. Ancestor rites, called jesa, are still performed in many Korean homes today.

As you walk, pay attention to small objects. A brass rice bowl. A wooden pillow. A set of tiny shoes. Much of Korean folk culture lived in things people used every day, not in grand monuments. Look for the large earthenware jars called onggi. They are dark brown and slightly glossy. Families used them to ferment and store soy sauce, bean paste and kimchi. The clay can breathe a little, which helps the food ferment slowly. Many apartments in Seoul still keep onggi jars on their balconies.

Now step back outside. The open-air area around the museum is easy to miss, and many visitors walk straight past it. Here you will find stone figures, millstones, old gates and carved wooden poles. The tall poles with fierce painted faces are called jangseung. Villages once placed them at the entrance to the community. They marked the boundary and, people believed, kept out disease and evil spirits. Their wide eyes and big teeth were meant to frighten, but many visitors today find them rather funny.

Nearby, look for a small circle of stone animals. These are the twelve animals of the East Asian zodiac. The rat, the ox, the tiger, the rabbit, and onward to the pig. In Korea, as in China and Japan, each year belongs to one of these animals. People often ask each other their zodiac animal as a friendly way to talk about age. Find your own birth year animal and stand beside it. It is a small ritual, and it often starts a conversation.

Here is the second surprise. Somewhere in the outdoor area, you can walk into a recreated street from Korea's recent past. It shows the decades of rapid growth in the later twentieth century. You will see old shop fronts, a barber, a small comic book shop and painted signs. For many older Korean visitors, this street is not history. It is childhood. Watch the grandparents here. They often stop, point and start telling stories to the younger people with them.

That moment is perhaps the real purpose of this place. Folk culture is not finished. It changes and continues. The straw rope at the gate has mostly disappeared, but families still mark a baby's first hundred days and first birthday with care. The village guardian poles are rare now, but Koreans still bow to elders and still gather for the harvest holiday of Chuseok. The museum keeps the objects, but the customs live on in kitchens, apartments and family homes across the country.

Before you leave, turn and look at the tower once more. Notice how the curved roof edges lift slightly at the corners, a classic feature of Korean roofs. Behind it, the mountains to the north rise above the city. To one side, the palace rooftops spread out in long grey lines. Listen again for the gravel under your feet and the distant city traffic. You are standing between a royal past and an ordinary present... and this museum belongs, quietly, to the ordinary present.
