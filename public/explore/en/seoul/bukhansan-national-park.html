The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the chatter of hikers, there is a steady sound of water running over smooth stone. The air carries pine resin and damp leaves. Look up through the branches and you will see pale grey granite domes, almost white where the sun hits them. This is Bukhansan National Park, seen from its northern edge near Uijeongbu, where the city ends and the mountains begin.

The park is unusual because of where it sits. It wraps around the northern rim of Seoul and spills into the neighbouring cities of Gyeonggi Province, including Uijeongbu, Yangju and Goyang. Millions of people live within an hour of these trails. Korea designated the area a national park in 1983. Few parks anywhere in the world have so much wild rock so close to so many apartment towers.

The name itself is simple. Buk means north, and han refers to the Han River, the great river that runs through Seoul. So Bukhansan is the mountain north of the Han. For centuries, this ridge protected the capital from the north. It worked as a natural wall long before anyone built a stone one along its crests.

And people did build one. In the early eighteenth century, during the reign of King Sukjong of the Joseon dynasty, the court completed Bukhansanseong, a mountain fortress that follows the ridgelines. The Joseon dynasty ruled Korea for about five hundred years, and its kings had suffered invasions in the previous century. The fortress was a refuge for the court in times of war. Sections of the wall and its gates still stand along the high trails today.

The park has two main mountain groups. In the south rises Bukhansan proper, with its highest peak, Baegundae, and the sheer face of Insubong, a favourite of rock climbers. Here in the north, closer to Uijeongbu, rises Dobongsan. Its crown is a line of jagged granite spires, including Jaunbong, Seoninbong and Manjangbong. On a clear day, they look like a row of broken teeth against the sky.

Why is the rock so pale and so smooth? This is granite, formed deep underground from slowly cooling magma. Over a very long time, the softer rock above it wore away. As the pressure lifted, the granite cracked and peeled in curved layers, a little like an onion. That process left these rounded domes and steep slabs. Run your hand over the stone and you will feel its coarse, sugary grain.

Here is something many visitors miss. Uijeongbu has its own quiet doorways into the park. Near Hoeryong Station, a path climbs to Hoeryongsa, a Buddhist temple tucked into the Dobongsan foothills. Another trail system leads up to Sapaesan, a broad granite summit on the Uijeongbu side. These routes are often calmer than the famous trailheads in Seoul. You share the stone steps with local walkers rather than tour groups.

Hoeryongsa carries a story worth knowing, though it belongs to legend rather than to the archive. Locals say the name means the returning dragon. According to one popular tale, the dragon was Yi Seong-gye, the founder of the Joseon dynasty, who came back to this place after a period of exile. In Korean tradition, the dragon was a symbol of the king. Whether or not the tale is true, it ties this quiet valley to the birth of a dynasty.

On the slopes of Dobongsan you may also find Mangwolsa, a temple that tradition dates back many centuries. Temples like these shape the rhythm of the mountain. You may hear a wooden fish drum, a hollow knocking sound used to call monks to prayer. In the early morning or late afternoon, a deep temple bell can roll down the valley. Please walk quietly near the halls. These are working places of worship, not museums.

Now notice the people around you. Korean hiking culture is very visible here. Many hikers wear full technical gear, bright jackets and trekking poles, even for a short walk. At rest spots, groups unwrap gimbap, which is rice rolled in seaweed, along with fruit and sometimes a small bottle of makgeolli, a milky rice wine. After the hike, restaurants near the trailheads serve savoury pancakes and warm noodle soups to tired walkers.

The seasons change this place completely. In spring, pale pink azaleas appear among the rocks. Summer brings heavy green shade and the sound of cicadas, along with sudden rain that turns streams into small waterfalls. Autumn is the most loved season. Maples turn red and orange against the grey granite, and the trails grow crowded. In winter, ice forms on the north faces, and the ridges look sharper under a thin covering of snow.

A word of honest caution. These mountains look gentle from the city, but the upper trails are steep. Some sections use iron cables, stairs and handholds bolted into the rock. The granite can be slippery after rain or frost. Rangers close certain routes for safety or for nature recovery, so follow the posted signs. Start early, carry water, and turn back before the light fades. The mountain will still be here next time.

Before you go, take one more look around. The pines lean out of narrow cracks in the rock, holding on with very little soil. The stream keeps running over stone polished by countless years of water. Behind you, the city hums faintly. Ahead of you, the granite rises in silence. As you walk away, listen for that small change in sound, the moment where the forest ends and Uijeongbu begins again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the Hoeryong Station side for quieter trails.
Start early to avoid weekend crowds on ridges.
Wear shoes with strong grip for smooth granite.
Carry enough water; summit routes have few sources.
Keep voices low near working temple halls.
