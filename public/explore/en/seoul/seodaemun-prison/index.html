The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Traffic hums somewhere beyond the walls, but inside the compound the loudest sound is often gravel under your own shoes. Rows of dark red brick rise around you, plain and solid. Behind them, the granite slopes of Inwangsan mountain climb toward the sky. It is a calm place now, with trees, benches and school groups. But the calm took a long time to arrive here.

A small note on location first. Seodaemun Prison stands in the Seodaemun district of Seoul, near Dongnimmun Station, a short trip from Goyang across the city's northwestern edge. Many visitors come from Goyang for the day. Today the site is a museum called the Seodaemun Prison History Hall. Before that, for most of the twentieth century, it was a working prison. And for much of that time, it held the people who fought to keep Korea free.

The story begins in 1908. Korea was still officially the Korean Empire, but Japan already controlled its foreign affairs and much of its government. In that year, the authorities opened a new prison here, first called Gyeongseong Prison. Gyeongseong was the colonial-era name for Seoul. Two years later, in 1910, Japan formally annexed Korea. The prison was renamed Seodaemun Prison soon after, and it grew quickly.

Why did it grow? Because resistance grew. Throughout the colonial period, which lasted until 1945, Koreans organised secret societies, published banned newspapers and joined armed independence groups abroad. Many of those who were caught came through these gates. The prison became the central place of detention for independence activists. Some served long sentences. Some were tortured during interrogation. Some never left. The buildings around you were built to hold far more people than they were first designed for.

The largest wave came in 1919. On the first of March that year, Koreans across the country gathered and read out a declaration of independence. The protests were peaceful. People simply shouted for independence in streets and market squares. Today Koreans call it the March First Movement. The colonial police answered with mass arrests, and this prison filled far beyond its capacity. Cells built for a few people held many more, packed shoulder to shoulder.

One of those prisoners was a young woman named Yu Gwan-sun. She was a student who helped lead protests in her home region during the March First Movement. She was arrested, sentenced and brought here. Even in prison, she kept calling for independence. She died here in 1920, while still a teenager. Today she is one of the most honoured figures in modern Korean history. Many Korean schoolchildren learn her name very early.

Walk toward the central cell blocks and look at their shape. The long wings spread out from a central point, like the spokes of a wheel. From that hub, a single guard could look down several corridors at once. This design was popular in prisons of the period because it made constant watching easy and cheap. Step inside and look along the corridor. Notice how the cell doors face each other in long, identical rows.

The cells themselves are small. Look at the thick wooden doors and the tiny openings in them. Guards passed food through these openings and watched prisoners through them. Many visitors miss a detail here — the small hinged signal flaps beside some doors. A prisoner could push one out to call a guard. Stand inside a cell for a few seconds, if you can. The space is quiet and close, and you will feel the walls very near you.

In the exhibition hall, the basement holds reconstructions of interrogation rooms. They show the methods used against prisoners during the colonial years. Some displays are difficult to look at, and some families with young children choose to skip this level. Upstairs, the walls are covered with small identity photographs of prisoners. These are real record cards. Thousands of faces look back at you — young students, farmers, teachers, monks. Each card is one person who passed through here.

At the far end of the grounds stands a modest wooden building, surrounded by a high wall. This was the execution building. Just outside its entrance grows a poplar tree. According to one popular tale, condemned prisoners held this tree and wept on their way inside, so locals call it the Wailing Poplar. Legend also says a second poplar inside the wall never grew well. Whatever the truth, people often stop here in silence.

Here is a detail most visitors walk straight past. Behind the execution building, there is a low tunnel opening in the ground. This tunnel was used to carry bodies out of the prison without anyone seeing. Part of it has been restored so that visitors can see the entrance. It is easy to miss because it looks like an ordinary drain. Once you know what it is, it is hard to forget.

The prison did not close in 1945. After liberation, the new Korean state kept using it. Through the years of war, military rule and political struggle, it held a new generation of prisoners, including pro-democracy activists. It finally stopped operating as a prison in 1987, when its functions moved to a new facility outside the city. In the 1990s, the site reopened as a park and museum. Several original buildings were preserved, and others were carefully reconstructed.

Before you leave, walk out through the park toward Independence Gate. This stone arch was built in the late nineteenth century to mark Korea's independence from older foreign influence. It stands only a few minutes from the prison walls. The two sit side by side — a gate for freedom, and a prison built to crush it. As you go, look back once at the red brick against the mountain. The bricks are still. The trees are still. And above them, the ordinary sky of Seoul.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take Seoul Metro Line 3 to Dongnimmun Station.
Allow at least two hours for the full grounds.
Visit on a weekday morning to avoid large school groups.
Prepare children before entering the basement interrogation displays.
Combine the visit with a walk to nearby Independence Gate.
