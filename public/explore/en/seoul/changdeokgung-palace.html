The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the gate, the traffic of central Seoul fades into a softer sound. You hear gravel shifting under shoes, wind moving through old pines, and sparrows on the curved roof tiles. Above you, the eaves of the gate turn upward at the corners, painted in green, red and blue. The air smells faintly of wood and earth. Welcome to Changdeokgung, the Palace of Prospering Virtue, and one of the five grand palaces of the Joseon kings.

The gate you have just passed is Donhwamun. It was rebuilt in the early seventeenth century, and it is the oldest surviving main palace gate in Seoul. That is the first surprise here. Many buildings in this city look old but were reconstructed recently. This gate has stood for roughly four hundred years. Look up at the thick wooden brackets under the roof. They hold the weight of the tiles without a single metal nail. Joseon carpenters locked the timbers together like a puzzle.

A little history helps here. The Joseon dynasty ruled Korea for about five centuries, from the late fourteenth century to the early twentieth. Its first great palace was Gyeongbokgung, a short walk west of here. Changdeokgung was built in 1405 as a secondary palace, a second home for the royal court. But history had other plans. In 1592, Japanese armies invaded Korea, and the palaces of Seoul burned during that long war.

When the court rebuilt, it chose to restore Changdeokgung first. Gyeongbokgung stayed in ruins for more than two centuries. So this palace, the second palace, became the true centre of royal life. Kings lived, ruled and died here for roughly 270 years. If you want to imagine where Korean royalty actually spent their days, this is the place. It is quieter than Gyeongbokgung, and many visitors say it feels more lived in.

Now notice something about the layout. Most royal palaces in East Asia follow a strict straight line, gate after gate, hall after hall. This one does not. The buildings turn, step and bend to follow the hills behind them. The designers worked with the land instead of flattening it. This harmony between architecture and nature is a key reason UNESCO added Changdeokgung to its World Heritage list in 1997. The palace is the slope, the trees and the buildings together.

As you walk toward the throne hall, you cross a small stone bridge called Geumcheongyo. It is easy to miss, and that is a shame. This bridge dates from the early fifteenth century, and it is considered the oldest surviving stone bridge in Seoul. Look along its sides for carved stone animals. In palace design, water and guardian figures at the entrance were believed to keep bad spirits away. Every visitor, even the king, crossed this line of protection.

Ahead is Injeongjeon, the main throne hall. Here the king received foreign envoys, held coronations and met his officials. Stand in the stone courtyard and look at the two rows of small stone markers. They showed officials where to stand, ordered by rank. Civil officials stood on one side, military officials on the other. Picture the courtyard full of men in coloured robes, silent and in perfect lines, facing the throne.

Now look inside the hall, and here is the second surprise. You may see electric lights, glass windows and Western-style curtains. These were added in the early twentieth century, during the final years of the dynasty. The Korean Empire was trying to modernise quickly, under heavy pressure from Japan. So this hall holds two worlds at once. There is the ancient Confucian throne, and around it, the first signs of modern life.

Just east of the throne hall is Seonjeongjeon, a smaller hall where the king met his ministers for daily business. Look closely at its roof. The tiles are a deep blue-green glaze, not the usual grey. It is the only surviving palace building in Korea with blue roof tiles. In the morning light, the colour can look almost like the sea. Many people walk straight past it without noticing.

Further along is Nakseonjae, a group of simple buildings with unpainted wood. The plain style was a deliberate choice. It reflects a Confucian ideal of modesty. This area also carries the last chapter of the royal story. Members of the final royal family lived here well into the twentieth century. The last of them passed away in 1989. So the palace was a home not only centuries ago, but within living memory.

Behind the palace lies Huwon, often called the Secret Garden. It covers a large wooded hillside, and entry is usually by guided tour with a separate ticket. Inside, paths climb past ponds, pavilions and very old trees. The most famous spot is Buyongji, a square pond with a small round island. In traditional thought, the square stood for the earth and the circle stood for the heavens. The whole universe sits in one quiet pool.

Above that pond rises Juhamnu, a two-storey pavilion. King Jeongjo, a scholar king of the late eighteenth century, used this area for the royal library and for his court scholars. He wanted the best minds of his time close to him. Imagine young officials taking royal exams here, writing poems beside the water. Deeper in the garden, a small stream called Okryucheon runs over carved rock, where kings once floated cups of wine and composed verses.

Before you leave, pause somewhere in the shade. Notice how the wooden pillars have faded and cracked. Look at how the roofs curve like the ridges of the mountains behind them. Listen again to the wind in the pines. Kings walked these same paths, through war, recovery and the end of an empire. As you step back through Donhwamun into modern Seoul, look up once more at those painted eaves. They have watched this city for four hundred years.

What to look for

Blue-green glazed roof tiles on Seonjeongjeon hall.
Stone rank markers lining the throne hall courtyard.
Electric lights and curtains inside Injeongjeon hall.
Round island inside the square Buyongji pond.
Carved guardian animals on the Geumcheongyo bridge.
Before you go

Practical tips

Book the Secret Garden tour early; places are limited.
Arrive in the morning for softer light and fewer crowds.
Wear comfortable shoes for the hilly garden paths.
Combine your visit with neighbouring Changgyeonggung Palace.
Visit in autumn for red maples around the garden ponds.
