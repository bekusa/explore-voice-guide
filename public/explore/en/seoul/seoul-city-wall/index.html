The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the stone. It is cool, even on a warm afternoon, and slightly rough under the palm. Look closely and you will see that the blocks are not all alike. Some are small and uneven, almost like field stones. Others are large, squared, and fitted tightly together. That mix of shapes is the first clue to the story of this wall. It was not built once. It was built, repaired, and rebuilt by different generations, each leaving its own handwriting in stone.

This is the Seoul City Wall, known in Korean as Hanyangdoseong. Hanyang was the old name for Seoul. The wall was first raised in 1396, a few years after a new royal family took power. That family was the Joseon dynasty, which went on to rule Korea for about five centuries. Its founder, King Taejo, moved his capital to this valley. Then he ordered a wall to protect it and to mark its edges clearly.

The choice of this valley was not random. Hanyang sits inside a ring of four mountains. To the north is Bugaksan. To the east is the low hill of Naksan. To the south is Namsan, where the tall broadcast tower stands today. To the west is Inwangsan, with its pale granite cliffs. The builders did not draw a neat square on a map. Instead, they ran the wall along the ridges of these four peaks, so the land itself became part of the defence.

The result is a long, winding loop of roughly eighteen kilometres. In places it climbs steep slopes, then drops into hollows, then climbs again. Walking the full circuit takes most of a day, and many people split it into sections over several visits. On the mountain stretches you can see the wall rising and falling ahead of you like a line drawn across the hills. Down in the city, it slips between apartment blocks, schools, and busy roads.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. On some stones you can find carved characters. These are not decoration. During construction, the work was divided into sections, and labour came from different regions of the kingdom. The inscriptions record which district built which part, and in later repairs, the names of the officials and craftsmen in charge. It was a system of responsibility. If a section collapsed, everyone knew exactly who had built it. Look along the lower courses for these marks.

The early wall was partly built of packed earth. In 1422, under King Sejong, the ruler best remembered for creating the Korean alphabet, large stretches were rebuilt in stone. Then, in the early eighteenth century, another major repair campaign used larger, evenly cut blocks. That is why the wall can look like a patchwork. Rough, small stones often belong to the earliest phases. The neat rectangular blocks usually belong to later work. Reading the wall is a little like reading tree rings.

The wall had eight gates. Four were great gates, facing roughly north, south, east, and west. Four smaller gates sat between them. The southern great gate is Sungnyemun, which many locals still call Namdaemun, meaning simply the Great South Gate. The eastern great gate is Heunginjimun, or Dongdaemun. Both names now belong to entire neighbourhoods and famous markets. So when a taxi driver mentions Namdaemun Market, the old gate of the wall is hiding inside that name.

Heunginjimun has one feature the other gates lack. Around its front curves a short, half-moon wall. This outer ring forced attackers into a tight space, where defenders could strike from several sides at once. The eastern side of the city sat on lower, flatter ground, so it needed extra protection. Stand near the gate and follow that curve with your eyes. It is a simple shape, but it shows how carefully the builders thought about weak points.

Not every gate survived. The western great gate, Donuimun, was torn down in 1915, during the period of Japanese colonial rule. In those years, long sections of the wall were removed to make room for roads, tram lines, and new buildings. A city that had grown inside its wall for centuries suddenly spilled outside it. Today, in some places, a line of different paving or a small marker shows where the wall once ran, even though no stone remains.

The twentieth century brought another surprise, this time on the northern mountain. In 1968, a squad of North Korean commandos came down these slopes in an attempt to reach the presidential residence nearby. After that attack, the Bugaksan section of the wall was closed to the public for decades. It only began to reopen in the 2000s. If you hike that stretch, you may still notice the military history around you. Ask locals, and some remember when this ridge was completely off limits.

There is also a story of loss and repair at the southern gate. In 2008, a fire set by an arsonist badly damaged the wooden upper structure of Sungnyemun. For many Koreans, watching it burn on the news was a shock, like losing a member of the family. The gate was carefully restored using traditional methods and reopened in 2013. When you stand in front of it today, you are looking at both an old monument and a very recent act of care.

Along the eastern hill of Naksan, the wall passes close to small residential lanes, with painted walls, steep stairs, and laundry drying on rooftops. At night, the wall here is softly lit, and the line of stone glows against the dark city. Couples, joggers, and older residents out for an evening walk all share the path. This is one of the easiest places to feel how the wall still belongs to daily life, not only to the past.

For most of its history, this wall did two jobs. It protected the capital, and it defined it. Inside was the royal city. Outside was everything else. The gates opened at dawn and closed at night, and the rhythm of the city followed them. Today, the wall no longer keeps anyone out. Instead, it gives people a reason to walk, to climb, and to see their city from above.

Before you move on, look once more at the stones in front of you. Notice which ones are rough and which are smooth. Notice any carved marks, or the moss in the joints. Then look up and follow the wall as it disappears around the next corner, or over the next ridge. Somewhere ahead, it keeps going, the way it has for more than six centuries, quietly holding the edges of Seoul.

Paving markers showing where vanished wall sections once ran.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk one section at a time rather than the full loop.
Wear proper shoes for steep stairs on Bugaksan and Inwangsan.
Bring identification for the Bugaksan stretch near military areas.
Visit the Naksan section after dark for the lit wall.
Start near Dongdaemun to combine the wall with a museum visit.
