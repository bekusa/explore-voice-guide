The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, south of the Beni Hasan cliffs, the wind moves dry sand across the limestone with a faint hiss. Behind you, far off, the green strip of the Nile valley shimmers in the heat. In front of you, the pale rock face opens into a row of square pillars and a shadowed doorway. The air near that opening feels cooler on your skin. This is the Speos Artemidos, a temple cut straight into the living mountain.

The name comes from the Greeks, who arrived in Egypt more than a thousand years after this temple was carved. Speos means cave. Artemidos means of Artemis, the Greek goddess of the hunt and of wild places. The Greeks looked at the goddess worshipped here and saw their own huntress in her. Local people use a very different name. They call this place Istabl Antar, the Stable of Antar, after a heroic warrior from Arabic folk poetry. Locals say the hero kept his horse here, but that is legend, not history.

The goddess of this valley was Pakhet. She was a lioness, a fierce hunter of the desert edges. Her name is often translated as she who scratches, or she who tears. Picture a lioness waiting at the mouth of a dry valley, watching the paths where animals came down to the water. That is the image the ancient Egyptians held of her. She guarded the frontier between the farmed land and the open desert, and this narrow valley was her home.

The temple belongs to the reign of Hatshepsut, one of the very few women to rule Egypt as pharaoh. She governed in the fifteenth century before Christ, during the period historians call the New Kingdom, when Egypt was rich and powerful. Hatshepsut built on a grand scale across the country. Her most famous monument is her terraced temple at Deir el-Bahari, near Thebes. This rock-cut sanctuary is much smaller, but it holds something her larger works do not.

Look at the facade above the pillars. Hatshepsut placed a long inscription here, and historians consider it one of the most important texts of her reign. In it, she speaks about restoring temples that had fallen into ruin. She blames the decay on the Hyksos, foreign rulers from western Asia who had controlled northern Egypt about a century before her time. She describes them ruling from their city of Avaris, in the Nile delta, and she presents herself as the ruler who repaired the damage.

This is the first surprise of the place. That inscription is more than a record. It is political messaging, carved in stone. The Hyksos had already been driven out before Hatshepsut was born. By reminding readers of that dark time, she cast herself as a restorer of order. For a woman on a throne usually held by men, that argument mattered. Historians still debate how much real damage the Hyksos caused, and how much is royal storytelling.

Now look at the pillars of the portico. They were cut from the same rock as the cliff, not built from separate blocks. Several are broken or damaged, and the gaps let the sunlight fall at sharp angles across the floor. Notice how the stonecutters worked. They started from the outside and dug inward, removing rock instead of adding it. Every surface you see was once inside the mountain, and the chisel marks show the slow labour of that work.

Step into the shade of the first hall. The temperature drops, and the light becomes soft and grey. This broad chamber runs across the width of the temple, behind the pillars. From here, a short passage leads deeper into the rock, toward a small sanctuary at the back. That inner room was meant to hold the image of Pakhet. Let your eyes adjust before you move forward. The details on the walls appear slowly.

Here is the second surprise. Much of the carved decoration inside is not by Hatshepsut at all. It belongs to Seti the First, a pharaoh of the nineteenth dynasty, who ruled more than a century later. After Hatshepsut's death, her successors removed her name from many monuments. Seti added his own reliefs and his own name here. So when you look at these walls, you are looking at layers of competing memory, with one ruler written over another.

In the reliefs, look for the familiar scenes of a king making offerings to the gods. Pakhet appears with the head of a lioness. Watch the way the carved figures face each other across the stone, king and goddess, in a quiet exchange. Some areas are crisp and sharp. Others are worn smooth or never finished. The sanctuary itself shows signs of incomplete work, as if the cutting stopped before the plan was complete.

The ground around the temple holds another story. The goddess here was a great cat, so cats were sacred in this valley. Over many centuries, people buried mummified cats nearby as offerings. In the late nineteenth century, local farmers uncovered a vast number of these mummies. Many were shipped abroad, and some were ground up to fertilise fields in Britain. It is a strange fate for animals that were once wrapped with such care.

The temple is also part of a larger landscape. A short distance north, the cliffs of Beni Hasan hold the rock-cut tombs of regional governors from the Middle Kingdom, centuries older than this sanctuary. Those tombs are famous for painted scenes of wrestlers, hunters and daily life along the river. Together, the tombs and this temple show how people kept returning to the same limestone hills. The rock here was easy to carve, and the high ground stayed safely above the yearly flood.

Before you leave, walk back out into the light and turn around. Look at the dark doorway and the line of pillars against the pale cliff. Somewhere above you is the voice of a queen, arguing her case to the future in carved stone. Listen again to the wind across the valley floor. A lioness goddess once watched this path. Now it is your footsteps on the sand, and the slow walk back toward the river.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning before the desert heat builds.
Combine with the Beni Hasan tombs to the north.
Bring a torch for the dim inner sanctuary.
Wear closed shoes for the sandy, uneven valley path.
Carry plenty of water; shade is scarce outside.
