The guide

The story

Stop for a moment and listen. Up here, the wind is the loudest thing on the hill. It moves through dry grass, across black basalt, and over stone foundations that once held palaces. Under your feet the rock is rough and dark, warm in the sun, slick in the monsoon. Far below, the valleys of the Sahyadri, the Western Ghats of Maharashtra, fold away in green and grey ridges. You are standing on Raigad, the hilltop capital of Chhatrapati Shivaji.

The first thing to understand is the shape of this place. Raigad sits on a wide, flat-topped hill with sheer cliffs on almost every side. Nature built most of the defences before any human laid a stone. Builders only had to close the weak points with walls, gates, and bastions. That is why the fort felt so secure. An army could stand at the foot of these cliffs for months and still find no easy way up.

Before it became Raigad, this hill was known as Rairi. It was held by the More family, local chiefs who controlled the area. In 1656, Shivaji took it from them. He was a Maratha leader who had spent years building power in these hills, fort by fort, against much larger states around him. He saw what this mountain could become. Over the following years, the fortifications were expanded, and Rairi became Raigad, which means royal fort.

The great moment came in 1674. Here, on this summit, Shivaji was crowned Chhatrapati, a title that means sovereign or emperor. The coronation was performed with full Vedic ritual, the ancient Hindu ceremonies of kingship. For the Marathas this was a declaration. It said that a kingdom born in these hills now stood as an equal to the empires on the plains. Raigad became the capital of that new state.

If you climbed on foot, you came up through the Maha Darwaja, the great main gate. Look again at how it is placed on your way down. The entrance sits between two tall bastions, and the path bends before it reaches the doorway. An attacker could not charge straight in. Anyone approaching would be exposed to defenders above for the whole final stretch. The design is simple, practical, and very effective.

Many visitors today ride the ropeway instead, and that is a good choice in heat or rain. But the old stone path, with well over a thousand steps, tells you something the cable car cannot. You feel how far this summit is from the world below. Every bag of grain, every block of stone, every messenger had to make that climb. The distance was the point. It kept the capital safe.

Now walk toward the heart of the fort, the royal court. At its centre is a statue of Shivaji seated beneath a canopy, placed where his throne once stood. Directly opposite is the Nagarkhana Darwaja, a tall gateway where drums once sounded for the court. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Locals say the hall was planned so that a voice at the throne carries clearly toward the gate. Try it quietly with a companion.

Around the court, look at the ground. You will see the low remains of walls, room outlines, and steps that lead nowhere now. These are the foundations of the palace complex, including the queens' quarters. The upper floors are long gone. What survives is a plan drawn in stone, and you have to fill in the rest with your imagination. Wooden beams, painted walls, and cloth canopies once rose above these lines.

Walk on and you reach a long street lined with raised stone platforms on both sides. This was the market, the bazaar of the capital. Traders sat on these platforms with their goods. Some say the platforms were built high enough that a rider on horseback could shop without dismounting. Whether that is true or not, stand in the middle of the street and look down its length. It is easy to picture the noise and the crowd.

Raigad also has its stories, and one is loved across Maharashtra. Legend has it that a milkmaid named Hirkani came up to sell milk and was trapped inside when the gates closed at sunset. Her baby was waiting at home in the village below. Desperate, she climbed down the cliff in the dark. The tale says Shivaji was so moved that he honoured her courage. A bastion called Hirkani Buruj carries her name today.

There is a darker edge to the fort as well. Takmak Tok is a sharp point of rock above a deep drop. Tradition holds that condemned prisoners were thrown from here. Stand back from the edge, especially in wind or rain, because there is little between you and the valley. On a clear day the view is enormous. On a misty monsoon morning, the cloud fills the drop completely, and the world seems to end at your feet.

Shivaji died here at Raigad in 1680. His samadhi, a memorial platform, stands near the Jagadishwar temple, a plain stone temple to Shiva. Close by is a small statue of a dog called Waghya. According to a popular tale, the dog leapt into his master's funeral fire. Historians debate the story, so treat it as legend. The capital did not stay safe for long. Mughal forces took the fort in 1689, and much later, in 1818, British guns badly damaged it.

So much of what you see is ruin, and that is part of its power. The roofs fell, the wood burned, the court went silent. Yet people still come up in large numbers, many of them from Maharashtra, some to pray, some to remember. You may see families bowing at the samadhi or schoolchildren reciting names they learned in class. For them this is not only a monument. It is the place where a kingdom was declared.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot along the edge and look out once more. Notice the ridges, the clouds, the wind pushing at your clothes. The same view greeted the guards on these walls centuries ago. As you walk back toward the gate or the ropeway, look down at the worn steps and the black rock. Many feet have passed this way. Today, yours are part of that long line too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the ropeway in heat or heavy monsoon rain.
Climb the stone steps early morning to avoid midday heat.
Wear shoes with firm grip for slick basalt paths.
Carry water; the summit is exposed with little shade.
Stay well back from the edge at Takmak Tok.
