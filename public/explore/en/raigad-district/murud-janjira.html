The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against black stone. A sail creaks overhead. As the small wooden boat leaves the jetty at Rajapuri, the fort ahead looks almost unreal, as if it grew out of the Arabian Sea. Its dark walls rise straight from the waves, with no beach and no shoreline. The salt wind is in your face. For a long moment, you cannot see any way in at all.

This is Murud-Janjira, a sea fort on a small rocky island just off the Konkan coast of Maharashtra, in western India. The name is a clue to its past. Murud is the nearby fishing town. Janjira is widely believed to come from the Arabic word jazira, which means island. So the name tells you two things. It tells you where the fort stands, and it hints at the people who held it for centuries.

Those people were the Siddis. Their ancestors came from East Africa, often from the region of Abyssinia, which is the old name for Ethiopia. Many Africans reached India across the Indian Ocean as soldiers, sailors, traders, and sometimes as enslaved people. Some rose to great power. The Siddis of Janjira first served the Ahmadnagar Sultanate, a kingdom of the Deccan plateau. Over time they became rulers in their own right, later known as the Nawabs of Janjira.

Now comes the first surprise. Look at the shoreline-facing wall as your boat comes closer. The main gate is there, but it stays hidden until you are almost beside it. The builders angled the entrance so that it blends into the walls. From a distance, an enemy fleet saw only solid stone. This simple trick of design made a direct assault far harder... and it still catches visitors off guard today.

How did the fort begin? Here, history and folklore mix. According to a popular local tale, the first fort on this rock was a wooden stockade built by Koli fishermen to protect themselves from pirates. Legend has it that a Siddi commander arrived posing as a merchant, with barrels that hid armed soldiers. The fishermen let him in, and the island changed hands. It is a vivid story, but treat it as legend, not documented record.

What is better established is that the stone fort you see grew over the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Its walls are built of large dark blocks of basalt, a volcanic rock common along this coast. Rounded bastions bulge outward at regular intervals. Each one gave defenders a wide field of fire across the water. The masonry is heavy and tight, and the sea has worn the lower courses smooth.

And here is the fact that makes Janjira famous across India. This fort was never taken by force. The Portuguese tried. The English tried. The Mughals had their own complicated dealings with the Siddis. The Maratha ruler Shivaji, who built a powerful kingdom in western India in the seventeenth century, also tried. So did his son Sambhaji. Every attempt failed against these walls and the open water around them.

The Maratha efforts were serious. To counter Janjira, Shivaji built another sea fort nearby, called Padmadurg or Kasa fort. You may be able to spot it on the water from here. Accounts also describe Sambhaji trying to build a stone causeway out toward the island, so his army could cross on foot. That plan, like the others, did not succeed. The sea and the Siddi defenders held firm.

Step through the gate, and look up before you go further. Above the entrance is a carved panel. It shows a large striped beast, like a tiger or a mythical lion, gripping several elephants in its claws. Many guides read it as a message of strength from the rulers inside. Whatever its exact meaning, it greeted every visitor and every envoy who came through this door.

Inside, the fort opens into a wide, uneven space of ruins, grass, and stone. You will pass the remains of a palace, mosque walls, and old living quarters. Roofs have fallen. Arches stand alone against the sky. Goats of stone are not here, but real crows and kites often are, circling above the bastions. The noise of the sea drops away a little. In its place you hear wind, footsteps, and other visitors calling to each other.

Here is the second surprise. In the middle of an island surrounded by salt water, there is a large tank of fresh water. A garrison under siege needs water above all else. This reservoir helped the defenders survive long blockades, when no boat could safely bring supplies. Stand at its edge for a moment. Its calm, green surface feels strange when the open sea is only a short walk away.

Now walk up onto the ramparts. Huge iron and bronze cannons still rest on several of the bastions. Some carry local names, such as Kalal Bangadi, and guides often point out these old guns with pride. Put your hand on the metal, warm from the sun. From up here you see the full circle of the defences. You see the coast, the fishing boats, and the line of hills behind Murud.

The Siddi rulers kept their small state for a very long time. After India became independent in 1947, the princely state of Janjira joined the new Indian union. The fort fell silent as a military post. Today it belongs to the visitors, the boatmen, and the sea. The boatmen still raise their sails much as sailors did here for centuries, carrying people across the same short stretch of water.

Before your boat returns, take one more look along the walls. Notice the waterline, where the dark stone meets the moving sea. Notice the gate, already fading back into the wall as you pull away. For hundreds of years, this rock held its ground against fleets and armies. Now it simply stands in the light, with the wind in your ears and the coast waiting ahead.

Before you go

Practical tips

Board the sailboats at Rajapuri jetty near Murud town.
Wear shoes with good grip for uneven stone ruins.
Carry water and a hat; there is little shade inside.
Check sea conditions, since boats may stop in monsoon.
Watch the boat timing so you do not miss your return.
