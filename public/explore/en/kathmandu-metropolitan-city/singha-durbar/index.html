The guide

The story

Stand for a moment and look through the railings. A long white façade runs across your view, row after row of columns, pale against the green of a wide lawn. The traffic of central Kathmandu hums behind you, with motorbikes, horns, and the call of a bus conductor. In front of you, everything is ordered and still. Two worlds touch here, the restless modern capital and a calm, deliberate European symmetry that seems to belong somewhere else entirely.

This is Singha Durbar. The name means, roughly, the Lion Palace. In Nepali, singha is a lion, and durbar means a royal court or palace. For more than a century, this compound has been one of the centres of power in Nepal. Today it holds government offices and ministries, so most visitors see it the way you are seeing it now... from the outside, through the gates, across the lawn. That view still tells a remarkable story.

To understand the building, you need to know about the Ranas. From the middle of the nineteenth century until 1951, Nepal was ruled not by its kings but by a family of hereditary prime ministers called the Rana dynasty. The kings remained on the throne, but the real authority sat with the Ranas. They held that power for about a hundred years, and they expressed it in architecture. Across the Kathmandu Valley, they built enormous palaces in a style that looked to Europe rather than to the valley's own traditions.

That taste for Europe has a clear starting point. The first Rana ruler, Jung Bahadur, travelled to Britain and France in 1850. It was a rare journey for a South Asian ruler at the time. He came home with new ideas about how power should look. Over the following decades, his family filled Kathmandu with stucco, tall columns, grand staircases, and imported chandeliers. Singha Durbar became the largest and most ambitious of all these Rana palaces.

The palace was completed in 1908 for the Rana prime minister Chandra Shamsher. Accounts often say it went up with astonishing speed, in less than a year, using a very large workforce. It is frequently described as having been the largest palace in Asia in its day. That claim is hard to measure, so treat it as a popular description rather than a precise record. Even so, the original complex was vast, with many courtyards and a very large number of rooms.

Look closely at the style. This is neoclassical architecture, which means it borrows from the temples of ancient Greece and Rome. You can see this in the columns, the triangular pediments, the balanced windows, and the strong horizontal lines. Now compare it in your mind with the old temples of Kathmandu, with their carved wooden struts, brick walls, and tiered roofs. Here there is almost no wood carving and no pagoda roof. The message was modern, international, and powerful.

The palace was not only an office. It was a private world. The Rana rulers lived here with their households, and they received guests in grand halls lined with mirrors and portraits. Ordinary Nepalis had little reason to come close. For many people in the valley, a building like this stood for a distant elite that lived very differently from everyone else. That gap between rulers and ruled helps explain what happened next.

In 1951, Rana rule came to an end. Political movements and pressure from the king brought the old system down, and the first steps toward a new political order began. Singha Durbar changed its role. It stopped being a family palace and became the seat of government administration. Ministries moved into its rooms. The halls built for one ruling family now served the offices of a state. It is still, in a sense, the address of Nepal's government.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The building you see is only part of the original palace. In 1973, a great fire swept through the complex and destroyed much of it. The front section, with its famous white columns, was restored. Much of what stood behind it was lost, and newer government buildings now fill parts of the compound. So the grand face you are looking at is partly a survivor and partly a reconstruction.

The twenty-first century brought more trials. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake, which caused great loss of life across Nepal, also damaged parts of Singha Durbar. Then, in September 2025, during large youth-led protests in Kathmandu, parts of the complex were set on fire. These events are recent and painful for many Nepalis. You may still see signs of repair, scaffolding, or heightened security around the compound. If you do, you are looking at history that is still being written.

Now take in the setting itself. The wide lawn and the long approach are deliberate. They create distance, so the façade appears slowly and with ceremony. Notice the fountain and the formal lines of the grounds. In the soft light of early morning or late afternoon, the white surface turns warm and golden. At midday it can look almost too bright to study. The building was designed to be seen from exactly this kind of distance.

Around you, the neighbourhood carries the weight of the compound. Government workers pass through the gates. Security staff stand at the entrances. Nearby, Maitighar Mandala is a well-known gathering point where people often meet for demonstrations and public events. Tea stalls and small shops serve the daily flow of office workers. In this part of the city, politics is not an abstract idea. It is part of the street, the noise, and the conversations at the corner.

As you stand here, remember that photography rules near government buildings can be strict. Respect any instructions from the guards, and keep a polite distance from the gates. You do not need to go inside to understand this place. The view through the railings already shows you a century of change. A family palace became a public office. Fire, earthquake, and protest have all left their mark. Each time, the white columns have been rebuilt.

So take one more look before you go. The columns, the lawn, and the long white line of the façade still hold their calm order against the movement of the city. Listen to the traffic again, and notice how quickly the modern capital closes in around you as you step away. That contrast is the real lesson of the Lion Palace. Power in Kathmandu has always tried to look permanent... and the city around it has never stopped moving.
