The guide

The story

Start at the base and tilt your head back. A pale, slender column rises above the rooftops of central Kathmandu, white against the haze. Around you, horns sound from the ring of traffic, motorbikes thread between buses, and the smell of exhaust mixes with frying dough from a nearby stall. Underfoot, the paving is new and smooth. That smoothness is a clue. Almost everything around you was rebuilt in the last few years, and the story of how that happened is the real story of this place.

This is Dharahara, also known as Bhimsen Stambha, which means Bhimsen's Tower. For generations it was the tallest structure in the Kathmandu Valley. It was a lookout, a landmark, and a place where families came on holidays to climb the narrow stairs and look out over the city. Many people in Nepal can tell you exactly where they were when they first saw the view from the top. And many can tell you where they were when it fell.

The tower takes its name from Bhimsen Thapa, one of the most powerful figures in Nepal's early nineteenth century. He served as the country's chief minister, or Mukhtiyar, for about three decades. He built up the army, reshaped the capital, and left his mark on its skyline. The original tower went up in the 1830s. At that time, Kathmandu was a city of brick temples, carved wooden windows, and pagoda roofs. A tall white column was something entirely new.

Here is the first surprise. Dharahara was not originally alone. Bhimsen Thapa built two towers, and the first is associated with Queen Lalit Tripurasundari, who held power as regent in those years. The pair stood as a statement of authority. Then, in 1834, a major earthquake struck the valley. One of the two towers collapsed and was never rebuilt. The survivor became the Dharahara that later generations knew. Earthquakes, you will notice, run through this story like a thread.

Look at the shape of the column. It does not resemble the temples of Durbar Square, just a short walk away. Instead, it borrows from outside traditions. Historians often describe its style as a blend of Mughal and European influences — the Mughals being the Muslim dynasty that ruled much of northern India for centuries. In Bhimsen Thapa's time, ambitious rulers across South Asia used architecture like this to show they were modern and connected to the wider world. The tower was a political message in plaster and brick.

The tower also had a practical job. It served the military. According to accounts of the period, bugles were sounded from the top to summon soldiers, and the parade ground of Tundikhel lies close by to the east. A high platform in a flat valley is useful. From up there you could watch the roads, see smoke, and signal across a wide distance. So the tower was both a monument to one man's power and a working tool of the state he served.

The next blow came in 1934. A great earthquake shook Nepal and northern India, and Dharahara was badly damaged. It was restored afterwards under the Rana rulers, the family of hereditary prime ministers who controlled Nepal for about a century. The restored tower stood for the rest of the twentieth century. It saw the end of Rana rule, the arrival of democracy, the growth of Kathmandu from a valley town into a crowded capital. It became part of everyday life.

Then came the morning of 25 April 2015. It was a Saturday, a day when the tower was open and visitors were climbing it. At just before noon, the Gorkha earthquake struck central Nepal. Dharahara broke apart and collapsed, leaving only a short stump of its base. Many people inside and around it were killed. Across the country, thousands of lives were lost and whole villages were flattened. For many Nepalis, the image of the broken tower became the image of that day.

Now turn and find that stump. This is the second thing many visitors walk past without noticing. The remaining base of the old tower was preserved, not cleared away. It stands near the new column as a memorial. Pause beside it for a moment. The old brick and the new concrete stand within sight of each other. One records the city that was lost. The other records the decision to build again in the same place.

Reconstruction began a few years after the quake, and the new Dharahara opened in 2021, on the Nepali New Year. It is taller than the original and was designed with modern engineering to resist earthquakes. The government presented it as a symbol of recovery. Not everyone agreed with the project. Some people questioned the cost, others the design, and others felt it should look more like the old tower. That debate is itself part of the monument's meaning.

The site around the tower changed too. The area is now a larger public complex, with open plaza space, gardens, and facilities below ground. Look at how people use it. Students sit on the steps with their phones. Families pose for photographs with the column behind them. Office workers cut through on their way somewhere else. A tower that once stood as a minister's private statement is now, above all, a public square for a busy city.

Walk to the edge of the complex and listen. Just nearby is Sundhara, a neighbourhood named after an old stone water spout. Kathmandu has many of these sunken spouts, called dhunge dhara, built centuries ago to bring water from underground channels. Around here you will find bus stops, money changers, and small shops selling everything from phone cards to tea. New Road and the old core of the city are a short walk away. The tower stands at the meeting point of old Kathmandu and the newer, faster one.

If you go up, take your time on the viewing level. On a clear day, especially after rain or in the cooler months, the haze can lift. Then you may see the green rim of hills that encloses the valley, and sometimes the white line of the Himalaya far beyond. Below, the city spreads out in brick, concrete, and water tanks on rooftops. Try to pick out a temple roof among the newer buildings. It helps you understand how the valley has grown.

Before you leave, stand once more at the foot of the column and look up. The white surface, the traffic noise, the low brick stump nearby — together they hold almost two centuries of history. A chief minister built it. Earthquakes broke it twice and then brought it down. The city rebuilt it anyway. As you walk back into the streets, notice how many other buildings around you carry fresh concrete or new brick. Dharahara is the tallest of them. It is not the only one.
