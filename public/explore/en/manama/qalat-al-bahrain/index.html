The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind comes off the Gulf and moves through the date palms behind you. Under your shoes, the path crunches with pale sand and broken stone. Ahead, a fort of rough, honey-coloured limestone sits on a low hill. The walls are thick and plain, with round towers at the corners. At first glance it looks like one building. But the hill under it matters even more than the fort on top.

This is Qal'at al-Bahrain, the Bahrain Fort, on the northern coast of the island, a short drive west of central Manama. The hill is not natural. Archaeologists call this kind of mound a tell. A tell forms when people live in the same place for thousands of years. They build, abandon, rebuild, and level the ground again, and each layer sits on top of the last. Here, those layers reach back more than four thousand years.

Here is the first surprise. The fort you see is the youngest thing on this hill. Its stone walls date mostly from the sixteenth century, when Portuguese forces controlled Bahrain. Portugal took the island in 1521, as it tried to control trade routes across the Gulf and the Indian Ocean. Underneath that fort lie the remains of towns, temples, and markets that were already ancient when the Portuguese arrived.

The deepest layers reach back to around 2300 BC. At that time, this coast belonged to a civilisation called Dilmun. You may not know the name, but the merchants of ancient Mesopotamia knew it well. Mesopotamia is the old land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, in today's Iraq. Its clay tablets describe Dilmun as a trading partner. Boats carried copper, timber, and other goods through these waters, and this harbour was one of the places where they stopped.

Why here? The answer is under the ground and under the sea. Bahrain has fresh water springs, some of them bubbling up even offshore. In a hot, dry region, that water made this island valuable. Ships could take on water, food, and dates here. Traders could exchange goods from the north with goods from the east. A port city grew, and over many centuries it became the capital of Dilmun.

The island's water and green palms also fed a famous story. In one Sumerian myth, Dilmun is a pure and blessed land, a place of sweet water and no sickness. The Sumerians were among the earliest peoples of Mesopotamia. Many writers link that mythical Dilmun with Bahrain. Remember, though, that this is legend, not a historical record. Still, standing among the palms after the bare desert, the idea makes some sense.

Modern knowledge of this hill began in the 1950s. A Danish expedition started digging here in 1954, and Danish teams returned for many seasons. Later, French and Bahraini archaeologists continued the work. They cut deep trenches into the tell and found streets, houses, and large public buildings. Only part of the mound has been excavated. Much of the ancient city is still sealed under the earth you are walking on.

The second surprise is small and strange. In one later level of the site, the excavators found clay bowls buried under house floors. Some bowls held the bones of snakes, and sometimes a single bead. Nobody knows exactly why people did this. It may have been an offering, or a ritual of protection. You can see examples of these bowls in the site museum, and they are worth finding.

After Dilmun, other powers came and went. Greek writers later called the island Tylos, in the centuries after Alexander the Great. Then came Islamic periods, with their own fortifications near the shore. Each era left walls, pottery, and coins in the soil. Because of this long sequence, UNESCO added the site to its World Heritage List in 2005, as the ancient harbour and capital of Dilmun.

Now walk up into the fort itself. Notice the thickness of the walls and the narrow openings for defence. Inside, the courtyards are open to the sky. In places, you can look down into excavated areas where older walls appear below newer ones. The stones change in size and shape from layer to layer. That change is the timeline of the site, written in building materials instead of words.

From the walls, look out toward the sea. On a clear day you can see the water stretching flat and grey-blue to the horizon. Below you, a channel and a small sea tower are linked to the shore. Look inland, too. The palm groves and nearby villages show how people still live close to this coast. The modern skyline of Manama rises in the distance, behind the green.

Take time for the site museum near the entrance. It is a long, low, modern building that faces the fort. Inside, the displays follow the layers of the tell, from Dilmun to the Islamic periods. You will see pottery, seals, tools, and those snake bowls. Visiting the museum first gives you a map in your head. Then the walls outside become easier to read.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and look back at the hill. The wind is still moving through the palms. The limestone is changing colour as the light shifts. Under your feet lie four thousand years of kitchens, streets, and harbour work, one layer on top of another. As you walk back to the road, look at the ground beside the path. A broken shard of pottery may still be resting in the sand.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the site museum before walking up to the fort.
Come in late afternoon when heat and glare are softer.
Bring water, a hat, and sunscreen for open ground.
Wear closed shoes for uneven stone and sandy paths.
Walk the seaward walls for views along the coast.
