The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the mound and look up. The keep rises in pale grey stone, rough and speckled, against a wide Suffolk sky. A sea breeze carries salt and the cry of gulls from the marshes. The grass under your feet slopes in ridges and hollows. Those bumps are not random. They are the buried outline of walls that once ringed this hill. Only the great tower still stands, and it looks almost too tall for the quiet village around it.

Welcome to Orford Castle. It sits a short drive south of Aldeburgh, in the small village of Orford, on a stretch of coast where land and water still shift. The castle was built in the second half of the twelfth century by King Henry the Second. He was one of the most energetic rulers England ever had. Here, he wanted to make a point, and he made it in stone.

The point was aimed at a rival. In the 1160s, much of East Anglia sat under the influence of Hugh Bigod, the Earl of Norfolk. Bigod held strong castles of his own at Framlingham and Bungay, not far inland. Henry had no royal fortress in this corner of Suffolk. So he built one at Orford, on the coast, where the king's power could watch the sea and the roads together. This tower was a message: the crown had arrived.

Here is something most visitors never hear. Orford is one of the best documented castles of its age in England. The royal treasury kept yearly accounts called the Pipe Rolls, named for the rolled-up parchment they were written on. Those rolls record money spent on building Orford, year after year. Historians can follow the progress of the work through the royal accounts. For a building more than eight hundred years old, that is a rare and precious kind of paper trail.

Now look closely at the shape of the keep. Most English castles of this period have square towers. This one is different. From the outside, it is a many-sided polygon, almost round, with three tall rectangular turrets set against it like buttresses. Inside, the main rooms are circular. Builders were experimenting at this time, and Orford is one of their boldest results. Walk slowly around the base, and watch the walls catch the light differently on every face.

The stone itself tells a local story. Much of the wall is built from septaria, a hard, brownish stone formed in clay along this coast. That is why the surface looks lumpy and mottled. Around the corners and windows, the masons used smoother, finer limestone brought in from farther away. Some of it came from across the Channel, from Caen in Normandy. Look for that contrast: rough local stone for the bulk, polished imported stone for the edges.

Soon after it was finished, the castle faced its first real test. In 1173, a great revolt broke out against Henry the Second. His own sons joined it, and so did Hugh Bigod. Rebel forces and hired soldiers moved through East Anglia. Orford was held for the king through this dangerous period. The revolt failed, and Bigod lost much of his power. In a sense, the tower did exactly the job it was built to do.

When you go inside, notice how the rooms stack on top of each other. The keep was not only a military post. It was a place to live, to cook, to pray, and to receive guests. Small chambers, passages and stairs are tucked into the thickness of the walls and turrets. There is a chapel, a kitchen area, and spaces that once held beds. Climbing the stairs feels like moving through the layers of a stone honeycomb.

And now a stranger tale. A medieval chronicler, Ralph of Coggeshall, wrote about a wild man caught in the nets of Orford fishermen. According to the story, he was covered in hair, would not speak, and ate raw fish. He was held in the castle for a time. Then, the tale says, he slipped back into the sea and was gone. Nobody knows what lies behind this legend. Locals still call him the Wild Man of Orford.

The coast itself has changed more than the tower. In the Middle Ages, Orford was a working port with ships coming and going. Over the centuries, a long ridge of shingle called Orford Ness grew southwards along the shore. It slowly pushed the open sea away from the village. Trade moved elsewhere, and Orford became quieter. The castle, built partly to guard the harbour, ended up watching over marshes and a river instead.

If you climb to the roof, you will understand why the king chose this hill. The view opens in every direction. To the east lies Orford Ness, a strip of grey and green between river and sea. In the twentieth century, that spit was used for secret military research, and some strange concrete buildings still stand there. To the west, the fields and woods of Suffolk roll away. On a clear day, the horizon feels very far off.

As you walk back down, look again at the ground around the keep. The ditches and ridges mark where the outer curtain wall and its towers once stood. Over time, people took that stone away for other buildings, and the walls disappeared. Only the keep survived in such good shape. That makes Orford feel lonely now, but it also lets you see the tower's bold design clearly, with nothing standing in the way.

Before you leave, pause on the mound for a moment. Listen to the wind moving over the grass, and the gulls over the river. Look at the rough, speckled stone that Henry's masons laid by hand. As you walk down into the village, glance back once more. The tower still rises over the rooftops, just as it did for the fishermen, the rebels, and perhaps even the wild man from the sea.
