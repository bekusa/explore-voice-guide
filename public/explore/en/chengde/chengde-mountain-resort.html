The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the gate, water moves slowly over stones. Pine needles shift in the wind. The air here is cooler and drier than on the plains far to the south, and that one detail explains why this place exists at all. You are standing at the edge of the largest imperial garden that still survives in China, a summer world built for the emperors of the Qing dynasty.

The Chinese name is Bishu Shanzhuang. It means, roughly, the Mountain Villa for Escaping the Heat. The Qing rulers were Manchus, a people from the cold northeast, and Beijing summers were hard on them. So in 1703, the Kangxi Emperor began to build a retreat here, in the hills north of the Great Wall. Work continued for most of the eighteenth century. It was finally completed in 1792, under his grandson, the Qianlong Emperor.

Think about that span for a moment. Nearly ninety years of building, across the reigns of three emperors. This was never a single design drawn on one sheet of paper. It grew slowly, hall by hall, pavilion by pavilion. A long wall now wraps around the whole estate, rising and falling over the ridges for roughly ten kilometres. Inside it lie lakes, meadows, forests and steep valleys — a whole landscape held behind one boundary.

The resort was not only a holiday home. It was a working seat of power. For months each year, the emperor governed the empire from here. Officials travelled up from Beijing. Envoys arrived from Mongolia, Tibet and Central Asia. Chengde sat close to the frontier lands, and receiving those leaders here, in a setting that felt partly like the steppe, was a quiet piece of diplomacy.

Start with the palace zone near the main gate. Compared with the Forbidden City, it feels restrained. The roofs are grey tile, not golden glaze. The timber is often left in its natural brown, without bright paint. The main hall is built from nanmu, a fragrant and costly hardwood from southern China. On a warm day, some visitors say they can still catch a faint scent from the old beams. The plainness was deliberate. It sent a message of modesty and frugality.

Now walk north, and the formal courtyards give way to water. This is the lake zone, and it holds a small surprise. Much of it copies the gardens of the south — the region the Chinese call Jiangnan, the lands below the Yangtze River. Kangxi and Qianlong both made long tours of the south. They admired its famous gardens and towers. Then they had versions of them rebuilt here, in the northern hills, so the south could come to them.

Look out for the tower standing on a small island, a building known as the Misty Rain Tower. It was modelled on a tower in the southern city of Jiaxing. Elsewhere, a hill with a temple and a pagoda echoes a famous site in Zhenjiang, on the Yangtze. Walking these shores, you are moving through a kind of three-dimensional travel diary. Each corner records a place an emperor once saw and wanted to keep.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. The old name of this area was Rehe, which means hot river. A spring inside the resort feeds the lake, and its water is warm enough that a patch near it is said to stay open even in winter. Rehe was also how many foreign visitors knew Chengde — older European books often call it Jehol. The name points back to this one small flow of warm water.

To the north of the lakes, the land opens into a wide grassy plain. Here the mood changes completely. This part was meant to recall the Mongolian steppe. Emperors set up large felt tents, like yurts, and held banquets, wrestling matches and horse displays. In 1793, the Qianlong Emperor received a British embassy led by Lord Macartney during his stay at Chengde. Britain hoped to open trade. The emperor declined, and the meeting became a famous moment of misunderstanding between two worlds.

Near the plain, look for a library pavilion beside a small pond. It once held one of the few complete copies of the Siku Quanshu, a vast imperial collection of Chinese books gathered under Qianlong. The building was modelled on a famous private library in the southern city of Ningbo. Libraries feared fire above all things, so water was placed close by. That simple pond was, in its way, insurance.

Beyond the plain, the land rises steeply. The mountain zone covers by far the largest share of the resort, and it is the wildest part. Paths climb through pine and oak. Small pavilions sit on ridges where emperors once paused for the view. Kangxi gave thirty-six scenes in the resort poetic four-character names. Qianlong later added thirty-six more. Together they became the seventy-two famous views, many still marked today.

The resort also saw darker days. In 1820, the Jiaqing Emperor died here. Forty years later, the Xianfeng Emperor fled to Chengde as British and French troops marched on Beijing during the Second Opium War. He died here in 1861. The power struggle that followed helped raise a young consort to power — the woman later known as the Empress Dowager Cixi. After that, the Qing court largely stopped coming, and the gardens slowly fell quiet.

Today, the resort is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 1994 together with the temples that ring it on the surrounding hills. As you walk, lift your eyes now and then. On the slopes beyond the wall you may glimpse their roofs and towers. Some were built in Tibetan style to welcome visiting lamas and Mongol princes. The garden and the temples were always meant to be seen together.

So take a last look at whatever is in front of you right now — a ripple on the lake, a grey roof edge, a pine leaning over a path. Emperors stood near this spot and felt the same cool air. As you walk on, listen again for the water. It has been running through this valley far longer than any palace stood beside it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for the hilly mountain zone paths.
Consider a boat or electric cart to cover the lakes.
Come early morning for cooler air and quieter shores.
Pair your visit with the outer temples on nearby hills.
Bring water and layers for changing mountain weather.
