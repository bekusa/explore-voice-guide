The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across a low terrace of pale sand and broken stone. Behind you, cliffs of soft red and cream sandstone rise in rounded folds. Underfoot the ground feels loose and gritty. At first glance there is very little here, just low walls, curved outlines and scattered rubble. But you are standing in one of the oldest villages in this region. People lived on this spot long before the Nabataeans carved Petra, and thousands of years before the first pyramids.

Beidha lies a few kilometres north of Petra, close to the narrow canyon known as Little Petra. Many visitors pass this way on the road between the two. Most of them look up at the carved facades and never notice the quiet terrace below. That is the first surprise of this place. The most important story here is not carved high in the rock. It sits at knee height, in the foundations of houses built by some of the region's early farmers.

Archaeologists date the main village to the Neolithic period. The word simply means New Stone Age. It is the era when people in this part of the world began to settle in one place, grow crops and keep animals. The best-known phase at Beidha belongs to what specialists call the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. The name is literal. These villagers built houses and farmed, but they did not yet make clay pots. Their containers were baskets, stone bowls and perhaps leather bags.

The site became known through the work of the British archaeologist Diana Kirkbride. She excavated here over several seasons, starting in the late 1950s. Her team worked carefully through layer after layer of collapsed walls and ash. What they found was not one village but several, built one on top of another over many generations. Later researchers returned to her records and studied the finds again. Their work continues to shape how archaeologists understand early village life in the southern Levant, the lands along the eastern Mediterranean.

Look closely at the shapes of the walls. In the older levels, the houses are round or oval. They often sit close together, like cells in a honeycomb. Some appear to have been dug slightly into the ground, with stone walls and a roof of timber and brush. Imagine a family inside one of them on a winter night. A small fire burns. Smoke escapes through the roof. The sandstone walls hold some warmth after the sun goes down.

Now look for the later buildings. Here the plan changes. The walls run straight and meet at corners. Rooms become rectangular. This shift from round to square houses appears at many early farming sites in the Middle East. It probably reflects changes in how families lived, stored food and divided space. At Beidha you can see both forms in one place. That is the second surprise. A major turn in human building history is visible here in a few steps.

Some of the later structures are known as corridor buildings. They have a central passage with small rooms on either side. Archaeologists think the small rooms on the lower floor were used for storage and work. Living space may have been on an upper floor, now lost. Excavators found tools, beads and worked bone in some of these rooms. The picture is of a busy household, with people grinding grain, shaping stone blades and making small ornaments.

What did these people eat? Evidence from the site suggests they gathered and cultivated cereals such as barley and early wheat. They collected wild plants, including pistachio and other nuts from the hills. Animal bones show that goats played an important role. Hunting remained part of life too, with wild animals taken from the surrounding landscape. So Beidha sits at a turning point. It shows a world where farming and herding were growing, but the old skills of hunting and gathering had not disappeared.

There is also evidence of an even earlier presence. Before the farming village, groups of hunter-gatherers known as the Natufians used this area. The Natufian culture is older than farming in this region. Its people were among the first to stay in favoured places for long periods. Traces of their camps lie beneath the Neolithic levels. So this terrace carries several chapters of human life, one layer resting on another, like pages in a closed book.

Near the excavated remains, you may see reconstructed buildings. These were built in recent years to show how the houses may have looked when complete. They use stone, timber and mud in the manner suggested by the excavation. Step inside one if it is open. Notice how small it feels, how low the light is and how quiet the air becomes. A reconstruction is an informed guess, not a photograph of the past. Still, it helps the eye make sense of the low ruins outside.

Now turn and look at the landscape around you. The cliffs give shelter from wind. The wadi, a seasonal valley, brings water after rain and holds soil for crops. The hills once supported more trees and shrubs than you see today. Early villagers chose this place for good reasons. Stone for building lay all around. Water, food and fuel were within walking distance. The same features later drew Nabataean farmers and traders to the area, many thousands of years afterwards.

The contrast with nearby Little Petra is striking. There, carved facades, stairways and cisterns belong to the Nabataeans, the Arab people who built Petra. Here, the walls are rough fieldstone set by hand. Yet both places answer the same question. How do people make a home in this dry, beautiful, demanding landscape? The answer at Beidha is older, simpler and in some ways more personal. These walls were not made to impress. They were made to keep a family warm and dry.

Before you leave, take one more slow look across the terrace. Follow a curved wall with your eyes until it meets a straight one. Notice the colour of the stone, warm in the sun and grey in shadow. Listen to the wind and, perhaps, the bells of goats somewhere in the hills. People and goats have shared this valley for a very long time. As you walk back towards the road, the ground under your shoes is the same ground those early villagers crossed every day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with nearby Little Petra the same day.
Come early or late to avoid harsh midday sun.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose sand and uneven stone.
Carry water because shade on the terrace is limited.
Stay on paths to protect the fragile low walls.
