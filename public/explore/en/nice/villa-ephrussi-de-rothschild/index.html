The guide

The story

Listen first. Water falls into a long basin, the gravel crunches under your shoes, and somewhere below the terraces the sea moves against the rocks. The air smells of pine, warm stone, and cut grass. In front of you stands a house painted the soft pink of a seashell. It sits on a narrow ridge, with water on almost every side.

You are on Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat, a small peninsula a short drive east of Nice. This is the Villa Ephrussi de Rothschild. The house and its gardens were built in the early twentieth century, roughly between 1905 and 1912. Today they belong to the Académie des Beaux-Arts, a French institution for the fine arts. But for a few short years, the whole place belonged to one woman and her very particular imagination.

Her name was Béatrice de Rothschild. She was the daughter of Alphonse de Rothschild, a leading figure in the French branch of the banking family. She married Maurice Ephrussi, a banker from another wealthy family. The marriage was not a happy one, and the couple eventually separated. When Béatrice came to the Riviera, she arrived with a large fortune, strong opinions, and a deep love of eighteenth-century French art.

The land she chose was not easy. The ridge here was rough and rocky. Before building could start, workers had to level the top of the hill, and explosives were used to clear the rock. Look around at the flat, wide terraces and try to picture the bare hill that stood here before. Everything that looks natural in this garden was planned, moved, or carried in by hand.

Béatrice was a famously demanding client. Accounts from the period describe architects coming and going, and plans being drawn, rejected, and drawn again. One popular story says she had full-size mock-ups built so she could judge the effect before approving anything. Whether every detail of that tale is true, it fits what we see — a house where nothing feels accidental. The pink walls, the arched loggias, the Venetian-style details all follow one person's taste.

Here is the first surprise. The whole estate was designed to feel like a ship. Béatrice loved the sea, and the house was named Villa Île-de-France, after an ocean liner she admired. Stand at the far end of the main garden and look back. The long, narrow shape of the grounds, with water on both sides, feels like the deck of a vessel. Legend has it that the gardeners even wore sailor-style uniforms. Locals still tell that story with a smile.

Now turn to the garden directly in front of the house. This is the French garden, the largest of the themed gardens here. A long central pool runs down the middle, lined with lawns and clipped hedges. At regular intervals, the fountains in the pool rise and fall to music. Many visitors walk past without noticing the schedule, so it is worth lingering. At the far end, on a small rise, sits a round temple with columns. It is modelled on the Temple of Love at Versailles.

Beyond the French garden, the estate opens into a sequence of smaller gardens, each with its own character. There is a Spanish garden with shade and water. There is a Florentine garden, inspired by Italian Renaissance design, which means formal shapes and careful symmetry. There is a Japanese garden with stone lanterns and small bridges, an exotic garden full of cactus and succulents, and a stone garden filled with carved fragments. A rose garden and a Provençal garden complete the walk.

The stone garden holds the second surprise. Look closely at the carvings tucked among the plants. Many are old architectural pieces — capitals, reliefs, gargoyles — gathered from different places and periods. Béatrice collected them the way others collect paintings. They sit half-hidden in the greenery, and many people walk straight past. Take a moment here. Some of these stones are much older than the villa itself.

Inside, the house feels less like a home and more like a private museum. Béatrice filled the rooms with the art she loved most. You will find fine French furniture, tapestries, paintings, and delicate porcelain from the royal factories at Vincennes and Sèvres, two famous French porcelain makers. The rooms are arranged around a central covered patio with columns, which brings soft light into the heart of the house. It is calm, quiet, and full of detail.

Despite all this effort, Béatrice did not spend many years here. She lived elsewhere for much of her life and travelled often. When she died in 1934, she left the villa and its collections to the Académie des Beaux-Arts. Her wish was that the place should stay together and be shared. That is why the rooms and gardens still look so much the way she arranged them.

Take a moment now on one of the terraces with a view. On one side you can see the bay of Villefranche, deep and sheltered, often with boats at anchor. On the other side lies the coast toward Beaulieu. The light here changes quickly. In the morning the water is pale and silver. Later in the day it turns a deep blue, and the pink walls of the villa grow warmer.

So before you leave, stand still for a few breaths. Feel the gravel under your feet, the sun on the stone, the breeze coming off the sea. Notice the sound of the fountains behind you. A woman once shaped this hilltop into a ship of gardens, and then let it go. As you walk back toward the gate, look over your shoulder once more — and watch the pink house settle into the green.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wait in the French garden to catch the musical fountains.
Walk the full garden loop before touring the house.
Bring a hat; many terraces have little shade.
Visit later in the day for warmer light on the walls.
Allow extra time for the quieter Japanese and stone gardens.
