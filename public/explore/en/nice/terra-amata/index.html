The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along the boulevard. A scooter whines past. Somewhere behind you, boat rigging taps against masts in the old port. This is an ordinary corner of Nice, with apartment balconies, parked cars and the smell of exhaust mixed with sea salt. Yet beneath these buildings lies one of the oldest known traces of human life in Europe. People camped right here, on what was then a beach, long before anyone could have imagined a city.

The place is called Terra Amata. In Italian, the words mean "beloved earth". That name suits it. This patch of ground on the lower slopes of Mont Boron, the green hill that rises east of the harbour, held its secret for an enormous span of time. Most people walk past without a second glance. The entrance to the museum sits at street level, at the foot of a residential building. It is easy to miss, and that is part of its charm.

The story of its discovery begins in 1966. Builders were digging foundations for new apartments when they uncovered ancient stone tools in the earth. Work paused. A rescue excavation began, led by the French prehistorian Henry de Lumley. His team worked quickly, over a period of months, while the construction project waited. They carefully exposed layer after layer of sand and pebbles. What they found changed how many researchers thought about early life on the Mediterranean coast.

The evidence pointed to visits by early humans roughly 400,000 years ago. Pause on that number for a moment. That is long before our own species, Homo sapiens, appeared. The people who came here belonged to an older human group. Scientists often describe them as Homo erectus or as early Homo heidelbergensis, a species that may be ancestral to the Neanderthals. They walked upright, made tools and lived by hunting and gathering. They did not know metal, farming or writing.

The coastline looked very different then. The sea level was higher, so the shore reached this spot. Imagine a beach of sand and rounded pebbles, with dunes behind it and a small stream nearby. The hills above were wooded. Animals moved through the landscape that now seem exotic for southern France. Bones found at the site come from straight-tusked elephants, rhinoceros, red deer, wild boar and wild cattle. These hunters clearly knew how to find food here.

Now for the first surprise. De Lumley interpreted certain patterns in the ground as the remains of shelters. He saw oval outlines marked by stones and post holes. In his reading, these were huts made of branches, perhaps leaning together and weighed down with rocks. If correct, they rank among the oldest built dwellings known anywhere. Other archaeologists have questioned this interpretation. Some think the patterns may have formed naturally. The debate continues, and the museum presents the hut idea as the excavator's reconstruction.

The second surprise involves fire. Inside some of these areas, the excavators reported small hearths, patches of burnt earth and blackened stones. Some hearths appeared sheltered by a low line of pebbles, perhaps to protect the flame from the sea wind. Controlled use of fire is a major step in human history. It brings warmth, cooked food and a reason to gather at night. Here too, specialists disagree about how strong the evidence is. Still, the idea of a small fire on this beach is hard to forget.

The stone tools tell a clearer story. The people at Terra Amata shaped beach pebbles into choppers, scrapers and pointed hand axes. These tools belong to the Acheulean tradition, a long-lasting toolmaking culture. Its signature object is the bifacial hand axe, a stone worked on both sides into a teardrop shape. The raw material came from the beach itself. Picture someone kneeling on the sand, striking one pebble against another, and the sharp crack of stone ringing out across the water.

There is also something more personal. During the excavation, researchers identified what appeared to be a human footprint pressed into the ancient sediment. A footprint is different from a tool or a bone. It records one single moment, one step taken by one individual. Standing above this spot, you are walking on ground that someone else crossed hundreds of thousands of years ago. The scale of time is difficult to grasp, but the gesture is completely familiar.

The museum itself is compact and quiet. It does not try to dazzle. Instead, it guides you through the evidence with displays of tools, animal bones and explanatory panels. A reconstruction shows what one of the proposed huts may have looked like, with its frame of branches and its central hearth. The museum has also displayed casts taken from the excavated ground surface. These let you see the original arrangement of stones and objects, preserved as the archaeologists found them.

It is worth thinking about how close this site came to vanishing. Without the alertness of the builders and the speed of the excavation team, the layers could have disappeared under concrete. The apartments were eventually built, and the museum now occupies space at their base. Daily life continues above the evidence. Residents hang laundry, children walk to school, and delivery vans double-park outside. Very few cities place their deepest past in such an everyday setting.

When you step back outside, try to see the street with new eyes. Look towards the sea, just a short walk away. Look up at Mont Boron and its pines. The light here is bright and clear, especially in the morning. The hunters who camped on this shore saw the same sun rise over the same horizon. The landscape has shifted, the sea has moved and the animals are gone, but the basic shape of the coast is still recognisable.

So take a slow breath of the salty air. Listen again to the traffic, the gulls and the boats in the harbour. Notice the ordinary pavement under your feet. Somewhere below it, the sand of an ancient beach still holds the marks of people who sat beside a fire and watched the same sea. As you walk away, carry that thought with you. In Nice, the very first chapter of the story begins on a quiet residential street.
