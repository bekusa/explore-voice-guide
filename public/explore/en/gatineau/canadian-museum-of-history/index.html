The guide

The story

Stand on the riverside terrace for a moment and run your hand along the stone. It is cool, pale, and slightly rough. Look closely and you will see darker swirls and blotches running through it. Those marks are not stains. They are the traces of ancient sea creatures, pressed into limestone long before there were people here to see them.

Across the water, the Ottawa River runs wide and steady. On the far bank, the green copper roofs of Parliament Hill rise above the cliffs. You are not in Ottawa, though. You are on the Quebec side, in Gatineau. This is the Canadian Museum of History, the most visited museum in the country, and it was built to face that view on purpose.

Now turn around and look at the building itself. There are almost no straight lines. The walls curve and roll like a shoreline. The roofs swell and dip like hills. The architect, Douglas Cardinal, has Indigenous roots in the Canadian Prairies. He wanted this museum to look as if wind, water, and melting glaciers had shaped it, the same forces that shaped the land of Canada itself.

The complex has two main parts. One wing holds the collections, the laboratories, and the offices. Its layered, terraced form is often compared to the rock of the Canadian Shield, the ancient bedrock that covers much of the country. The other wing is the public space, with its great glass wall and flowing copper roofs. That side is often read as the shape of retreating glaciers. One side is stone, and the other side is ice.

The museum opened in 1989 under a different name. For more than twenty years it was called the Canadian Museum of Civilization. In 2013, the federal government renamed it the Canadian Museum of History, and the focus shifted toward the story of Canada itself. The institution is much older than this building, though. Its roots go back to the collections of the Geological Survey of Canada in the mid-nineteenth century. Those collections began with rocks and fossils, which fits the stone under your hand.

Step inside, and the first space most people reach is the Grand Hall. Here is the first surprise. Before the history lessons begin, the museum gives this enormous room to the First Peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast. A row of house fronts runs along one side, built in the style of coastal villages. In front of them stand totem poles, carved from great cedar trunks, some reaching high toward the ceiling.

This is one of the largest indoor collections of totem poles in the world. Take your time with them. Each pole carries figures stacked one on top of another — ravens, bears, eagles, whales, and human faces. These are not decorations. They record family histories, rights, and stories, and they were made to be read by people who knew the language of the crests. Behind you, the tall windows frame Parliament across the river. The poles and the seat of government face each other across the water.

Look for a large dark canoe filled with strange passengers. This is a cast of The Spirit of Haida Gwaii by Bill Reid, a celebrated Haida artist. The boat carries a crowd of figures from Haida stories — Bear, Raven, Frog, Eagle, and more. They push and argue and pull in different directions, yet they all share one boat. Many visitors see it as a picture of Canada itself. Another casting of the same work stands in Vancouver's airport, in green-black bronze.

Now raise your eyes. Above the upper level, the dome holds a vast circular painting called Morning Star, by the Dene artist Alex Janvier. It is easy to miss if you walk through with your eyes on the floor. Bright ribbons of colour swirl outward from the centre. The painting tells the story of Indigenous peoples on this land, from the distant past toward a hopeful future. Find a spot to stand still and follow the colours around the circle.

Upstairs, the Canadian History Hall carries the main story of the country. It opened in 2017, for the 150th anniversary of Confederation. The galleries move roughly through time, from the earliest human presence in North America to the modern day. You will find objects both small and large — tools, clothing, signs, and everyday things that ordinary people once held. The hall does not hide the hard chapters. It includes difficult stories of colonization and loss alongside stories of invention and building.

Here is the second surprise. Tucked inside this serious national museum is a children's museum, built around the idea of a journey around the world. Children can climb aboard vehicles, explore marketplaces, and play in rooms full of objects from other cultures. If you hear laughter and running feet somewhere in the building, it is probably coming from there. For many families in the region, this is the first museum they ever remember visiting.

The museum also has a sister institution across the river, the Canadian War Museum. The two are run together as part of one national organization. If the conflicts you meet in the History Hall leave you with questions, the War Museum takes those stories much further. Between the two buildings, and the parliament on the hill, this short stretch of river holds a great deal of the country's memory.

Before you leave, go back out to the terrace. Notice how the building seems to lean toward the river, its curved walls catching the light. In the late afternoon, the sun warms the pale stone and the copper roofs turn darker against the sky. Boats move on the water below. Cyclists pass along the riverside paths. Gulls call overhead.

Take one last look at the stone under your hand. Those small fossils lay at the bottom of an ancient sea, long before any of the stories inside these walls began. The people carved into the cedar poles, the figures in the black canoe, the faces in the History Hall — they all came much later. As you walk away, look back once at the curved walls against the river. The building was designed to look as if the land itself had shaped it.
