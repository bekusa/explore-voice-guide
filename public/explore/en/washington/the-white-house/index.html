The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic sounds fade here, because the avenue in front of you has been closed to cars since the 1990s. Instead you hear footsteps on pavement, a few voices in a dozen languages, and the soft clink of a tall iron fence as someone leans against it. Beyond the bars, a lawn of clipped green runs up to a house that looks smaller than most people expect. It is pale, calm, and very bright, even on a grey day.

This is the White House, at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue. It has been the home and office of every American president since John Adams. That last detail is the first surprise for many visitors. George Washington chose the site and approved the design, but he never lived here. He died in 1799, a few months before the house was ready. The man whose name the city carries never slept under this roof.

The design came from an architecture competition held in 1792. The winner was James Hoban, an Irish-born architect who had trained in Dublin. His plan was a dignified country house, not a palace. Many historians point out its likeness to Leinster House in Dublin, a grand Georgian mansion. Georgian here means the restrained, symmetrical style of eighteenth-century Britain and Ireland. Look at the even rows of windows and the balanced front. That calm order was the point.

Now look at the colour of the walls. The house is not built of white stone. It is built of sandstone from Aquia Creek in Virginia, a soft, greyish stone that absorbs water. To protect it, the builders coated the walls in a lime-based whitewash in the late 1790s. So the famous white began as a practical solution to a weather problem. The name came later. People called it the White House for decades, and Theodore Roosevelt made the name official in 1901.

The hands that built it deserve a moment of attention. Scottish stonemasons carved much of the exterior decoration. Alongside them worked free labourers, immigrants, and enslaved African Americans, whose work in the quarries and on the building site is recorded in government payment records. Their owners were paid for that labour. It is a hard fact, and the White House Historical Association now tells it openly. The elegant walls in front of you carry that history too.

John Adams moved in during November 1800, while the plaster was still damp and many rooms were unfinished. On his second night, he wrote a letter to his wife, Abigail. In it he wished that only honest and wise men would ever rule under this roof. More than a century later, Franklin Roosevelt had those words carved into the mantel of the State Dining Room. A private letter became part of the building itself.

Then came fire. In August 1814, during the War of 1812, British troops entered Washington and set the house alight. Only the blackened outer walls survived. Hoban himself returned to supervise the rebuilding. Here is the second surprise. During a restoration in the late twentieth century, workers stripped away many layers of old paint. Underneath, on some of the stones, they found scorch marks from that fire. A few were deliberately left visible as a quiet record of 1814.

The house kept changing shape. The two porticoes — the columned porches at the front and back — were added in the 1820s. The curved South Portico faces the long lawn and the Washington Monument. The North Portico, with its flat front and columns, faces you on this side. In 1902, offices moved into a new West Wing, so the family finally had some privacy. The Oval Office took its current place in that wing in the 1930s.

By the late 1940s, the old building was in serious danger. Floors sagged, and a leg of a piano reportedly broke through a floor upstairs. Under Harry Truman, engineers took a dramatic step. They removed almost the entire interior and kept only the outer walls. Inside that historic shell, they built a new structure of steel and concrete. The family lived across the street for several years. So the walls you see are old, but much of what stands behind them dates from the early 1950s.

Truman left one more mark you can see from the south side. He added a balcony behind the columns of the South Portico, on the second floor. Critics complained that it spoiled the classic lines of the house. Truman built it anyway, and today it is simply called the Truman Balcony. Presidents and their families use it to look out over the lawn, the fountains, and the monuments beyond.

Inside, the house is larger than its front suggests. It holds around 130 rooms across several levels, including floors hidden below ground. The public rooms on the ground and state floors carry names by colour: the Blue Room, the Red Room, the Green Room. The East Room is the largest, used for receptions, press events, and, in sadder times, for presidents lying in state. Visitors on a public tour walk through some of these rooms.

Those tours need planning. Americans request them through their member of Congress. International visitors usually ask through their own country's embassy. If you cannot go inside, the White House Visitor Center, a short walk east, shows furniture, photographs, and models of the house. And around you, the setting is part of the story. Lafayette Square lies behind you to the north. The ornate Eisenhower Executive Office Building stands to the west, and the Treasury to the east.

Before you walk on, look again at the house through the fence. Notice how quiet it seems, with its neat windows and its slim columns. Watch the uniformed officers, the protesters with handmade signs in the square, the families taking the same photograph again and again. All of them belong to this place. Then turn slowly toward the square, and listen to the footsteps around you. This is still a working home, and today you are one more visitor at its gate.

What to look for

Before you go

Practical tips

Request public tours well ahead through Congress or your embassy.
Visit the White House Visitor Center if you cannot tour inside.
Walk to the Ellipse for the South Portico view.
Stand in Lafayette Square for the classic north facade photo.
Expect security screening and pack light for any tour.
