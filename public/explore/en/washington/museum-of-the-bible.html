The guide

The story

Stand in front of the entrance for a moment. Two enormous bronze panels rise above you, darker than the pale stone around them. Lean closer and you will see that the surface is not smooth. It is covered in raised letters, row after row, like a page pressed into metal. The street behind you is quiet compared with the National Mall a few blocks north. Here, in this corner of southwest Washington, you hear delivery trucks, the rattle of the rail line, and office workers heading for lunch.

Those bronze panels are worth a second look. They are cast with the opening chapter of Genesis, in Latin, taken from the Gutenberg Bible. That was the first major book printed with movable type in Europe, in the fifteenth century. So the very first thing the building shows you is a printed page. It is a quiet statement about the subject inside. This museum is less about faith itself and more about a book... how it was written, copied, carried, printed and argued over.

The Museum of the Bible opened in November 2017. It is a private museum, not part of the Smithsonian, even though it sits close to several Smithsonian buildings. Its main backers were the Green family, who own the Hobby Lobby chain of craft stores in the United States. Their collection of manuscripts and artifacts formed the core of the project. That origin shapes how many people talk about the museum, and it is part of the story you will hear today.

Here is the first surprise. This building was not designed as a museum. Its brick bones belong to an industrial structure from the early twentieth century, a refrigerated warehouse built near the railway. Food was stored here, kept cold for a growing capital city. When the museum took it over, architects kept the old brick shell and added a new glass and metal structure on top. Look up at the roofline and you can see where the old building ends and the new one begins.

Step inside and the scale changes. The main hall is tall and open, and the ceiling carries a long digital screen. Images move slowly across it — stained glass, skies, architectural patterns. Many visitors walk straight past without looking up. Take a moment to stand still and watch it change. It is one of the details that turns an old warehouse into something closer to a cathedral nave, the long central hall of a church.

The museum is organised by floors, and each floor has a theme. One floor looks at the history of the Bible as a physical object. You will find scrolls, early manuscripts, and printed Bibles from across the centuries. Another floor tells the stories inside the Bible, the narratives of the Hebrew scriptures and of the New Testament. A third floor looks at impact — how the book shaped law, music, art, fashion and politics, especially in the United States.

On the history floor, pay attention to how translation is treated. The Bible has been translated into thousands of languages. The museum shows how each new translation could be dangerous, even illegal, in its time. Some translators were persecuted for putting scripture into the everyday language of ordinary people. Think about that while you listen to this guide in your own language. The question of who gets to read a text, and in which tongue, is very old.

Now the second surprise, and it is an important one. The museum displayed fragments presented as pieces of the Dead Sea Scrolls from its opening in 2017. In 2018, after expert investigation, the museum announced that these fragments were modern forgeries. The museum made those findings public. It is rare for an institution to announce that its own showpieces are fake, and the episode changed how many scholars study these fragments.

That was not the only question about the collection. Before the museum opened, Hobby Lobby reached a settlement with the United States government over ancient artifacts that had been imported from the Middle East improperly. Thousands of clay tablets and seals were returned to Iraq. Later, a cuneiform tablet with part of the Epic of Gilgamesh, one of the oldest known works of literature, was also sent back to Iraq. The museum now talks openly about provenance, meaning the documented history of where an object came from.

It helps to hold both stories at once. This is a place with real treasures, careful conservation and serious scholarship. It is also a place that learned hard lessons in public. Many visitors find that tension makes the visit more interesting, not less. As you look at any object behind glass, try reading the label for its origin. Ask yourself how it travelled from an ancient hillside to a gallery in Washington.

The narrative floor is more theatrical. There are walk-through spaces that retell stories from the Hebrew Bible with light, sound and projection. There is also a recreated village meant to show daily life in the time of Jesus of Nazareth. You can walk among stone houses, a workshop and a small synagogue, which is a Jewish house of prayer and study. Costumed interpreters sometimes explain crafts and customs. It feels more like a stage set than a gallery, and that is the intention.

If you have time, head up to the top of the building. The upper level has a garden planted with species mentioned in the Bible, such as olive and fig trees and fragrant herbs. Rub a leaf between your fingers and smell it. From the terrace, the city opens out. You can see the dome of the Capitol to the northeast and the tops of federal buildings all around. It is one of the less crowded viewpoints in this part of Washington.

When you come back down and step out through those bronze doors, look at them once more. The letters on the metal are the same words that open the book this whole building studies. Around you, the ordinary city carries on — trains, traffic, people with coffee cups. A refrigerated warehouse became a museum about a book that has been copied by hand, printed, smuggled, forged and translated. Take that thought with you as you walk toward the Mall.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use Federal Center SW Metro station for the shortest walk.
Start at the top floor and work your way down.
Visit the rooftop garden for views toward the Capitol.
Check whether special exhibits or rides need a separate ticket.
Allow at least half a day for all the floors.
