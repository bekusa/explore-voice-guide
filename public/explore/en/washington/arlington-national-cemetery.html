The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the traffic noise from the far side of the river, there is a softer sound here: wind moving through old oaks, a mower somewhere down the slope, and footsteps on paved paths. Ahead of you, rows of white marble headstones run over the hills in long, exact lines. From almost any angle, they line up again, like a field of planted stone. This is Arlington National Cemetery, and the quiet here has weight.

First, a small surprise. Most people think of this place as part of Washington, D.C., but you are standing in Virginia. The Potomac River runs between you and the capital. On a clear day, you can see the city across the water: the white needle of the Washington Monument, the dome of the Capitol. That view matters. It is one of the main reasons this hillside became what it is today.

Long before it held graves, this land was a plantation. The house on the crest of the hill, with its heavy columns, is Arlington House. It was built in the early nineteenth century by George Washington Parke Custis, the grandson of Martha Washington. His daughter, Mary Anna Custis, married a young army officer named Robert E. Lee. For decades, this was the Lee family home, and enslaved people worked the land and kept the house running.

When the Civil War began in 1861, Lee left the United States Army to lead Confederate forces. His family left Arlington, and Union troops soon occupied the estate. It was too important to ignore. From these heights, artillery could reach the capital. So the Union army took the hill, and for the rest of the war, the house stood behind its own lines.

By 1864, Washington faced a terrible problem. Its hospitals were full, and its cemeteries were running out of space. The army's Quartermaster General, Montgomery Meigs, chose the Arlington estate for new military burials. The first soldier buried here was a young Union private named William Henry Christman, in May of that year. Meigs had graves placed close to the house itself. Many historians read that as a deliberate message to Lee: he would never live here again.

He never did. But the story has a strange legal ending. After the war, Lee's eldest son took the government to court, arguing the land had been seized unfairly. The case went all the way to the Supreme Court, and in 1882 the court ruled in his favour. By then, thousands of graves covered the hills. So the government bought the land from him. The cemetery you walk through today exists on property the nation paid for twice over, in money and in lives.

There is another chapter that many visitors miss. During the war, the army set up a settlement on part of the estate called Freedman's Village. It was a home for formerly enslaved people who had escaped to Union territory. It had houses, a school, a hospital, and farms. For about three decades, it was a real community. Many of its residents are buried here too, in a section marked with the simple word citizen.

Now walk toward the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, on the hill above the Memorial Amphitheater. In 1921, an unidentified American soldier from the First World War was brought here and buried. Unknowns from later wars were added beside him. The white marble tomb carries a line that many people read twice: Here rests in honored glory an American soldier known but to God.

Watch the sentinel. Since 1937, someone has guarded this tomb every hour of every day — through snow, heat, and hurricanes. The guards come from the Third United States Infantry Regiment, known as the Old Guard. The sentinel takes twenty-one steps across the mat, turns, and pauses for twenty-one seconds. The number echoes a twenty-one gun salute, the highest military honour. The changing of the guard is slow, precise, and almost completely silent. People in the crowd tend to stop talking without being asked.

Here is a quieter fact about the tomb. One crypt once held an unknown from the Vietnam War. In 1998, modern DNA testing identified him as an Air Force pilot named Michael Blassie, and his family brought him home. That crypt now honours all missing service members from that war. Science gave one family an answer — and left the space open for many others still waiting.

Below Arlington House, on the slope facing the city, a small flame burns at a grave of rough stone. This is where President John F. Kennedy was buried in 1963, with his wife Jacqueline beside him. Nearby lie his brothers Robert and Edward. Look up the hill from here, too. In front of the house, a flat stone marks the grave of Pierre L'Enfant, the French-born engineer who designed the street plan of Washington. He was moved here in 1909, so that he could look out over the city he imagined.

It would be easy to see this place only as history. It is not. Arlington is still an active cemetery. On most weekdays, there are many funerals here. You may hear a distant rifle volley, or the slow notes of taps played on a bugle. In Section 60, near the southern edge, lie service members from the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Families visit often. Some leave photographs, coins, or small notes on the headstones.

As you walk on, look again at those white headstones. Generals and privates lie under the same simple marble, in the same straight rows. The grass is cut short, and the paths curve gently over the hills. Somewhere close by, a family may be standing in silence. Take your time here, keep your voice low, and let the rows lead you forward — one name, and then another, and then another.
