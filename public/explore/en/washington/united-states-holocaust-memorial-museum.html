The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the pavement outside. Behind you, the open lawns of the National Mall stretch toward the Washington Monument. Families carry ice cream. Joggers pass. In front of you is a building of pale limestone and dark red brick, with a facade that looks calm and official, like its government neighbours. Then you notice the details. The brick towers are slightly too heavy. The windows are narrow. Something here feels quietly wrong, and that feeling is intentional.

This is the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum. It is the national memorial to the six million Jews murdered by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, and to the millions of other victims of Nazi persecution. It opened in April 1993, after more than a decade of planning. The idea began in the late 1970s with a presidential commission. Then, in 1980, Congress voted unanimously to create the museum. The land was federal, but the building was paid for largely through private donations.

The architect was James Ingo Freed. As a child, Freed left Germany for the United States to escape the Nazis. Before he designed this building, he travelled to the sites of former ghettos and concentration camps in Europe. He studied the materials there — the brick, the steel, the bolted frames, the watchtowers. He did not copy the camps. Instead, he brought back their language of hard industrial materials and placed it beside the classical architecture of Washington.

Look at the two faces of the building. On one side, the limestone matches the formal stone of the federal buildings around it. On the other side, the brick becomes darker and rougher. Freed wanted the museum to sit politely in official Washington. At the same time, he wanted it to unsettle visitors as they enter. The building does both things at once, and the tension is part of the experience.

Inside, you arrive in the Hall of Witness. It is a long, tall space under a glass skylight. Look up. The steel trusses above you are twisted and skewed, as if the roof is under strain. The skylight does not sit flat. Bolts and steel plates are left exposed. Light falls into the hall, but it does not feel warm. Many visitors look up here without knowing why the space makes them uneasy. Now you know — the structure itself is slightly off balance by design.

Here is a detail that surprises many first-time visitors. Before the permanent exhibition, you can take an identification card. Each card tells the story of one real person who lived through the Holocaust years. Some survived. Many did not. As you walk through the floors, you carry this person with you. At the end of the visit, you can read what happened to them. The card turns millions into one face, one name, and one life.

The permanent exhibition begins at the top of the building. You ride a steel elevator up, and the doors open onto the story. From there, you walk downward, floor by floor, through time. The first part follows the rise of the Nazis in the 1930s, and the steady removal of rights from Jewish people in Germany. The second part covers the ghettos, the deportations, and the killing centres. The final part tells of liberation, survival, and the long search for justice afterwards.

The objects are real, and that changes everything. You will walk through a railway freight car of the kind used to deport people to the camps. You can step inside it. On another floor, you will see a large mass of shoes. They came from the Majdanek camp in Poland. They are worn, crushed, and grey with age. Visitors often say the smell of old leather stays with them long after they leave the room.

One of the quietest spaces is the Tower of Faces. It rises through several floors, and the walls are covered with family photographs. They show the Jewish community of one small town, Eishyshok, in what is now Lithuania. The pictures show weddings, school groups, children on bicycles, and parents in their best clothes. In 1941, nearly all the Jews of this town were murdered in just a few days. The photographs were gathered after the war by a survivor from the town.

The museum is not only a memorial. It is also a centre for research and education. It holds a very large archive of documents, films, photographs, and recorded testimonies from survivors. Researchers come here from around the world. The museum also works on genocide prevention today. It studies places where mass atrocities are happening now, and it warns governments and the public about the early signs.

The building carries a more recent memory as well. In June 2009, a gunman attacked the museum entrance. A security guard, Stephen Tyrone Johns, was killed while protecting visitors. The museum honours him, and his story is a reminder that the hatred described inside these walls did not end in 1945. Security here is careful for a reason. Please be patient at the entrance.

After the exhibition, many visitors walk into the Hall of Remembrance. It is a six-sided room, filled with soft natural light. It is very quiet. An eternal flame burns here, and the walls carry inscriptions. Visitors can light memorial candles. Soil from concentration camps and ghettos is placed in this hall. People sit on the steps, alone or together, and simply rest. You do not need to say anything here.

If you are visiting with children, it helps to know that the main exhibition is intended for older children and adults. Some images are very difficult. The museum offers a separate exhibition called Daniel's Story, designed for younger visitors. It tells the history through the eyes of a boy growing up in Germany. Talk with your children before and after. Many families say the best part of the visit is the conversation on the walk home.

When you step back outside, the Mall will look the same. The grass, the monuments, the traffic on the avenue. But take a moment before you rejoin the crowd. Turn and look once more at the brick towers, the narrow windows, the pale stone. Think of the name on your identification card. Then walk on slowly, carrying that one person with you, out into the open light of the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take an identification card before starting the permanent exhibition.
Check whether a timed pass is needed for the main exhibition.
Allow at least two or three hours for the full visit.
Start at the top floor and walk downward chronologically.
Bring younger children to Daniel's Story instead.
