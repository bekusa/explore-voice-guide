The guide

The story

Feel the river wind. Gulls wheel over the Potomac. Rowing shells cut the water like long knives, eight oars moving as one. Cyclists ring their bells along the path. Fountains splash behind you, and the smell of the tide mixes with coffee from the cafes. Georgetown's waterfront is the place where a working port learned to relax.

You are standing where Georgetown began. The town was founded in 1751 on the Maryland shore of the Potomac, decades before Washington itself existed. Ships anchored here to load tobacco from inland farms. Wagons rolled downhill carrying hogsheads, the great barrels of cured leaf. The port made the town wealthy, and Georgetown kept its own city government until 1871.

For two centuries this shoreline worked hard. Tobacco gave way to flour, coal, and stone. Mills, lime kilns, and warehouses crowded the bank, and barges from the canal unloaded coal beside the wharves. A power plant rose above the river. The waterfront was loud, smoky, and useful, and almost nobody came here for pleasure.

The twentieth century changed the river's job. Shipping moved to deeper ports. The factories and rail yards closed one by one. For years, much of this shore was simply parking lots and rubble, a blank space between the city and the water. Old photographs show cars parked where you now see lawns and elm trees.

Then Georgetown turned around to face its river again. In 1986, the Washington Harbour complex opened just east of here, with restaurants, terraces, and a large fountain circled by sculptures. Boats tie up beside the diners on summer evenings. In winter, the fountain becomes an ice rink, one of the largest outdoor rinks in the region.

The green heart of the shoreline came later. Georgetown Waterfront Park opened in stages and was completed in 2011. It covers about ten acres between Key Bridge and the harbor, built where the parking lots used to be. It is a national park, run by the National Park Service. Neighbors campaigned for decades to turn this shore into parkland, and its lawns now slope gently to the water's edge.

The park rewards slow walking. Wide steps descend to the river like seats in a theater. A pergola frames the view. Near the foot of Wisconsin Avenue, a fountain sprays straight from the pavement, and children run through it on hot days. A stone labyrinth invites you to walk in circles on purpose. Benches face the water every few steps.

Look upstream at the arches of Key Bridge, finished in 1923. It is named for Francis Scott Key, the lawyer who wrote the words of the national anthem. His house once stood near the bridge's Georgetown end. At night, lamps trace the bridge's arches across the dark water. Beyond it, the river turns wild quickly, and herons hunt along the rocks.

Rowing is the waterfront's oldest surviving sport. Boathouses have lined this bank since the nineteenth century. University crews and club rowers train here in every season. If you come at dawn, you will hear the coxswains calling the rhythm across the water before you see the boats. Race days fill the river with color and shouting.

Look across the river. The wooded island in midstream is Theodore Roosevelt Island, a memorial forest for the conservation president. The towers behind it belong to Rosslyn, Virginia. Downstream, on a clear day, you can pick out the white roof of the Kennedy Center. Airliners climb the river corridor from the airport, following the water's curve.

The waterfront also connects walks in every direction. The Capital Crescent Trail begins near here and follows an old rail line upstream toward Maryland. Runners use it as a green highway out of the city. One block inland, the old C and O Canal runs parallel to the shore, so you can loop between river and canal in minutes.

Watch one rowing shell pass, from first sight to last. The oars swing, the boat glides, the wake fades. Ships full of tobacco once anchored in that same channel, and kayakers now chase the sunset where the barges tied up. The port is gone, but the river still sets the pace here. Sit on the river steps for a moment before you go, and let it set yours too.
