The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the gravel in front of the portico. Listen to the crunch of it under your feet, and then to the quiet. Below you, the hillside falls away in long green rows of white headstones. Beyond them, the Potomac River runs grey or silver, depending on the light. And across the water, the city of Washington spreads out like a map laid flat on a table.

One small correction before we begin. The guide lists this house under Washington, but you are actually standing in Virginia. Arlington House sits on a ridge on the Virginia side of the river, inside Arlington National Cemetery. That position is the whole point of the place. The house was built to look at the capital, and the capital was built, in a sense, to look back at it.

The man who built it was George Washington Parke Custis. He was the step-grandson of George Washington, and he was raised at Mount Vernon by George and Martha Washington themselves. When he inherited this land, he decided to build a house that would serve as a kind of living memorial to the first president. Construction began in the early 1800s. It went slowly, wing by wing, and the main house was finished around 1818.

Now look up at those columns. They are thick and heavy, and they have no fancy tops — just plain, cushion-like capitals. This is the Doric style, borrowed from ancient Greek temples. The architect, George Hadfield, was an English-born designer who had also worked on the early Capitol. His portico is one of the earliest examples of Greek Revival architecture in the United States. It was a bold choice at the time, and the style later spread across the country.

Here is the first surprise. Walk close and look at the surface of a column. It is not marble, and it is not solid stone. The columns are made of brick, covered in stucco, and painted to look like stone. From the city, they read as gleaming white marble. Up close, you can see the honest material underneath. It was a clever, affordable way to make a grand statement on a family budget.

In 1831, Custis's only surviving daughter, Mary Anna Randolph Custis, married a young army officer in the family parlor of this house. His name was Robert E. Lee. The couple made Arlington their home for about thirty years. Several of their seven children grew up here, running across these lawns. Lee's career kept him travelling, but this house was the centre of family life, and Mary filled it with Washington relics from Mount Vernon.

The house has another history, and it matters just as much. Arlington was a plantation, and it ran on the labour of enslaved people. Many enslaved men, women and children lived and worked here. Behind the mansion, you can still visit the quarters where some of them lived. Custis's will ordered that the people he enslaved be freed within five years of his death. Lee, as executor of the estate, carried out that emancipation at the end of 1862.

One of those enslaved women deserves her name spoken here. Selina Gray was the housekeeper. When the Lee family left in 1861, Mary Lee handed her the keys. As Union soldiers moved in, Selina Gray worked to protect the Washington heirlooms from looting, and she alerted officers when items began to disappear. Some of what survives today from George Washington's household survives because of her.

In April 1861, Virginia left the Union, and Lee resigned from the United States Army to side with his home state. He left Arlington and never lived here again. Within weeks, Union troops crossed the river and occupied the estate. The ridge was too valuable to leave in Confederate hands. From here, artillery could reach the heart of the capital. The house became an army headquarters, and the grounds filled with camps and fortifications.

Then came the decision that changed this land forever. In 1864, with Washington's hospitals overflowing and its cemeteries full, Union Quartermaster General Montgomery Meigs chose the Arlington estate for a new military cemetery. He deliberately placed early graves close to the house, including near Mary Lee's garden. Many historians read this as a pointed choice — to make sure the Lees could never live here again. That cemetery became Arlington National Cemetery.

Here is the second surprise. The government had taken the property over unpaid taxes, through a law that demanded payment in person. Years later, Lee's eldest son took the case to the Supreme Court. In 1882, the court ruled in his favour. The government then bought the estate from him for 150,000 dollars. By that time, of course, thousands of soldiers lay buried on the hillside, and no family could ever move back in.

In the twentieth century, Congress turned the house into a memorial, and the National Park Service now cares for it. A major restoration was completed in 2021, bringing back period rooms and telling the stories of the enslaved community more fully. Down in front of the house, look for a single flat tomb. It belongs to Pierre L'Enfant, the French-born engineer who designed the plan of Washington. He was moved here in 1909, so that he could look out over the city he imagined.

Now turn around once more and face the river. Straight ahead, across the Memorial Bridge, sits the Lincoln Memorial. The bridge was meant as a symbol, linking Lincoln's memorial with Lee's old home — North and South joined across the water. As you walk back down the hill, notice how the rows of headstones line up in every direction, and how quiet the place stays, even with the city so close. This view holds a whole country's arguments with itself... and it is still being looked at, every day, by people standing exactly where you stand now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to the house early for quieter views across the river.
Wear comfortable shoes for the steep cemetery walk uphill.
Visit the enslaved quarters behind the main house.
Allow extra time for cemetery security and walking distances.
Speak softly — this is an active military cemetery.
