The guide

The story

Start at the bottom of the steps and look up. The marble is pale, almost white, and in morning light it holds a faint warmth. Behind you, the long Reflecting Pool stretches east toward the Washington Monument. Voices drift up and down the stairs in a dozen languages. And at the top, framed by columns, a seated figure waits in the shadow of the inner hall. Take the steps slowly. The building is designed to make you look up as you climb.

This is the Lincoln Memorial, built to honour Abraham Lincoln, the sixteenth president of the United States. Lincoln led the country through the Civil War, the conflict between the northern states and the southern states that had broken away. He was assassinated in 1865, just days after the war's main fighting ended. Plans for a national memorial began soon after his death. They took decades to settle, and the building you see was finally dedicated in 1922.

The architect was Henry Bacon, and he looked back to ancient Greece for his model. The memorial is shaped like a Greek temple, similar in spirit to the Parthenon in Athens. Count the columns around the outside. There are thirty-six. They stand for the thirty-six states in the Union at the time of Lincoln's death. Above them, along the top of the walls, the names of those states are carved into the stone. It is a quiet piece of national arithmetic.

Look higher still, to the very top of the building. Another band of names runs there. These are the forty-eight states that existed when the memorial was dedicated in 1922. Alaska and Hawaii joined the country later, in 1959. There was no room left on the stone for them. So their names were added on a plaque on the approach to the memorial instead. Most visitors walk past without noticing it.

The stone itself comes from across the country. The outer marble is from Colorado. The inner walls use limestone from Indiana. The floor is pink marble from Tennessee. The ceiling panels are thin sheets of marble from Alabama, treated so that daylight glows softly through them. The choice was deliberate. A memorial to the man who held the Union together is built from the materials of many states, gathered in one place.

Now step inside, and let your eyes adjust. Lincoln sits in a large chair, gazing east across the Mall. The statue was designed by the sculptor Daniel Chester French. It was carved by the Piccirilli Brothers, a family of Italian-born stone carvers working in New York. The figure is about nineteen feet tall, close to six metres. It is built from many blocks of white marble from Georgia, fitted together so closely that the joins are hard to see.

Look carefully at Lincoln's face and hands. One hand is clenched, the other open and relaxed. Many people read this as two sides of the man: firm resolve and gentle mercy. You may also hear a popular story that his hands form the letters A and L in sign language. Treat that one as legend. It is a well-loved tale among guides, but it is not a documented intention of the sculptor.

Above the statue, carved into the wall, is the dedication. It reads: In this temple, as in the hearts of the people for whom he saved the Union, the memory of Abraham Lincoln is enshrined forever. The word temple is important. Bacon and his team wanted this space to feel sacred, not just civic. The hush inside, even on crowded days, shows that they succeeded.

Turn to the side chambers. On the south wall, to your left as you face Lincoln, is the full text of the Gettysburg Address. It is a short speech, given in 1863 at a battlefield cemetery in Pennsylvania. On the north wall is his Second Inaugural Address, delivered weeks before his death. Above each text is a large painted mural by the artist Jules Guerin, showing allegorical figures of themes like unity, justice, and reconciliation.

Here is a detail almost everyone misses. In the Second Inaugural, look for the word future. The carver originally cut an E where the F should be, so the word briefly read euture. The mistake was later filled in and corrected. But if you stand close and look carefully, you can often still see the faint outline of the extra stroke. Even monuments carved for eternity carry small human errors.

The memorial did not stay only a monument. It became a stage. On Easter Sunday in 1939, the singer Marian Anderson performed on these steps. She was a celebrated Black contralto, and she had been refused permission to sing in a concert hall in the city because of her race. Instead, she sang here, in the open air, to a crowd of tens of thousands. Lincoln's statue sat behind her as she sang.

In 1963, the March on Washington filled the Mall with people demanding civil rights. From these steps, Martin Luther King Junior delivered the speech now known as I Have a Dream. Walk down to the landing on the steps and look at the ground. An inscription marks the spot where he stood. There is a hard irony in this place, too. At the 1922 dedication, Black guests were seated separately from white guests. Change came slowly, and it came here.

Before you leave, stand on that landing and turn to face east. You are looking along the same line Lincoln looks along. The Reflecting Pool runs toward the Washington Monument, and beyond it, on a clear day, you can see the dome of the Capitol. This straight line of memory was planned. It links the first president, the president who preserved the Union, and the seat of Congress in a single view.

Take a moment here. Listen to the footsteps on the marble, the water birds on the pool, the city humming in the distance. Millions of people have stood exactly where you are standing, some in protest, some in celebration, some simply to look. As you walk back down the steps, glance behind you once more. In the changing light, the seated figure will look a little different each time you turn around.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for softer light and thinner crowds.
Return after dark when the statue is dramatically lit.
Climb to the landing and face east toward the Capitol.
Walk around the outer colonnade, not just the main hall.
Wear comfortable shoes for the many steps and long Mall.
