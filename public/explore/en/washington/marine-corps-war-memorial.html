The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, a cotton flag snaps and ripples in the wind. Below it, six bronze figures lean into a single pole. Their boots grip a rough slope of rock and earth. The grass around you is wide, open and quiet. Traffic hums faintly in the distance. Then you notice the scale. These men are giants, many times your height, and yet they look tired, strained and very human.

This is the Marine Corps War Memorial. Many people call it simply the Iwo Jima Memorial. It honours every United States Marine who has died in service since the Corps was founded in 1775. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Although it belongs to the story of Washington, the memorial does not stand inside the city. It stands in Arlington, Virginia, just across the Potomac River, beside Arlington National Cemetery.

The scene you are looking at comes from a photograph. On February 23, 1945, Associated Press photographer Joe Rosenthal climbed Mount Suribachi. This is a volcanic peak on Iwo Jima, a small island in the Pacific. American forces were fighting a long and costly battle there against Japanese troops. At the summit, a group of Marines and a Navy corpsman raised a large American flag. Rosenthal pressed the shutter in a fraction of a second. That single frame won the Pulitzer Prize.

Now for the second surprise. The flag in that photograph was not the first flag raised on Suribachi that day. Earlier, Marines had planted a smaller flag on the summit. Commanders wanted a larger one that troops across the island could see. So a second group carried up a bigger flag and raised it on a length of pipe. Rosenthal captured that second raising. The moment you see in bronze is a replacement... and it became the image the world remembers.

The photograph moved the sculptor Felix de Weldon very quickly. He was serving in the Navy at the time. He began building a model of the scene within days of seeing the image. Over the following years, he developed the design into this full monument. The memorial was paid for by private donations from Marines, former Marines and supporters. No public funds were used for the sculpture itself.

The finished work was dedicated on November 10, 1954. That date was chosen with care. November 10 is the birthday of the Marine Corps, and Marines around the world mark it every year. President Dwight Eisenhower attended the ceremony. The monument has stood on this low ridge ever since. It looks east, across the river, toward the capital.

Take a moment to study the figures. Each one is cast in bronze, and each is roughly thirty feet tall. The flagpole rises well above them. Look at how the bodies form one diagonal line. The man at the back still pushes from the ground. The man at the front holds the pole near its base. Their combined effort pulls the eye upward, from the rocks to the flag. It is a composition built on teamwork, not on any single hero.

The identities of the six flag raisers have a complicated history. For decades, official records listed one set of names. Later, careful study of photographs and film led the Marine Corps to revise two of them, in 2016 and in 2019. Today the six are recognised as Michael Strank, Harlon Block, Franklin Sousley, Ira Hayes, Harold Schultz and Harold Keller. Strank, Block and Sousley were killed in later fighting on the island. They never saw this memorial.

Now look down at the base. It is made of dark, polished granite. Around it, in gold lettering, runs a list of places and conflicts. These are engagements in which Marines have fought, from the early years of the Corps to recent decades. Walk slowly around the full base and read them. On one face you will find a line from Admiral Chester Nimitz about Iwo Jima. It reads: uncommon valor was a common virtue.

Look up again at the flag. It is real cloth, not bronze. By presidential proclamation, the American flag flies here day and night, around the clock. That is a rare honour. Only a small number of places in the country hold this permission. When the wind is strong, the flag pulls hard against its halyard. On calm days it hangs almost still, and the bronze figures seem to hold their breath.

There is also a popular tale you may hear from other visitors. People like to count the hands on the sculpture. Some say there are thirteen, with the extra hand standing for the hand of God. It is a lovely idea, but it is folklore. Most careful counts find twelve hands for the six men. Count them yourself... it is a good way to slow down and really look at the sculpture.

Turn around for a moment and face east. On a clear day, this ridge offers a long view across the river. You can see the white tower of the Washington Monument. Nearby, the dome of the Capitol may be visible too, depending on where you stand. This sight line is part of the experience. The Marines on the hill seem to face the city they served.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Notice the crunch of the path under your feet, the sound of the flag above, and the open sky over the lawn. Families pause here. Veterans sometimes stand quietly in front of the names on the base. As you walk away, look back once. From a distance, the six figures merge into one shape — a single push toward a flag in the wind.
