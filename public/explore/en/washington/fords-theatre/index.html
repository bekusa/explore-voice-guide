The guide

The story

Stand on Tenth Street for a moment and listen. Traffic hums between the office blocks. Footsteps tap on the brick sidewalk. In front of you is a plain, pale brick building with arched doorways and tall shuttered windows. It looks modest, almost quiet. It is easy to walk past. But on a spring night in 1865, this building became the setting for one of the most painful moments in American history.

This is Ford's Theatre. Today it is a working playhouse, a museum, and a memorial, all under one roof. The National Park Service cares for the historic site, and a nonprofit partner runs the stage productions and education programs. So the same room where history broke apart still fills with audiences, actors, and applause. That combination is rare, and it shapes everything you will feel inside.

The building did not begin as a theatre. In the early nineteenth century, a Baptist congregation worshipped on this site. Later, a theatre manager from Baltimore named John T. Ford bought the property and turned it into a playhouse. A fire destroyed his first theatre here, and he rebuilt in brick. By the middle of the Civil War, Ford's Theatre was one of the busiest entertainment venues in the capital.

Abraham Lincoln loved the theatre. During the war, plays gave him a few hours away from casualty reports and political pressure. He came to Ford's several times. He especially enjoyed Shakespeare, and he was known to laugh easily at comedy. For a tired president, a seat in a darkened room was a kind of rest... and that habit is part of why this story unfolded here.

On April 14, 1865, it was Good Friday. Only five days earlier, General Robert E. Lee had surrendered his army at Appomattox in Virginia. The city was celebrating the end of four years of war. That evening, Lincoln and his wife, Mary Todd Lincoln, came to see a comedy called Our American Cousin. With them were a young army officer, Major Henry Rathbone, and his fiancée, Clara Harris.

The party sat in the presidential box, high on the right side of the stage. The box was decorated with flags and a framed portrait of George Washington. When the president arrived, the orchestra played and the audience stood to cheer. Then the play continued. It was a light, silly comedy, and the crowd was in a good mood. Nobody in the house expected anything other than an ordinary evening out.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The man who entered that box was not a stranger to this building. John Wilkes Booth was a famous actor from a well-known theatrical family. He had performed at Ford's before. He knew its corridors, its backstage passages, and the rear alley. He even collected his mail here. That familiarity let him move through the theatre without drawing attention — and it shaped his escape.

During the third act, Booth entered the box behind the president and fired a single shot from a small pistol called a derringer. Accounts say he chose a moment when a big laugh line filled the house with noise. Major Rathbone struggled with him, and Booth slashed the officer with a knife. Then Booth jumped from the box down to the stage. He landed badly, but he still crossed the stage and ran out the back to a waiting horse.

Witnesses reported that Booth shouted a Latin phrase as he crossed the stage: sic semper tyrannis, which means "thus always to tyrants." A popular telling says his spur caught on one of the flags draped over the box, and that this caused his fall. Historians still debate how and when he broke his leg. Either way, Booth escaped into the night. He was found and killed by soldiers in Virginia about twelve days later.

Lincoln was not taken to the White House. Doctors judged that the journey over rough streets would kill him. So soldiers carried him across Tenth Street to a boarding house owned by a tailor named William Petersen. If you turn around, you can see it directly opposite the theatre. There, in a small back bedroom, Lincoln died the next morning, April 15. He was the first American president to be assassinated.

What happened to the theatre afterward is the second surprise. The federal government took over the building, and it never reopened as a playhouse in the nineteenth century. Its interior was gutted and turned into offices and storage, including a home for the Army Medical Museum. Then, in 1893, the floors inside collapsed. Twenty-two government clerks were killed. For many people, the building now seemed to carry a double tragedy.

For decades, Ford's Theatre stood as a shell. Then, in the twentieth century, a careful restoration rebuilt the auditorium to look as it did in 1865. Researchers used photographs, drawings, and witness accounts to recreate the seats, the stage, and the decorated box. The theatre reopened to audiences in 1968. Downstairs, the museum displays objects connected to that night, including the derringer that Booth used. Across the street, a modern education center holds a tall tower of books written about Lincoln.

When you go inside, look up at the presidential box. The flags and the portrait of Washington are there again. The rocking chair you see is a reproduction; the original chair now belongs to a museum in Michigan. Notice how close the box sits to the stage. Measure the drop with your eyes. Then imagine the sound of a full house, the laughter, and the sudden silence.

When you step back out onto Tenth Street, pause for a breath. Look at the narrow facade, then at the Petersen House across the road. The two buildings face each other at a distance of just a few dozen steps. That short walk connects the last laugh Lincoln heard to the room where his life ended. Notice the ordinary city moving around you... and carry that small distance with you as you go.
