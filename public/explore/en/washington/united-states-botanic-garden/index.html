The guide

The story

Push through the glass doors and the air changes at once. It is warm, heavy, and damp against your skin. The smell of wet soil and green leaves rises up to meet you. Somewhere a fan hums, and water drips onto broad leaves. A moment ago you were on the edge of the National Mall, with traffic and marble all around. Now you are standing inside a living collection of plants from across the world.

This is the United States Botanic Garden. It sits at the foot of Capitol Hill, close enough that the great white dome feels like a neighbour. That closeness is not an accident. Congress created this garden in 1820, and it is still run under the legislative branch today, cared for by the office of the Architect of the Capitol. Few visitors realise that one of the oldest botanic gardens in the country belongs, in a sense, to Congress itself.

The idea was older than the garden. In the early years of the republic, leaders including George Washington discussed a national garden in the new capital. They imagined a place to study plants that might feed, heal, and enrich a young nation. A learned society in the city pushed the plan forward. For its first decades, though, the garden was modest. It needed something to fill it.

That something arrived by sea. Between 1838 and 1842, the United States Exploring Expedition sailed around the world. It is often called the Wilkes Expedition, after its commander, Charles Wilkes. The ships came home with living plants, seeds, and dried specimens from the Pacific and beyond. Many of those plants came to Washington, and they gave the botanic garden a real collection at last. The garden has said that a few plants in its care may descend from that voyage... a quiet thread back to the age of sail.

Now look up. The building around you is the Conservatory, a structure of glass and metal set on a stone base. It opened in the 1930s, replacing earlier greenhouses that stood closer to the Capitol. Its tallest space, the glass pavilion above the jungle, catches the sky and fills the room with soft, scattered light. Around the year 2000, the Conservatory went through a long renovation, and it reopened in 2001 with new systems for climate, water, and light.

The Conservatory is not one room but many. Each one holds a different climate, and the doors between them work like borders between continents. You can walk from a damp tropical forest to a dry desert in less than a minute. Pay attention to your skin and your breathing as you move. The change in air is part of the exhibit. It tells you what each plant needs to survive, without a single sign.

The jungle room is the heart of the building. Palms, vines, and huge leaves fill the space under the high glass. Look for the elevated walkway that runs along the upper level. From up there, you look down into the green crowns of the trees. This is the view a bird or a monkey might have. Most people only see a forest from the ground, so this angle is a small gift. Take your time up there.

In other rooms, the mood shifts. One space is given to medicinal plants, the ones that people have used for healing for thousands of years. Another holds orchids, with delicate flowers in shapes that seem almost designed. There are rooms for deserts, for plants of Hawaii, and for rare and endangered species. Each label is worth a glance. Many of these plants are under threat in the wild, and gardens like this one help protect them.

Here is a surprise for many visitors. Every so often, a giant plant called the corpse flower blooms in this garden. Its proper name is titan arum. When it opens, it gives off a smell like rotting meat, which draws the insects that pollinate it. Blooms are rare and short, often lasting only a day or two. When one happens, the news spreads fast, and long lines form outside. People wait for the chance to smell something truly terrible.

Step back outside, and the garden keeps going. To the west of the Conservatory lies the National Garden, which opened in the mid-2000s. It holds a rose garden, a butterfly garden, and a water garden named for the First Ladies. There is also a regional garden filled with plants native to the mid-Atlantic, the part of the country around Washington. This outdoor space changes most with the seasons, from spring blossom to the warm colours of autumn.

Now cross Independence Avenue to Bartholdi Park, also part of the botanic garden. At its centre stands a large cast-iron fountain. It is the work of Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, the French sculptor who also created the Statue of Liberty. The fountain was first shown at the great Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia in 1876. After that, it came to Washington. Look closely at the figures holding up the basin, and at the water falling between them.

The gardens around the fountain are planted as ideas for home gardeners. Many plants here would grow well in an ordinary backyard in this region. That is part of the garden's purpose today. It is not only a museum of rare species. It is also a teacher, showing everyday people how to grow food, attract pollinators, and care for the land around them. Throughout the year, the garden hosts classes, seasonal shows, and displays about plants and people.

Before you go, find a quiet spot and simply stand still. Listen to the water in the fountain or the drip inside the glass house. Notice the Capitol dome rising beyond the trees, white against the sky. Two kinds of national life meet here — the work of government and the slow work of growing things. As you walk back toward the Mall, look at the trees and flower beds along the way. Some of them, too, are part of the long story that began here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to the jungle room's elevated walkway for treetop views.
Wear layers; the tropical rooms are warm and humid.
Wipe camera lenses after leaving the humid jungle room.
Cross Independence Avenue to visit Bartholdi Park.
Check the garden's news for rare corpse flower blooms.
