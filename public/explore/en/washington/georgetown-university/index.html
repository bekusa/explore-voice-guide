The story

The brick walkways underfoot are worn smooth by two centuries of footsteps — students rushing to lectures, Jesuits pacing in thought, visitors like you tracing the contours of America's oldest Catholic university. Georgetown sits on a bluff above the Potomac River, its spires and Georgian facades rising over a neighborhood that predates the capital itself. The air here feels different from the rest of Washington — quieter, older, threaded with ivy and the faint scent of old books.

The university was founded in seventeen eighty-nine, the same year George Washington took the oath of office just a few miles downriver. John Carroll, America's first Catholic bishop, envisioned a school that would educate young men regardless of their faith — a radical idea in a nation still wary of Rome. He chose this hilltop because it overlooked the port of Georgetown, then a thriving tobacco town where ships came and went, bringing ideas along with cargo. The college opened with just one student. Within a decade, it had become a proving ground for the young republic's experiment in religious tolerance.

The centerpiece of campus is Healy Hall, the grey stone building with the clock tower that dominates every postcard view. Completed in eighteen seventy-nine, it is named for Patrick Healy, a Jesuit priest who served as the university's president and transformed it into a modern research institution. Healy was also the son of an enslaved woman — a fact the university did not publicly acknowledge for more than a century. His tower, with its Flemish spires and Romanesque arches, stands as both monument and question mark, a reminder that Georgetown's history is tangled with the history of slavery in ways the institution is still reckoning with.

Walk toward the front gates and you will pass Dahlgren Chapel, a small Gothic Revival sanctuary tucked behind the main quad. Its stained glass filters the light into pools of blue and amber, and the wooden pews still bear the scuff marks of generations of students seeking a moment of stillness between exams. The chapel is named for a Civil War admiral, but it feels less martial than meditative — a pocket of silence in a place that hums with debate and ambition.

Georgetown has always been a crossroads. The School of Foreign Service, founded after the First World War, trained diplomats who would shape the Cold War and beyond. Walk through the halls of Walsh or Intercultural Center and you might overhear a dozen languages, students arguing over coffee about trade policy or human rights. The university's location — a short metro ride from the State Department, the World Bank, the embassies lining Massachusetts Avenue — means that theory and practice blur here. Professors slip away mid-semester to brief Congress. Students intern at think tanks and return to class with classified information still fresh in their minds.

The campus itself is a patchwork of architectural eras. Red brick Federal-style buildings from the early eighteen hundreds stand alongside brutalist concrete structures from the nineteen sixties. Lauinger Library, a stark modernist tower of poured concrete, divides opinion — some call it an eyesore, others a bold statement of intellectual ambition. Inside, the top floors offer sweeping views of the Potomac and the spires of the National Cathedral in the distance. On clear days, you can see the Washington Monument, a pale needle on the horizon.

Georgetown's relationship with slavery is written into the landscape, though you have to know where to look. In eighteen thirty-eight, the university sold two hundred and seventy-two enslaved people to pay off mounting debts. The sale kept the school solvent but tore apart families, sending men, women, and children to plantations in Louisiana. For more than a century and a half, this history was barely mentioned. In recent years, the university has begun to confront it — renaming buildings, establishing a fund for descendants, hosting forums where students and scholars wrestle with what reconciliation might mean. The process is ongoing, uncomfortable, necessary.

The students you see crossing the quad today are heirs to all of this — the Jesuit ideals of service and inquiry, the diplomatic ambitions, the unresolved debts. Georgetown remains a place where ideas are tested against the world, where the abstract becomes concrete. The university's motto, carved above doorways and printed on diplomas, is two Latin words: Utraque Unum. Both into one. It was meant to describe the union of Church and state in education, but it speaks just as well to the way history and present tense collide here, neither one ever fully resolved.

As you leave, glance back at Healy Hall. The clock in the tower keeps time over a campus that is always in motion — students debating on the steps, professors hurrying to meetings, tourists like you pausing to take it all in. The bricks remember more than any of us can carry, but they do not offer easy answers. They simply hold the question open, inviting you to keep looking.
