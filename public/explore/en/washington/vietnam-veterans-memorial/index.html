The guide

The story

Start with your own reflection. As you walk down the gentle slope, the black stone beside you begins to rise, and your face appears in it. Behind your face are the trees, the sky, and the people walking with you. Over all of it run thousands of carved names. The grass is soft at the edges of the path. The city noise grows quieter as you go down. People here tend to lower their voices without being asked.

This is the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, set in Constitution Gardens on the National Mall. Most people simply call it the Wall. It is not a tower or an arch. It is a long V of polished black granite cut into the earth. The two arms meet at a low point in the middle, where the stone stands at its tallest, roughly three metres high. At the far ends, the walls narrow to almost nothing and sink back into the ground.

Look along each arm and follow its line. One wall points toward the Washington Monument. The other points toward the Lincoln Memorial. The designer chose this on purpose. She tied this new memorial to two older symbols of the nation, the founder and the president who held the country together. You are standing in a quiet fold of land that connects them both.

That designer was Maya Lin. Here is the first surprise. When she won the national design competition in 1981, she was a young architecture student at Yale University. Her entry was chosen from more than a thousand submissions. The judges did not know who she was, because the entries were reviewed without names. Her drawings were simple, almost abstract. She described the Wall as a cut in the earth, a wound that is closed and healing.

The idea for the memorial came from veterans themselves. Jan Scruggs, who had served as an infantryman in Vietnam, led the effort to create it. He and other veterans founded the Vietnam Veterans Memorial Fund. The money came largely from private donations, from ordinary people across the country. The memorial was dedicated in November 1982, less than a decade after the last American combat troops had left Vietnam.

The design was not welcomed by everyone. Some critics called it a black gash. They wanted something white, heroic, and traditional, like the other monuments on the Mall. The argument became bitter and very public. In the end, a compromise was reached. A bronze statue and a flagpole were added nearby, close to the entrance. Maya Lin's Wall itself stayed as she had imagined it.

Now look closely at the names. There are more than 58,000 of them. Here is the second surprise. They are not in alphabetical order. They are listed by the date each person died or went missing. The list begins at the centre, at the tallest point, with the earliest losses from 1959. It runs out to the end of the east wall. Then it starts again at the far tip of the west wall and runs back toward the centre, ending in 1975.

This means the first and last names meet at the apex. The war begins and ends in the same place. Maya Lin wanted visitors to read the war as a story in time, not as a phone book. Men who died on the same day, sometimes in the same battle, are listed beside each other. Their friends can find them together, as they were.

Beside each name you will see a small symbol. A diamond marks a person whose death was confirmed. A cross marks a person who was missing at the end of the war. If a missing person's remains are later identified, the cross can be changed into a diamond. Names are also still added from time to time, when the government confirms that a death was caused by wounds from the war.

The stone is black granite from India, polished until it works like a mirror. This was a key part of the design. When you read a name, you also see yourself. The living and the dead appear on the same surface. On a bright afternoon, the reflection is sharp. On a grey day, it softens. At night, low lights along the base make the letters glow.

At both entrances you will find large books, directories that list every name with its panel and line number. Volunteers and park rangers are often nearby to help. Many visitors make a rubbing. They place a strip of paper over a name and rub it gently with a pencil. The letters rise up in grey on the white paper. It is a small, physical way to carry a name home.

You may notice objects at the foot of the Wall. Flowers, letters, photographs, military medals, cans of beer, small flags, children's drawings. People have left things here since the first days. The National Park Service collects these items and keeps many of them in a special archive. Over the years, it has stored a very large collection of these private offerings, each one a message to someone on the stone.

Before you leave, walk to the statue near the entrance. It shows three young servicemen in bronze, by the sculptor Frederick Hart. They seem to look toward the Wall, toward the names. A short walk away is the Vietnam Women's Memorial, added in 1993. It honours the women who served, many of them as nurses. Together, these pieces complete the story the Wall begins.

As you climb back up the path, notice how the stone slowly lowers beside you. The names grow fewer. The city returns, the sound of traffic, the voices, the open sky. Turn once and look back. The black V lies quietly in the grass, almost hidden. From here, it is easy to miss. Up close, it holds every one of those names, and your reflection with them.

Practical tips
