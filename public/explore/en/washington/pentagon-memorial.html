The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel first. It shifts and crunches under every step, a loose, pale surface that never quite lets you walk silently. Then listen past it. Beneath the low benches around you, water moves in shallow pools, a soft and steady sound. Overhead, the jets from Reagan National Airport pass now and then. Each one is a reminder of why this place exists.

You are standing at the Pentagon Memorial. It sits on the southwest side of the Pentagon, just across the Potomac River from Washington, in Arlington, Virginia. On the morning of September 11, 2001, American Airlines Flight 77 was flown into this side of the building. One hundred and eighty-four people were killed. Fifty-nine of them were on the plane. One hundred and twenty-five were inside the Pentagon.

The memorial opened on September 11, 2008, seven years after the attack. It was the first national memorial to the events of that day to be completed. The design came from an open competition, and the winners were two young architects, Julie Beckman and Keith Kaseman. Their idea was simple. There would be no single monument and no tall statue. Instead, there would be one place for each person, set on open ground.

Look around you, and you will see those places. There are 184 of them. Each one is a long, curved bench of brushed steel that sweeps up from the ground and seems to float in the air. Under each bench is a small pool of flowing water. At the end of each bench, a name is engraved. The benches all point in the same general direction, like a field of lines frozen in motion.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. The benches are not arranged by alphabet or by rank. They are arranged by age. Thin steel strips run across the gravel. These are called age lines, and each one stands for a birth year. The youngest victim was three years old. The oldest was seventy-one. Every person sits on the line of the year they were born.

And there is a second detail, quieter still. Walk to the end of a bench and read the name. Now notice which way you are facing. For the people who died inside the Pentagon, you read the name while facing the building. For the people on Flight 77, you read the name while facing away from it, toward the sky the plane came through. Two groups, two directions — and one shared field.

The benches themselves are aligned with the flight path of the plane. If you follow the long lines of the memorial with your eyes, you are tracing the last moments of that flight. It is a hard thing to realise while standing here. Many visitors only notice it after they look up from a name and see the wall of the Pentagon ahead of them.

Now look at the edge of the site. A low stone wall runs along one side. It begins very low, only a few centimetres high, and slowly rises as it runs on. The rise follows the ages of the victims, from the youngest to the oldest. The wall gives the whole space a gentle sense of enclosure. It also separates the memorial from the busy road nearby.

Among the benches, you will see small trees. These are paperbark maples. Their bark peels away in thin, copper-coloured curls, and their leaves turn red and orange in autumn. The trees were chosen to give shade and to grow slowly over the years. As they mature, the memorial changes too. A visitor today sees a different place from the one that opened in 2008.

Pay attention to the small pools under the benches. Some carry more than one name. Where family members died together, their names are linked, so a visitor can see who was lost alongside whom. Near the youngest age lines, the names belong to children who were on the plane with their families, heading west on what should have been an ordinary trip.

People leave things here. You may see a single flower on a bench, a coin, a folded note, a small flag, or a photograph tucked against the steel. These offerings come and go. Some are left by family members on birthdays. Others are left by strangers who simply want to stop for a moment. Please leave them where they are.

If you can, come back after dark. At night, the pools under each bench are lit from below. The light shines up through the water and onto the steel, so each bench glows softly against the gravel. The building behind becomes a dark shape, and the field of lights in front of it looks very calm. Many people find the night visit the most moving one.

This is still an active military headquarters. You will see security staff, and there are rules about photographing the Pentagon itself. Read the signs at the entrance and follow them. Inside the memorial, keep your voice low, and take your time. There is no right order in which to walk. Some people look for one name. Others walk every age line, from start to end.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Hear the water under the benches. Feel the gravel shift under your shoes. Look at the light on the steel and the curled bark of the maples. Then turn toward the building, and toward the sky beside it. When the next plane passes overhead, you will hear it differently than you did when you arrived.
