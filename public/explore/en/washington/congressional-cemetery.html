The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees you might hear dog tags jingling, a woodpecker in an old oak, and somewhere beyond the wall, the low hum of traffic near the Anacostia River. The grass is soft and uneven underfoot. Rows of pale stone blocks run across the slope. They look almost like a small city of their own. You are on Capitol Hill, only a short walk from the halls of Congress. Yet this place moves at a very different speed.

This is Congressional Cemetery, one of the oldest burial grounds in the nation's capital. It began in 1807 as the burial ground of Washington Parish, connected to Christ Church, an Episcopal congregation nearby. At that time Washington was a muddy, half-built town. The young federal government had no official place to bury its own. So this quiet parish ground slowly became the place where the capital laid its dead to rest.

The name comes from that close relationship with Congress. In the early nineteenth century, a member of Congress who died in office could not easily be carried home. Roads were rough, and journeys took many days. So many lawmakers were buried here, close to the Capitol. Over time, Congress bought burial sites and helped support the grounds. The link grew so strong that the parish ground became known by the name it still carries today.

Now look at those rows of squat, blocky monuments. They are cenotaphs — memorials that honour a person whose body may lie somewhere else entirely. Their design is credited to Benjamin Henry Latrobe, one of the architects of the United States Capitol. Each one is made of sandstone, with a square base and a rounded, dome-like top. Here is the surprise. Many of these stones stand over empty ground. The person they name is buried hundreds of kilometres away.

For decades, Congress placed a cenotaph here for almost every member who died in office, wherever they were actually buried. The rows grew longer and longer. Not everyone admired them. A well-known story says one senator joked that the thought of being honoured with one added a new terror to death. Whether he said it exactly that way or not, the custom faded in the later nineteenth century. The stones stayed, and they are now among the most recognisable sights here.

Walk a little further and you will find a stone vault built into the ground. This is the Public Vault, a holding tomb for bodies waiting to be moved or buried. Some very famous people rested here for a time. Presidents John Quincy Adams, William Henry Harrison, and Zachary Taylor were all placed in this vault before their final journeys. Dolley Madison, the wife of President James Madison, rested here too. It is easy to walk past without knowing.

The permanent residents are just as remarkable. John Philip Sousa, the composer famous for American military marches, is buried here. He was born in Washington and grew up close to the Marine Barracks, not far from these grounds. Elbridge Gerry, a signer of the Declaration of Independence and a vice president, lies here as well. His name lives on in the word gerrymandering — the practice of drawing voting districts to favour one party.

Other graves tell darker or more complicated stories. J. Edgar Hoover, the long-serving director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation, is buried here. His close companion and deputy, Clyde Tolson, lies only a short distance away. The photographer Mathew Brady also rests in these grounds. His camera recorded many of the people and battlefields of the Civil War. Pushmataha, a Choctaw leader who died while visiting Washington in the 1820s, was buried here too.

One small headstone draws visitors from across the country. It belongs to Leonard Matlovich, a Vietnam War veteran and an early public voice for gay service members. His stone does not carry his name. Instead, it carries these words: When I was in the military, they gave me a medal for killing two men and a discharge for loving one. Today the area around his grave has become a place where other people from the gay community have chosen to be buried.

There is also a memorial to a terrible accident during the Civil War. In 1864, an explosion at the Washington Arsenal killed a group of young women. They had been working there, making ammunition. Many were Irish immigrants. Their funeral was a large public event in a city at war. A tall monument in this cemetery honours them. It is a reminder that the war touched ordinary working families here in the capital, not only soldiers on distant fields.

By the later twentieth century, these grounds had fallen into poor condition. Stones were broken, and weeds grew tall. Then local neighbours began to help. A preservation association took over care of the site. One of their ideas was unusual. Dog owners could join a program to walk their dogs here, and that support helped pay for upkeep. Their presence also brought more people and more eyes to the grounds. In some years, goats have even been brought in to clear the overgrowth.

So this is not a silent, sealed-off place. Neighbours jog along the paths. Families stop to read old inscriptions. People come for tours, talks, and special events through the year. The cemetery is still an active burial ground, too, so new stones stand beside very old ones. That mix is part of its character. Here, the history of the capital and the daily life of Capitol Hill share the same shade.

Before you leave, take one more slow look around. Notice the weathered sandstone of the cenotaphs, softened by more than two centuries of rain. Look at the names, the carved lambs and urns, the small flags, the fresh flowers on a stone you may never have heard of. Every one of them was once a person with a story. As you walk back toward the city streets, listen again for the jingle of a dog tag... and the trees moving quietly overhead.
