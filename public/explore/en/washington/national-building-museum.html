The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Your footsteps carry far across the floor, and the sound drifts upward into a space so large it seems to hold its own weather. Light falls from high windows onto red brick, cream plaster, and columns that look far too tall to be real. You are inside one of the largest enclosed rooms in Washington, and almost everything around you is made of brick.

This is the National Building Museum. For most of its life, though, it had a plainer name: the Pension Building. It went up in the 1880s to house the Pension Bureau, the federal office that paid benefits to Union veterans of the American Civil War. It also paid their widows and their orphans. After that war, the number of claims was enormous, and the government needed a single large workplace for the clerks who handled them.

The man behind the design was Montgomery C. Meigs. He was not a trained architect by profession. He was an army engineer, and during the Civil War he served as Quartermaster General of the Union Army, the officer in charge of supplying troops with food, clothing, and equipment. Meigs had already shaped this city. He oversaw the Washington Aqueduct, which brought fresh water into the capital, and he played a part in work on the Capitol itself.

Meigs looked to Italy for inspiration. The outside of the building draws on the grand Renaissance palaces of Rome, especially the Palazzo Farnese — a sixteenth-century family palace known for its strong, orderly walls and rows of windows. But Meigs had a practical goal as well. He wanted a building that would not burn. Paper records filled government offices in that era, and fire was a constant fear. So he chose brick, and he used millions of them.

Here is the first surprise. Look up at the giant columns in the centre of the hall. They seem to be polished marble, warm and mottled in tones of gold and brown. They are not. Each column is built of brick, covered in plaster, and painted to imitate marble. They rise roughly seventy-five feet, and they rank among the tallest interior columns in the world. The illusion is so convincing that many visitors leave without ever knowing.

Meigs also thought carefully about air and light. In the years before electric fans and air conditioning, a room full of clerks in a Washington summer could become very hot. The open hall works like a giant chimney. Warm air rises toward the high windows, and fresh air enters lower down. Look closely at the brickwork beneath the outer windows. In places, small openings let air move through the walls. It is a quiet piece of engineering, hidden in plain sight.

The second surprise is on the outside, so remember to walk around the building before you leave. A long band of terra-cotta runs around the walls, between the first and second floors. This frieze — a continuous sculpted strip — was created by the sculptor Caspar Buberl. It shows a parade of Union forces: infantry marching, cavalry on horseback, artillery wagons, sailors at work. Supply wagons and quartermaster scenes appear too, a fitting tribute to Meigs's own wartime role.

The frieze is not a single heroic battle scene. It is a long procession of ordinary soldiers and sailors, repeated and varied along the walls. That choice matters. The building existed to serve veterans, and the frieze places those veterans on its outer skin, at eye level from the street. If you follow it slowly, you will notice small changes in the figures, the animals, and the equipment as the procession moves around the corners.

Not everyone admired the building at first. A popular story claims that General William Tecumseh Sherman, the famous Union commander, looked at it and said the worst thing about it was that it was fireproof. The tale is told often, but it is best treated as a well-loved anecdote rather than a confirmed quote. Either way, it captures how bold and unusual this red-brick giant seemed to some Washingtonians in its day.

The great hall has always been a gathering place. Even before the building was finished, it hosted a presidential inaugural ball in the 1880s, and many more inaugural celebrations followed over the decades. Picture the hall decorated with flags and garlands, filled with music and evening clothes. Today it still hosts large events, and it is also known for ambitious summer installations — playful structures built inside the hall that turn the vast floor into a space for exploring.

The pension offices eventually moved out, and other government agencies used the building for years. By the middle of the twentieth century, some people saw it as old-fashioned and even talked about tearing it down. Preservation advocates pushed back. In 1980, Congress created a museum dedicated to architecture, design, engineering, and the building trades, and this structure became its home. It is a museum about building, housed inside one of the city's most remarkable buildings.

That match makes the visit unusual. In most museums, the walls are simply a backdrop for the objects. Here, the walls are part of the lesson. As you move through the galleries, try to notice the building itself — the arched openings of the upper floors, the rhythm of the balconies, the way the brick changes colour in shade and in sunlight. Exhibitions come and go, but the hall stays, and it rewards slow looking.

Before you go, find a quiet spot near one of the columns and look straight up. Follow the painted marble to its capital, then let your eye travel across the arcades to the windows and the roof. Listen again to the echo of voices below. When you step back outside, onto the brick and stone of Judiciary Square, glance once more at the procession of soldiers in clay. They have been marching around this building for well over a century.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full outside perimeter to follow the terra-cotta frieze.
Arrive on a sunny day to see light through high windows.
Use Judiciary Square Metro station for the easiest arrival.
Check ahead for special events that may close the hall.
Climb to an upper arcade for a view across the hall.
