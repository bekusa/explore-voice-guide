The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Taxis hum along Massachusetts Avenue, a bus sighs to a stop, and somewhere a siren fades toward Capitol Hill. Then look at the small patch of green in front of you. On a stone pedestal stands a bronze woman, both hands raised, holding a torch above her head. She is not tall by Washington standards. Yet she carries the weight of a much larger story, and that story begins far from here.

This is the Victims of Communism Memorial. It sits on a small triangle of land where Massachusetts Avenue and New Jersey Avenue meet. Union Station is a short walk away. The dome of the Capitol is only a few blocks south. Thousands of commuters pass this corner every day. Many never stop. That is part of what makes the place unusual. It is a memorial to enormous loss, set quietly into an ordinary city intersection.

The figure is a bronze copy of a statue that existed for only a few days. In the spring of 1989, students gathered in Tiananmen Square, in the centre of Beijing, to call for political reform. Art students built a tall white figure from foam and papier-mâché over a metal frame. They called her the Goddess of Democracy. They placed her so that she faced the giant portrait of Mao Zedong, the founder of the People's Republic of China.

She did not survive. On the night of the third and fourth of June, 1989, the Chinese army moved into the square to end the protests. Soldiers pulled down the statue and destroyed it. Many people were killed in and around Beijing that night. The exact number is still unknown, because the government has never allowed an open count. The original goddess lasted less than a week... but photographs of her travelled around the world.

Here is the first surprise. The statue in front of you was not cast from the original, because there was nothing left to cast. The sculptor Thomas Marsh worked from photographs and film of the Beijing figure. He made her in bronze, a material meant to last for centuries. So a statue built quickly from fragile materials, and destroyed in a single night, now stands in permanent metal on another continent.

Why use a Chinese image for a memorial about communism around the world? The planners wanted one symbol that could speak for many countries at once. The goddess was made by ordinary people under a communist government, asking for freedom. She was not a monument made by a state. She was a monument made against one. That choice tells you a lot about how this memorial wants to be read.

The idea for the memorial took shape in the early 1990s, just after the Soviet Union collapsed. Congress authorised the project in 1993, and the law was signed by President Bill Clinton. The government gave approval and the site, but not the money. A private foundation, the Victims of Communism Memorial Foundation, raised the funds. It took more than a decade to move from law to bronze.

The memorial was dedicated on 12 June 2007, by President George W. Bush. That date was chosen with care. It was exactly twenty years after Ronald Reagan stood at the Berlin Wall and called on the Soviet leader to tear the wall down. So this small park links two cities, Beijing and Berlin, and two very different moments at the end of the Cold War.

Now walk closer and read the inscriptions on the pedestal. One side dedicates the memorial to the more than one hundred million victims of communism, and to those who love liberty. Another side is dedicated to the freedom and independence of all captive nations and peoples. The figure of one hundred million is an estimate. Historians still debate how to count deaths from famine, labour camps, executions and war across many countries.

Think about the scale for a moment. The victims this memorial remembers lived in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, in China, Cambodia, North Korea, Cuba, and other places. They spoke dozens of languages. Some died in Siberian labour camps. Some died in famines. Some simply disappeared. No single statue could hold all of those stories. Instead, the designers chose one raised torch and left the rest to the visitor.

Here is the second surprise. On some days you may find small objects at the base of the statue. Flowers, candles, sometimes a handwritten note in a language you do not recognise. Each year, around the anniversary of the dedication, diplomats and families from many countries gather here for a wreath-laying ceremony. Communities from Ukraine, the Baltic states, Vietnam and elsewhere use this corner as a shared place of remembrance. For them, it is not history. It is family.

Look at the details of the figure itself. Her face is calm, not angry. Her robes fall in simple folds, close to the style of the Beijing original. The torch is lifted with both hands, not one. Visitors often compare her with the Statue of Liberty in New York. The students in Beijing knew that image too. They borrowed it, changed it, and made it their own. Bronze now holds that conversation between two continents.

The setting matters as well. This is not the National Mall, with its long lawns and crowds. It is a city corner with traffic lights, office buildings and people walking to work. Some visitors find that disappointing at first. Others find it fitting. The people this memorial remembers were mostly ordinary citizens, living ordinary lives, until history reached their street. Here, the memorial lives on an ordinary street too.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute and look up at the torch against the sky. Notice the sound of the traffic, the people crossing at the light, the small benches and planted borders around the base. Then look south toward the Capitol dome. As you walk away, you carry a simple picture with you: a statue that lasted five days in Beijing, now standing quietly in Washington, still holding her light up.
