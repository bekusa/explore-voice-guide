The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The ground here is flat, open grass, wide enough that the wind moves freely across it. The sound of the city drops back. You hear footsteps on the curving path, the hum of traffic on Constitution Avenue, and maybe a flag snapping somewhere overhead. Look north, past the iron fence. The white south face of the White House sits at the end of the lawn, closer than many visitors expect.

This oval of grass is the Ellipse. Its official name is President's Park South. It belongs to the larger President's Park, the open ground around the White House, and the National Park Service looks after it. The name comes from its shape. A road and a footpath trace an almost perfect oval, and inside it lies a single sweep of lawn. Few places in the city give you this much sky.

The open ground is not an accident. When the capital was planned at the end of the eighteenth century, the land south of the President's House was meant to stay open. Planners wanted a clear line of sight from the mansion toward the river. For decades, though, the area was rough and marshy. A canal ran nearby, and the low ground was often wet and unpleasant, especially in summer.

The Civil War changed this field completely. Union troops camped here, and the grounds filled with horses, mules, wagons, and supply corrals. Soldiers and teamsters came and went every day, within sight of the president's windows. In those years, people called the area the White Lot, after the whitewashed fence that once surrounded it. It is hard to picture now. Where you see smooth lawn, there was mud, noise, and the smell of livestock.

After the war, engineers drained and graded the ground. By the late nineteenth century, it had taken the oval form you see today. The grass became a public space for the people of Washington. Over the years, residents played sports here, held gatherings, and walked in the evenings. The Ellipse became a kind of front yard for the city, shared between the president and everyone else.

Now walk toward the north edge of the oval, close to the White House fence. Look down near the path for a small, square granite post. It stands only about knee high, and many visitors walk right past it. This is the Zero Milestone, dedicated in 1923. The idea was that distances on American highways would be measured from this spot. On its top, look for a compass rose, a circle marked with the points of direction.

That same year, 1923, began another tradition on these grounds. President Calvin Coolidge lit the first National Christmas Tree. Since then, the lighting has become a yearly event. In winter, a large tree stands on the Ellipse, surrounded by smaller trees that represent the states and territories. If you come in December, the lawn fills with lights, music, and families in heavy coats. In other seasons, the same spot is simply open grass.

Here is a detail that surprises many people. Near the northwest corner of the Ellipse stands a small marble fountain. It honours two men, Archibald Butt and Francis Millet. Butt was a military aide to the president, and Millet was an artist. Both men died in 1912, when the Titanic sank in the North Atlantic. Their friends raised this quiet memorial to them. It is one of the few monuments to the Titanic in the city.

The Ellipse holds several other memorials around its edges. On the west side, a tall column topped with a gilded figure of Victory honours the First Division of the United States Army. To the south, near Constitution Avenue, a large monument honours the Second Division. Its centre is a stone doorway with a flaming sword held by a bronze hand. Elsewhere you will find a memorial to the Boy Scouts and a granite marker for the early settlers of the District.

Now look for the second surprise, at the southern corners of the Ellipse, along Constitution Avenue. Two small stone buildings stand there. They look like tiny classical temples, with simple columns and carved details. These are gatehouses designed by the architect Charles Bulfinch for the grounds of the United States Capitol. Workers later moved them here, a long way from their original home. Most people pass them without knowing they once guarded Capitol Hill.

Turn around and face southeast. The Washington Monument rises above the trees, a white stone shaft against the sky. From the Ellipse, you can see how the city's great buildings connect. The White House stands to the north, the monument stands to the southeast, and the National Mall stretches beyond it. This oval is a hinge between the president's home and the public grounds of the nation.

The Ellipse has also been a place for public voices. Crowds have gathered here for rallies, protests, celebrations, and concerts. Permits and security rules shape what happens on the lawn, and fences sometimes close parts of the grounds. On a quiet weekday, though, you may share the grass with only a few joggers, a school group, and a pair of tourists taking pictures through the fence.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the centre of the lawn. Notice the curve of the path around you, and how the oval holds the space together. Listen for the traffic, the wind, and the voices of other visitors. Look once more at the white house to the north and the white stone shaft to the southeast. As you walk away, keep an eye on the ground near the path. Small markers here are easy to miss, and each one carries a piece of the city's story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full oval path to find every memorial.
Come in December to see the National Christmas Tree display.
Expect security fences and closures near the White House side.
Bring water on summer days because the lawn has little shade.
Visit on a weekday morning for a quieter lawn.
