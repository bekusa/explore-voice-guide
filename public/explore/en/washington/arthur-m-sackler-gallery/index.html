The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you walk in. Traffic hums along Independence Avenue, and somewhere behind you the gravel paths of the garden crunch under other visitors' shoes. Then you look at the building in front of you. It is a small granite pavilion, modest and almost shy. It does not look large enough to hold a major museum. That is the first surprise here. Most of what you are about to see is not in front of you at all. It lies underground.

This is the Arthur M. Sackler Gallery, part of the Smithsonian Institution. Today it forms one half of the National Museum of Asian Art. The other half is the older Freer Gallery of Art, the stately building to your west. The two galleries share a mission and a staff, and they are linked below the surface. You can walk from one to the other without stepping outside. Many visitors never realise they have crossed from one museum into another.

The gallery opened to the public in 1987. Its founding gift came from Arthur M. Sackler, a New York physician and medical publisher. He was also one of the most active collectors of Asian art in the twentieth century. He gave the Smithsonian roughly a thousand objects, along with funds toward the building. Sadly, he died in 1987, only months before the doors opened. He never walked through the finished galleries.

The name on this building carries a complicated story today. Members of the wider Sackler family later became linked, through the company Purdue Pharma, to the opioid crisis in the United States. Arthur himself died years before that company's best-known painkiller reached the market. Even so, the family name has been debated in museums across the world. Several institutions have removed it from their walls. Here, that public conversation is part of the building's modern life.

Now think about why so much of the museum sits below ground. The answer is the land around you. This site lies on the National Mall, right behind the red sandstone Smithsonian Castle. Planners wanted new museum space without crowding the historic skyline. So the architects hid most of the building beneath a garden. The result is a complex often called the Quadrangle. Its visible pavilions are small, but the galleries below spread out wide and deep.

The garden on top is the Enid A. Haupt Garden. It is worth a slow walk before or after your visit. Near the Sackler entrance, look for the Moongate Garden. It uses circles and squares of pink granite and still water, drawing on traditional Chinese design. In Chinese thought, the circle was often linked with heaven and the square with earth. Across the garden, near the National Museum of African Art, a different garden takes ideas from Islamic courtyard design.

Notice how the two pavilions answer each other. The Sackler pavilion rises in sharp, pyramid-like roof forms. Its neighbour, the African Art pavilion, uses rounded domes. Angles on one side, curves on the other. The design makes a quiet conversation across the garden. When you step through the Sackler doors, you begin a descent. Stairs and a light well carry you down, level by level, into cooler and calmer air.

Down here, the city noise falls away. The light becomes softer and more controlled. This is deliberate. Many of the objects in these rooms are fragile. Old paper, silk, ink, and pigments fade quickly under strong light. So the galleries often feel dim and hushed, almost like a library at night. Give your eyes a minute to adjust. Small details on a scroll or a manuscript page will slowly become clearer.

The collection reaches across a huge part of the world. Chinese art is a deep strength, especially ancient jades and bronzes. Some of these bronzes were made for ritual use thousands of years ago. They held food and wine offered to ancestors. Look closely at their surfaces. Many carry patterns of stylised creatures and masks, and a green crust that formed over centuries in the ground. That crust is not paint. It is the slow chemistry of time on metal.

The gallery also holds important art from the Islamic world, Persia, South Asia, Japan, and Korea. Illustrated manuscripts from Iran and India are a highlight when they are on view. Their colours can still look bright, because they were kept closed in books for centuries. Japanese prints and painted screens appear in rotating shows too. Because paper and silk need rest from light, the displays change often. What you see today may be gone in a few months.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Freer side of the museum has strict rules from its founder. Charles Lang Freer, a businessman, gave his collection in the early twentieth century with firm conditions. For a long time those rules limited lending and borrowing. The Sackler gave the Smithsonian more freedom. It could host travelling exhibitions and borrow from other museums. In a sense, this underground gallery became the flexible partner of its strict older neighbour.

If you have time, follow the underground passage into the Freer. There you can see the Peacock Room, a dining room painted by the American artist James McNeill Whistler. It is covered in deep blue-green and gold, with painted peacocks spreading across the walls. It was moved here from London. Standing in it after the quiet Sackler rooms feels like opening a jewellery box. The contrast tells you a lot about how differently collectors once displayed Asian art.

Take a moment before you leave. Notice how calm the lower galleries feel, how the air seems still and measured. Then climb back toward the light. When you step outside, the garden opens around you again, and the Castle's red towers rise above the trees. You may glance down at the gravel and the flower beds. Remember that beneath your feet, in the cool dark, centuries of art are resting quietly.
