The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the wind. Up on this ridge, east of the Kathmandu Valley, the air moves differently. It comes over the hills cool and steady, it tugs at prayer flags, and it carries the faint ring of a temple bell. Then you lift your eyes, and a figure of Shiva fills the sky, its metal skin catching the light.

This is the Kailashnath Mahadev Statue. It stands at Sanga, on the edge of the Kathmandu Valley, where the road climbs out toward Banepa and the eastern hills. If you came here from Boudhanath, you crossed most of the valley floor to reach it. The trip from the great white stupa to this hilltop is a short one on a map. In spirit, it is a long way. You leave a Buddhist world of circling pilgrims and butter lamps, and you arrive at one of the largest Hindu images in the Himalaya.

First, the name. Mahadev means 'the great god', and it is one of the most common names for Shiva. Shiva is one of the central gods of Hinduism, often described as both destroyer and renewer. Kailash is the sacred mountain in Tibet where tradition says Shiva lives. So the name, Kailashnath, means 'Lord of Kailash'. In a country where the high peaks are always close, that name carries real weight.

Now the scale. The statue is usually given as 143 feet tall, roughly forty-four metres. For years it was promoted as the tallest statue of Shiva in the world. Larger Shiva figures have since been built in India, so that title has passed on. But stand at the base and look up, and the numbers stop mattering. The figure is simply enormous, taller than any building you can see from here.

The statue is a modern creation, not an ancient one. It was built in the first decade of this century and opened to the public in the early 2010s. That surprises many visitors. In a valley full of temples many centuries old, this is one of the newest sacred landmarks. Its builders were private sponsors, not a medieval king. It shows that the habit of building for the gods is still alive in Nepal today.

Look at the surface. The figure is built over a structure of concrete and steel, and it is clad in metal, with copper and zinc commonly named among the materials. That is why the colour changes so much through the day. In soft morning light it looks warm and bronze. Under a hard midday sun it can look almost silver. When monsoon clouds roll in, it turns dark and heavy against the grey.

Shiva is one of the easiest gods to recognise, once you know the signs. Many images show him with a trident, a three-pointed spear that stands for his power. A snake often curls around his neck. His hair is piled high in matted locks, the hairstyle of a wandering holy man. Take a moment to find these details on the statue. Each one tells a devotee a story they have known since childhood.

Here is something many visitors miss. Shiva is often shown as a great yogi, a god of stillness and meditation as much as of power. That is why the figure feels calm rather than fierce. Look at the face. The expression is quiet, almost inward. On a hilltop this exposed, with the wind and the wide view, that stillness is the point. The statue invites you to slow down.

Turn around now, away from the statue. The view is half the reason people come up here. On a clear day the Kathmandu Valley spreads out below you, a patchwork of terraced fields, brick houses and rising haze. To the north, if the weather is kind, you may see a white line of Himalayan peaks above the hills. Winter mornings after rain usually give the sharpest view.

Notice the people around you, too. Some are pilgrims, here to pray. They may ring a bell, offer flowers, or touch their foreheads before an image. Others are families on a day out from Kathmandu, taking photographs and buying snacks. You may also hear devotional songs playing from a speaker. Here, worship and leisure sit side by side, and nobody finds that strange.

There is one more surprise. Sanga sits close to the border between the Kathmandu Valley and Kavrepalanchok, the district that runs east toward the Sun Kosi River. For centuries, traders and travellers passed through this gap in the hills. The ridge was a gateway long before the statue arrived. Today the god stands at that same threshold, facing the valley and the old road home.

A few words of respect. This is an active place of worship, not only a viewpoint. Remove your shoes where you see others do it. Walk around shrines in a clockwise direction, the custom here in both Hindu and Buddhist practice. Ask before you photograph anyone at prayer. Leather items may be unwelcome near the inner shrine areas, so watch for signs or follow the lead of local visitors.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the wind again. Listen for the bell, and for the flags snapping on their lines. Look up once more at the metal face, now perhaps a different colour than when you arrived. Then look down the valley toward Boudhanath, somewhere in the haze. Two very different sacred places, one landscape... and you have now stood in both.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning for clearer air and mountain views.
Bring a warm layer; the ridge is often windy.
Arrange a car or taxi from Kathmandu for the drive.
Remove shoes where you see local worshippers doing so.
Walk clockwise around shrines, following local custom.
