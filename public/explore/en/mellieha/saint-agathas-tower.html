The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here on the ridge it rarely stops. It comes off the sea in long, salty gusts, and it carries the dry smell of wild thyme and sun-baked limestone. Under your feet the ground is rough and pale, broken by low scrub and old field walls. Then you look up, and there it is. A square block of fortress, painted a deep, rusty red, standing hard against the blue sky. Locals rarely use its formal name. They simply call it the Red Tower.

You are standing on Marfa Ridge, the long finger of land at the northern tip of Malta. Officially, this is Saint Agatha's Tower, named for a saint deeply loved on these islands. Agatha was an early Christian martyr from Sicily, just across the water to the north. She became a protector of Malta, and her name appears on churches and chapels across the country. Giving her name to a fortress was a kind of prayer. It asked for protection from the sea, and from whatever might sail in across it.

The tower dates from the middle of the seventeenth century. It was built in the late 1640s for the Knights of Saint John, the religious military order that ruled Malta for more than two hundred and fifty years. The Grand Master at the time was Giovanni Paolo Lascaris, a French noble of Italian descent. His name is linked to a series of coastal towers raised during his rule. The engineer usually credited with the design is Antonio Garsin, a military architect who served the Order.

Why build a fortress here, on an empty ridge? Look out across the water and the answer is in front of you. To the west lies the island of Gozo. Between Malta and Gozo sits little Comino, with the open channel on either side. For centuries, corsairs and raiders used these channels. They struck small coastal villages, took captives, and vanished back to sea. A watch post on this ridge could see boats long before they reached the shore.

The tower was more than a lookout. It was one link in a chain. Each tower along the coast could see at least one other. When a lookout spotted danger, the garrison sent signals with fire at night and smoke by day. The warning passed from tower to tower, faster than any rider could carry it. From this ridge, the signal could reach Gozo, and the defenders on Comino, and travel on toward the heart of Malta.

Now look closely at the shape. The tower is a square keep with a small, square turret at each corner. These corner turrets let defenders cover the walls below from several angles. The walls slope outward at the base, a feature called a batter. This thickened the foot of the walls against attack and made it harder for anyone to undermine them. It is a compact building, but every line of it was designed for defence.

The tower was also built to stand alone. A garrison here could not count on quick help. So the building held stores of food, water and powder, enough to hold out for a time against a siege. Soldiers lived, slept and kept watch within these thick stone walls. Imagine the long nights here, with only the wind, the dark sea, and the lamps of a few fishing boats far below.

And then, of course, there is the colour. Most old buildings in Malta wear the warm honey tone of local limestone. This one does not. The deep red stands out from almost every direction, and that may well have been part of the point. A red tower on a high ridge is hard to miss. Historians still discuss how the tower looked over the centuries. Today, the red has become its identity, and the reason for its everyday name.

The tower did not retire when the Knights left. Malta later came under British rule, and the military kept using this high ground. During the Second World War, the ridge served as a radar and signals site, watching the sea and the sky. In the decades after, the armed forces of independent Malta also used the area. So the same job continued for about three hundred years. People stood here and watched the horizon for whatever was coming.

By the late twentieth century, though, the building had suffered. Weather, neglect and military use had all left their mark. Its rescue came from Din l-Art Ħelwa, a Maltese heritage trust. The name means, roughly, "the land is sweet", and the trust cares for historic sites across the islands. Volunteers and specialists restored the tower and opened it to visitors. Much of what you see today exists because ordinary people decided this place was worth saving.

If the tower is open when you visit, go inside and climb to the roof. The rooms are simple and cool, with heavy walls that keep out the heat. From the top, the whole northern seascape opens up. You can follow the coast of Gozo and pick out the low shape of Comino. Turn the other way, and you look back over Mellieħa Bay, with its long curve of sand and the town on the hillside above.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Stand on the roof and try to spot the other towers. On a clear day you may pick out Saint Mary's Tower on Comino, an older and larger fort of the Knights. Seeing it with your own eyes makes the whole signal chain real. That small square on the distant island was a partner in the same system of watching and warning that guarded these waters for generations.

The ridge itself is worth a little time. This is open country, with rocky garrigue, a low Mediterranean scrub of tough, scented plants. In spring, small wild flowers push up between the stones. In summer, the land turns gold and dry, and the cicadas fill the air with their steady buzz. Birds ride the wind along the cliffs. It is one of the quieter corners of a busy island, and that quiet is part of its character.

Before you leave, stand back from the walls for a moment. Listen to the wind again, the same wind the soldiers heard. Look at the red stone against the sky, and then out to the channel, where ferries now cross calmly between the islands. Nobody here watches for raiders any more. But the tower still keeps its post on the ridge... and as you walk away, turn around once. You will see how far its colour carries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Bring water and sun protection for the exposed ridge.
Wear sturdy shoes for the rocky ground around the tower.
Climb to the roof if the interior is open.
Choose a clear day for the longest views to Gozo.
