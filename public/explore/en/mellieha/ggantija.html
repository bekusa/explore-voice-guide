The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. Under your shoes the path is pale and dusty, and the air smells of dry earth and wild fennel. A low breeze comes up from the fields of Gozo. Ahead of you, a curved wall of rough honey-coloured stone rises from the ridge. Some of those blocks are taller than you are. People set them in place long before anyone on Earth had written a single word.

A quick note on where you are standing. Ġgantija is not in Mellieħa itself. It sits on the Xagħra plateau on Gozo, the smaller island to the north of Malta. Many visitors start from the Mellieħa side and cross on the ferry from Ċirkewwa, at the northern tip of Malta. So if the trip felt like a small journey, that is right. You crossed a stretch of sea to reach one of the oldest buildings in the world.

How old? These temples date to the Neolithic period, the late Stone Age. The oldest parts go back to roughly 3600 BCE. That makes Ġgantija older than Stonehenge in England. It is also older than the great pyramids of Egypt. The people who built it had no metal tools and no wheel, as far as we know. They worked with stone, bone, wood, rope, and enormous patience.

The name comes from the Maltese word for giant. And that brings us to the local legend. Folklore says a giantess built these walls. In one popular version she carried the huge stones on her head, while holding a baby in her other arm. She built by day and night, and fed on beans and honey. It is only a story, of course. But stand beside the largest blocks, and you can see why earlier islanders reached for giants.

What you see is actually two temples, side by side. They share one great outer wall, so from a distance they look like a single structure. The southern temple is the older and the larger one. The northern temple was added later and is a little smaller. Both face roughly the same direction, toward the southeast. Each one opens onto the wide, curved space in front of the facade.

That curved space is a forecourt. Archaeologists believe gatherings took place here, outside the temple doors. Picture a crowd of farming families standing on this terrace. Some of them may have brought animals, grain, or offerings. Behind them, the land falls away toward the sea. In front of them stood a high wall of stone and a single dark doorway leading inside.

Now look closely at the stone itself. Two kinds of local limestone were used. The outer wall uses a hard, rugged coralline limestone, rough and full of pits. Inside, the builders often chose globigerina limestone, which is softer, smoother, and easier to shape. So the tough stone faces the weather, and the finer stone sits indoors. It was a sensible choice, and it shows how well these builders knew their island.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. The space between the outer and inner walls was packed with rubble and earth. That fill made the whole structure stable. It may also have helped support a roof. Nobody knows exactly how the temples were covered. Some researchers think of timber beams. Others suggest the walls curved inward with stepped stone. The honest answer is that the roofs are gone, and the debate continues.

Step through the main entrance of the southern temple. Inside, the plan opens into a series of rounded chambers called apses. They branch off a central passage, a little like the petals of a flower. In the southern temple there are five of these lobes. As you walk deeper, the space feels more enclosed. In the past it would have been dim, lit perhaps by fire or by the light from the doorway.

Inside the chambers, look for altars, niches, and low stone features. You may see a large hearth area and small holes cut into the floor. Some may have held liquid offerings. Excavations around the Maltese temples found animal bones, which suggests that sacrifice or feasting took place. There were once traces of red ochre plaster on the inner walls as well. Imagine these rooms painted, smoky, and full of people.

After the temple builders vanished, the site slowly filled with soil and stone. For thousands of years it was a mound of ruins on a hilltop. In the 1820s, the site was cleared of earth, sadly without careful recording. Much information was lost in that rush. Even so, artists of the period made drawings of the ruins. Those images now help us understand what the temples looked like before modern conservation.

Today Ġgantija is part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing, together with other megalithic temples on Malta and Gozo. A visitor centre near the entrance tells the story of the site. It displays objects found on Gozo, including figures and pottery. Not far away, on the same plateau, lies the Xagħra Stone Circle, an ancient underground burial site. Together they show that this hilltop was a sacred landscape, not a single building.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the forecourt once more. Run your eyes along the outer wall, block by block. Notice how the largest stones sit at the bottom, and the smaller ones rise above. Listen to the wind moving across the plateau, and the distant sounds of the village. Five thousand years of weather have touched these stones. And now, for a few quiet minutes, so have you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the ferry from Ċirkewwa to reach Gozo.
Visit early morning to avoid heat and tour groups.
Start at the visitor centre to see finds first.
Wear sturdy shoes for the dusty, uneven paths.
Bring water and a hat; there is little shade.
