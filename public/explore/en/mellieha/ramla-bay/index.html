The guide

The story

Slip off your shoes for a moment. The sand here is soft and warm, and it is not the pale gold you might expect. It is a deep orange-red, almost the colour of paprika in strong sunlight. Listen, and you will hear the low, steady roll of the sea. Behind you, the land rises in green terraces and dry hills. This is Ramla Bay, and few beaches in the Maltese islands look quite like it.

A quick word on where you are standing. Ramla Bay is not inside Mellieħa itself. It lies on the north coast of Gozo, the smaller island across the channel. Most people who stay in Mellieħa reach it by driving down to Ċirkewwa, at the northern tip of Malta, and taking the short ferry across. So if the journey felt longer than a beach trip, that is why. You have crossed water to get here.

The name itself is simple. In Maltese, "ramla" means sand, or a sandy beach. Locals often call this place Ir-Ramla il-Ħamra — the red sand. On islands that are mostly rocky coastline, a wide sweep of soft sand is unusual. That rarity is part of why Gozitan families have come here for generations, long before tour buses found the narrow road down through the valley.

So why is the sand red? The colour comes from the rocks around the bay. Over a very long time, wind and water have worn down the surrounding rock, and the iron-rich grains settle here. Pick up a handful and look closely. You will see a mix of tones — rust, amber, a little cream. In the late afternoon, when the sun drops lower, the whole beach glows warmer still.

Now turn and look toward the back of the beach. Behind the sand, you will see low dunes, held in place by tough coastal plants. These dunes are fragile and protected. They are one of the last sand dune systems of their kind in the Maltese islands. Small creatures and specialist plants depend on them. That is why you may see fences or signs asking visitors to stay off certain areas. The dunes are not decoration. They are a living habitat.

Here is something many visitors miss. Near the beach, archaeologists have found the remains of a Roman villa. Not much stands above ground today, and you need to know where to look. But it tells you something important. People in the Roman era also chose this bay. They valued the shelter of the valley, the fresh water in the land around it, and the easy landing from the sea.

That easy landing had a darker side. A wide, gentle beach is a gift to swimmers, but it is also a gift to invaders. Through the centuries, raiders could pull their boats straight onto this sand. Historical accounts record a devastating Ottoman attack on Gozo in 1551, when much of the island's population was carried off into slavery. Ramla is widely described as one of the landing places of that raid.

After attacks like that, the Knights of Saint John — the military religious order that ruled Malta for over two centuries — worked to defend the coast. At Ramla, they built fortifications to stop enemy boats. One part of these defences was a wall built out into the water, designed to block landings. Traces of these works still exist around the bay, though the sea and the sand have hidden much of them.

Look up now, toward the cliff on the western side of the bay. Up there, on the hill near the village of Xagħra, is a place known as Calypso's Cave. Legend has it that this is where the nymph Calypso kept the hero Odysseus for seven years, in Homer's ancient Greek poem, the Odyssey. Many Gozitans proudly link their island to Ogygia, Calypso's island in the story. It is a tale, not a proven fact — but a very old and much-loved one.

One honest note. In recent years, access to the cave itself has been restricted because of rockfalls and safety concerns. You may not be able to go inside. But the viewpoint near the cave is worth the climb if you have time. From above, the bay opens out below you in a clean curve — red sand, blue water, and the green valley running inland like a funnel.

Back down on the beach, you may notice a white statue of the Virgin Mary standing on a rocky base. Faith runs deep on Gozo, and you will find small chapels, statues and shrines all over the island. Here, the statue looks out over swimmers and sunbathers. Some local people see it as a quiet guardian of the bay, and of the fishermen and families who have used it for so long.

The water here is often calm and shallow near the shore, which makes it popular with families. On windier days, though, the north wind can push in strong waves. Watch for the beach flags, and keep an eye on children. In summer, simple kiosks near the road sell drinks and snacks. Out of season, the bay can feel almost empty, with only the wind, the waves and a few walkers on the sand.

The valley behind the beach is worth a glance too. Small fields run up the slope, divided by old rubble walls of stacked stone, built without mortar. This is a farming landscape, shaped by many generations of hands. In spring, the fields turn green and bright with wildflowers. By late summer, the land dries to gold and brown, and it frames the red beach like a warm picture frame.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Feel the fine sand on your feet, and notice how it clings to your skin. Look along the line of the dunes, then up to the cliff where the old story of Calypso lives on. Romans, raiders, knights and fishermen have all stood on this same red shore. Now it is your turn. Take your time — and leave the sand exactly where you found it.

Before you go

Practical tips

From Mellieħa, take the ferry from Ċirkewwa to Gozo.
Arrive early in summer to find space on the sand.
Check the beach flags before swimming on windy days.
Stay off fenced dune areas to protect the habitat.
Climb to the Calypso viewpoint for a full view of the bay.
