The guide

The story

Listen first. Water is everywhere here. It drips from moss, runs under your boots, and gathers in brown pools between tussocks of grass. The air is cool and wet, and it smells of earth and wood smoke from the villages below. Above you, the cloud sits low on the ridges. It may lift for a minute, or it may stay all day. Somewhere above that grey ceiling there is snow, almost on the equator.

You are standing at the edge of Rwenzori Mountains National Park, in western Uganda. The range runs north to south along the border with the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The park covers roughly one thousand square kilometres of steep valleys, forest, bog and rock. Uganda declared it a national park in the early 1990s. Soon after, in 1994, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List for its natural beauty and its unusual plant life.

Here is the first surprise. These mountains are not volcanoes. Kilimanjaro and Mount Kenya are both volcanic. The Rwenzori are different. They are a huge block of ancient rock, pushed upward between the faults of the Albertine Rift, the western branch of the great rift system that splits East Africa. That is why the range looks so jagged and broken. It is less a single cone and more a crowded cluster of peaks.

The highest point is Margherita Peak, on Mount Stanley. It reaches about 5,109 metres, which makes it the third highest summit in Africa. Around it stand other massifs named after nineteenth century explorers, including Mount Speke and Mount Baker. Most visitors never see the summits at all. Cloud and rain cover the high ground for much of the year, and many days pass without a clear view.

The range has carried a famous nickname for a long time — the Mountains of the Moon. The phrase goes back to Ptolemy, the Greek geographer of Alexandria, who wrote of snowy mountains feeding the source of the Nile. Nobody can prove that he meant these exact peaks. But the link stayed alive, and European travellers in the late nineteenth century were eager to find the mountains he described.

And in a way, the old idea was not wrong. The rain and melting ice from these slopes flow into rivers that feed Lake George, Lake Edward and Lake Albert. From there, the water joins the long journey of the Nile. So the drops running past your feet may one day pass through Sudan and Egypt. It is a long way from this muddy trail to the Mediterranean.

The peaks received their European names in 1906. An Italian expedition, led by the Duke of the Abruzzi, climbed and mapped many of the high summits. Margherita Peak honours the Queen of Italy at the time. But the mountains had names long before that. The people who live on these slopes have farmed, hunted and travelled here for generations, and the high ground has its own place in their stories.

Those people are mainly the Bakonzo. Their villages climb the lower slopes, with terraces of beans, cassava and coffee cut into the hillsides. Many porters and guides on the trails come from these communities. According to local tradition, the snow on the peaks is linked to a powerful spirit, and some families still treat the high ground with respect and caution. As you walk, notice how easily the porters move on ground that tires most visitors quickly.

Now for the second surprise — the plants. As you climb, the forest gives way to bamboo, then to heather that grows as tall as trees and hangs with grey moss. Higher still, you enter a zone of giants. Lobelias rise like green columns. Groundsels grow into odd trees with thick trunks and leaves bunched at the top. These same families of plants stay small in Europe. Here, cold nights, constant moisture and strong equatorial sunlight push them to enormous sizes.

The animals are often harder to find than the plants. The forest holds monkeys, many birds, and small creatures that live nowhere else. Birdwatchers hope to see the Rwenzori turaco, a bird with bright green, red and violet feathers. Look closely at branches and you may spot a chameleon. Elephants and chimpanzees also live in the lower forest, but they are shy, and most hikers only see their signs.

The ice is changing fast. Explorers a century ago described wide glaciers across the high peaks. Today much of that ice has gone, and scientists who study the range have recorded a steady retreat. The remaining glaciers are smaller and thinner each decade. For anyone who reaches the snow line, this adds a quiet weight to the view. You may be seeing ice that later visitors will not see.

Most trekkers come for a few hours or a full day, walking into the foothill forest and community trails. Longer routes take a week or more and circle the high valleys, passing glacial lakes and huts above the bogs. These treks need guides, porters and good preparation. The ground is often deep mud. The weather changes quickly. Even short walks feel different here, because every step is slower and every view is earned.

Before you leave, stop for a moment and look up. Watch how the cloud moves across the ridge, opening and closing like a slow curtain. Listen to the water again, and to the birds in the trees. If the cloud breaks, look for a pale gleam of snow above the green. And as you walk back down, notice the terraces, the villages, and the rivers — all carrying something from these mountains towards the wider world.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack waterproof boots and rain gear even for short walks.
Book a licensed local guide for any trail into the park.
Hire porters from local communities to support village incomes.
Start early, since cloud often thickens after midday.
Allow extra time for slow progress through muddy ground.
