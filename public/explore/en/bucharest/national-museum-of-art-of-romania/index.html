The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the wide stone square and look up at the long pale façade. Traffic hums along Calea Victoriei, one of the oldest streets in Bucharest. Pigeons settle on the cornices. The building in front of you is calm and symmetrical, with tall windows and a quiet weight to it. It does not look like a museum at first. It looks like a palace, and that is exactly what it was.

This is the National Museum of Art of Romania. It lives inside the former Royal Palace, the home of Romania's kings for much of the twentieth century. The site began as a large boyar residence in the early nineteenth century. A boyar was a member of the old landed nobility in this part of Europe. Over time, the house grew into a royal seat, and the city grew around it.

The palace you see today is largely a rebuilding. A fire in the 1920s badly damaged the older structure. Through the following decade, the palace was reconstructed in a grander and more formal style. The result is the restrained, monumental front facing the square. Notice how the design feels heavy and orderly rather than playful. It was built to project authority — a stage for state ceremony as much as a home.

After the Second World War, the monarchy came to an end, and the new communist government turned the royal rooms to public use. The palace became a home for the national art collections. So the paintings you are about to see now hang in rooms where kings once received guests. Keep that in mind as you walk through. The ceilings, the staircases, and the marble floors are part of the exhibition too.

Now look across the square behind you. This open space is Revolution Square, and the name is not decoration. In December 1989, this square sat at the centre of the uprising that ended the regime of Nicolae Ceaușescu. The large building opposite once housed the Communist Party's Central Committee. From its balcony, Ceaușescu gave his final speech, and the crowd turned against him.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the fighting that followed, this palace was caught in the crossfire. Gunfire struck the building, and parts of the interior burned. Many artworks were damaged, and some were lost. The museum spent years on restoration before the galleries could fully reopen. If you look carefully at the stonework outside, you may still find small marks and patches of repair. The building carries that night in its walls.

Inside, the collection is divided into large galleries. One of the most moving is the gallery of medieval Romanian art. Here you will find icons, carved wooden objects, silver work, and embroidered textiles. Many came from monasteries and churches in Wallachia and Moldavia, the historic regions that later joined to form modern Romania. The rooms are often dim, to protect fragile materials. Let your eyes adjust, and the gold begins to glow.

Take your time with the embroideries. These are not simple fabrics. They are religious textiles, stitched with silk and metal thread, sometimes showing saints, sometimes showing the people who paid for them. Look for small kneeling figures at the edges of some pieces. These are often the donors, a prince or a noble family, placing themselves in the picture with quiet humility. It is a very human detail inside a very sacred object.

The next great section is the gallery of modern Romanian art. This is where the country's own painters take centre stage. You will meet Nicolae Grigorescu, who painted peasants, carts, and open countryside with soft, airy light. Grigorescu is often seen as a founding figure of modern Romanian painting. His work shows a nation looking at its own villages and landscapes and finding them worthy of art.

The modern gallery also holds sculpture, and here comes a second surprise. Romania is the birthplace of Constantin Brâncuși, one of the most important sculptors of the twentieth century. He later made his name in Paris, where his smooth, simplified forms changed the direction of modern sculpture. In this museum, you can see some of his earlier works. Look at how the surfaces begin to simplify — you can watch a style being born.

Then there is the European art gallery. Much of it grew from the private collection of Romania's first king, Carol the First, in the late nineteenth century. The king was a serious collector, and he bought old master paintings from across Europe. Among the treasures are works attributed to El Greco, the Greek-born painter who worked in Spain, known for his long, flickering figures. Seeing these here, in Bucharest, surprises many visitors.

As you move between galleries, pay attention to the palace itself. Some ceremonial rooms survive, including the grand spaces once used for official receptions. Look at the staircases, the polished stone, and the scale of the doorways. Then look out of the windows towards the square. From inside, you can imagine how the city once looked to the royal family, and later to the crowds of 1989 below.

When you step back outside, pause on the square once more. The traffic still runs along Calea Victoriei. Across the way, the old party building stands in the afternoon light. Nearby, the domed concert hall of the Romanian Athenaeum rises among the trees. Palace, revolution, and art all sit within a few steps of each other here. Take one last look at that long pale façade... and then let the street carry you on.

Practical tips

Visit the Romanian Athenaeum nearby to round out the area.
