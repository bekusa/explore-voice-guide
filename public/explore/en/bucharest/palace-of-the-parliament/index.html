The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The pavement around this building is wide and open, and it seems to keep going. Look up, and the white facade fills your whole field of vision. Row after row of windows, columns and balconies repeat until your eye gives up counting. Traffic hums somewhere behind you, but up close the stone absorbs the sound. Few buildings on Earth make a person feel this small. That feeling was planned.

This is the Palace of the Parliament in Bucharest. It is often listed among the largest administrative buildings in the world, and among the heaviest. Romanians have given it many names. It was planned as the House of the Republic. Many people still call it the House of the People. Today it is formally the seat of Romania's parliament. Each name carries a different chapter of the same story.

The story begins with Nicolae Ceaușescu, the communist leader who ruled Romania from the 1960s until 1989. In the early 1970s he visited North Korea and China. He came home impressed by grand state architecture and mass parades. Then, in 1977, a powerful earthquake damaged Bucharest. For Ceaușescu, the rebuilding became an opportunity. He wanted a new political centre for the capital, with one enormous building at its heart.

The site he chose was a hill on the southern side of the old city. To make room, a large part of historic Bucharest was demolished. Thousands of homes disappeared. So did churches, monasteries, synagogues and old market streets. Tens of thousands of residents were moved out, often with very little warning. When you look out from the palace steps, much of what you see stands on ground that once held an older neighbourhood.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Not every church was destroyed. Engineers moved several of them instead. The church of the Mihai Vodă Monastery, an old princely foundation, was lifted and rolled on rails to a new spot, out of sight of the new boulevard. Other churches were hidden behind tall apartment blocks. If you walk the nearby streets, you can still find these small survivors tucked into unexpected corners.

The chief architect was Anca Petrescu. She was a young architect, only around thirty when she led the project. Behind her stood a large team of architects, and a workforce that sources describe in the tens of thousands. Work went on day and night. Construction began in 1984. Ceaușescu visited often and changed plans as he went. Staircases were rebuilt. Rooms were redesigned. The building grew in all directions while the country outside faced shortages of food and heating.

That contrast matters. During the 1980s, many Romanian families lived with rationed food and cold apartments in winter. Meanwhile, this palace absorbed huge amounts of national resources. Almost everything inside was made in Romania. There is marble from Transylvania, carved wood, crystal chandeliers, and heavy woven carpets. The idea was to show off what Romanian craftsmen could produce. For many people at the time, it also showed what the regime valued most.

Then history changed direction. In December 1989, revolution spread across Romania. Within days, Ceaușescu and his wife Elena were captured, tried and executed. The palace was still unfinished. Its main builder never governed from these halls. For a while, Romanians debated what to do with it. Some wanted it torn down. But a building of this size is very hard to remove. In the end, the new democracy moved in.

Today the palace houses both chambers of the Romanian parliament, the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate. It also hosts conferences and official events. One wing holds the National Museum of Contemporary Art, which has its own entrance. So the building that once symbolised one man's power now holds elected politicians and modern artists. Many Romanians see a kind of quiet justice in that.

Inside, a guided tour shows only a small fraction of the rooms. Even that fraction is large. Corridors seem to run on forever. Ceilings rise high above you, heavy with gilded plaster. Chandeliers hang from them like frozen waterfalls of glass. Large parts of the building are still not fully used. Some areas below ground have never been opened to the public, and people in Bucharest like to trade rumours about what lies down there.

Now turn around and look away from the palace. A long, straight boulevard runs east from the front. Under communism it was named the Boulevard of the Victory of Socialism. Today it is called Unirii Boulevard. Locals often say it was designed to be a little longer than the Champs-Élysées in Paris. Rows of uniform apartment blocks line it, built for party officials. The palace and the boulevard were planned as one single composition.

There is one lighter story that guides love to tell. In 1992, a famous pop star came to Bucharest and stepped out onto the palace's central balcony to greet his fans. According to the popular tale, he called out a cheerful hello... to Budapest, the capital of neighbouring Hungary. Whether or not every word is remembered correctly, the moment became part of the building's folklore. Ceaușescu built that balcony for his own speeches.

So take a moment before you leave. Stand back from the steps and let the facade fill your view again. Notice how quiet the space around it feels, even in the middle of a capital city. Think about the old streets that once stood here, and the people who lived in them. As you walk away down the slope, look back once or twice. The building gets smaller slowly — and that, too, was planned.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring your passport or ID for the guided tour.
Book the interior tour in advance when possible.
Walk Unirii Boulevard east for the full perspective.
Visit the contemporary art museum through its separate entrance.
Wear comfortable shoes for long corridors and stairs.
