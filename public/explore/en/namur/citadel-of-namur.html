The guide

The story

Feel the ground underfoot first. Beneath your shoes there is stone, then earth, then more stone, layered over many centuries. A breeze comes up from the valley carrying the smell of river water and wet grass. Far below, two rivers meet in a wide, slow embrace. Welcome to the Citadel of Namur, a fortress that grew on this rock for more than a thousand years.

Look down toward the point where the waters join. The Sambre flows in from one side, and the Meuse runs broad and steady from the other. This meeting of rivers is the whole reason the citadel exists. Whoever held this rocky spur could watch, tax, and block traffic on two great waterways. Armies understood that very early. So did merchants.

The story starts long before cannons. People were already living on this spur in very early times. Later, in the Middle Ages, the counts of Namur built their castle up here. Their power came from this rock and from the trade that moved past below it. Some of the oldest masonry around you belongs to that early medieval phase. The stones are smaller, rougher, and set in a more irregular pattern than the later work.

Then gunpowder changed everything. A tall medieval wall stops arrows well, but it shatters under cannon fire. So over the following centuries the fortress spread outward and downward. Its builders added thick earth ramparts, angled bastions, and deep ditches. Each new ruler of the region seemed to add another layer. Spanish, French, Austrian, and Dutch engineers all left their mark on this hill.

The most famous chapter came in the late seventeenth century. In 1692, the army of the French king Louis the Fourteenth besieged Namur. The great French engineer Vauban directed the attack. Inside the walls, the defence was organised by the Dutch engineer Menno van Coehoorn. Think about that for a moment — two of the finest military engineers in Europe, facing each other across these same slopes. The French took the fortress.

They did not hold it for long. In 1695, William the Third, king of England and ruler of the Dutch Republic, came back and besieged Namur again. This time the allies won. The siege was so famous that it entered literature. In the eighteenth century novel Tristram Shandy, by Laurence Sterne, the gentle Uncle Toby is wounded at the siege of Namur. He spends years rebuilding its fortifications in miniature on his bowling green. Many visitors walk these ramparts without knowing they are standing inside a famous English novel.

The French poet Boileau also wrote an ode celebrating the French capture of the town. So this rock was not only a military prize. It was a symbol, and writers on both sides used it to tell their stories of glory. Today, the old rivalries are gone. What remains is the shape of the ground, which still follows the logic of attack and defence.

Much of what you see today comes from the nineteenth century. After the fall of Napoleon, this region became part of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands. The Dutch rebuilt the citadel in a more modern style. Look at the long, clean lines of the walls and the careful brick and stone work. Those buildings often date from that period. Soon afterwards, Belgium became independent, and the fortress passed to the Belgian army.

Now here is something many visitors miss. The citadel is not only above ground. Under your feet runs a network of underground passages, cut into the rock over centuries. Soldiers used these tunnels to move troops, store supplies, and reach firing positions in safety. Guided tours take visitors through part of this underground world. Inside, the air turns cool and damp, and every footstep echoes against the stone.

By the twentieth century, a fortress like this could no longer stop a modern army. Still, the Belgian military kept a presence here until the 1970s. After the army left, the site slowly turned into a place for walking, picnics, and culture. The old parade grounds became lawns. The barracks found new uses. Families now spread blankets where soldiers once drilled.

As you walk the esplanade, look for a large golden sculpture. It shows a man riding on the back of a giant turtle. It is the work of the Belgian artist Jan Fabre. The turtle is slow, steady, and protected by its shell, much like a fortress. The gold catches the light even on a grey Belgian day. Children love it, and photographers often frame it against the valley behind.

Now turn back toward the rivers once more. Near the point where they meet, below the rock, stands the seat of the Walloon Parliament. Namur is the capital of Wallonia, the French-speaking southern region of Belgium. So the old pattern continues in a new form. Power still gathers at this confluence. The difference is that today it works through debate and votes, not cannons.

Take a moment before you leave. Listen to the wind moving through the trees on the slopes. Notice the boats sliding along the Meuse, and the rooftops of the old town spread out below you. Every wall around you was built because someone feared what might come up that river. Today, the only thing coming up is the next group of walkers, out of breath, smiling at the view. As you walk away, look back once at the rock — and remember how many people have stood here watching the same two rivers meet.
