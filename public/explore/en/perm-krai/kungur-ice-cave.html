The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the cold. It comes out of the hillside to meet you, a breath of air that smells of wet stone and old snow. Outside it might be a warm Ural summer afternoon, with grass and birch leaves moving by the river. A few steps into the passage, and your own breath turns white. The ground under your shoes changes from gravel to something damp and hard. This is Kungur Ice Cave, and it begins the way all good stories do, with a change in the air.

The cave lies inside a long ridge called Ice Mountain, near the small town of Kungur, in Perm Krai. Here the Sylva River curves along the foot of the slope, and the village of Filippovka sits close to the entrance. From the outside, the hill looks modest. It is wooded and gently rounded, with nothing dramatic on its face. But under that green surface, water has worked through the rock for many thousands of years. It has left behind a maze of halls, tunnels, and underground lakes.

To understand the place, it helps to know what the hill is made of. This is not limestone, the stone that forms most of the great caves people know. It is mostly gypsum and anhydrite, soft minerals that dissolve far more quickly in water. Run a hand near the walls, without touching, and you can see pale grey and white layers, sometimes almost sugary in texture. Because gypsum dissolves so easily, caves like this one grow and change faster than limestone caves. The ceilings here are always slowly rearranging themselves.

Now, the ice. Why should a cave in the middle of a continent stay frozen in July? The answer is air. In winter, heavy cold air sinks into the lower entrance halls and settles there, like water in a bowl. In summer, the warmer air outside is lighter, so it cannot easily push that cold pocket out. The result is a set of grottoes near the entrance that stay below freezing all year. Deeper inside, the temperature rises a little and stays steady, a few degrees above zero.

That trapped winter air is what builds the ice you see. Moisture drips from the ceiling and freezes into icicles and columns. Moisture in the air settles on the rock and grows into crystals. In the coldest months, some of these crystals become delicate plates and needles, larger than any snowflake. They catch the light like broken glass. In warmer months, many of the finest crystals soften or disappear. So the cave you walk through today is never quite the same cave a visitor saw in a different season.

People have known about this hill for a very long time. Local tradition connects it with Yermak, the Cossack leader whose campaign in the late sixteenth century opened Siberia to Russia. Legend has it that his men sheltered near the cave before moving east. There is no firm proof of this, and it is best heard as a local tale. What is better documented is the work of Semyon Remezov, a Siberian mapmaker. In the early eighteenth century, he drew one of the earliest known plans of the cave.

Remezov's drawing matters because it shows that people were already exploring and recording these passages three centuries ago. Over the following generations, scholars, travellers, and local guides came here with candles and lamps. Organised visits began in the early twentieth century. Since then, the cave has become one of the best-known show caves in Russia. Surveyors have mapped more than five kilometres of passages. Visitors walk only part of that network, along a route of roughly a kilometre and a half.

As you move along the path, the grottoes open one after another. Each has its own name, often chosen for its shape or its mood. One of the first is called the Diamond Grotto, after the way the frost crystals sparkle under the lights. Another is the Polar Grotto, where the cold feels deepest and the ice lies thickest. The names are simple, but they are useful. They help you remember a place that, underground, can start to feel endless.

Further in, the cave gives you something unexpected — water. Dozens of underground lakes lie in the hollows of the floor. They are fed by groundwater rising up through the rock, and in many places the water is so clear that it is hard to see where the surface is. Lights placed along the route reflect off the lakes, so the ceiling seems to continue below your feet. Stop for a moment beside one of them. Without the sound of voices, you can sometimes hear a single drop fall.

Look up, too. Some ceilings are smooth and arched. Others are broken into heavy blocks that hang at odd angles. In several halls, large slabs of rock lie on the floor where they fell long ago. This is part of the natural life of a gypsum cave. Water dissolves the rock, the ceiling loses support, and pieces come down. The guides and the cave service keep the visitor route safe, but the piles of fallen stone are an honest record of how the cave grows.

There is life in this darkness, even if it is hard to see. Small crustaceans live in some of the lakes, adapted to cold, clean water and no sunlight at all. Bats are known to use parts of the cave. Scientists have studied the climate, the ice, and the water here for decades, and there has long been a research station nearby. For them, the cave is a natural laboratory. It records changes in temperature and water over long periods, in a place where the outside world reaches only slowly.

Part of the experience is simply the way the cave changes how you feel. Sound is muffled by the stone and ice. Light comes only from the fixtures along the path, so colours are softer and shadows are deeper. Many visitors find they speak more quietly without being asked. The cold also sharpens the senses. You notice the smell of damp gypsum, the sharp feel of the air in your nose, and the faint crunch of frost near the entrance.

When you walk back toward daylight, pay attention to the moment the temperature turns. The air grows heavy and warm again, and the smell of grass and river water returns. Look back once at the dark opening in the hillside. Behind you, winter is still waiting in the lower halls, as it waits every summer. Then look out across the Sylva toward the fields and the town. The hill looks ordinary again — but now you know what it holds.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm jacket, even on a hot summer day.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp, icy paths.
Book a guided tour in advance during busy seasons.
Visit in winter for the largest frost crystals.
Pause beside a lake when your group moves on.
