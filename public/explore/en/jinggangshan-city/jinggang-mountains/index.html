The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves through the bamboo with a dry, hollow rattle, like distant applause. The air here is cool and damp, even on a warm day. Mist often sits low in the valleys, and the green of the slopes looks almost blue where the cloud thins. You are standing in the Jinggang Mountains, a range of steep ridges and folded valleys in the southwest of Jiangxi Province, close to the border with Hunan.

At first glance, this is simply mountain country. There are dense forests, terraced fields, waterfalls, and narrow roads that switch back and forth up the slopes. For centuries, it was a remote place, hard to reach and hard to govern. Villages were small and scattered. Travel happened on foot, along stone paths cut into the hillsides. That remoteness is the key to everything that happened here in the twentieth century.

In the autumn of 1927, China was in turmoil. The alliance between the Nationalists and the Communists had collapsed earlier that year, and Communist forces were being hunted in the cities. A young organiser named Mao Zedong had led an uprising in Hunan, known as the Autumn Harvest Uprising. It failed. With a small and exhausted band of survivors, Mao turned away from the cities and climbed into these mountains in October 1927.

Here is the first surprise. Mao did not arrive in empty country. The mountains already had their own armed groups, led by two local men, Yuan Wencai and Wang Zuo. Official histories often call them bandit chiefs or local militia leaders. Mao negotiated with them rather than fighting them, and their men joined his force. Without their knowledge of the paths and the villages, the newcomers may not have survived their first winter.

In the spring of 1928, a second army arrived. It was led by Zhu De and included a commander named Chen Yi. The two forces met and merged, and together they formed what became known as the Fourth Red Army. This meeting is remembered as one of the founding moments of the Chinese Communist military. Around the region, you will see monuments and paintings that show Mao and Zhu side by side, and the meeting site is preserved for visitors.

Life in the base was hard. Food was scarce, salt was scarce, and medicine was almost impossible to find. One of the most popular stories here is about a carrying pole. Locals say Zhu De, already a senior commander, carried rice up the mountain paths alongside ordinary soldiers. When his men hid his pole to make him rest, he made another one. Whether every detail is true or not, the story is told again and again, and a pole is often shown to visitors.

The mountains were also a fortress. Five passes controlled the main routes into the area, and the Red Army guarded them closely. The best-known is Huangyangjie, a high, windswept pass. In the summer of 1928, a small defending force held it against a much larger attacking army. Mao later wrote a short poem about that battle. Today, a memorial stands at the pass, and on clear days the view runs across ridge after ridge.

The base did not last long in its first form. Under heavy pressure from Nationalist forces, Mao and Zhu De led the main army out of the mountains in early 1929, moving east into southern Jiangxi. Those left behind faced harsh reprisals. The villages here paid a high price. That is part of why the region is treated with such seriousness today. It is seen as the starting point of a path that later led through the Long March to the founding of the People's Republic in 1949.

In the town of Ciping, at the heart of the mountains, you can visit the revolutionary museum and the martyrs' cemetery. The cemetery is a quiet place. It holds the names of thousands of people who died in the struggle, many of them local farmers. Visitors often bring flowers. You may see school groups standing in neat rows, or older visitors walking slowly and reading every inscription. Whatever your own views, the mood here asks for quiet.

Here is the second surprise. Mao came back. In 1965, decades after he left as a hunted rebel, he returned to the Jinggang Mountains as the leader of China. He wrote a poem about that return, often translated as "Reascending Jinggangshan". It describes the climb, the clouds, and the changes he saw in the land. Lines from it appear on stone tablets and in the museum, so keep an eye out for carved calligraphy as you walk.

But the mountains are not only a history lesson. They are a large and very green landscape, and many visitors come mainly for the nature. Bamboo covers huge areas of the slopes, tall and straight, with pale stripes on the stems. In spring, wild azaleas, a kind of flowering mountain shrub, bloom in red and pink across the hillsides. The Five Dragon Pools area is a chain of waterfalls and clear pools linked by walking paths and steps.

The weather shapes the experience. Rain is common, and cloud can drop over a viewpoint in minutes and then lift again just as quickly. This makes the light change constantly. A ridge that looks flat and grey can suddenly turn bright green when the sun breaks through. On the trails, the stone steps can be slippery, and the sound of running water follows you almost everywhere. In the evening, the valleys fill with mist again.

The town of Ciping itself is worth a slow walk. It sits in a small basin surrounded by hills, with a lake near the centre. You will find small restaurants serving Jiangxi food, which is often spicy and simple, with bamboo shoots, smoked meats, and local mushrooms. Shops sell red stars, old-style army caps, and copies of the uniforms worn here in the late 1920s. Some visitors even dress up for photos.

As you stand here now, take one more breath of the cool, wet air. Look at the shape of the ridges around you, how close together they stand, how deep the valleys run between them. It is easy to see why a small army chose to hide here. And it is easy to forget, among the bamboo and the birdsong, how much happened in these quiet hills. As you walk on, listen again to the bamboo... it is still moving in the wind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a rain jacket because the weather changes very quickly.
Wear shoes with good grip for wet stone steps.
Visit in spring to see the wild azaleas in bloom.
Base yourself in Ciping to reach most sites easily.
Keep your voice low inside the martyrs' cemetery.
