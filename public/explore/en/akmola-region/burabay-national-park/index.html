The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the pine tops with a long, soft hiss. Somewhere below, small waves tap against granite. The air smells of resin and cold water. Under your feet, the ground is a mix of pine needles, sand, and pale grey stone. For hours on the road from Astana, the land was flat, open steppe. Then, almost suddenly, the forest rose up around you. Welcome to Burabay National Park, in the north of Kazakhstan.

That sudden change is the first thing to understand about this place. Burabay is an island of forest, rock, and lakes surrounded by grassland. Geologists describe it as part of the Kokshetau Upland, a raised area of old granite hills. Rain and snow collect between those hills and form lakes. The higher ground holds more moisture than the steppe around it. So pine and birch can grow here, where the open plain supports mostly grass.

People across Kazakhstan, and across the former Soviet Union, have long called this area "Kazakh Switzerland". You will hear the nickname often. It is a comparison, not a description. There are no snowy Alpine peaks here. But the mix of blue lakes, dark forest, and rocky hills does feel very different from the steppe. For generations, families have come here for summer holidays, for clean air, and for rest in the old sanatoriums built along the shores.

The area became a state national park in 2000. Its purpose is to protect the forests, the lakes, and the animals that live between them. Today the park covers a wide territory with several lakes. The best known are Lake Burabay, Lake Shchuchye, and the larger lake usually called Bolshoye Chebachye. Each has its own character. Some shores are busy with hotels and boats. Others are quiet, with only pines and rock at the waterline.

Now, the name. "Bura" means a male camel in Kazakh. According to a popular legend, a white camel once lived in these hills. Whenever enemies came near, the camel cried out and warned the people. Locals say the camel finally turned into stone, or simply disappeared into the mountains. Treat this as folklore, not history. But it tells you something true: this landscape has always felt protective, even sacred, to the people who lived around it.

Look toward the highest hill around Lake Burabay. This is Mount Kokshe, also known by its Russian name, Sinyukha, which means "the blue one". It rises to just under a thousand metres above sea level. That is not high by mountain standards. But it stands out sharply above the flat land beyond the park. From a distance, especially in hazy light, the forested slopes really do look blue. That colour gave the mountain both of its names.

Now turn to the water of Lake Burabay itself. Not far from the shore, a strange rock stands up from the lake. Its name is Zhumbaktas, which means "mystery stone" in Kazakh. Here is the surprise. Its shape seems to change depending on where you stand. From one side, many visitors see the face of a young woman. From another angle, they see an old woman. Walk slowly along the shore and watch the profile shift.

Above the lake stands another famous rock, Okzhetpes. The name means roughly "the arrow cannot reach". It is a tall, stacked column of granite, and from some angles it looks like an elephant. Legend has it that a beautiful young woman lived here long ago. Many suitors wanted to marry her. She promised to marry the man whose arrow could reach the top of the rock. No arrow ever did. Locals still tell versions of this story by the water.

Many other stones here carry names and stories too. Granite cracks and weathers into rounded blocks, flat slabs, and stacked towers. Wind, frost, and water have shaped them for a very long time. On one ridge, people see a sleeping warrior lying on his back. Another meadow is linked in local tradition with Ablai Khan, an eighteenth-century Kazakh ruler. Legends say he gathered his warriors here. Visitors often stop there in quiet respect.

Take a moment to look at the forest itself. The tall pines have straight, orange-brown trunks. White birches grow between them, especially in damp hollows. In early summer, the forest floor turns green with grass and small flowers. In autumn, the birches turn bright yellow against the dark pines. Animals live here too, including elk, roe deer, foxes, and many birds. Walk quietly in the early morning, and you have a better chance to see them.

The water changes through the year as well. In summer, people swim, rent boats, and sit on the warm rocks. The lakes are cool, even in July. In winter, the surface freezes, and the park becomes white and silent. Cross-country skiers and walkers move between snowy pines. Many visitors say the winter light here is very clear. The sky can turn pale gold over the frozen lakes in the late afternoon.

The village of Burabay and the town of Shchuchinsk are the main gateways. You will find sanatoriums, guesthouses, small cafes, and markets near the shore. Some vendors sell smoked fish, honey, and dried herbs. A small nature museum in Burabay village shows the plants and animals of the park. It is a good stop before a longer walk. Marked trails lead into the forest and up to viewpoints above the lakes.

A quiet word about care. This forest is surrounded by dry steppe, and fire is a real danger here, especially in summer. Use only the official places for barbecues and campfires. Take your rubbish with you. Stay on the marked paths, because the thin soil on the rocks damages easily. Small actions like these help keep the forest alive for the next generation of visitors.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Look at the line where the pines meet the sky. Listen again to the wind in the branches and the water at the stones. When you drive away, watch the forest thin out behind you. The steppe will open up again, wide and flat. And you will understand why people here have always treated these lakes and rocks as a place apart.
