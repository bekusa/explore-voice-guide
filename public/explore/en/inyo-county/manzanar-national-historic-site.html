The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Here in the Owens Valley it rarely stops. It moves across the flat ground, lifts fine grey dust, and pushes it against your ankles. To the west, the Sierra Nevada rises like a wall, often capped with snow even in warm months. To the east stand the drier Inyo Mountains. Between them lies this open stretch of sagebrush, old foundations, and rows of young and old trees. It feels quiet now. For more than three years during the Second World War, it was anything but quiet.

You are standing at Manzanar, one of ten camps where the United States government confined people of Japanese ancestry during the war. Most of them came from the West Coast, many from the Los Angeles area. At its peak, the camp held about ten thousand people. A detail that surprises many visitors: roughly two thirds of the people held across these camps were American citizens, born in the United States. Many were children. They had committed no crime.

The story begins with Executive Order 9066, signed by President Franklin Roosevelt in February 1942, a few months after the attack on Pearl Harbor. The order allowed the military to remove people from large areas of the West Coast. Families received short notice. They could take only what they could carry. Homes, farms, shops, and fishing boats were sold quickly, often at a heavy loss, or simply left behind. By the spring of 1942, the first people arrived here in the desert.

The name itself carries an older story. Manzanar is a Spanish word that means apple orchard. Before the war, a small farming town stood on this ground, with orchards of apples and pears watered by streams from the mountains. Then the city of Los Angeles bought up land and water rights across the Owens Valley, and the water went south through an aqueduct. The town faded. Some of those old fruit trees survived, and incarcerated people later tended them again.

The story goes back further still. Long before the orchards, this valley was the home of the Owens Valley Paiute people, who built irrigation ditches and gathered food across these slopes. Their communities are still part of the valley today. So when you look around, you are seeing a place with many layers: Native homeland, farming town, wartime camp, and now a protected historic site managed by the National Park Service.

The camp itself was laid out in a strict grid. The housing area covered about one square mile, divided into thirty-six blocks. Each block held rows of wooden barracks covered in tarpaper, along with a shared mess hall, laundry room, and latrines. Families lived in single rooms, often separated from strangers only by thin walls or hanging blankets. Privacy almost did not exist. Former residents remembered the dust most of all — it came through the cracks in the floorboards and covered everything.

If you walk to Block 14, you can enter reconstructed barracks and a mess hall. The rooms show different moments in camp life. Early on, the spaces were bare, with army cots and straw-filled mattresses. Later, people built shelves, furniture, and partitions from scrap lumber. Inside, notice how small the rooms feel when you imagine a family of six living there, through hot summers and cold desert winters.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The people of Manzanar made gardens. In the dry ground, they dug ponds, set stones, and planted trees and flowers. Some built traditional Japanese gardens beside their mess halls. In the decades after the war, wind and sand buried many of them. Archaeologists and volunteers have since excavated several of these gardens, uncovering stone borders and pond outlines. They are a quiet record of people trying to make beauty in a place they had not chosen.

Camp life was not peaceful at every moment. In December 1942, tension inside the camp boiled over. Military police fired into a crowd, and two young men were killed. The event left deep divisions within the community. Later, a loyalty questionnaire forced people to answer difficult questions about their allegiance, splitting families and friends. These hard chapters are part of the story here, and the exhibits do not hide them.

There is also a story about cameras. Cameras were forbidden to incarcerees at first. The photographer Toyo Miyatake, held here, is said to have brought a lens with him and built a camera from scraps to record daily life. In time, he was allowed to photograph openly. The famous landscape photographer Ansel Adams also came to Manzanar and made a series of portraits here. Together, their images show weddings, classrooms, baseball games, and work in the fields.

The visitor center sits inside the camp's old high school auditorium, one of the few original buildings still standing. Inside, exhibits gather photographs, personal objects, and recorded voices of former residents. Near the entrance, a large wall lists the names of people who were held here. Many visitors stop at this wall for a long time. Some search for a family name. Others simply read, one name after another.

At the western edge of the site, in the cemetery, stands a white obelisk. It was built during the war by Ryozo Kado, a stonemason held here, with help from others in the camp. Its Japanese characters are often translated as soul consoling tower. Visitors leave origami cranes, coins, and small offerings at its base. Every spring since 1969, a pilgrimage has gathered here, bringing together survivors, descendants, and others who come to remember.

The camp closed in late 1945, after the war ended. Many families left with very little, and returned to cities where their homes and businesses were gone. Decades later, in 1988, the United States government formally apologized and paid redress to surviving incarcerees. Manzanar became a National Historic Site in 1992, so that this ground would not be forgotten.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look west toward the mountains. The people who lived here saw this same view every day — the same ridgeline, the same snow, the same changing light. Feel the wind again, and the dust under your shoes. As you walk back to the road, notice the old trees, the low concrete foundations, and the empty spaces between them. Each one marks where someone once lived.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start inside the visitor center to watch the introductory film.
Drive the dirt loop road to reach outlying gardens and cemetery.
Bring water, sun protection, and a layer for strong winds.
Visit in late April to join the annual Manzanar Pilgrimage.
Allow at least half a day to explore without rushing.
