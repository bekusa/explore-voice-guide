Shdugra Waterfall

First you hear it.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

First you hear it. A low, steady roar, growing louder as you walk. Then the trees open, and there it is, a long white ribbon of water falling down the dark rock. Spray drifts toward you on the wind and cools your face. You have reached Shdugra Waterfall, one of the tallest in the country.

You are in the Becho valley, in the mountains of Svaneti, in the northwest of Georgia. The walk begins in the village of Mazeri. From there a path of about three kilometres leads up through forest and open meadow to the foot of the falls. Some people also call it the Ushba waterfall, after the great mountain that towers above it.

The water does not drop in a single clean line. It falls in stages, sliding and leaping over ledges of grey stone, breaking into white as it comes. The full drop is more than a hundred metres from top to base. People often call it the most voluminous waterfall in all of Georgia, because so much water pours through it at once.

Where does all this water come from? Look up, far above the falls. The streams begin in snow and ice high on the shoulders of the mountains. Glaciers up there melt slowly under the summer sun. The meltwater gathers into a single stream, and that stream becomes the waterfall now thundering down in front of you.

Above the whole valley stands one of the great peaks of the Caucasus, Mount Ushba. It has two sharp summits, often hidden in cloud. Climbers know it as one of the hardest mountains in the entire range to reach. On a clear day, its twin points watch over the meadows and the falling water far below.

The waterfall changes through the year. In spring and early summer, when the snow melts fast, it is at its strongest and its loudest. The flow can be wild, and the spray reaches far across the rocks. By late autumn the water grows thinner and quieter. In the deep cold of winter, parts of it freeze into hanging columns of ice.

The hike itself is part of the reward. The path leaves Mazeri and climbs gently at first, through pine and birch. You cross small streams on stones and fallen logs. The forest smells of resin and damp earth. Then the trees fall away and the valley opens, green and wide, with the white falls waiting ahead of you.

In the warm months the meadows fill with wildflowers. Bees move between them. Cattle and horses graze where the ground lies flat. Marmots whistle from the slopes and vanish into their burrows. The air is clean and thin, and the light has that sharp brightness you only ever find high up in the mountains.

As you near the base, the sound becomes a roar you can feel inside your chest. The rock is wet and dark and slick underfoot. A cold wind blows off the falling water, even on a hot day. Stand close, and a fine mist settles on your skin and hair. Look for small rainbows where the sunlight cuts through the spray.

Take care here. The stones near the water are slippery, and the cold can surprise you. The flow is powerful, and this is not a place to climb or to swim. The mountains ask for respect. Watch the weather as well. Clouds can gather quickly over the peaks, and rain makes the path back down harder and longer.

Find a dry rock and sit for a while. Watch the water fall and fall and never stop. It has been pouring down this cliff for longer than anyone can say, fed each year by the same patient snows above. Mountains rise on every side, old and indifferent. There is something steadying in it, this endless motion in such a wild and quiet place.

When you are ready, turn and start back down toward Mazeri. The roar will slowly fade behind you, and then disappear among the trees. But the sound stays with you in memory, and the cold touch of the spray on your skin. Few things in Svaneti will draw you back as surely as the falling water of Shdugra.

Key facts

The trail runs about 3 kilometres from Mazeri village.
The water drops more than 100 metres down grey rock.
Often called Georgia's most voluminous waterfall.
Fed by glacial meltwater below Mount Ushba.
Strongest flow comes in spring and early summer.

What to look for

The twin cloud-wrapped summits of Mount Ushba above.
Rainbows forming where sunlight cuts the rising spray.
Wildflowers and grazing horses across the open meadow.
Marmots whistling and darting across the slopes.

Practical tips

Start the hike from Mazeri, not from central Mestia.
Wear waterproof shoes; the final rocks stay wet.
Bring water and start early to avoid afternoon clouds.
Do not climb or swim near the powerful flow.
