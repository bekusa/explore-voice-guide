The story

Breathe in and notice how thin and clean the air feels. You are standing around one and a half thousand metres up, on the steep southern slopes of the Taurus mountains. Wind moves across broken marble. Far below, the valley floor spreads out in fields and the small town of Ağlasun. And somewhere ahead of you — listen — water is splashing. That sound will matter later. Sagalassos is a Roman city in the sky, and it greets visitors with running water.

The people who built the first city here were the Pisidians, tough mountain highlanders with a reputation as warriors. They chose this dizzying site deliberately. The slope gave them defence, cold springs gave them water, and the height gave them a view of every approach. Cities on the coast were rich and vulnerable. Sagalassos was hard — hard to reach, hard to farm, and very hard to attack. That stubborn geography shaped everything that happened afterward.

One visitor refused to be discouraged. In 333 BC, Alexander the Great arrived with his army during his march through Anatolia. Ancient historians recorded that the Pisidians met him on the heights, and that his troops had to fight uphill against defenders who knew every rock. He took the city anyway. It is a strange distinction, but a real one: the slope you are catching your breath on today once tested the most successful military commander in ancient history.

Under Rome, the mountain stronghold blossomed into a showpiece. Sagalassos earned the favour of emperors, and Hadrian honoured it as the first city of Pisidia, a title it advertised proudly. Marble monuments climbed the slope in terraces — two agoras, a council house, baths, temples, fountains, a library. For a provincial mountain town, the ambition was enormous. Civic pride here was a competitive sport, and the players spent fortunes on stone.

Now follow that splashing sound to the upper agora, because it leads to something almost unreasonable. The Antonine Fountain — a monumental wall of columns, niches, and statues from the second century — collapsed in earthquakes long ago. Archaeologists rebuilt it from thousands of original pieces, and in 2010 they turned the water back on. Cold mountain spring water now pours through the same ancient channel again. You are not imagining a Roman fountain. You are hearing one.

The excavations here have a habit of finding giants. In 2007, in the ruins of the Roman baths, archaeologists uncovered fragments of a colossal statue of the emperor Hadrian — a head nearly a metre tall, and enormous sandalled legs. The following year, a matching colossal head of Marcus Aurelius emerged nearby. The finds made news worldwide. The originals are displayed in the museum in Burdur, but they were found in the rubble you can walk past today.

Climb higher and you reach the theatre, resting near the top of the city. It could seat somewhere near nine thousand people, and its rows hang above a drop with the mountains as a backdrop. Look closely and you will see the builders never entirely finished it — blocks lie about with their final carving incomplete, exactly where masons set them down centuries ago. Sit on a high row and recover your breath. Roman audiences did the same climb.

Sagalassos also worked for a living. The city mass-produced fine red-glazed pottery, known to archaeologists as Sagalassos red slip ware, and exported it across the eastern Mediterranean for centuries. Local clay beds fed a whole quarter of workshops and kilns. Plates and cups made on this mountainside have turned up at ancient sites in distant lands. If the fountain was the city's face, pottery was its pay cheque — a factory town wearing marble.

The end came from below the ground. Severe earthquakes struck in the sixth and seventh centuries, and plague and insecurity gnawed at the region in the same era. The survivors gradually drifted down toward the valley, where Ağlasun stands today. The city was never seriously rebuilt or replundered — too high, too remote. Soil crept slowly down the slopes and buried the streets. What ruined Sagalassos also preserved it, like a sealed archive at altitude.

The archive was opened properly in 1990, when a team led from the Belgian university of Leuven began systematic excavations that continue to this day. Their approach made the site famous among archaeologists: rebuild monuments using the original stones wherever possible. The fountain is their signature achievement. Each summer, researchers return to lift another corner of the soil. What you see today is perhaps a fraction of the city — the rest is still waiting under the hillside.

Give the site the slow walk it demands. The terraces connect by stone paths and stairs, and every landing reframes the valley below. Look for the council house, the library with its mosaic floor, and the long colonnaded street. Up here the weather writes the schedule — sun can turn to cold wind within minutes, even in summer. Carry water, wear real shoes, and turn around regularly. The view behind you is half the architecture.

Before you head down, stand once more where you can hear the fountain. That sound crossed eighteen centuries to reach you — silenced by earthquakes, buried under soil, and released again by patient hands. The mountains that defeated armies now guard an empty marble city and one working tap of Roman water. Cup the cold air in your palms. Down in the valley, life went on. Up here, remarkably, a small piece of it came back.
