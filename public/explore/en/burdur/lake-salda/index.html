The guide

The story

Look down at the ground before you look at the water. The shore here is white — not sand-coloured, but truly white, fine as flour, and it packs and squeaks softly underfoot. Then lift your eyes. The water runs through impossible colours: milky white in the shallows, then pale turquoise, then a deep blue-green far out. Pine forest rings the bowl of hills. The air smells of resin and cold water. Lake Salda does not look like the rest of Turkey. It barely looks like Earth.

You are standing in the mountains of Burdur province, near the small town of Yeşilova, at more than one thousand metres above sea level. The lake fills a deep basin ringed by hills, fed by springs and mountain streams, with no river flowing out. That closed system concentrates its unusual chemistry. Salda is also among the deepest lakes in Turkey — its bottom lies roughly one hundred and eighty metres down. The colours you see are, in part, a depth chart.

Now, about that white shore. It is not sand at all. It is a mineral called hydromagnesite, a magnesium carbonate that forms from the lake's own water. The surrounding rocks are rich in magnesium, the springs carry it into the basin, and the mineral settles out along the shallows in pale terraces and beaches. In other words, the lake manufactures its own shoreline. Very few lakes on the planet do this at such a scale.

That chemistry is why space scientists came here. Before NASA launched its Mars rover Perseverance, researchers needed a place on Earth resembling Jezero Crater, the ancient Martian lake bed chosen for landing. Orbital instruments had detected carbonate minerals along Jezero's rim — and Salda is one of the rare lakes whose shoreline carries similar carbonates. Scientists studied this shore to prepare for that mission. The rover landed in Jezero in 2021. Some of its context was written here.

There is more. Along parts of the shoreline lie lumpy, cauliflower-shaped rock mounds called microbialites, close cousins of stromatolites. They are built slowly by communities of microbes that encourage minerals to harden around them. Structures like these are among the oldest evidence of life on Earth — billions of years old in the fossil record. At Salda, the process is still running. Those unglamorous lumps at the waterline are one of biology's oldest tricks, performed live.

The colour show is simple physics wearing a costume. White mineral floor, clear water, and depth do the work together. Sunlight scatters off the pale sediment in the shallows, producing that tropical turquoise, and the colour deepens as the bottom falls away. The palette shifts hour by hour — chalky and bright at midday, steel blue under cloud, striped at sunset. Wind redraws the boundaries constantly. Photograph it whenever you like; the lake will change its mind anyway.

Turkish social media crowned Salda the Maldives of Turkey, and the crowds followed — hundreds of thousands of visitors in peak years, concentrated into a few summer weeks. The attention brought risk, and the response came in 2019, when the lake and its surroundings were declared a special environmental protection area. Some stretches of the white shore, including the most famous terraces, are now closed to entry or to swimming. Boardwalks and rangers guide the flow. The rules protect what everyone came to see.

You may see people coating their arms in white mud where it is permitted. Locals have long believed the lake's mud and mineral-rich water are good for the skin. Treat that as local tradition rather than medicine — the claims are belief, not proven treatment. What is certain is that digging pits or carrying away sediment damages a shoreline that took thousands of years to form. Enjoy the water where swimming is allowed, and let the minerals stay where they are.

Salda wears the seasons dramatically. In winter, snow caps the surrounding ridges, and the white beach meets a white shoreline of another kind. Spring brings meltwater and thin crowds. High summer is warmest, though the water keeps a mountain chill that surprises most swimmers. Autumn may be the secret best season — clear light, empty boardwalks, and pine forest turning aromatic in the cooling air. Whatever the month, evenings arrive cold at this altitude. Bring a layer.

Give the place time and the details emerge. Ducks and other waterbirds work the reedy corners. Wind moves through the pines in long breaths. Sheep bells sometimes carry across from the hills, where herding continues as it has for generations. The town of Yeşilova goes about its business a few kilometres away, unbothered by the fame at the shore. Fame, protection, science — all of it is recent. The lake itself has been quietly making its white shore for millennia.

Walk a stretch of the permitted shoreline slowly, and keep to the marked paths where they exist. Feel the odd, soft firmness of the mineral surface. Watch the boundary where white shallows tip into turquoise depth. If you crouch at the waterline, you may spot the lumpy microbialite shelves just under the surface. This is a landscape best appreciated at walking pace, at ankle height, with your phone in your pocket at least part of the time.

Before you leave, face the water and hold still for one minute. Somewhere overhead, a satellite could be photographing this shore the way another one photographed a crater on Mars. Under your feet, microbes are laying down stone a fraction of a millimetre at a time. You stand between those two scales — the planetary and the microscopic — on a beach the lake built for itself. The wind is cold, the water is patient, and both were here long before us.
