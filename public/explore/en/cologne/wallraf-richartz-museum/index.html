The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the square outside and look at the walls. The stone is grey and slightly rough, cut into clean, repeating blocks. Nearby, church bells and tram wheels mix with the low talk of people crossing toward the old city hall. The building does not shout for attention. It waits, calm and square, at the edge of one of the oldest corners of Cologne. Inside, it holds one of Germany's oldest public art collections. And that collection began with a single man who simply refused to let his city lose its treasures.

His name was Ferdinand Franz Wallraf. He was a priest, a teacher, and a scholar in late eighteenth-century Cologne. He also served as the last rector of the city's old university before French rule closed it. Those were years of upheaval. Churches and monasteries were shut down. Their paintings, books, and relics were sold, scattered, or carried away. Wallraf watched this happen, and he began to collect. He bought what he could, often with very little money, and he saved what others were ready to throw out.

Think about what that means. This was not a rich prince filling a palace. It was one stubborn citizen, rescuing altarpieces and manuscripts one piece at a time. His rooms reportedly filled until there was barely space to move. When he died in 1824, he left everything to the city of Cologne. Paintings, coins, prints, and books — thousands of objects passed into public hands. That gift became the seed of the museum around you.

But a collection needs a home. That is where the second name on the building comes in. Johann Heinrich Richartz was a Cologne merchant who had made his fortune in trade. In the mid nineteenth century, he offered the money for a proper museum building. It opened in 1861. So the museum carries two names for a reason — one man gathered the art, and another man gave it walls. Together, they turned a private passion into a civic institution.

That first building did not survive. Like much of Cologne, it was badly damaged by bombing in the Second World War. The city was left in ruins, and the museum had to rebuild more than once. Over the following decades, the collection also changed shape. Its modern and contemporary works moved to a separate museum, the Museum Ludwig, near the cathedral. The Wallraf kept its focus on older art — from the Middle Ages up to the early twentieth century.

The building you see today opened in 2001. It was designed by Oswald Mathias Ungers, a Cologne architect known for his strict use of geometry. He loved the square, and you can feel it everywhere. Look at the facade again. The windows, the stone panels, and the proportions follow a careful grid. Inside, the same rhythm returns in rooms, doorways, and floor patterns. Some visitors find it cool. Others find it quiet and restful — a plain frame that lets the paintings carry the colour.

Here is a detail many people miss. The building's location matters. It stands in the heart of medieval Cologne, close to the historic city hall and the Gürzenich, the city's old festival hall. Under these streets lie layers of Roman and medieval history. So when you walk inside to see medieval art, you are only a few steps from the ground where that art was first made and used. The paintings have not travelled far from home.

The museum is generally arranged by time, and the medieval rooms are a good place to begin. Cologne was one of the richest cities of the Holy Roman Empire, and its painters had their own style. You will see gold backgrounds, soft faces, and saints with small, gentle gestures. Many of these panels once stood on church altars. Wallraf rescued a large number of them. Without him, much of this local school of painting might simply have disappeared.

The star of these rooms is Stefan Lochner, Cologne's most celebrated painter of the fifteenth century. Look for his Madonna of the Rose Bower. Mary sits on a cushion in a small garden, surrounded by roses and tiny angels making music. The colours are deep blue, red, and gold. Lean in close. The angels are almost like small children at play, and the flowers are painted with real care. It is a devotional image, but it also feels warm and human.

Move up through the building and the mood changes. The Baroque galleries hold works by painters such as Peter Paul Rubens and Rembrandt. Rubens has a special link to this region — he spent part of his childhood in Cologne. Rembrandt is here with a late self-portrait. He shows himself old, laughing, his face loose and lined. It is not a flattering picture. That honesty is exactly why people stand in front of it for so long.

Then comes a second surprise. In 2001, a Swiss collector named Gérard Corboud placed his large collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist art with the museum. That is why its full name now includes the Fondation Corboud. Suddenly, a museum known for medieval gold and Baroque shadows also held bright works by French painters. You can find Monet, Renoir, and others here. Among them hangs a painting by Vincent van Gogh of a drawbridge near Arles, full of southern light.

The museum also keeps a large collection of prints and drawings, much of it reaching back to Wallraf's own gathering. Works on paper are sensitive to light, so they are shown in rotation. What you see on one visit may not be there on the next. It is a reminder that a museum is not a fixed object. It is a living archive, cared for carefully, and slowly shared with each new group of visitors.

When you leave, pause again on the square. Look back at the grey stone grid, and then at the old city hall tower nearby. Somewhere between them is the story of Cologne itself — destroyed, rebuilt, and still holding on to what matters. One quiet priest started all of this by saving what others ignored. As you walk back into the streets, notice the churches, the old stones, and the small details around you. Some of them, too, are worth saving.
