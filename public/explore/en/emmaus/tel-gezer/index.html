The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through dry grass and the low branches of olive trees. Somewhere below, a tractor hums across a field. Under your shoes, the path is pale, chalky earth mixed with chips of limestone. Every step here lands on layers of old cities. The rounded hill under your feet is not a natural hill at all. It is a tel, a mound built up over thousands of years as people built, burned, and rebuilt on the same spot.

This is Tel Gezer, in the low hills that Israelis call the Shephelah, the foothills between the coastal plain and the mountains of Jerusalem. The village and ruins of Emmaus are a short drive away. Look west on a clear day and the land opens toward the sea. Look east and the ground climbs toward the Judean hills. That position is the whole story of Gezer. Whoever held this hill could watch the road between the coast and the highlands.

Ancient armies understood this very well. Egyptian pharaohs wrote about Gezer. The city appears in the records of Thutmose the Third, who campaigned through Canaan in the fifteenth century before Christ. Later, in the fourteenth century, rulers of Gezer sent letters to Egypt. These clay tablets are part of the Amarna letters, a diplomatic archive found in Egypt. In them, local kings complain, ask for help, and accuse their neighbours. Politics, it seems, has always sounded familiar.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Gezer is also named on the Merneptah Stele, a carved Egyptian victory monument from the late thirteenth century before Christ. That same stone carries one of the earliest known mentions of a people called Israel. So this quiet hill, with its olive trees and wild fennel, sits inside one of the most discussed inscriptions in the history of the region.

Walk toward the row of tall standing stones. There are ten of them, rough limestone pillars set in a line. Some rise well above a person's height. They date to the Middle Bronze Age, roughly the first half of the second millennium before Christ. Nobody knows exactly what they meant. Many scholars think they marked a sacred place, perhaps for oaths, treaties, or the memory of ancestors. Stand in front of them for a moment. They have stood in this line for close to four thousand years.

Gezer also has deep defences. Excavators uncovered a massive Canaanite gate and stretches of thick walls built from large stones. Archaeologists found that the Bronze Age city was surrounded by strong fortifications, with a tower and an earthen rampart. The scale tells you something. This was not a farming village. It was a fortified city, rich enough and worried enough to build walls this heavy.

Now comes the story most visitors arrive for. The Hebrew Bible, in the First Book of Kings, says that a pharaoh captured Gezer, burned it, and gave it as a wedding gift to his daughter, the wife of King Solomon. The same passage says Solomon rebuilt Gezer, together with Hazor and Megiddo. In the twentieth century, the Israeli archaeologist Yigael Yadin noticed that a gate here looked very much like gates at those two sites.

That gate is the six-chambered gate you can still walk through. It has three rooms on each side of a central passage, where guards stood and, perhaps, elders sat to judge disputes. Yadin linked these matching gates to Solomon's building programme. Other scholars date them later. The debate is still open, and it is one of the liveliest arguments in biblical archaeology. Run your hand along the stones and you touch the question itself.

One of the smallest objects found at Gezer is also one of the most famous. In 1908, during excavations led by the Irish archaeologist Robert Macalister, a small limestone tablet came to light. Today it is called the Gezer Calendar. It lists the farming year in short lines: months of olive harvest, sowing, flax, barley, grapes, and summer fruit. It is among the oldest known Hebrew inscriptions. Some people think a student wrote it as a writing exercise. The original is kept in Istanbul, not here.

Read those lines again in your mind while you look at the fields around you. Olives. Grain. Vines. The same crops still grow in this landscape. That is the second quiet surprise of Gezer. The ancient calendar is not only a text for scholars. It describes the view in front of you.

Look for the great water system too. Bronze Age engineers cut a deep shaft and tunnel into the bedrock to reach water under the city. Recent excavations cleared much of it again. Elsewhere in the area, rocks carry carved Hebrew and Greek words meaning boundary of Gezer. They date to the Hellenistic period, centuries after the Bronze Age. In the second century before Christ, the city was taken by Simon, one of the Maccabee brothers, who led a Jewish revolt against Greek rule.

Today the hill feels peaceful. Families from nearby communities walk here in spring, when the slopes turn green and red anemones open in the grass. Birds of prey circle above the valley. In summer the sun is hard and white, and the chalk throws the light back up at you. There is little shade on the summit, so the best moments come early in the morning or late in the afternoon, when the stones turn gold.

Before you leave, stand still on the high ground once more. Feel the breeze coming in from the coast. Look at the line of standing stones, the dark openings of the gate, the farmland spreading below. Pharaohs, kings, farmers, and scribes all knew this view. Now it is yours for a few minutes. As you walk back down the path, listen to the chalk crunch under your feet. You are walking across the top of a city that has not finished telling its story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid harsh heat.
Bring a hat and water; the summit has little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven chalk and stone paths.
Come in spring for green slopes and wild anemones.
Pair the visit with nearby Emmaus on the same day.
