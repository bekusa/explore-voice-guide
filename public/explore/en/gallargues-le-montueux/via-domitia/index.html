The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves through the dry grass. Somewhere a cicada starts and stops. Under your feet is pale ground, worn smooth in places and broken in others. It does not look like much at first. But this is one of the oldest planned roads in France. More than two thousand years ago, soldiers, merchants, mules and messengers walked this line between Italy and Spain.

The road is called the Via Domitia. It takes its name from Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus, a Roman general and magistrate. Late in the second century before Christ, Rome was building its first province beyond the Alps. Domitius organised a road to hold that territory together. Work began around 118 BCE. Historians usually describe it as the first Roman road built in Gaul. Gaul is the Roman name for the lands that later became France.

Think about the scale of the plan. The road ran from the Alpine passes in the east down to the Rhône valley. It continued through Nîmes, close to where you stand now. Then it crossed the plains of Languedoc toward Narbonne, and finally climbed into the Pyrenees toward Spain. It linked two great regions of the Roman world. Here, near Gallargues-le-Montueux, you are standing on one small piece of that long chain.

Why was a road so important to Rome? For an empire, a road was power you could walk on. Armies could march quickly in any season. Officials could carry orders and collect taxes. Traders could move wine, oil, pottery and grain to new markets. And the road made a statement. It told everyone in the region that Rome had arrived, and that Rome intended to stay.

Now look closely at the surface and the edges of the track. Roman engineers did not simply clear a path. They usually dug down and built the road in layers. Larger stones went at the bottom for strength. Smaller stones and packed gravel came above, so water could drain away. In towns and on difficult ground, they sometimes laid heavy paving slabs on top. Out in the countryside, a firm gravel surface was often enough.

The line of the road matters too. Roman surveyors liked straight routes, and they chose them with care. They kept the road on dry, solid ground where they could. They avoided marshes and flood plains when possible. That choice makes sense here. The Vidourle river runs nearby, and its floods are famous in this region. Locals even have a word for them — the vidourlades. A road that stayed above the water was a road that stayed open.

Here is something many visitors miss. Distances on this road were marked with milestones. These were tall stone columns set at regular intervals along the route. A Roman mile was about one and a half kilometres. Many milestones from the Via Domitia have been found across the region. Some carry carved inscriptions with the names and titles of emperors who repaired the road. So each stone worked as a distance marker — and as political advertising.

Not far to the west, across the Vidourle, lies the ancient site of Ambrussum. It was a hilltop settlement and a stopping place on the Via Domitia. Travellers could rest, change horses and find food there. Archaeologists have excavated a section of paved road at Ambrussum. Deep grooves from cart wheels still mark the stones. If you have time, it is a good place to see what this road looked like at its best.

At Ambrussum, the road crossed the river on a stone bridge. Today it is known as the Pont Ambroix. Only part of it still stands — the floods of the Vidourle carried the rest away over the centuries. In the nineteenth century, the painter Gustave Courbet painted this broken bridge. So the same river that the Roman engineers feared later became a subject for French art.

Now imagine the people who used this stretch. A soldier walking toward Spain with a heavy pack. A merchant leading mules loaded with jars of wine. A courier on horseback, carrying letters to Narbonne. A family walking to market at Nîmes. Most of them left no names. But together they wore this route into the land. The road did more than connect places. It shaped the daily life of everyone who lived along it.

The road also had a long life after Rome. When the empire fell, people did not forget a good route. Medieval travellers, pilgrims and traders kept using sections of it. Later roads often followed the same corridor. Look around and listen to the traffic in the distance. Modern highways in this region still follow the same corridor between the Rhône and Spain. The geography that guided the Roman surveyors still guides drivers today.

The village of Gallargues-le-Montueux sits on its hill nearby. The name refers to its high position above the plain. From up there, people could watch the flat land and the river below. Villages in this area grew among vineyards, olive trees and dry stone walls. Their limestone houses have the same warm colour as the soil. When you walk back toward the village, notice how closely the land, the stone and the road belong together.

Before you leave, take one last look at the track. It is quiet now. There are no carts, no soldiers, no market crowds. Just stones, dust and the open sky over Languedoc. But the line is still here, and it still points somewhere. As you walk away, think of the long road ahead of you — east toward the Alps, west toward Spain — and of all the feet that walked this way before yours.

Practical tips

Bring water and a hat for exposed summer heat.
Combine your walk with a visit to nearby Ambrussum.
Come early morning when the light is soft and low.
Check weather before visiting after heavy rain near the Vidourle.
