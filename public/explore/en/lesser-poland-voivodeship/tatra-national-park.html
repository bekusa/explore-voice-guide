The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water runs somewhere below you, loud and cold, over grey stone. The air smells of wet spruce and crushed grass. Above the tree line the rock goes bare and pale, and the light changes fast as clouds slide over the ridges. This is the Tatras, the highest mountains between the Alps and the Caucasus. Welcome to Tatra National Park, on Poland's southern border with Slovakia.

The park was established in 1954. It protects the Polish side of a compact, steep range. The Slovak side has its own national park, and together the two form a single cross-border biosphere reserve recognised by UNESCO. So the ridge in front of you is not really a border at all. It is one mountain world, shared by two countries. Chamois, marmots and bears cross it freely. They do not need a passport.

The range is small on a map. You could drive around it in an afternoon. But inside that small area, the land rises very quickly. Valleys of meadow and forest climb into walls of rock, and the rock climbs into sharp, jagged peaks. That steepness is why the Tatras feel so much bigger than their size. A short walk takes you from cows and wooden huts into a landscape that looks almost alpine.

Look closely at the stone, and you will notice the park has two characters. In the east rise the High Tatras, built mostly of hard granite. Those peaks are grey, sharp and rough, with deep lakes in their hollows. In the west the Western Tatras carry more limestone. Limestone dissolves slowly in water, so the western valleys hold caves, springs and softer, greener slopes. Same range, two very different textures underfoot.

Those lakes have a local name. Poles call them stawy, which simply means ponds. The word is modest, because these are glacial lakes. Ice once filled the valleys here, carving bowls into the granite. When the ice melted, water filled them. The most famous is Morskie Oko, the Eye of the Sea. It is one of the largest lakes in the whole range, framed by cliffs that rise straight from the water.

Here is something many visitors miss. The highest point in Poland is inside this park. It is Rysy, and the Polish summit stands just under 2,500 metres. But the mountain has more than one top, and its highest summit lies a little further on, in Slovakia. So when you climb to Poland's highest point, you are standing on a shoulder, looking up at a neighbour. It is a good reminder of how the border cuts through the rock.

Now turn toward the north side of the range. A long rocky profile rises above the town of Zakopane. This is Giewont, and locals say its shape looks like a sleeping knight. Legend has it that the knight will wake when Poland needs him. On the summit stands a large iron cross, raised at the start of the twentieth century. On a clear day you can see it from the streets below, small and dark against the sky.

Zakopane itself is the gateway to the park. In the nineteenth century it was a poor mountain village. Then doctors began to praise the clean mountain air, and artists and writers followed. One physician, Tytus Chałubiński, did a great deal to make the place known. Painters, composers and poets came to walk these trails. Soon the village had its own style of wooden architecture, inspired by the houses of the local highlanders.

Those highlanders are the Górale, the people of the mountains. Their culture is still very much alive. You will hear their dialect, and sometimes their sharp, fast fiddle music. In the meadows, shepherds still graze sheep in summer, and they make oscypek. That is a firm, smoked sheep's cheese, often pressed in carved wooden moulds. The pattern on the crust comes from the mould. Look for it on stalls near the trailheads.

And now, a word about the wind. The Tatras have a famous one, called the halny. It is a warm, dry wind that falls down the northern slopes from the south. It can arrive suddenly and blow very hard, bending forests and tearing roofs. Locals say it also changes people's moods, making them restless and irritable. Whether or not that is true, the halny shows how fast mountain weather can turn here.

That is the most important practical lesson of this park. The weather changes quickly. A warm, blue morning can become a cold, wet afternoon, with storms building over the ridges. Lightning is a real danger on the high, exposed paths, especially in summer. The higher routes also include chains and steep rock, and some are serious climbs. Choose a trail that matches your fitness, and turn back early if the sky darkens.

The animals here are the other reason to walk slowly. The chamois, a nimble mountain goat-antelope, is the symbol of the park. You may see one on a far ledge, almost the same colour as the rock. Listen for a high, sharp whistle. That is usually a marmot warning the others. Brown bears live here too, mostly out of sight. Keep your food packed away, and never feed any wild animal.

So pause here for a minute before you move on. Feel the cool air on your face, coming down from the stone. Look at the line where the dark forest stops and bare rock begins. Notice how the clouds catch on the peaks and then let them go. As you walk on, listen again for running water. It has been shaping these valleys since the ice left, and it is still working now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start early to avoid crowds and afternoon summer storms.
Pack waterproof layers even when the morning looks clear.
Stay on marked trails to protect plants and yourself.
Turn back early if thunderclouds build over the ridges.
Choose high routes with chains only if you have experience.
