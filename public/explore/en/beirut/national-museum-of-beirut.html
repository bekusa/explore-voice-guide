The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the foot of the front steps. The stone under your hand is warm and sandy-coloured, a pale ochre that holds the Beirut sun long after noon. Behind you, traffic hums along a wide avenue. Ahead, tall columns rise in a style that looks almost Egyptian, with heavy, simple lines. It is a calm building. The calm makes its story more surprising, because for fifteen years this was one of the most dangerous places in the city.

This is the National Museum of Beirut, the country's main home for archaeology. Inside are objects that cover thousands of years of life along this coast. There are prehistoric tools, Bronze Age figurines, Phoenician sarcophagi, Roman mosaics and medieval pottery. The collection is not huge by world standards. But almost every piece comes from Lebanese soil, and many of them changed how scholars understand the ancient Mediterranean. Let us walk through it slowly, one room at a time.

First, a word about the building itself. The collection began to take shape after the First World War, when Lebanon was under French mandate. The museum was designed by two architects, Antoine Nahas and Pierre Leprince-Ringuet. Their design draws on the Egyptian Revival style, a fashion in the early twentieth century for forms borrowed from ancient Egypt. Construction ran through the 1930s, and the museum opened to the public in the early 1940s. The local limestone gives it that soft, honey-like tone you see now.

Now look at the road outside. During the Lebanese civil war, from 1975 to 1990, this avenue became part of the Green Line, the front that divided east and west Beirut. The name came from the weeds and trees that grew across the empty ground. The museum sat directly on the line. Militias used the building as a position. Shells hit the walls. For years, almost no one could safely approach the front door you just walked through.

Here is the first surprise. The collection survived, and mostly intact. The museum's director, Emir Maurice Chehab, and his wife Olga took quiet, practical steps as the fighting grew. Small objects were moved to the basement, and the doorways were sealed with walls. The great stone sarcophagi were too heavy to move. So the team built concrete cases around them, right where they stood. Floor mosaics were covered with concrete too. The museum hid its treasures inside itself.

After the war, the work of uncovering began. Restorers broke the concrete away, piece by piece. Some objects had suffered from water and damp in the sealed basement, and they needed long years of care. The museum reopened in stages in the late 1990s. As you walk the ground floor, notice how clean and bright the stone looks. Remember that much of it spent more than a decade in darkness, wrapped in cement, while the city fought outside.

The most important single object here may be the sarcophagus of Ahiram, a king of Byblos, an ancient port city north of Beirut. It is a large stone coffin, carved with mourners and a seated king. But the real treasure is the line of writing cut along its lid. It is one of the earliest known inscriptions in the Phoenician alphabet. Scholars date it to around 1000 BC, though the exact date is still debated.

Why does that matter to you, standing here? Because the Phoenician alphabet is an ancestor of the Greek alphabet. Greek letters in turn led to the Latin letters you are using to read the signs in this room. Look closely at those worn marks on the lid. In a real sense, they are early relatives of the letters on your phone screen. It is a small band of carving, easy to walk past. Many visitors do.

From Byblos also come many small bronze figurines, some covered with gold leaf. They were found buried near a temple there, and they show slim standing figures, often wearing tall pointed hats. They are offerings, left by worshippers thousands of years ago. Their size is the surprise — many are no taller than your hand. Lean in close to the glass. The gold still catches the light, and you can see the rough marks where the metal was shaped.

In the lower level, the mood changes. This is the world of the dead. Rows of white marble sarcophagi from the area around Sidon, a southern coastal city, show calm human faces carved on the lids. They mix Phoenician custom with a Greek sense of form. Nearby are richly carved Roman sarcophagi from Tyre, further south, with scenes from Greek myth. The room is cool and quiet. Footsteps sound softer here, and the faces seem to watch you pass.

The basement also holds something unexpected — mummified bodies from the medieval period. They were found in a cave in the Qadisha Valley, a deep mountain gorge in northern Lebanon known for its old monasteries. The dry air of the cave preserved the bodies and even some of their clothing. These were ordinary people, including children, not kings. Many visitors find this the most moving corner of the museum. Take your time here, and keep your voice low.

Upstairs, the galleries are smaller and closer to daily life. You will find pottery, glass, jewellery, coins and weapons, arranged roughly by period. Roman glass from this coast is especially fine, thin and pale green. Then look down. Large mosaic floors spread across parts of the museum, with geometric patterns, animals and figures. They once lay in homes and public buildings. Now you walk beside them, seeing what their owners saw under their own feet.

The museum also shows a short film about the rescue of the collection and its restoration after the war. If it is running when you visit, it is worth a few minutes. You see the concrete blocks, the dust, and the people who worked on them. It turns the quiet rooms around you into a story of care. The building is not only about the ancient past. It also carries the recent memory of the city.

When you step back outside, pause again on those front steps. Look along the avenue that once divided Beirut. Cars move freely now. Trees line the road. The pale stone of the façade still carries small traces of repair, if you look carefully. Behind you are kings, gods and ordinary families from thousands of years of this coast. Ahead of you is the living city. For a moment, you are standing exactly on the line between them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start on the ground floor to see the large sarcophagi first.
Watch the short restoration film if it is showing.
Save the basement funerary galleries for when you have time.
Bring a light layer for the cooler lower level.
Step back across the avenue to photograph the full façade.
