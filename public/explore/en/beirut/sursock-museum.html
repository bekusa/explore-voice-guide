The guide

The story

Start with the colour. On a bright Beirut morning, the façade of this villa glows a clean, chalky white. Pointed arches frame tall windows of coloured glass. When the sun is low, those panes throw small pools of red, blue and amber onto the floors inside. Out on the street, you hear car horns from Achrafieh and the soft scrape of shoes on the steps. Then you walk through the door, and the noise of the city drops away.

You are standing at the Sursock Museum, on Rue Sursock in the Achrafieh district. This is one of the oldest Christian quarters of Beirut. For generations it was home to wealthy merchant families. The street takes its name from one of the most prominent of them, the Sursock family. Their mansions still line the slope here, with high garden walls, old trees, and gates that keep their secrets well.

The building began as a private home. Nicolas Ibrahim Sursock, a collector and patron of the arts, had it built in the early twentieth century, around 1912. When he died in 1952, he left the villa to the city of Beirut. His wish was clear: the house should become a museum. That is a small surprise for many visitors. This building was not a palace for a ruler. It was one man's gift to his city.

Look closely at the architecture. The villa mixes two worlds. You can see the Lebanese mansions of the late Ottoman period, the era when Beirut was part of the Ottoman Empire. You can also see the style of Venetian palaces, with their delicate arches and decorated windows. Wealthy Beirut families often blended local traditions with European fashions. Here, the result is light and almost playful — a house that looks across the Mediterranean in both directions.

The museum opened its doors in 1961. That same year, it launched the Salon d'Automne, which means the Autumn Salon in French. It is an exhibition of work by artists living in Lebanon. Over the decades, this salon became an important stage for Lebanese painters and sculptors. A place in the Autumn Salon could help a young artist build a name. Many well-known careers in Lebanese art passed through these rooms.

Inside, one room usually stops people. It is the Arab Salon, a formal reception room from the original house. The walls and ceiling carry richly carved and painted wood. The room shows how an elite Beirut family once received guests. Take a moment here. Notice the patterns, the warm tones of the wood, and how the coloured light from the windows touches the surfaces. It is the clearest view into the villa's first life as a home.

Nicolas Sursock's private office has also been preserved. It gives a quieter, more personal feeling than the grand salon. You can imagine a collector at his desk, writing letters or studying a new painting. These original rooms are a gentle reminder. The museum did not begin with a committee or a government plan. It began with one person's taste, and with the objects he loved enough to keep close.

Now, here is something many visitors miss. Much of the modern museum lies under your feet. A major renovation and expansion took place over several years, led by the architects Jacques Abou Khaled and Jean-Michel Wilmotte. When the museum reopened in 2015, it had several new floors built below ground. These added exhibition halls, storage, a library and an auditorium. From the street, the historic villa looks almost unchanged. Underneath, it is a fully modern institution.

The collection focuses on modern and contemporary art, especially art connected to Lebanon. You will find paintings, sculptures and works on paper. The museum also cares for historic prints and old photographs. Among them is the Fouad Debbas Collection, a large archive of images from Lebanon and the wider region. These photographs show Beirut as it once looked — streets, ports, costumes and faces that have long since changed. Exhibitions rotate, so each visit can feel different.

Then comes the hardest chapter. On the fourth of August 2020, a huge explosion tore through the port of Beirut, not far downhill from here. The blast killed and injured many people and damaged whole neighbourhoods. The Sursock Museum was badly hit. Its stained glass shattered. Doors and ceilings broke. Many artworks were damaged. For this city, the museum's wounds were part of a much larger grief shared by thousands of families.

Restoration took several years. Specialists repaired the building, the glass and many of the damaged works, with support from international partners. The museum reopened to the public in 2023. Some visitors look at the windows today and wonder what is old and what is new. That question has its own meaning. The building carries both its original story and the story of how Beirut chose to rebuild what it loved.

Before you leave, give yourself time outside as well. The front steps and the garden area offer a calm pause from the traffic. Look up at the façade again and follow the line of the arches. Then look along Rue Sursock at the neighbouring mansions. Some are restored. Others still show cracks, scaffolding or empty windows. Together, they show a street, and a city, still in the middle of repair.

So, as you stand here, notice the light one more time. See how it moves across the white stone and catches the coloured glass. Listen to the city returning as you step back toward the street. This house was a home, then a gift, then a gallery, then a wound — and now, once more, a place where people come to look carefully. Take that slower way of looking with you as you walk on.
