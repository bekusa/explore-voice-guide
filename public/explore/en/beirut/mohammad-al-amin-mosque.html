The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the edge of the square. Traffic hums along the wide roads of downtown Beirut, and a sea breeze carries the faint smell of salt. Above you, a broad blue dome sits on walls of warm, honey-coloured stone. Four slender minarets rise at its corners. When the call to prayer begins, the sound rolls out across the open ground, and the noise of the city seems to step back for a moment.

This is the Mohammad Al-Amin Mosque, one of the largest mosques in Lebanon. Its name honours the Prophet Muhammad. Al-Amin is a title he carried even before his prophethood, and it means 'the trustworthy one'. The mosque serves the city's Sunni Muslim community. It is also a public landmark, and visitors of every faith come here to look and listen. Its scale is the first thing most people notice. Up close, the scale feels larger still.

The building looks centuries old, but it is not. Construction began in the early 2000s, and the mosque opened in 2008. The design deliberately follows the classical Ottoman style. The Ottomans were the Turkish empire that ruled Lebanon for roughly four hundred years. Many visitors compare the silhouette to the great imperial mosques of Istanbul. You can see why: the cascade of domes, the pencil-thin minarets, the strong symmetry. Here that old language of architecture is spoken in fresh, clean stone.

The man most closely tied to this mosque is Rafic Hariri. He was a businessman who became prime minister of Lebanon, and he championed the rebuilding of Beirut's centre after the civil war. He supported the mosque project from its early stages. He never saw it finished. In February 2005, a massive bomb killed him and others on the Beirut seafront. His death shook the country. It also changed the meaning of this building forever.

Here is something many visitors miss. Hariri is buried right beside the mosque. His tomb sits in a covered memorial area next to the main building, alongside others who died in the same attack. People still come to pause there, to pray, or simply to stand quietly. So this place is not only a house of worship. It is also a grave, and for many Lebanese it is a political memorial.

Now turn and look at the ground you are standing on. This is Martyrs' Square, the old heart of the city. Its name recalls Lebanese and Syrian nationalists executed here by Ottoman authorities during the First World War. A bronze statue honours them nearby. Its surface still carries bullet holes from later fighting. Over the past century, this square has seen markets, cinemas, tram lines, and front lines. It has always been a place where Beirut gathers.

During the civil war, which lasted from 1975 to 1990, this area became a dividing line between the city's east and west. Buildings stood empty and broken. Grass and trees grew through the ruins. After the war, a large reconstruction effort cleared and rebuilt much of downtown. The mosque rose in the middle of that rebuilding. To some people, it represents recovery. To others, it represents a rebuilt centre that feels distant from ordinary life. Both views are part of its story.

In 2005, weeks after Hariri's death, huge crowds filled this square. They demanded the withdrawal of Syrian troops from Lebanon. That movement became known as the Cedar Revolution, named after the cedar tree on the Lebanese flag. The new mosque stood behind the protesters like a backdrop on a stage. Years later, in 2019, crowds returned to this same open space during nationwide protests. The blue dome appears in countless photographs of both moments.

Now look just to the side of the mosque. Very close by stands Saint George Maronite Cathedral, a Christian church with its own tall bell tower. The minarets and the bell tower rise almost side by side. This is not an accident of a single plan. It is the shape of Beirut itself, a city where many religious communities live close together. Locals often point to this view. Sometimes the bells and the call to prayer can be heard at nearly the same time.

If you go inside, the mood changes at once. Shoes come off at the door, and soft carpet replaces stone underfoot. Women are asked to cover their hair, and modest clothing is expected from everyone. Loose robes are often available at the entrance for visitors who need them. The noise of the square fades. Light falls from high windows onto the prayer hall, and the space feels wide and calm.

Lift your eyes toward the ceiling. The interior of the central dome is decorated with painted patterns and Arabic calligraphy. In Islamic art, sacred words often take the place of images of people. Lettering becomes the main decoration. Notice how the patterns repeat and interlock, guiding the eye upward. A large chandelier hangs low over the prayer hall. Its many lights add warmth to the cool blues, creams, and golds of the walls.

On the outside, take a slow walk around the building if you can. Look at the arches, the small half-domes stepping down from the central dome, and the balconies on each minaret. In older mosques, the muezzin climbed to those balconies to call the faithful. Today loudspeakers do the work, but the design keeps the old form. The stone is still fairly pale and fresh. In strong sun, it glows almost golden.

Before you leave, stand back again at the edge of the square. Look at the blue dome, the minarets, the bell tower beside them, and the bronze statue across the open ground. Listen to the traffic, the gulls, perhaps a prayer call or a church bell. This young building already carries a great deal of Beirut's recent story. As you walk away, glance back once more. The dome stays visible for a long way, like a fixed point in a city that keeps rebuilding itself.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees before you enter.
Women should bring a scarf to cover their hair inside.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers out of respect.
Remove your shoes at the entrance before stepping onto carpets.
Ask before photographing worshippers inside the prayer hall.
