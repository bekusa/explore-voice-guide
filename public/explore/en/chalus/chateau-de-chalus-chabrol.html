The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves across the hilltop, and somewhere below you the small town of Châlus goes about its day. Under your feet the ground rises toward a round stone tower. Its walls are grey and rough, patched in places, and darker where rain has run down them for centuries. This is the Château de Châlus-Chabrol. It is a modest castle in the green hills of the Limousin, in west-central France. Yet one evening in the spring of 1199, the course of English and French history changed here.

The man at the centre of that story was Richard the First of England, remembered as Richard the Lionheart. He was King of England, but he was also a great lord in France. He was Duke of Aquitaine, the wide southwestern region his mother, Eleanor, had brought into the family. This countryside was part of his inheritance. Its local lords, however, were often in revolt, and Richard spent much of his life riding from one rebellious castle to the next.

In 1199 his quarrel was with Aimar, Viscount of Limoges, the lord connected to this fortress. The chroniclers of the time give different reasons for the siege. One popular account says a peasant had dug up a hoard of ancient gold nearby. Richard, so the tale goes, demanded the treasure as overlord and besieged Châlus when it was refused. Many historians now treat the treasure story with caution. A dispute over loyalty and power in a restless province is the more likely cause.

What the sources broadly agree on is what happened next. The castle was small and lightly defended. Richard came close to the walls one evening to inspect the work of his soldiers. He was not wearing his full armour. From the ramparts, a defender raised a crossbow and fired. The bolt struck the king in the shoulder, near the neck. It did not seem to be a fatal wound at first, and the siege went on.

Here is the first surprise most visitors miss. The wound itself did not kill Richard quickly. The removal of the bolt was clumsy, and the injury became infected. Over roughly the next eleven or twelve days, gangrene spread. Richard understood that he was dying. He settled his affairs and named his brother John as his heir. He died on the sixth of April, 1199. One of the most famous warriors of the Middle Ages was brought down not on a great battlefield, but at a minor castle in the Limousin hills.

The identity of the crossbowman is still uncertain. Chroniclers give him different names, and no single version is fully secure. A well-known story says that the dying king had the man brought before him. Richard asked why he had fired. The young man answered that the king had killed members of his family. According to this account, Richard forgave him and ordered his release. Some chroniclers then add a darker ending: after Richard died, the archer was seized and executed anyway. It is a powerful scene, but treat it as part legend and part record.

Now look at the tower in front of you. This round keep is the heart of the castle, and it is the oldest and most dramatic part of the site. A round keep was harder to undermine than a square one, because there were no sharp corners for attackers to weaken. Notice how few openings the lower walls have. Small, narrow windows let in light while keeping arrows and stones out. The thick masonry was built for defence, not for comfort.

Beside the keep stands a later residential building. Over the centuries, many French castles changed in the same way. Once the danger of constant warfare faded, owners wanted larger windows, warmer rooms and more light. So the fortress slowly became a home. As you walk around, compare the two buildings. One belongs to an age of siege and suspicion. The other belongs to an age of daily life, with families living behind its walls.

Here is the second surprise. Richard's body was not buried in one place. Medieval kings were sometimes divided after death, so that several places could share in their memory. Tradition holds that his entrails were buried here at Châlus. His heart was taken to Rouen in Normandy. His body was carried to the abbey of Fontevraud in the Loire Valley, where he lies near his father, Henry the Second, and his mother, Eleanor of Aquitaine. So, in a sense, this small castle holds part of the Lionheart forever.

Châlus also has a second castle, lower down in the town. People here sometimes speak of the two fortresses together, the upper and the lower. Chabrol, where you stand now, is the one tied to Richard's death. Today the castle is one of the stops on a tourist route named after Richard the Lionheart. The route links castles and towns across this part of France that are connected to the king and his family. It is a reminder that the Plantagenets, the royal family of medieval England, were rooted deeply in French soil.

Take a moment to look outward from the hill. You can see why a castle was built here. The height gave the defenders a clear view of the roads and valleys around them. This was border country between rival lords, and whoever held the high ground could watch who came and went. The rolling fields, the hedges and the patches of woodland still look like quiet farmland. In 1199, however, this landscape was full of soldiers, tents and the noise of a siege camp.

Try to picture that last evening. The light is fading. The king walks forward to look at the walls. The defenders are tired, frightened and short of weapons. Someone on the battlements makes a single decision and lets go of the trigger. In that moment, a crown moved toward John, a king whose reign would bring loss of territory in France and, later, the charter known as Magna Carta. It is hard to stand here without thinking about how much depended on one short flight of a bolt.

Before you leave, rest your hand on the cool stone if you can, and look up at the curve of the keep against the sky. Listen again to the wind and the small sounds of the town below. The castle does not announce its history with grand gates or golden halls. It is quiet, weathered and human in scale. As you walk back down the hill, notice how quickly the tower slips behind the trees. It is easy to pass by. Nine centuries ago, a king did not.
