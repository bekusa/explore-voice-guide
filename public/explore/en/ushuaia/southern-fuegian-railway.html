The guide

The story

Listen first. Before the whistle, there is the hiss of steam escaping from a small locomotive. It is a soft, steady sound in the cold air. Then you notice the smell, coal smoke and damp earth, mixed with something green and sharp from the forest. Around you the mountains of Tierra del Fuego rise in grey and white. The air feels clean and thin, and even in summer it carries a chill. You are at the station of a railway that people often call the train at the end of the world.

Its formal name is the Southern Fuegian Railway, or in Spanish, the Ferrocarril Austral Fueguino. Today it is a heritage tourist line. It runs on narrow track, only about half a metre wide, from a station a short drive west of Ushuaia into the edge of Tierra del Fuego National Park. The carriages are small and painted in bright colours. They look almost like toys against the scale of the landscape. But the story behind this little line is not a gentle one.

To understand it, you need to go back to the early twentieth century. Argentina wanted to establish a firm presence at the far southern tip of the continent. One of its tools was a prison. The government sent convicts to Ushuaia, and the prisoners helped build the town itself. They laid streets, put up buildings, and worked in the surrounding forest. Ushuaia grew, in large part, around the walls of that penal colony.

The prisoners needed timber. They needed it for construction, and they needed it for heat through the long, dark winters. So a railway was built to carry wood from the forest back to the prison. Convicts rode out on the train in the morning, cut trees under guard, and rode back with the logs. For this reason, many people still call it the prisoners' train. Almost every rail you ride over today follows the memory of that daily journey.

Here is something many visitors miss as they settle into their seats. The original line did not run on this exact route in its full form, and it did not survive. The prison closed in the late 1940s, and the old railway closed around the same time. For decades the forest took back the track. The train you are riding was rebuilt and reopened as a tourist line in the 1990s, partly along the old course. So what you see is a careful revival, not an unbroken survivor.

As the train pulls away, look out at the valley. The line follows the Pipo River, a fast, cold stream that runs over smooth stones. Locals say the river takes its name from a prisoner nicknamed Pipo. According to the popular tale, he escaped into these woods and was never seen alive again. Whether every detail is true is hard to say. But the story tells you how people here think of this land: beautiful, and very hard to survive in alone.

Now look closely at the ground beside the track. You will see hundreds of tree stumps, cut low and left behind. This open, scarred area is sometimes called the tree cemetery. These stumps are the direct physical record of the prison work. Each one is a tree that convicts cut by hand. Because the climate is so cold and wet, wood here breaks down slowly. That is why the stumps are still visible so many decades later.

Between the stumps, notice the soft, spongy ground. This is peat bog, a kind of wetland built from layer upon layer of slowly rotting moss. It is common in Tierra del Fuego, and it holds water like a sponge. In places it glows in shades of red, rust and bright green. On a grey day, these colours are often the warmest thing in the view. They change with the seasons, so no two visits look quite the same.

Part of the way along, the train usually makes a stop near a waterfall known as La Macarena. You can step out, stretch your legs, and walk a short path for a better view. Near the stop there is also a reconstruction of a camp of the Yámana people. The Yámana, sometimes called the Yaghan, lived on these shores and channels long before any prison or railway. They travelled by canoe and lived from the sea, in a climate most outsiders found almost impossible.

That stop is worth a quiet moment. The Yámana population fell sharply after European settlers, missionaries and new diseases arrived in the nineteenth century. Their history here is much older and much deeper than the railway's. The reconstructed shelters are simple, made of branches and covering materials. They give only a small sense of a whole way of life. Still, they remind you that this end of the world was home to people for thousands of years.

Back on board, watch the locomotive itself when the line curves. You can sometimes see it from your window, working hard at the front. Steam engines are used on this line, which is rare for any railway today. The heavy puffs of smoke rise and drift across the trees. Listen to the rhythm of the wheels on the narrow track. It is slower and more patient than any modern train you might be used to.

The ride ends inside the national park. Tierra del Fuego National Park stretches along the Beagle Channel toward the border with Chile. From the final station, many visitors continue by road or on foot to explore the trails, lakes and coastline. The forest here is dominated by southern beech trees. Some of them grow bent and twisted, shaped by the constant wind. It is a landscape that feels old, quiet and very far from anywhere.

The railway is often described as the southernmost working railway in the world. That claim is part of its appeal, and part of its marketing. But perhaps the more interesting idea is what the train asks you to hold in your mind at once. There is the beauty of the valley. There is the memory of men in prison uniforms cutting wood in the cold. And there is the much longer story of the Yámana, who were here first.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment by the track. Look at the low stumps, the red and green bog, the river running over its stones. Listen for the hiss of steam, or for the silence after the train has gone. Feel the cold on your face, the same cold the prisoners knew. As you walk away, notice how small the line looks against the mountains... and how much history it carries on such narrow rails.
