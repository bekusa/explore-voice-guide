The guide

The story

Start with your hand near the stone, without touching it. The sandstone is warm, the colour of weak tea, and the edges have gone soft after many centuries of wind and monsoon rain. Around you, the countryside of Lalitpur is quiet. You may hear birds in the scrub, a motorbike on a distant road, and perhaps the faint movement of the Betwa River not far away. This small, square building does not look like much at first. But you are standing in front of one of the oldest surviving stone Hindu temples in northern India.

The temple belongs to the Gupta period. The Guptas were a dynasty that ruled much of northern India between roughly the fourth and sixth centuries. Scholars usually date this shrine to around the early sixth century of the Common Era. That makes it about fifteen hundred years old. It was built in a time when temple builders in the north were still working out what a Hindu temple in stone should look like. So this is not a copy of an older model. In many ways, it is one of the early experiments.

The temple is dedicated to Vishnu, the Hindu god who preserves the order of the world. The name Dashavatara means 'ten avatars'. An avatar is a form that Vishnu takes when he comes down to earth. The name was given to the temple much later, and it is not certain that the original builders used it. Still, the carvings here do show Vishnu in several of his forms and stories. So the name is a fair description, even if it is not the first one.

Look at how the building sits. The shrine stands on a wide, raised platform, and you climb up to reach it. Historians describe the original layout as a panchayatana plan. In this plan, one main shrine sits at the centre, with four smaller shrines placed at the corners of the platform. Those corner shrines are gone today. What remains is the central cell, alone on its stone base. Try to picture four more small buildings around you — the platform would have felt much more crowded and much more complete.

Now look up. Above the shrine, you can see the broken stump of a tower. This is one of the earliest known examples of a shikhara, the tall, tapering tower that later became the signature of north Indian temples. In later centuries, these towers would rise high above towns and villages. Here, the idea is still young and modest. Only part of it survives. But when you look at the great temples of later periods, you can remember that a version of the same idea stood here first.

Walk around to the doorway, which faces west. This is the finest piece of carving at the site, and it rewards slow looking. The door frame is built in bands, one inside another, each carved with a different pattern. You can find scrolling plants, small human figures, and couples standing close together. Near the top corners, look for two female figures. They are the river goddesses Ganga and Yamuna, the two sacred rivers of northern India. They stand at the threshold to purify anyone who enters.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. In Gupta-period doorways like this one, the river goddesses appear at the top of the frame. In many later Indian temples, they move down to the bottom, near the feet of the visitor. So the position of these two small figures is one of the clues that helps scholars place the temple in time. It is a quiet detail, but it is like a date written in stone.

The other three walls do not have real doors. Instead, each one holds a large carved panel set into a frame, like a false doorway. Each panel tells a story about Vishnu. Walk to the south side first. Here you will find Vishnu lying on the coils of the great serpent Shesha, floating on the cosmic ocean, resting between the creation of one world and the next. From his navel, a lotus rises, and the god Brahma sits on the flower. Other gods gather above him.

On the north side, the panel shows the story of Gajendra Moksha, which means 'the release of the elephant king'. According to the story, an elephant was bathing in a lake when a crocodile, or in some versions a serpent, seized its leg. The elephant could not escape, and it called out to Vishnu. The god came down on his eagle mount, Garuda, and set the elephant free. Look for the elephant in the water and the god arriving from above. The story is about rescue and devotion, and it is easy to read even without words.

The east side shows Nara and Narayana, two sages in meditation. In Hindu tradition, they are understood as a form of Vishnu himself, practising austerity in the Himalayas. Their faces are calm, and their bodies are relaxed. Compare this quiet scene with the drama of the elephant panel. Together, the three walls show three moods of the same god — resting, rescuing, and meditating. It is a small temple, but the builders planned it to carry a large set of ideas.

The platform once held more stories. Its sides carried carved panels with scenes from the Ramayana, the epic of Prince Rama, and from the life of Krishna, another avatar of Vishnu. Many of these panels have been damaged, and some have been removed to museums for protection. As you walk the base, look closely at what remains. Even broken figures show the soft, rounded style of Gupta sculpture, with full faces and smooth, easy poses. This style influenced Indian art for centuries.

The temple was recorded by archaeologists of the Archaeological Survey of India during the nineteenth century, and it is now a protected monument. Deogarh itself is more than this one shrine. On the nearby hill, overlooking the Betwa, there is a large group of Jain temples from later centuries, filled with carved figures. If you have the time and energy, the two sites together show how this quiet place drew builders and believers of different faiths over many hundreds of years.

Before you leave, stand back from the platform and take in the whole building again. Notice how small it is. A few people could stand inside the shrine at once, no more. It was never meant for crowds. It was meant to hold the image of the god, and to show his stories to anyone who walked around it. That walk, in a circle around the shrine, is still a form of worship in India today.

So walk it once more, slowly. Pass the sleeping Vishnu, the rescued elephant, the two meditating sages, and the doorway with its river goddesses. Feel the warm stone under the afternoon light and listen to the open country around you. Fifteen centuries ago, someone shaped these blocks and carved these faces, without knowing who would come to look. Today, that person is you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk clockwise around the shrine to follow the panels.
Bring water and a hat; shade is limited.
Visit in late afternoon for warmer light on the carvings.
Combine your visit with the Jain temples on the hill.
Bring binoculars to study the upper doorway carvings.
