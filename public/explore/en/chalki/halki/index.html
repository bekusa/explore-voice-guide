The guide

The story

Listen first. A small motorboat engine idles somewhere in the harbour. Water slaps gently against stone. A bell sounds from a tall tower, and the sound carries across the bay. Then there is quiet again. The light here is strong and clean. It falls on rows of houses painted ochre, pale blue, rose and cream. Their wooden shutters face the sea. Welcome to Halki, one of the smallest inhabited islands of the Dodecanese, the island chain in the southeastern Aegean.

Halki sits just off the western coast of Rhodes, its much larger neighbour. On a clear day you can see the mountains of Rhodes across the water. The island is dry, rocky and steep. Low scrub, thyme and bare limestone cover most of its hills. There are no forests and few springs. Life on Halki has always looked outward, toward the sea. That simple fact shaped almost everything you see around you now.

You are most likely standing in Emporio, the harbour village, also called Nimborio. This is where almost all the island's people live today. The houses climb the slopes in an amphitheatre around the bay. Many of them show a neoclassical style, which means they borrow from ancient Greek design. Look for triangular pediments above the windows, simple columns and tiled roofs. This style was popular across Greek islands in the nineteenth century. It was a way for families to show prosperity and connection to the wider Greek world.

Where did that prosperity come from? Here is the first surprise. For generations, the wealth of Halki came from the seabed. The men of this island were sponge divers. They sailed out in small boats and dived deep for natural sponges, which sold across Europe and beyond. It was skilled work, and it was dangerous. Divers faced cold, darkness and pressure sickness. Many did not come home. Even so, the trade paid for these grand captains' houses along the waterfront.

The sponge trade did not last. In the early twentieth century, the island's economy declined, and many families left. A large number crossed the Atlantic to the United States. They settled in Tarpon Springs, a town on the Gulf coast of Florida, where Greek divers built a new sponge industry. Halki emptied. Many houses in Emporio stood abandoned for decades. Some still stand roofless today, between the restored ones. Notice the contrast as you walk.

The link with Florida never broke. That is the second surprise. The main road on the island, which runs from the harbour up toward the old inland village, carries the name Tarpon Springs Boulevard. It is a short road on a tiny island, with a grand American name. Locals say that emigrants in Florida sent money home over the years, to help families and to support village projects. The road name is a quiet reminder of that long family bridge across the ocean.

Now turn your eyes to the tall bell tower above the harbour. It belongs to the Church of Saint Nicholas, the patron saint of sailors in the Greek Orthodox tradition. For an island of divers and seamen, that choice makes sense. The tower is built of pale stone and rises in tiers. Step into the churchyard if it is open. Look down at your feet. The courtyard is covered with a pebble mosaic, called choklaki in Greek. Black and white sea stones are set on edge to form patterns.

These pebble floors are a craft found across the Dodecanese. Workers collected smooth stones from the beaches and sorted them by colour. Then they pressed them into the ground, one by one, to make stars, waves, flowers and geometric shapes. The result is beautiful, but also practical. The surface drains rainwater and stays cool in summer heat. Walk slowly across it and feel the ridges through your shoes. Every stone was placed by hand.

Look back along the waterfront for another landmark: a stone clock tower near the harbour. It is a small, square building with a clock face near the top. It has become one of the most photographed sights in Emporio, especially when the low sun turns its stone golden. Some stories connect it to gifts from emigrant families abroad, but the details vary depending on who tells them. Either way, it marks the time for a village that runs at a very slow pace.

If you have energy, follow the road inland and uphill. After a while you reach Chorio, the old village. For centuries this was the main settlement of Halki. People built it away from the coast, out of sight of pirates who raided Aegean islands. Today Chorio is largely abandoned. Stone walls, empty doorways and fallen roofs line the paths. Above it, on the hilltop, stand the ruins of a castle linked to the Knights of Saint John, the crusading order that ruled Rhodes in the late Middle Ages.

The climb to the castle is steep and rocky, with no shade. But the view from the top is the reward. You see the whole island at once: bare ridges, small bays, and the sea in every direction. You also see the logic of the place. From up here, watchmen could spot ships long before they reached the shore. When the threat of piracy faded, people moved down to the harbour. Chorio slowly fell silent, and Emporio grew.

Modern Halki remains very small. There are only a few hundred permanent residents, and the island has very few cars. Most people walk, take a boat, or use small local vehicles. In recent years, Halki has also been part of a Greek government programme to turn small islands toward cleaner energy and electric transport. So this old island of divers is now testing new ideas about how small communities can live with the sea and the sun. For visitors, the main effect is calm.

The best way to experience Halki is simply to slow down. Swim from the rocks or from a small beach such as Pontamos, a short walk from the harbour. Sit at a waterfront table in the evening. Watch fishermen sort their nets and cats wait patiently nearby. Listen to the bells of Saint Nicholas. Notice how the colours of the houses change as the sun drops. There is not much to do here, and that is exactly the point.

Before you move on, look once more at the harbour in front of you. See the restored houses and the empty ones side by side. Each of them belonged to a family that dived, sailed, left, or stayed. Some of their descendants now live thousands of kilometres away, in Florida. Some have come back. As you walk away, listen for the water against the quay, and the bell tower marking the hour. Halki keeps its own time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to Chorio castle early morning to avoid heat.
Bring water and sun protection for the shadeless hilltop path.
Wear sturdy shoes for rocky trails and uneven stone steps.
Walk to Pontamos beach for an easy swim near Emporio.
Check ferry schedules from Rhodes carefully before planning return.
