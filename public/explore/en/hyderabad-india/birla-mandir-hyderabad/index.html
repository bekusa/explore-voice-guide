The guide

The story

Start with your bare feet. The marble under them is cool, smooth, and almost white. On a hot Hyderabad afternoon that coolness comes as a small shock. Below you, the traffic noise of the city fades into a low hum. Ahead, carved white towers rise against the sky. The air smells faintly of flowers and camphor, the sharp, clean scent that rises from small flames during Hindu worship.

You are standing at Birla Mandir, a Hindu temple on a rocky hill near the centre of Hyderabad. The temple is dedicated to Lord Venkateswara, a form of the god Vishnu. Many people in South India call him Balaji. His most famous shrine is at Tirumala, in the hills of neighbouring Andhra Pradesh. This temple brings that devotion into the heart of the city, on a hill that anyone can reach.

The hill itself has a story. It is called Naubath Pahad, and pahad simply means hill. According to a popular account, the name goes back to the era of the Qutb Shahi kings, who ruled this region before the Nizams. Drummers are said to have stood up here and beaten large drums, called naubat, to announce royal orders to the city below. Whether every detail is true or not, it is easy to imagine the sound rolling down over the rooftops.

The temple was built by the Birla family, one of India's best-known industrial families, through their charitable foundation. The Birlas have built temples in many Indian cities, and you may hear all of them called Birla Mandir. This one was completed in 1976. So, unlike many of the temples you will visit in India, it is not ancient. It is a modern building that uses very old ideas about sacred architecture.

Now look closely at the stone. Almost the entire temple is made of white marble brought from Rajasthan, in northwest India. That is a very long journey for building stone. The marble gives the temple its glow. In the morning it looks soft and pale. At midday it is bright, almost hard on the eyes. After dark, when the lights come on, the whole hilltop seems to float above the city.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The design mixes several regional styles of Indian temple building. The tall gateway tower follows the South Indian tradition. Some of the carved towers and their curved shapes come from the temple styles of Odisha, on India's eastern coast. The fine marble carving recalls the craft of Rajasthan. In one hill in the Deccan, you see a meeting of north, south, and east.

And here is the second surprise. Listen. You will probably not hear temple bells. In most Hindu temples, worshippers ring a bell as they enter, and the sound is constant. Here, the temple was designed without the usual bells. The idea was to keep a calm, quiet atmosphere, closer to meditation. Even on a crowded evening, the mood often feels hushed compared with the city below.

As you walk through the complex, look at the walls. Lines of text are carved and painted into the marble. These are teachings and sayings drawn from saints and religious thinkers. The temple's founders wanted visitors to read as well as pray. Take a moment to pause by one of these inscriptions. It is a quiet way to understand what the builders hoped people would feel here.

The main sanctum holds the image of Lord Venkateswara. Around the complex there are also shrines to other deities. Among them is Padmavati, the consort of Venkateswara, and other gods widely honoured in Hindu homes. You will see families moving from shrine to shrine. Some stop to touch the threshold. Others fold their hands, close their eyes, and move on. Watch for a while, respectfully, and the rhythm of worship becomes clear.

A few practical customs shape every visit. You leave your shoes before you climb into the temple. Mobile phones and cameras are not allowed inside the main temple area, so you deposit them first. For some visitors this feels strange. For many, it becomes the best part. Without a screen in your hand, you simply look. You notice the carving, the faces around you, the light on the stone.

Now turn and look outward. From the terraces, the city spreads in every direction. To one side lies Hussain Sagar, the large lake that sits between Hyderabad and its twin city, Secunderabad. In the middle of the water stands a tall statue of the Buddha on a small island. On a clear day you can follow the ring road along the shore. At sunset the water turns copper, then grey, then fills with reflected lights.

The area around the hill is worth a little time too. Close by are the Birla Planetarium and the Birla Science Museum, also linked to the same family foundation. Down near the lake, Lumbini Park offers a green place to sit near the water. So this hilltop is not only a place of worship. It is part of a small cultural district where science, religion, and city life sit side by side.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Feel the marble under your feet, still holding the heat or coolness of the day. Notice the quiet where you might expect bells. Look down at the lake, and the city moving around it. Then, as you walk back down the slope toward your shoes and your phone, listen to the noise of Hyderabad slowly return.
