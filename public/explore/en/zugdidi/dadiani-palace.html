The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Leaves move overhead, and gravel shifts under your shoes. Somewhere behind the trees, the traffic of Zugdidi carries on. Ahead of you, pale walls rise among old trees, with pointed windows and small turrets. They look more like a country house in northern Europe than anything you might expect in western Georgia. That surprise is a good place to begin. This is the Dadiani Palace, once the home of the ruling family of Samegrelo.

First, a little geography. Samegrelo, also called Mingrelia, is the green, humid lowland of western Georgia. It stretches from the Black Sea coast towards the Caucasus foothills. For centuries it was a principality with its own princely house, the Dadiani. The Dadiani princes were lords of a small, proud realm. Their position sat between larger powers, from the Ottoman world to the south and west to the Russian Empire to the north. Zugdidi became their seat, and this palace became the centre of their court.

The buildings you see now date mostly from the nineteenth century. This was the period when Samegrelo came under Russian rule. The style is often described as Gothic Revival, or more loosely as eclectic. In simple terms, the architects borrowed the look of medieval European castles and churches, such as pointed arches and battlement-like details. They then used those shapes for a comfortable aristocratic home. Notice the contrast. Traditional Georgian buildings tend to be heavy and stone-built. This palace looks lighter, more decorative, and more interested in fashion than defence.

At the heart of this place stands one remarkable woman, Ekaterine Dadiani. She was born into the Chavchavadze family, one of the leading noble houses of eastern Georgia. Her father, Alexander Chavchavadze, was a celebrated poet and general. Her sister Nino married the Russian writer and diplomat Alexander Griboyedov. Ekaterine married David Dadiani, the ruling prince of Samegrelo. After his death she governed the principality as regent for their young son. She was a capable and determined ruler, and the court here was closely tied to her.

Her story reached far beyond Georgia. During the Crimean War of the 1850s, Ottoman forces entered Samegrelo. Ekaterine organised resistance and refused to give way. Later she spent time at the imperial court in Saint Petersburg, where she became known for her beauty and her strong character. Visitors often expect a provincial palace to tell a local story. Here, the threads run outward to Russia, to Constantinople, and to Paris.

Paris, in fact, leads to the first great surprise inside the museum. Ekaterine's daughter, Princess Salome Dadiani, married Prince Achille Murat. He was a descendant of Joachim Murat, the French marshal who married Napoleon's sister Caroline and became King of Naples. Through this marriage, objects linked to the Bonaparte family came to Zugdidi. The best known is a death mask of Napoleon Bonaparte. It is one of only a small number of such casts in the world, and it is perhaps the last thing you expect to find in a Georgian provincial town.

Take a moment with that mask if you see it on display. A death mask was made by pressing material onto the face shortly after death. Casts were then taken from that mould. So this is not an artist's idea of Napoleon. It is a record of his actual features at the end of his life, in exile on the island of Saint Helena. Many visitors walk past the cases quickly. This is one object worth standing in front of a little longer.

The collections here go well beyond the Napoleonic story. The museum has gathered material connected to the Dadiani family and to Samegrelo more widely. There are portraits, furniture, weapons, textiles, and objects of daily court life. There are also religious treasures, such as icons and church metalwork. Displays change over time, so you may not see every piece described in guidebooks. Still, the general picture remains clear. These rooms hold the memory of a whole principality, not just one household.

The second surprise is a religious one. For a long time the palace collection was associated with a relic that believers venerate as part of the robe of the Virgin Mary. For many Georgian Orthodox Christians this relic has deep meaning, and it draws pilgrims to Zugdidi. Arrangements for where such relics are kept can change. Ask the museum staff where it is today, and treat it with respect if you see it. For many visitors, this is not a museum object at all, but something sacred.

Now look back towards the trees. The palace grounds open into the Zugdidi Botanical Garden, which grew out of the Dadiani family's private park. The princes and their circle planted trees and shrubs from many regions. The warm, wet climate of Samegrelo helped them to grow well. Today the garden feels calm and shaded. Tall trunks rise above the paths, and the air is cooler under the canopy. On a summer day you will notice the humidity, and the green smell of damp earth.

The twentieth century was not easy for this place. After the Russian Revolution, Georgia was absorbed into the Soviet Union. Aristocratic homes like this one were taken by the state and turned to public use. Here, that meant a museum. This step protected the collections, even as the family's way of life ended. In more recent years the palace has been restored. The work has brought back much of the pale, crisp look of the facades that you see today.

Zugdidi itself sits close to the Enguri River and the line with Abkhazia, a region that has been outside Tbilisi's control since conflict in the early 1990s. Many people displaced by that war settled in and around this town. So the history here does not feel distant. The Dadiani once ruled across this whole borderland. Today the palace stands as a reminder of a time when the region was joined together under one court.

Before you leave, walk slowly along the front of the palace. Look up at the rooflines, the pointed windows, and the small towers. Then listen again to the leaves and to the town beyond the gate. A princess regent once received guests here. A French prince married into this family. A dead emperor's face came to rest in these rooms. And all around it, the trees the Dadiani planted continue to grow.

Before you go

Practical tips

Leave time to walk the adjoining botanical garden.
Ask staff where the Virgin's robe relic is kept.
Dress modestly if you plan to view religious relics.
Visit on a dry morning for softer light on facades.
Bring insect repellent for the humid garden paths in summer.
