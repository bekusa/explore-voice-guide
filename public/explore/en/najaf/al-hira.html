The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground. It is pale and dusty, and it rises in low, soft swells. The wind off the desert carries fine grit and the faint smell of dry earth. Nothing here looks like a capital city. Yet under these gentle mounds lie the remains of one of the most important Arab cities before Islam. This is al-Hira, a short distance from Kufa, where the farmland of the Euphrates meets the open steppe.

For several centuries, roughly from the third century to the early seventh century, al-Hira was the seat of the Lakhmids. The Lakhmids were an Arab dynasty. They ruled as allies and clients of the Sasanian Persian Empire, the great power to the east. Their job, in a sense, was to guard Persia's desert frontier. They watched the tribes of Arabia and faced the Byzantine Empire and its own Arab allies to the west. Al-Hira was their court, their market, and their window onto the wider world.

Think about the position of this place. To the east lay the rich, irrigated lands of Mesopotamia, the old heartland between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. To the west and south stretched the Arabian desert. Caravans, envoys, and merchants passed between these two worlds, and many of them passed through here. Al-Hira became a meeting point. Arabic, Persian, and Aramaic were all heard in its streets. Goods, ideas, and religions moved through the town with the traders.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Al-Hira was a strongly Christian city. Its people included a community known as the Ibad, Arab Christians who lived in the town. The city was home to bishops of the Church of the East, the branch of Christianity that spread across Persia and later reached as far as China. Monasteries and churches stood around the town. When you picture pre-Islamic Arabia, you may imagine only tents and idols. This city tells a far more complicated story.

Archaeology supports that picture. In the 1930s, an expedition from Oxford dug at the site and uncovered the remains of churches built of mud brick and decorated with carved stucco. Later work, including recent projects by Iraqi and German archaeologists, has continued to map the buried town. Much of what they study is not visible to the eye. It sits under the soil, and today parts of the ancient area also lie beneath modern houses, roads, and fields.

The court of al-Hira was famous across Arabia for its poets. The great poets of the pre-Islamic era, whose verses Arabs still memorise today, travelled here to perform for the kings. They praised their patrons, and sometimes they mocked them. Poetry was power in the Arab world. A good poem could raise a ruler's reputation across the desert. A bitter one could damage it for generations. So the palace halls of al-Hira were also a kind of stage.

Those palaces gave rise to one of the best-known tales of the place. Two royal palaces are remembered in Arabic tradition, called al-Khawarnaq and al-Sadir. Legend has it that a skilled builder named Sinnimar designed al-Khawarnaq for a Lakhmid king. According to the story, when the work was finished, the king threw the builder from the top, so that he could never build anything so fine for anyone else. Arabs still use the phrase "the reward of Sinnimar" for good work repaid with cruelty. It is folklore, not documented history — but it shows how deeply this city lived in memory.

There is a second surprise, and it concerns the letters of the Arabic language itself. Some scholars believe that al-Hira played a part in the early spread of Arabic writing. The town was a centre of literacy, with Christian scribes and a lively trade that needed records. The exact role of al-Hira is still debated. But it is possible that the script now used across much of the world owes something to the scribes who once worked on this plain.

The last Lakhmid king was al-Nu'man the Third, who adopted Christianity. Around the start of the seventh century, he fell out with the Persian shah, Khosrow the Second, and he lost his throne and his life. The Persians then ruled al-Hira more directly. The old balance on the frontier broke. Soon afterwards, Arab tribes defeated a Persian force at a battle remembered as Dhi Qar. Many historians see it as a sign of the shift that was coming.

That shift arrived in the 630s. Muslim armies moved north out of Arabia, and al-Hira came to terms with them early in the conquest of Iraq. A few years later, the new rulers founded a garrison city nearby. That city was Kufa. Kufa grew quickly. It became a political and religious centre of early Islam, and it drew people, trade, and building materials away from its older neighbour. Over time, al-Hira faded, and its Christian communities slowly declined.

So the story of this place is a story of handover. One city rose as the other sank. Kufa, and later nearby Najaf with its great shrine, became the places that pilgrims and travellers came to see. Al-Hira became a name in poems and history books. Its bricks crumbled back into the earth. Today it is easy to drive past without knowing that a royal court, churches, and a famous school of poetry once stood here.

As you walk, pay attention to the land itself. Notice how flat it is, and how far you can see. Notice the colour of the soil, and any scatter of broken pottery or brick fragments on the surface. These small pieces are the most direct trace of the old town. If you see them, leave them where they are. They are part of an archaeological site that researchers are still working to understand.

Listen, too. You may hear traffic from the roads between Kufa and Najaf, the call to prayer from a distant mosque, or birds over the fields. In the time of the Lakhmids, the sounds would have been different — church bells, perhaps, camel caravans, the voices of traders bargaining in several languages. The place is quiet now, but it was never empty. People have lived on this edge between river and desert for a very long time.

Before you leave, look once more at the low mounds and the wide sky. Somewhere beneath your feet lie the foundations of churches, houses, and perhaps palaces. Kings listened to poets here. Scribes may have shaped letters that millions now read. As you walk back toward Kufa, notice how close the two cities sit. One is still alive and growing. The other rests under the dust, waiting for the next careful spade.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid strong heat.
Bring water, a hat, and sun protection for open ground.
Ask a local guide to point out the excavated areas.
Combine your visit with Kufa's Great Mosque and Najaf.
Leave pottery and brick fragments exactly where you find them.
