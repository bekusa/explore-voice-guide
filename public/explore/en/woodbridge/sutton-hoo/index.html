The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through dry grass, and somewhere below the ridge a curlew calls over the river. Underfoot the path is soft and sandy, pale where boots have worn it. Ahead of you, low humps rise out of the heath like sleeping animals under a blanket of turf. They do not look like much at first. That is part of their power. These quiet mounds held one of the most extraordinary discoveries ever made in British soil.

You are standing on high ground above the River Deben, across the water from the market town of Woodbridge in Suffolk. Around fourteen hundred years ago, this ridge was a place for the dead. The people who buried them were Anglo-Saxons, settlers whose ancestors had crossed the North Sea from what is now northern Germany and Denmark. The river below was their road. A burial up here could be seen by anyone sailing past. It was a statement, made in earth, about power.

For centuries the mounds kept their secret. Some were dug into by treasure hunters in earlier ages, and robbers' trenches cut into several of them. Then, in the late 1930s, the land belonged to a woman named Edith Pretty. She lived in the large house nearby, now called Tranmer House, and she had long been curious about the mounds on her estate. In 1938 she hired a local man to dig. His name was Basil Brown.

Brown was a self-taught archaeologist from Suffolk. He had left school young, but he knew the soils of this county better than almost anyone. He worked on several smaller mounds first. Then, in the spring of 1939, he turned to the largest one, now known as Mound 1. What he found there was not a pile of gold. It was something stranger... a pattern of iron rivets, rusted, sitting in neat lines in the sand.

Here is the first surprise. The ship itself was gone. The timber had rotted away completely in the acidic, sandy soil. But the wood had left its shape behind — a ghost of a ship, pressed into the earth, with every rivet still in its place. Excavators traced a vessel of roughly twenty-seven metres, built from overlapping planks. It had been dragged up from the river and lowered into a trench, then covered over. A burial chamber stood in the middle.

Inside that chamber lay a treasure that changed how historians see this period. There was a great gold buckle, heavy and hollow, covered in interlaced patterns. There were shoulder clasps of gold set with red garnets, each stone cut to fit its tiny cell. There was a purse lid decorated with birds and beasts. There were silver bowls and dishes made far away in the eastern Mediterranean. People had called these centuries the Dark Ages. The objects from this mound said otherwise.

And then there was the helmet. It was found crushed into hundreds of rusted fragments. Conservators spent years piecing it together. The finished face mask has eyebrows of inlaid wire and garnet, a nose, and a moustache. Look closely at pictures of it and you may see a bird with outstretched wings. The nose and eyebrows form its body and wings. It is a face and a flying creature at the same time.

Here is the second surprise. No body was found. The acidic sand had dissolved the bones completely. For a long time some people wondered whether the grave was only a memorial. Later scientific work found chemical traces consistent with a body that had once lain there. So who was it? Many scholars believe it was Rædwald, a king of the East Angles who died in the early seventh century. That is the most popular theory, but it remains a theory, not a proven fact.

The timing of the dig was remarkable. The excavation of the ship took place in the summer of 1939, only weeks before Britain entered the Second World War. Professional archaeologists arrived to take over the burial chamber, and the finds were packed away. An inquest ruled that the treasure belonged to Edith Pretty. She chose to give it to the nation. Today the great finds are kept and displayed in the British Museum in London.

The story reached a new audience in 2021, when a film called The Dig retold the excavation. It brought many visitors here for the first time. The film is a drama, and it changes some details. But it captures something true. Basil Brown's quiet skill, and the long years before he received full credit, are now part of how this place is remembered. His name is spoken here with real respect.

Not every burial on this ridge belonged to royalty. Later excavations, many decades after the first dig, found graves of a very different kind. Some people buried here appear to have been executed, perhaps in the centuries after the great mounds were raised. Their remains survived only as dark shapes in the sand. It is a sober reminder. The same ground that honoured kings may later have served as a place of punishment.

Today the site is cared for by the National Trust. You can walk among the mounds, visit the exhibition with replicas of the finds, and explore Tranmer House, where Edith Pretty lived. A viewing tower now rises near the burial ground. From the top you can see the mounds as a group, the way the land falls to the river, and why this ridge was chosen. The pattern is much easier to read from above.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the largest mound. Feel the sandy ground, the same soil that kept the ship's outline and erased its timber. Look down toward the Deben, silver or grey depending on the sky. Imagine a long ship hauled up this slope by many hands. Then turn and walk back slowly. The heath will look the same as it did when you arrived — but you will know what lies beneath it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the viewing tower to see the mounds together.
Visit the British Museum later to see the originals.
Wear sturdy shoes for sandy, uneven heath paths.
Explore Tranmer House to understand Edith Pretty's story.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed ridge.
