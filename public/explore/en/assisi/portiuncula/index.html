The guide

The story

Step inside the great basilica, and the air cools at once. Your footsteps echo across a wide marble floor. Then, right in the middle of all that space, you see something unexpected. A small, rough stone chapel stands under the dome, like a single seed held in an open hand. This is the Portiuncula, and for many people it is the quiet heart of the Franciscan story.

The name means something like little portion, a small piece of land. That modest name fits. Long before the basilica existed, this chapel stood alone in the woods and fields on the plain below Assisi. The hill town rises above you to the east, pale against the slopes of Mount Subasio. Down here, the ground was once marshy and wooded, a place for travellers and the poor rather than for nobles and bishops.

In the early thirteenth century, the chapel was old, neglected, and close to ruin. A young man from Assisi named Francis began to repair abandoned churches with his own hands, carrying stones and mortar himself. This was one of them. The chapel belonged to the Benedictine monks of Mount Subasio, and according to the Franciscan tradition, they let Francis and his first companions use it. The brothers built simple huts around it and made this spot their home.

Take a moment to look at the walls. The stone is uneven and darkened, laid in irregular courses. It is a plain, almost rustic building — exactly the kind of structure Francis wanted. He preached a life of poverty and simplicity, and this little chapel became the model for that ideal. Franciscans still call it the cradle of their order, the place where their way of life took shape.

Here is a moment many visitors do not know about. One night, on Palm Sunday, a young noblewoman named Clare slipped out of her family home in Assisi and came down to this chapel. Francis and the brothers met her by torchlight. Here she cut her hair and put on a rough habit, giving up her wealth. That night began the community that later became the Poor Clares, the women's branch of the Franciscan family.

Now look up at the front of the chapel. Above the doorway, a large painting covers the upper facade. It was added in the nineteenth century by the German painter Friedrich Overbeck. It shows a vision connected to the Pardon of Assisi, with Christ and the Virgin surrounded by angels. The bright, smooth colours sit on top of the old rough stone, so you can see two very different centuries on one wall.

That Pardon is the second surprise. According to Franciscan tradition, Francis asked the pope for a special indulgence for anyone who came to this chapel with a sincere heart — a full forgiveness of the punishment for sin. At the time, this kind of grace was usually linked to long, expensive pilgrimages or crusades. Francis wanted it open to the poor, here, in this small room. Each year, in early August, pilgrims still crowd the basilica for the feast of the Pardon.

When you go inside the chapel, the light drops and the space closes around you. Behind the altar, a painted altarpiece fills the back wall. It dates from the late fourteenth century and tells the story of the Annunciation and the Pardon in gold and deep colours. Many visitors kneel or sit in silence here. Please move slowly, keep your voice low, and allow others their quiet moment.

So why is such a small chapel standing inside such a huge church? After Francis died, pilgrims came in growing numbers. In the late sixteenth century, work began on a grand basilica to shelter the Portiuncula and the crowds. The architect Galeazzo Alessi led the design. Rather than tear down the old chapel, the builders raised the new church around it. The little building was protected, not replaced.

The basilica itself has had a difficult life. In the nineteenth century, a strong earthquake brought down part of the nave, and the church had to be rebuilt. The facade you saw outside is later still, from the early twentieth century, topped by a gilded statue of the Virgin. If you stand back in the square, that golden figure catches the sun and you can see it from far across the plain.

Near the Portiuncula, toward the right side of the basilica, look for another small space called the Chapel of the Transito. This was once the infirmary cell where Francis died in 1226. He asked to be brought down here from Assisi at the end of his life. According to the accounts, he wanted to die close to the chapel he loved most. The room is plain and very small, which makes the moment feel close and human.

Before you leave, follow the signs to the rose garden. Legend has it that one night Francis fought a strong temptation by throwing himself into a thorny rose bush. Locals say the roses then lost their thorns. The bushes that grow here today are indeed a thornless variety, and the friars show them with quiet pride. Nearby, a statue of Francis often has living doves resting in a small niche, which children especially love to spot.

The area around the basilica is now a small town of its own, named after the church: Santa Maria degli Angeli, Saint Mary of the Angels. The railway station for Assisi is close by, so many visitors arrive here first before taking the road up the hill. Outside, you will find wide pavements, cafes, and shops selling rosaries and Franciscan crosses shaped like the letter T, called the Tau.

As you walk back out under the dome, turn once more toward the little chapel. Notice how the huge columns and arches seem to bend their attention toward it. Then step into the square and look up at Assisi on its hill. Francis walked between that town and this plain many times. Today, you are walking the same ground, on the same short road, under the same wide Umbrian sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Keep your voice low inside the chapel; people pray here.
Visit the rose garden after the chapel, not before.
Arrive by train; the station is a short walk away.
Expect large crowds around the early August Pardon feast.
Dress modestly with covered shoulders for this active place of worship.
