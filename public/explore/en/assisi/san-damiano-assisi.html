The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the walls of Assisi the traffic noise fades, and the path fills with other sounds. Gravel shifts under your shoes. Leaves move in the olive trees. Somewhere a bell rings, thin and unhurried. The air smells of dry grass and warm stone. In front of you stands a small church, low and plain, built from the pale pink and cream limestone of Monte Subasio. It looks almost too modest to matter. Yet this quiet building marks the place where the Franciscan story began.

This is San Damiano. In the early thirteenth century it was already old and close to ruin. It was a wayside chapel outside the town, served by a poor priest, with cracked walls and a leaking roof. Few people came here. That is part of its meaning. The young man who changed its fate was not looking for grandeur. He was looking for a quiet place to pray, far from the noise of the market streets above.

That young man was Francis, the son of Pietro di Bernardone, a wealthy cloth merchant of Assisi. As a youth he loved fine clothes, songs and parties. He dreamed of glory as a knight. Then came war with nearby Perugia, a year as a prisoner, and a long illness. When he came home, the old life no longer satisfied him. He began to walk alone in the countryside. He spent hours in caves and empty chapels like this one.

According to the earliest accounts of his life, Francis knelt here around 1205 before a painted crucifix. As he prayed, he heard a voice that seemed to come from the cross. It said, in substance: Francis, go and repair my house, which is falling into ruin. He took the words literally. He looked at the broken walls around him and decided to rebuild them, stone by stone, with his own hands.

The cross he saw was not a carved figure. It was a painted wooden cross in a style that came from the eastern Mediterranean, with large eyes and a calm, open face. Christ is shown alive, not suffering, surrounded by smaller painted figures. Here is something many visitors miss. The cross hanging in the church today is a copy. The original was taken up the hill in the thirteenth century, and you can see it now in the Basilica of Saint Clare in Assisi.

Rebuilding was not easy for a merchant's son. Francis needed money, so he sold cloth from his father's shop. Tradition says the priest here refused the coins, fearing the angry father, and Francis left them on a windowsill. His father was furious. The quarrel ended in a public scene before the bishop of Assisi. Francis gave back everything, even the clothes he wore. After that, he begged in the streets for stones and carried them here himself.

Now look at the church walls in front of you. They are rough and uneven, with patched sections and stones of different sizes. Inside, the single nave is narrow and dim, with a simple barrel vault overhead. The light comes in through small windows and falls softly on the plaster. There is no gold, no marble display. The building has been repaired many times over the centuries, but it keeps the scale and the poverty that Francis valued.

The second great figure of this place is Clare. She was a young noblewoman of Assisi who heard Francis preach and decided to follow him. In 1212 she left her family home at night and took a life of poverty. Soon Francis brought her here. With a growing group of women, she lived at San Damiano for more than forty years. This small convent became the first home of the order later called the Poor Clares.

The sisters lived inside an enclosure, behind walls and grilles. They prayed, worked, sewed and lived on what people gave them. Clare insisted on real poverty, not just a symbolic one. She fought for years for the right of her community to own nothing at all. Shortly before her death in 1253, the Pope approved her rule. It was the first rule for a religious community written by a woman.

A popular story belongs to these walls. Legend has it that in 1240 soldiers in the service of the emperor Frederick the Second attacked the convent. Clare was ill, but she had herself carried to the door. She held up a container with the consecrated bread of the Mass and prayed. The story says the soldiers fled. Many images of Clare show her holding this container. Treat it as a cherished tale, not as a proven event.

As you walk through the convent, you move through the rooms where the sisters lived. There is a small choir with old wooden stalls, darkened by centuries of use. There is a refectory, the dining room, with long rough tables. Tradition marks the place where Clare sat, often with flowers. Upstairs is the dormitory, where a cross marks the spot where Clare died. Below is a small cloister, a quiet courtyard open to the sky.

Here is a second surprise. Francis wrote one of the first great poems in the Italian language near this place. Around 1225, ill and nearly blind, he stayed close to the sisters. In pain and darkness, he composed the Canticle of the Creatures. It praises God through Brother Sun, Sister Moon, wind, water and fire. Later he added a verse about Sister Death. It is a song of joy written by a man in great suffering.

Today Franciscan friars care for San Damiano, and it is still a place of prayer. People come in silence, sit on the benches, and leave without speaking. Outside, the view opens across the Umbrian plain toward the town of Santa Maria degli Angeli and the distant hills. In the morning a light haze often lies over the fields. In the evening the stone warms to a soft gold, and the swallows circle the roof.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the entrance. Look at the worn steps, the low doorway, the simple bell. Listen again to the leaves in the olive trees. Eight centuries ago a young man knelt here and heard a call to repair what was broken. A young woman spent her whole adult life inside these walls. As you walk back up the hill, notice how the noise of the town slowly returns.
