The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through dry thyme and wild sage on the slope. Somewhere below, a car passes on the coast road. Under your feet are uneven grey stones, worn smooth at the edges. These stones are not a path laid for visitors. They are a street, and people walked on them more than three thousand years ago.

Welcome to Gournia, a Minoan town on a low ridge above the Gulf of Mirabello in eastern Crete. The Minoans were the Bronze Age civilisation of Crete, named by modern archaeologists after the legendary King Minos. Most visitors know them through the great palace at Knossos. Gournia is different. It is not a royal showpiece. It is an ordinary town, and that is exactly why it matters.

Take a moment to look around you. Low stone walls spread across the hillside like the outline of a maze. Narrow lanes climb and curve between them. Some lanes become short flights of steps where the ground rises. What you see are the foundations and lower walls of houses. The upper floors, probably built of mud brick and timber, are long gone. Only the stone bases survive, so the whole town now stands about knee high.

Here is the first surprise. The person who brought this town back into view was a young American woman named Harriet Boyd. In the early 1900s she led the excavations here. She is often described as the first woman to direct a major archaeological dig in the Aegean world. At that time this was very unusual. She travelled around Crete on muleback, searching for sites, and she found this one with help from local villagers.

Boyd's team worked over several seasons in the early twentieth century. Large numbers of local Cretan workers dug, carried and sorted the finds. The results were published in a large volume in 1908, and that book is still cited by scholars today. Because of the clear plan of streets and houses, people later called Gournia a kind of Minoan Pompeii. The comparison is not exact, but it helps you picture a whole town, not just one grand building.

The name itself is modern. Gournia comes from a Greek word for troughs or stone basins. Locals say the hill took the name from carved stone vessels that lay on the surface long before any excavation. Nobody knows what the Minoans called their town. Their writing, a script called Linear A, has still not been fully deciphered. So the town you are standing in has no known ancient name.

Now walk towards the top of the ridge. Here you find the largest building on the site. Archaeologists often call it the palace, though it is much smaller than Knossos or Phaistos. A better word may be a governor's house, or a local centre of power. It has finer masonry, larger rooms, and a long open space beside it. That open space was a public court, probably used for gatherings, trade, and perhaps ceremonies.

Look at the edges of that court. In some places, broad stone steps face the open ground. One idea is that people stood or sat here to watch events. Another idea is that the steps simply made the slope easier to use. Both may be true. What is clear is that the court was the heart of the town. Streets lead towards it from several directions.

The houses tell a quieter story. Many of the rooms near the ground were storerooms or workrooms. Excavators found tools of bronze and stone, loom weights for weaving, and many pots. Some spaces seem to have been workshops for crafts such as carpentry, metalwork and pottery. This was a town of farmers, fishermen and craftspeople. They kept oil, grain and wine close at hand, in large clay jars.

Here is the second surprise. Gournia has a small town shrine, set on a lane away from the main court. Inside it, excavators found clay figures of a goddess with her arms raised, along with clay tubes decorated with snakes. These objects date from a late phase of the site. They show that religion here was not only in palaces. Ordinary neighbourhoods had their own sacred places. The original figures are now in museums, not on site.

The town did not last forever. Like many Minoan sites, Gournia suffered destruction in the fifteenth century before Christ. Burned layers show that fire played a part. Historians still debate the causes across Crete, including earthquakes, conflict, and the arrival of mainland Greek rulers. After the destruction, people returned to parts of the hill for a time, but the busy town never fully recovered. Slowly the walls disappeared under soil and scrub.

Now turn and look out towards the sea. The town sits at a narrow part of Crete, where the island is only a short distance from north coast to south coast. This position probably helped trade. Goods could move across the island here without sailing all the way around. Research in the twenty-first century has also studied the coast below, looking for traces of Gournia's harbour and its links to the sea.

As you walk, notice the scale of everything. The lanes are narrow enough to touch both walls. The doorways are small. The rooms are modest. This is a human scale, built for families, neighbours and donkeys carrying loads. Notice the light, too. In the morning it falls soft across the stones. By midday the ridge is bright, hot and almost shadowless, so the outline of the town is sharpest early or late.

Before you leave, stand still on one of the old streets for a moment. Look down at the stones. Then look up at the bay, the hills and the same open sky. A potter, a weaver or a child once stood near this spot and saw a similar view. The houses are gone, but the plan of their lives is still here, set out in stone under your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning to avoid the strong midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone streets and steps.
Bring water and a hat; shade is very limited.
See the shrine finds later at the Heraklion museum.
Walk to the top first for the full town plan.
