The guide

The story

Listen first. Pine needles shift under your feet, and somewhere beyond the trees a bell strikes once, then again. The air smells of resin, damp earth and candle wax. Below the monastery the Zhizdra River runs slow and dark through the meadows. This is Optina Monastery, known in Russian as Optina Pustyn. The word pustyn means a hermitage, a place withdrawn from the world. It sits a few kilometres outside the small town of Kozelsk, in the old Kozelsk district of the Kaluga region.

Nobody knows exactly when the first monks settled here. One popular tale says that a repentant robber named Opta founded the hermitage, and that the monastery still carries his name. It is a good story, but it is a legend, not a documented fact. Historians agree on something simpler. Small forest monasteries like this one existed across central Russia for centuries before most of them left any written record. Optina grew slowly and quietly, and for a long time it was poor and almost forgotten.

Before you walk further, think about the town you passed on the way here. In 1238 the Mongol army of Batu Khan besieged Kozelsk. The chronicles record that this small wooden town held out for about seven weeks — far longer than many larger cities. When it finally fell, the attackers reportedly called it the evil city. That stubborn local pride still lives in Kozelsk today. The monastery and the town share the same landscape of river, forest and open field.

Optina became famous in the nineteenth century for one reason — its elders. In Russian they are called startsy. An elder was not an abbot or a bishop. He was a monk known for deep prayer and spiritual insight. People came to him for advice on everything — marriage, grief, money, faith. At Optina a line of these elders followed one another for about a hundred years. Leonid, Makary and Ambrose are the names you will hear most often.

Elder Ambrose is the best known of them. He was often ill and received visitors while lying in bed. Peasants, merchants and nobles stood together in the same queue outside his small house. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Some writers believe Ambrose was a model for Father Zosima, the gentle elder in Dostoevsky's novel The Brothers Karamazov. That link is not just a theory. Dostoevsky really came here.

In 1878 Dostoevsky travelled to Optina with the young philosopher Vladimir Solovyov. The writer was in deep mourning. His small son Alyosha had died that spring. Dostoevsky spoke with Elder Ambrose during the visit. Soon after, he wrote The Brothers Karamazov, and he gave its kindest hero the name Alyosha. When you read the monastery scenes in that novel, you are reading echoes of these paths, these trees and these quiet wooden cells.

Dostoevsky was not alone. Nikolai Gogol visited Optina in the nineteenth century, troubled and searching for peace. The philosopher Konstantin Leontiev spent his last years nearby. And Leo Tolstoy came several times, despite his long conflict with the Orthodox Church. The second surprise is the saddest. In 1910 the old writer left his home at Yasnaya Polyana in secret. One of the first places he went was Optina. He walked near the elders' skete but did not go in. Days later he died at a small railway station.

That skete is worth finding. A skete is a smaller, stricter community attached to a monastery. Optina's skete is dedicated to Saint John the Baptist, and it lies a short walk through the forest from the main gates. The elders lived there in modest wooden houses. Traditionally women were not allowed inside the skete itself. For that reason the elders received women in small rooms built into the skete wall, so that visitors could meet them without crossing the threshold.

Then came the twentieth century. After the revolution the Soviet authorities closed the monastery in the 1920s. Monks were dispersed, arrested or exiled. The buildings were used for other purposes over the following decades. Churches lost their domes and bells. The famous elders' houses stood empty. For a long time Optina existed mostly in books and in the memory of believers. Many Russians knew its name from Dostoevsky long before they could visit.

In 1987 the monastery was returned to the Russian Orthodox Church. It was one of the first major monasteries to come back in the late Soviet period. Restoration took years of hard work. Much of what you see today — the white walls, the bright domes, the clean paths — is the result of that rebuilding. If you look closely, you can still see the difference between older stonework and newer repairs on some of the walls.

The monastery's recent history also holds a tragedy. On Easter morning in 1993, three monks were killed here by an attacker. Their names were Vasily, Ferapont and Trofim. Many pilgrims come today especially to pray at their graves. Easter is the most joyful feast in the Orthodox year. That is why this event shocked believers across the country so deeply. Now the three monks are remembered as martyrs of the new era.

As you walk the grounds, notice how the place works as a living monastery, not a museum. Monks in black robes cross the courtyards quickly. Pilgrims queue quietly at the elders' graves. You may hear chanting from the main church, which is dedicated to the Entry of the Mother of God into the Temple. Many visitors also walk to the holy spring and bathe in the cold water. Women usually cover their hair, and quiet voices are expected everywhere.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the edge of the pines. Look back at the white walls and the domes above the trees. Listen to the bell again, and to the wind in the branches. For two centuries, people have arrived here tired, grieving or uncertain. Some were famous, most were not. As you walk back toward the river, notice how the forest closes behind you — and how quiet it stays.

Before you go

Practical tips

Women should bring a headscarf and a long skirt.
Men should wear long trousers and remove hats indoors.
Walk the forest path to the skete of John the Baptist.
Ask before photographing monks or people at prayer.
Visit the holy spring with a towel if bathing.
