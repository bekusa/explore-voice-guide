The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Beneath your feet, the river moves slowly, wide and grey-green, carrying the light of the sky. A tram bell rings somewhere behind you. Cyclists pass, and the railing under your hand is cool and smooth. This is Aioi Bridge, in the heart of Hiroshima. At first it looks like an ordinary city crossing. But look at its shape, because its shape is the reason this bridge entered history.

Aioi Bridge is not a straight line. It is shaped like the letter T. The main span crosses the Ota River system from east to west. Then a third arm branches south, onto the narrow tip of land between two rivers. Here the water divides into the Motoyasu River and the Honkawa River. That small island of land is now the northern end of the Peace Memorial Park. Very few bridges in the world have this form.

The name itself carries a gentle meaning. In Japanese, aioi suggests things that grow together from one root. It is often linked with paired pine trees, a traditional image of a couple growing old side by side. Locals say the name suits a bridge where rivers part and paths meet. It is worth keeping that meaning in mind... because what happened here stands in sharp contrast to it.

The T-shaped bridge was built in the early 1930s. It served a busy commercial district. Shops, homes, and offices crowded the riverbanks. Trams and people crossed here every day. Just beside the eastern end stood the Hiroshima Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall, a modern building with a green copper dome. It displayed local products and hosted exhibitions. For more than a decade, this was simply a lively crossroads of ordinary city life.

Then came the morning of August 6, 1945. American planners needed a target that a bombardier could identify clearly from high in the air. The T shape of Aioi Bridge was unmistakable from above. It became the aiming point for the atomic bomb carried by the bomber known as the Enola Gay. This is the detail that most visitors do not realise — the bridge you are standing on was the target itself.

At 8:15 that morning, the bomb exploded in the air. It did not detonate directly over the bridge. Wind and the speed of the aircraft carried it a short distance away. It exploded roughly three hundred metres to the southeast, above a hospital. From the bridge, you can walk to that spot in a few minutes. A small memorial marks the hypocentre today, on a quiet side street.

What happened next is hard to picture in this calm place. The blast and heat destroyed most of central Hiroshima in an instant. Fires followed. Tens of thousands of people died that day, and many more in the months and years after. The rivers around this bridge filled with people seeking water and relief from the burns. Survivors later described these riverbanks in testimony that you can read in the Peace Memorial Museum.

And here is the second surprise. The bridge survived. Because the blast came from above, the force pushed downward on the concrete deck. Parts of the structure were displaced and damaged, but the bridge did not collapse. People continued to cross it in the difficult years after the war. It carried the city through its recovery for nearly four decades, scarred but standing.

By the early 1980s, the old structure was worn out. It was replaced with the bridge you see now, completed in 1983. The new bridge keeps the same T shape, so the meaning of the place remains readable. Sections of the original bridge were preserved as physical witnesses. Look for these surviving fragments, which help connect the modern deck to the one that was there in 1945.

Now turn toward the east bank. The ruined building with its bare steel dome frame is the A-Bomb Dome. This is the former Industrial Promotion Hall. It stood very close to the hypocentre, and because the blast came almost straight down, its central walls partly survived. The city chose to keep it as it was. Today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. From this bridge, you see it framed against the water and the sky.

Walk to the stem of the T, and you step onto the northern edge of Peace Memorial Park. Before 1945, this land was a crowded neighbourhood with temples, shops, and homes. Now it is open space, trees, and quiet paths. Further south, you will find the Children's Peace Monument, the Cenotaph, and the museum. Many people begin their visit to the park here, crossing this arm of the bridge.

Notice how ordinary the bridge feels today. Trams cross it. Office workers walk quickly to meetings. Students lean on the railings and talk. In spring and autumn, boats carry visitors along the river below. This everyday life is not careless. For many people in Hiroshima, it is the point. A city that was destroyed chose to rebuild, to live, and to remember at the same time.

Before you move on, rest your hand on the railing again. Look down at the place where the two rivers separate around the island. Look east to the dome, and south toward the trees of the park. The sound of the tram, the water, the voices around you — all of this exists on the same spot where history once aimed. Take one slow breath here, and then walk on carefully.
