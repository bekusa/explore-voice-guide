The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the long, raised building, the air moves freely between concrete pillars. Footsteps echo softly on the paving. Cicadas buzz in the trees of the park in summer. Somewhere nearby, a school group folds paper cranes, and the small crackle of paper carries further than you might expect. This is the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum. It is a calm, open place built on ground that was once the centre of a ruined city.

At 8:15 in the morning on August 6, 1945, an American B-29 bomber called the Enola Gay dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima. The crew called the bomb Little Boy. It exploded in the air, roughly six hundred metres above the city. In a single instant, heat, blast and fire destroyed most of central Hiroshima. Tens of thousands of people died that day. Many more died in the months and years that followed, from burns, injuries and radiation.

The land under your feet was not empty before that morning. This area was a busy district of shops, homes, temples and small streets, close to where the rivers divide. After the war, the city decided not to rebuild it as it was. Instead, it became Peace Memorial Park, a space for memory. The museum opened in 1955, only ten years after the bombing, when the city was still recovering.

The main building was designed by the Japanese architect Kenzo Tange. He later became one of the best-known architects of his generation. Look at how the building stands on pillars, lifted above the ground. This lets people walk underneath and look straight through it. Tange designed it as part of a single line across the park. From under the museum, the view runs past the Cenotaph and the Peace Flame, all the way to the ruined dome on the far side of the river.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. That line of sight was planned on purpose. If you stand in the right spot and look north, the arch of the Cenotaph frames the Atomic Bomb Dome. The dome was once a modern exhibition hall, designed by a Czech architect, Jan Letzel, in the early twentieth century. It stood very close to the hypocentre, the point on the ground directly below the explosion. Its skeleton survived, and today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Inside, the museum is quiet. People speak in low voices or not at all. The exhibitions were renewed in recent years, and they now focus more closely on individual people. Instead of only large numbers, you see names, faces and personal objects. You read about a student on the way to work, a mother at home, a child playing outside. The idea is simple. A city of hundreds of thousands was made of single lives, one by one.

Some of the objects are very small. A wristwatch stopped at the moment of the blast, with its hands near 8:15. A school uniform, torn and burned. A lunch box with its contents turned to charcoal. A child's tricycle, twisted by the heat. These things were donated by families, often many years later. Each one comes with a short story about the person who owned it. Take your time with them. They carry more than any chart can.

The second surprise is one of the hardest things to see. It is a section of stone steps from the entrance of a bank that stood near the hypocentre. Someone was sitting on those steps when the bomb exploded. The intense heat bleached the stone around them, but where the person sat, the surface stayed darker. What remains looks like a shadow. Nobody can know exactly who it was. The stone has been moved into the museum to protect it.

The museum also explains what happened after that first day. You learn about black rain, the dark, sticky rain that fell later that morning and carried radioactive material. You learn about the survivors, known in Japanese as hibakusha, which means people affected by the bomb. Many of them lived with illness, scars and fear of discrimination for the rest of their lives. Many also spent decades telling their stories so that the world would remember.

One story stays with many visitors. Sadako Sasaki was a girl who was two years old when the bomb fell. Years later, she became ill with leukaemia. In Japan, there is a tradition that folding one thousand paper cranes can bring a wish to life. Sadako folded cranes while she was in hospital. She died young. Her story inspired the Children's Peace Monument in the park, where people from around the world still leave strings of colourful cranes.

Another part of the museum is less about the past and more about action. Since the late 1960s, the mayors of Hiroshima have sent letters of protest to governments every time a nuclear test takes place. Copies of these letters are shown here, one after another. Seeing how many there are is a quiet shock. The city has spent decades asking the world, again and again, not to repeat what happened here.

The tone of the museum is not angry. It does not ask you to hate anyone. Instead, it asks you to look clearly, and to remember. Many visitors leave feeling heavy, and that is normal. You can rest on benches inside, or step outside into the open air of the park. The trees, the river and the wide paths were planned to give people space to breathe after what they have seen.

When you walk out, stop under the building again, between the pillars. Look north along the line toward the Cenotaph and the dome. Notice the ordinary life around you — cyclists crossing the bridges, families eating rice balls on the grass, the sound of the river. This city was rebuilt by people who survived. As you walk on, the green park, the busy streets and the quiet dome are all part of the same story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow at least two unhurried hours for the main exhibitions.
Arrive early to avoid the largest school groups.
Bring tissues; many personal stories are deeply moving.
Pause in the park afterwards before continuing your day.
Visit the Children's Peace Monument after leaving the museum.
