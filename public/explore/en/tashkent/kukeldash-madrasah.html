The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, the traffic of Chorsu Square hums and honks. Somewhere close, a vendor calls out over a pile of melons. In front of you, a wall of pale, sun-baked brick rises above the street. It is warm to the touch on most afternoons, and it carries the dust of a city that has been trading on this spot for a very long time. This is the Kukeldash Madrasah, one of the oldest surviving buildings in Tashkent.

A madrasah is a religious school, a place where young men once studied the Quran, Islamic law, Arabic grammar, logic and poetry. Across Central Asia, these schools followed a familiar plan. There is a grand entrance gate facing the street, and a courtyard hidden behind it. Small rooms for students line that courtyard on every side. Kukeldash follows this pattern closely. But its location gives it a very particular character. It sits on raised ground at the edge of one of the city's great markets.

The building dates from the second half of the sixteenth century. This was the era of the Shaybanids, a dynasty of Uzbek rulers who controlled much of this region after the Timurid empire faded. The madrasah takes its name from the man traditionally credited with building it, a high official of the court. Here is the first surprise. Kukeldash is not really a family name at all. It is a title. It means something like foster brother of the ruler, a person raised alongside a future khan and trusted for life.

That detail tells you a lot about how power worked in this world. Loyalty was personal. A man who had shared a nursery with a prince could rise to enormous influence. And one of the clearest ways to show that influence was to build. A mosque, a school or a caravanserai served the community. It also put your name in brick and tile in front of everyone who passed. Here, the people passing were traders, pilgrims and caravans moving along the routes we now call the Silk Road.

Now look up at the main portal. In Persian and Central Asian architecture this tall, framed gateway is called a pishtaq. It is essentially a giant arch set inside a rectangular frame, built to be seen from far away. On either side, rounded towers anchor the corners of the facade. The brickwork is laid in patterns, and the arch is framed with glazed tiles. Notice the blues, the turquoise and the white set against the honey-coloured brick. Much of the tilework you see today comes from later restoration, but it follows the traditional palette.

The madrasah has not had a quiet life. Over the centuries it served many purposes beyond teaching. At times it was used as a caravanserai, an inn for merchants and their animals. During periods of conflict in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, its thick walls and high position made it useful as a fortress. Think about that as you look at the solid mass of the facade. This was a school, but it was also a building that could be defended.

There is a darker story attached to this gateway, and it is important to say clearly that it is a story. Legend has it that in the nineteenth century, the top of the portal was used for executions. Locals say that people found guilty of serious crimes, and according to some tellers, women accused of unfaithfulness, were thrown from the heights in sacks. It is difficult to confirm how much of this is true. But it is still told often, and it shows how the building lived in the city's memory as a place of authority, not only of learning.

Tashkent sits in an active earthquake zone, and the madrasah carries the marks of that too. Over its long history it was damaged several times. The great earthquake of 1966 destroyed much of the old city around it, and large parts of Tashkent were rebuilt in the decades that followed. Kukeldash survived, but it needed major repair. What you see today is a mix of old fabric and careful reconstruction. Look closely at the brick, and you may notice differences in colour and texture between older and newer sections.

If the gate is open, step through into the courtyard. The change is immediate. The noise of the square drops away, and the light softens against brick walls. Around you are rows of small arched doorways. Each one opens into a hujra, a modest cell where a student lived and studied. These rooms were simple and close together. Imagine them filled with voices reciting texts aloud, which was the standard way of learning for centuries.

Here is the second surprise. Many visitors assume the madrasah is purely a museum. In fact, in the years after Uzbekistan became independent, it returned to religious use and has functioned again as a place of Islamic education. That means this is a working building with its own rhythm. Dress modestly, speak quietly in the courtyard, and be aware that some areas may be closed to visitors. Treat it as you would a school in session, because in many ways that is what it is.

Now turn around and look at the neighbourhood. Below the madrasah lies Chorsu Bazaar, with its huge blue dome. The name Chorsu comes from a Persian phrase meaning four streams or four ways, a reminder that roads met here. Under that dome, traders sell spices, dried fruit, nuts and fresh bread. Nearby stands a large Friday mosque. Together, the market, the mosque and the madrasah form an old city centre where trade, faith and study sat side by side.

This closeness was not an accident. In the traditional Central Asian city, the market and the religious buildings fed each other. Merchants funded schools. Students and scholars shopped in the market. Pilgrims stayed nearby and spent money. Standing here, you can still sense that pattern. The smell of baking bread drifts up from the bazaar. Taxis drop off shoppers carrying bags of produce. And the brick gate watches over it all, much as it did when camels instead of cars filled the square.

Before you leave, take one last look at the portal from a little distance. Watch how the light moves across the brick as the sun shifts, warming the surface and deepening the shadows inside the arch. Listen to the mix of voices around you, in Uzbek, Russian and many other languages. Then walk down toward the market. As you go, notice how the madrasah stays in view over your shoulder, still marking the high ground of the old city.
