The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the foot of the building and look up. A wall of white concrete lattice rises in front of you, a grid of repeating cells that breaks the hard Tashkent sunlight into small squares of shadow. On a hot afternoon, the shade behind that screen feels cooler, almost like stepping into a courtyard. The traffic of the avenue fades behind you. Ahead is one of the deepest collections of Central Asian history anywhere in the world.

This is the State Museum of History of Uzbekistan. Its collection reaches from the Stone Age to the present day. The institution itself is older than the building that holds it. Its roots go back to 1876, when a public museum opened in colonial-era Tashkent. That makes it one of the oldest museums in all of Central Asia. Over the following century it changed names, buildings and missions many times, following the political tides of the region.

The building has its own story, and it is the first surprise. This white block was not built to tell the history of Uzbekistan. It opened in 1970 as a museum devoted to Lenin, at the height of the Soviet period. After independence in 1991, the city faced a question that many former Soviet capitals faced. What do you do with a monument to an ideology that has gone? Here, the answer was to fill it with something much older.

Look again at that façade before you go inside. The lattice is modern concrete, but the idea behind it is old. Across Central Asia, carved wooden and plaster screens called panjara have shaded windows for centuries. They let in air and light while keeping out the glare and the eyes of passers-by. The Soviet architects borrowed that local pattern and enlarged it to the scale of a whole building. It is a quiet meeting of two eras in one wall.

Inside, the light changes. The halls are calmer and dimmer, and the sound of footsteps carries on the stone floors. The displays usually move in time order, so a walk through the museum is also a walk through thousands of years. The earliest rooms deal with the first people in this land. Stone tools, bones and simple ornaments show how hunters lived in the river valleys and mountain caves of the region.

One of the most important finds from that deep past came from a cave called Teshik-Tash, in the mountains of southern Uzbekistan. In the late 1930s, archaeologists found the remains of a Neanderthal child there. The burial was surrounded by the horns of wild mountain goats. Some researchers have read this as a sign of early ritual, though scholars still debate it. Either way, it pushed the human story of this land back tens of thousands of years.

As you move forward in time, the objects become richer. This region sat at the heart of the Silk Roads, the web of trade routes that linked China, India, Persia and the Mediterranean. Ancient kingdoms rose here, with names that may be new to you: Bactria, Sogdiana, Khorezm. Their people traded silk, horses, glass and ideas. You will see pottery, coins and jewellery that carry the marks of many cultures at once.

Here comes the second surprise for many visitors. Long before Islam arrived, Buddhism flourished in the south of Uzbekistan, near the modern city of Termez. In the early centuries of the first millennium, this area was part of the Kushan Empire, a powerful state that stretched into what is now Afghanistan, Pakistan and northern India. Buddhist monasteries stood along the Amu Darya river. The museum holds some of the finest objects from that world.

Look for the sculpture of the Buddha seated beneath an arch, with two monks standing beside him. It came from a site called Fayaz Tepe, a Buddhist monastery near Termez. The carving is soft and calm, made from pale limestone. The faces show a blend of Indian and Hellenistic styles. Hellenistic means the Greek-influenced art that spread east after Alexander the Great. In one small stone, you can see how far ideas travelled along these routes.

Other rooms trace the older faith of the region, Zoroastrianism, an ancient Persian religion centred on fire and purity. Its followers often placed the bones of the dead in clay boxes called ossuaries. Some of these boxes carry painted or moulded figures, and they appear in archaeological collections across Uzbekistan. Watch for them. They are humble objects, but they tell you how people here thought about death and the afterlife many centuries before the mosques and minarets.

Later halls carry the story into the Islamic era, the time of the great cities of Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva. Glazed ceramics in turquoise and cobalt, metalwork, manuscripts and textiles fill these rooms. The age of Amir Timur, known in the West as Tamerlane, has its own place in the national story. Further on, the displays turn to the khanates, the Russian conquest, the Soviet decades, and finally independence. The building that once honoured Lenin now tells what came before and after him.

A practical note as you walk. The museum is large, and the rooms can feel dense with glass cases and labels. It helps to choose a few periods that interest you and slow down there, rather than trying to read everything. Labels are often in Uzbek, Russian and English, but the English can be brief. If you want deeper context, a guided tour or a quiet half hour with the most famous pieces is worth more than a fast march through every hall.

When you step back outside, the heat and the noise of the avenue return. Turn around and look at the lattice wall once more. You have just walked through a hundred thousand years of human life, from a child buried in a mountain cave to a nation that became independent within living memory. All of it fits inside a building that was meant for something else. Notice the small squares of shadow on the pavement... and the long story held behind them.
