The guide

The story

Look up before you walk in. A broad dome of deep blue tiles rises above you, ribbed like a melon and bright against the Tashkent sky. Around it, the city goes on with its day. Cars circle the square, leaves move in the plane trees, and somewhere a group of schoolchildren is laughing on the steps. This round building is the State Museum of the History of the Temurids, and it tells the story of one of the most feared and admired rulers Central Asia ever produced.

That ruler is Amir Timur, known in the West as Tamerlane. He was born in 1336, near the town of Shahrisabz, south of Samarkand. Over his lifetime he built an empire that stretched across Central Asia, Persia, and far beyond. The museum opened in 1996 to mark the six hundred and sixtieth anniversary of his birth. That timing matters. Uzbekistan had become independent only a few years earlier, and the new nation was looking for a founding figure. Timur became that figure.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The name Tamerlane is not really a name at all. It comes from the Persian phrase Timur-i Lang, which means Timur the Lame. In his youth he was wounded in the right leg and the right arm, and he walked with a limp for the rest of his life. His enemies used the nickname as an insult. Europe simply adopted it, and it stuck for six centuries.

Now look at the shape of the building itself. The circular plan and the blue dome are a deliberate echo of the great Timurid monuments in Samarkand. Timur and his descendants loved turquoise and cobalt tilework, tall portals, and ribbed domes that catch the sun. The architects of this museum borrowed that language and set it in a modern capital. Notice how the columns around the base give the building a steady rhythm, like a drumbeat circling the dome.

Step inside, and the light changes completely. The interior is warm with gold. The walls carry painted decoration, and high above the main hall hangs an enormous crystal chandelier. Take a moment under it. The chandelier is one of the details that people remember most. In the evening, when the lights are on, the gold and glass fill the hall with a soft, honey-coloured glow.

Around the walls, large murals show scenes from Timur's life and times. You will see armies on the move, rulers in council, and builders at work. These paintings are not ancient. They were created for the museum, and they show how modern Uzbekistan chose to picture its past. Read them as a kind of national storybook — heroic, colourful, and confident about who the hero is.

The display cases hold a wide range of objects connected to the Timurid era. There are weapons and armour, coins, manuscripts, ceramics, and costumes. Some pieces are original, and some are reproductions of objects held in other collections around the world. Look at the labels as you go. Part of the pleasure here is noticing how much Timurid culture travelled, from Persian poetry to Chinese porcelain to the courts of India.

One exhibit has a special connection to Tashkent. The museum displays a copy of the Quran of Uthman, an early manuscript of great importance to Muslims around the world. The manuscript that the city holds is kept in the Hazrati Imam complex in the old part of Tashkent. Tradition links it to the third caliph, Uthman, and says it reached Central Asia through Timur's campaigns. Scholars still discuss its exact age and history, so treat the full story as a mixture of record and tradition.

The museum is not only about Timur. It is about the dynasty he founded. His grandson Ulugh Beg ruled Samarkand and built an observatory where astronomers measured the stars with remarkable precision. Another descendant, Babur, crossed the mountains into India and founded the Mughal dynasty. That is the second surprise. The line of rulers that later built the Taj Mahal traces its roots back to the man celebrated in this room.

Of course, Timur's story has a darker side. His campaigns destroyed cities across Persia, the Caucasus, and the Middle East, and chroniclers of the time recorded terrible violence. Inside these walls, the emphasis is on the statesman, the builder, and the patron of science and art. As you walk through, it is worth holding both pictures in mind. A great empire was built, and many people paid a heavy price for it.

Timur died in 1405, during a winter campaign toward China. He was buried in the Gur-e-Amir mausoleum in Samarkand. In 1941, Soviet scientists opened his tomb, and their examination confirmed the injuries to his leg and arm. Locals tell a famous tale about that day. According to the legend, an inscription warned that whoever disturbed the tomb would unleash an invader, and Germany attacked the Soviet Union almost immediately afterwards. It is a story, not proven history, but you will hear it often in Uzbekistan.

When you step back outside, walk toward the centre of the square. There you will find the bronze statue of Timur on horseback, raised in the 1990s. During the Soviet period, this same square held monuments to very different figures. The change of statues tells the story of the country in a single glance. On the pedestal, look for the inscription often translated as Strength is in justice, a phrase traditionally attributed to Timur.

The square around you is one of the green centres of the city. Paths lead out under the trees, and on a warm day you will see families, couples, and older men talking on the benches. The tall hotel nearby, with its patterned concrete front, is a reminder of the Soviet decades that shaped modern Tashkent. Old empire, Soviet city, and independent nation all stand within a few hundred metres of each other.

Before you go, turn around once more and look at the dome. Notice how its blue changes as the light moves, darker in shade and almost bright turquoise in direct sun. The traffic still circles. The trees still move. And above it all, a modern building in an old language holds the memory of a man who limped, conquered, and built. Take that picture with you as you walk on into the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pair your visit with the equestrian statue in the square.
Visit Hazrati Imam to see the original Uthman Quran.
Ask at the entrance whether photography inside needs permission.
Rest afterwards on the shaded benches of Amir Timur Square.
Plan a later trip to Samarkand to see Timur's tomb.
