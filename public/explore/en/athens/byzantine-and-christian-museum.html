The guide

The story

Listen first. The traffic on Vasilissis Sofias Avenue is only a few steps behind you, yet inside these gates the sound drops away. Gravel shifts under your shoes. Pine needles and dry earth scent the warm air, and a pale courtyard opens ahead, framed by low wings and a calm, columned front. It feels more like a country house than a museum. That impression is correct, because this was once a private home, built for one of the most unusual women in nineteenth-century Athens.

She was Sophie de Marbois-Lebrun, known as the Duchess of Plaisance. She was a wealthy French-American aristocrat who fell in love with the Greek cause. After Greece won its independence, she settled in the new capital. In the 1840s she commissioned this residence and called it the Villa Ilissia, after the Ilissos river that once ran nearby. The architect was Stamatios Kleanthis, one of the first Greek architects to shape the young city. His design drew on Italian villas, with clean lines and a gentle symmetry.

The Duchess remains a figure of strong stories. She was generous, proud, and famously eccentric. Accounts from the period describe her grief after her daughter died young, and some say she kept the body close for years rather than bury it. These stories are part of local memory, so treat them with care. What we can say with confidence is simpler. She chose this open ground at the edge of the old city and built something quiet and graceful on it.

The museum itself was founded in 1914. For years its collection had no proper home. In 1930 it moved into the Villa Ilissia, and the duchess's rooms began to hold a very different treasure. The mission was clear from the start. The museum would gather the art of Byzantium, the Greek-speaking Christian empire that ruled from Constantinople for about a thousand years. It would also follow that art forward, into the centuries after the empire fell.

Here is the first surprise. Most of the museum is not in the building you see. It lies below your feet. In the early 2000s, a large expansion opened underground, designed by the architect Manos Perrakis. Ramps and wide halls descend into the earth, so the old villa keeps its scale above ground. As you walk down, you also walk forward through time. The route begins in late antiquity and moves, gallery by gallery, toward the modern era.

The first rooms show a world in transition. Here, Roman and Greek traditions meet the new Christian faith. You will see marble sculpture, carved reliefs, and early symbols that Christians used before they had a fixed visual language. A shepherd carrying a lamb. A fish. A simple cross set inside a wreath. These images borrowed freely from the pagan art around them. Look closely, and you can watch a new religion learning how to picture itself.

Further on, the galleries turn to the great age of Byzantium. Wall paintings, called frescoes, were removed from ruined churches and brought here to survive. Fragments of mosaic still hold their small cubes of coloured stone and glass. Gold backgrounds catch the low gallery light. The faces are long and serious, with large eyes. This style was not a failure of realism. Byzantine artists wanted the holy figures to look beyond this world, calm and timeless.

Then come the icons, the heart of the collection. An icon is a painted image of a holy figure, usually on wood, made for prayer and veneration. Many here were painted with egg tempera, a paint made by mixing pigment with egg yolk. Some icons are double-sided, because they were carried in processions and seen from both directions. One celebrated example shows the Archangel Michael, painted in the fourteenth century. His face is soft, almost tender, framed by curls and wings.

The second surprise is fabric. Textiles rarely survive for centuries, yet this museum holds remarkable ones. The most famous is the Epitaphios of Thessaloniki, a fourteenth-century cloth embroidered with gold and silver thread. An epitaphios shows the body of Christ laid out for burial, and it is carried in church during Holy Week. The stitching is so fine that it looks almost painted. Stand close, and you can see how each metallic thread was laid down by hand.

The story does not end in 1453, when Constantinople fell to the Ottoman Turks. The later galleries follow painters who kept the tradition alive. On the island of Crete, then under Venetian rule, workshops blended Byzantine forms with ideas from Italian art. This is often called the Cretan School. You will notice new touches here: more depth, softer drapery, even landscapes in the background. Faith stayed the same, but the eye of the painter was changing.

When you climb back to the surface, take time in the gardens. The grounds hold more than trees and benches. Just beside the museum, excavations in the 1990s uncovered the remains of the Lyceum, the school where Aristotle taught. This was a gathering place of ancient philosophy. So within one short walk, you pass from classical Athens, to a Christian empire, to a nineteenth-century villa. Few places in the city stack these layers so close together.

This part of Athens has its own character. Vasilissis Sofias is lined with embassies, ministries, and museums, and the National Gallery and the War Museum are close by. The streets here are wider and greener than in the old centre around the Acropolis. Office workers walk past with coffee cups. Orange trees shade the pavements. The museum feels like a pause in the middle of a working city, which may be the best way to enjoy it.

Before you leave, stand once more in the courtyard and face the villa. Notice how its pale walls hold the afternoon light, and how quiet the gravel sounds under each step. Below you, in the cool halls, the saints and angels are still looking out from their gold. Above, the city carries on. As you walk back toward the avenue, listen to the traffic slowly return, and carry a little of that stillness with you.
