The guide

The story

Step through the gates and the city noise begins to fade. The traffic on Amalias Avenue softens into a low hum behind the trees. Underfoot, the paths are gravel and packed earth, not marble or asphalt. The air feels a few degrees cooler here. It smells of damp leaves, pine resin, and, in spring, something sweet and floral. Somewhere ahead, a duck calls from a pond. You are only a few steps from the busiest square in Athens, yet it feels like a different place.

This is the National Garden, a large green space in the very centre of the Greek capital. It covers roughly fifteen hectares, which is a lot of shade in a city known for its heat and its stone. To the northwest stands the Hellenic Parliament on Syntagma Square. To the south lies the elegant Zappeion hall. Between them, this garden forms a long, leafy buffer of winding paths, ponds, and old trees. Locals come here to read, to jog, to escape the sun... or simply to sit.

The garden began as a royal project. In the 1830s, Greece had only recently become an independent kingdom. Its first king was Otto, a young Bavarian prince. His wife, Queen Amalia, wanted a private garden beside the new royal palace. That palace is the large building you can see near Syntagma, which today houses the Greek Parliament. The garden was designed as its backyard, a place for the royal couple to walk, rest, and receive guests.

Amalia took a strong personal interest in the project. Plants were brought to Athens from many parts of the world, and from different regions of Greece itself. This was not easy. Athens at that time was a small, dusty town, and the climate is hot and dry. Every tree you see here needed water, care, and patience to survive. According to popular stories, the queen spent long hours in the garden herself, watching it grow. Whether every tale is true or not, the garden clearly carries her mark.

For almost a century, this was the Royal Garden. Ordinary Athenians had limited access to it. That changed in 1923, when the garden was officially opened to the public and given the name it carries today... the National Garden. The shift says something about modern Greek history. Kings came and went, and the monarchy itself was later abolished. But the garden stayed, and slowly it became a space that belongs to everyone in the city.

As you walk, notice the variety of trees. There are tall palms, dark cypresses, broad pines, and many species that are not native to Greece. Some of the palm trees near the Amalias Avenue entrance rise high above the paths, their trunks rough and grey. Elsewhere, climbing plants wrap around old pergolas and railings. The garden holds hundreds of plant species in total. It works almost like a living collection, gathered over nearly two centuries.

Here is something many visitors miss. Beneath this garden lie traces of ancient Athens. During work in the garden, remains from the Roman period were found, including fragments of mosaic floors. Look carefully along some paths and you may spot sections of these old surfaces, now protected and on display. It is a quiet reminder that almost anywhere you dig in central Athens, you find another layer of the city's past. Even a royal flower garden sits on top of older homes and streets.

Now listen for the water. The garden has several ponds, and they are home to ducks and turtles. On a warm day, you can often see turtles resting on stones at the water's edge, their shells drying in the sun. Children gather along the railings to watch them. The ponds are shaded and green, with reflections of leaves moving slowly on the surface. It is one of the calmest corners in the garden, and a good place to pause for a few minutes.

You will also notice cats. Athens has many street cats, and the National Garden has its own population. They sleep under benches, walk along the low walls, and watch the ducks with great interest. Locals often leave food and water for them. Near one part of the garden, there is also a small enclosure with farm animals such as goats and chickens. It is modest, not a full zoo, but families with young children often make it their first stop.

As you explore, look out for statues and busts set among the trees. Several honour important Greek figures, including poets. One of them is Dionysios Solomos, the poet who wrote the words of the Greek national anthem. These monuments are quiet and often half hidden by leaves. They turn a walk through the garden into a small lesson in Greek culture. Take a moment to read the names on the bases — many of them are still well known to every Greek schoolchild.

The garden is also a place of daily life. In the morning, you may see older Athenians walking slowly along the paths, or runners passing through on their routes. At midday, office workers find a shaded bench for lunch. In the afternoon, parents bring children to the play areas. There is no single main attraction here. Instead, the pleasure comes from wandering, turning down a side path, and finding a quiet bench nobody else has claimed.

The edges of the garden connect to some of central Athens' best-known spaces. On the eastern side, along Herodou Attikou Street, stands the Presidential Mansion, guarded by the Evzones, the ceremonial guards in their traditional uniforms. To the south, the paths lead out towards the Zappeion and its open gardens. From there, it is a short walk to the Panathenaic Stadium and the columns of the Temple of Olympian Zeus. The National Garden works as a green bridge between them.

Before you leave, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Look up through the branches and notice how the light breaks into small patches on the ground. Listen to the mix of sounds — birds, a fountain, footsteps on gravel, and the distant traffic just beyond the trees. This garden was made for a queen, but today it belongs to the whole city. As you walk back out into the heat and noise of Athens, you may find you carry a little of its calm with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the morning before the midday heat arrives.
Bring water during summer months for longer walks.
Enter from Syntagma and exit towards the Zappeion.
Combine with the Evzones guards on Herodou Attikou Street.
Find a pond-side bench for a quiet rest.
