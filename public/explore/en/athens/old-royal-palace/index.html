The guide

The story

Stand at the top of Syntagma Square and listen. Traffic hums along the avenue below. Pigeons scatter across warm marble steps. Then, through the noise, you may hear something sharper. It is the slow click and scrape of hobnailed shoes on stone. In front of you rises a long, pale building with a calm, almost severe face. Today it is the Hellenic Parliament. It began life as something else entirely. It was the Old Royal Palace, home to the first king of modern Greece.

To understand why this building exists, picture Athens in the 1830s. Greece had just won its independence from the Ottoman Empire after a long and bloody war. The great powers of Europe decided the new state needed a king. They chose a teenage Bavarian prince named Otto. Athens was named the capital. But at that time it was a small, damaged town at the foot of the Acropolis. A royal capital needed a royal home. This palace was the answer.

The design came from Friedrich von Gärtner, an architect from Munich. He worked in a style called neoclassical. That means a style inspired by the temples and public buildings of ancient Greece and Rome. Notice the restraint here. There are no towers, no domes, no gilded flourishes on the outside. The building is long, symmetrical, and plain. Its quiet look was a statement in itself. Bavaria was presenting itself as the heir to classical Greece.

Construction began in the mid-1830s and the palace was ready in the early 1840s. Its position was chosen with care. It sits on a gentle rise, looking out across the square toward the city. From the upper windows, the royal family could look across the rooftops. And the city could look up at them. That relationship between the palace and the square became the centre of a dramatic moment in Greek history.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. The name of this square, Syntagma, means Constitution. It is named after an event that happened right where you stand. In September 1843, soldiers and citizens gathered in front of the palace. They were tired of rule by a king and his Bavarian advisers. They demanded a constitution. Otto gave way. Greece gained its first constitution as a kingdom, and the square took its name from that night.

Otto's reign did not last. He was forced out of Greece in 1862. A new royal dynasty followed, and the palace remained the royal residence. Then, in 1909, a fire broke out inside. It damaged a large part of the interior. The royal family did not return to live here in the same way. For the next two decades, the building had a strange and uncertain life.

During those years, the old palace served many purposes. At different times it housed a hospital. After 1922, when huge numbers of Greek refugees arrived from Asia Minor, it also gave shelter to displaced families. Think about that for a moment. Rooms built for royal receptions held people who had lost everything. The building was not only a symbol. It was also a roof over many heads.

In the late 1920s, the Greek state decided to give the palace a new role. After major renovation, it became the home of the Greek Parliament in the 1930s. It has served that purpose ever since. So the building where a king once lived is now the place where elected lawmakers debate and vote. The same walls that heard the demand for a constitution now hold the institution that constitution created.

Now lower your eyes to the foot of the building. Below the palace, set into a broad wall, is the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. It was completed in the early 1930s. At its centre is a carved relief of an ancient Greek warrior. He lies on his back, wearing a helmet and holding a shield. The figure is calm, almost asleep. He stands for every Greek soldier who died without a known grave.

Look closely at the words carved into the wall. They come from the Funeral Oration of Pericles, the Athenian statesman of the fifth century before Christ. The speech was recorded by the historian Thucydides. One line honours the missing dead. Another says that for famous men, the whole earth is their tomb. Around the monument, the names of battles are carved into the stone. They span many wars of modern Greek history.

The guards in front of the tomb are the Evzones, a ceremonial unit of the Greek army. Their uniform comes from the traditional dress of mountain fighters. The white pleated kilt is called a fustanella. A popular story says it has four hundred pleats, one for each year of Ottoman rule. That is a tale more than a record. The red shoes, called tsarouchia, carry large black pompoms and heavy metal nails on the soles.

Here is the second surprise. The guards hold perfectly still for long stretches, whatever the weather. When they move, the change of guard is slow and deliberate. Their legs swing high, their shoes strike the stone, and each movement is mirrored by the partner. It can look almost theatrical. But every step is part of a fixed ritual. On Sunday mornings, a larger ceremony takes place, with more guards and a military band.

Before you leave, turn around and look back down the square. Notice the old trees, the café tables, the steps to the metro below. Behind the palace, the shaded paths of the National Garden begin, laid out for Queen Amalia, Otto's wife. Take a breath of the cooler air there if you can. And as you walk away, listen once more for the click of hobnails on stone... a small sound that has outlived a kingdom.

Before you go

Practical tips

Watch the change of guard from the square's upper steps.
Come on a Sunday morning for the larger ceremony.
Keep a respectful distance from the guards on duty.
Walk behind the palace into the shaded National Garden.
Bring sun protection; the square has little midday shade.
