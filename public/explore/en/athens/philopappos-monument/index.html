The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Up here on the Hill of the Muses, it moves through the pine trees and dry grass with a soft, steady hiss. The path under your feet is loose stone and worn marble, polished by many walkers. And across the valley, very close, the Parthenon stands on its rock. Most people climb this hill for that view. But look at the broken marble tower behind you. It has its own story, and it is a strange one.

This is the Philopappos Monument. It was built in the early second century, during the Roman period in Athens. It is a tomb and a memorial for one man: Gaius Julius Antiochus Epiphanes Philopappos. That is a long name, and each part of it means something. It is a Roman name, a royal name, and a Greek name all at once. Together they tell you who he was. He was a prince without a kingdom.

Philopappos came from Commagene. This was a small kingdom in what is now southeastern Turkey, on the upper Euphrates River. Its kings claimed descent from both Persian and Greek royal families. In the year 72, Rome took over Commagene and ended its independence. The royal family lost its throne, but it kept its wealth and its pride. Philopappos was the grandson of the last king, Antiochus the Fourth.

So what does an exiled prince do? Philopappos made a new life in two worlds. In Rome, he entered the Senate and served as a consul, one of the highest offices in the empire. In Athens, he became a citizen and a generous patron. He held public offices in the city and paid for public works. The Athenians honoured him. When he died, his sister and the people of Athens raised this monument on the most visible hill they could choose.

Now look at the shape of the façade. It is not flat. It curves gently inward, like a shallow bowl. And it faces northeast, toward the Acropolis. This is not an accident. The dead prince was placed so that he looks forever at the sacred rock of Athens. Stand in front of the curve and you will feel it. The marble seems to hold you, the way an open hand holds water.

The upper level once had three niches with statues. In the centre sits Philopappos himself, in a relaxed pose. To the left is his grandfather, King Antiochus the Fourth. The niche on the right is now gone. It held Seleucus the First, a general of Alexander the Great who founded a large dynasty in the east. With these figures, Philopappos told every visitor a clear message: my family is old, royal, and connected to Alexander himself.

Below the statues runs a carved frieze. Here is the first surprise. The scene does not show a king. It shows Philopappos as a Roman consul, riding in a chariot in a formal procession. Men walk in front of him carrying bundles of rods, the traditional symbols of Roman authority. So the monument shows two identities, one above the other. Above is the Eastern prince. Below is the Roman official. He wanted to be remembered as both.

Look closely at the pilasters, the flat columns between the niches. They carry inscriptions. Some are in Latin, which list his Roman career. Others are in Greek, which name his royal titles and his Athenian ties. Even the languages are divided by role. Some letters are worn and hard to read now. But on a bright morning, with low side light, you can often still see the cut lines of the words.

Here is a second surprise. What you see today is only part of the original building. Much of the structure fell or was taken away over the centuries. Marble was valuable, and builders reused it. Travellers in the Renaissance made drawings when more of it still stood. Their sketches help scholars imagine the full monument. So what remains is a fragment, like a page torn from a book.

The hill itself is much older than the monument. The ancient Greeks called it the Mouseion, the hill of the Muses, the goddesses of art and poetry. The ancient writer Pausanias, who described Greece in the second century, recorded a tradition about it. According to that tradition, the poet Musaeus sang here, and he was buried on this hill. Pausanias also mentioned this monument briefly. He called it a tomb built for a Syrian man. Philopappos would perhaps not be pleased.

The hill had a military role too. Its height made it valuable to anyone who wanted to control Athens. In the Hellenistic period, after Alexander, a Macedonian ruler placed a fortress and a garrison up here. As you walk the paths, you may see traces of old walls and cuttings in the rock. On the slopes below, a rock-cut chamber is popularly called the Prison of Socrates. Locals and guides repeat this name, but there is no firm evidence that Socrates was ever held there.

On your way up or down, notice the paths themselves. In the 1950s, the Greek architect Dimitris Pikionis designed the walkways around this hill and the Acropolis. He used stones, marble pieces, and broken fragments set into patterns. The paths look natural, almost ancient, but they are modern art. Near the bottom stands the small church of Saint Demetrios Loumbardiaris. Legend has it that lightning once struck an Ottoman cannon aimed at the faithful, saving the people inside.

Now turn around and take in the whole view. The Parthenon is in front of you. To the right, the city spreads toward the sea at Piraeus. On a clear day, you can see the islands of the Saronic Gulf. Behind you is the prince who chose this spot to rest. He lost his kingdom, but he found a hill. He lost his throne, but he gained a view that kings still envy.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Listen again to the pine trees. Feel the warm marble under your hand, if you touch the base. Watch the light move on the Acropolis rock across the valley. As you walk down the stone path, look back once. Philopappos will still be there, gazing at Athens, as he has done for almost two thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb in the early morning for cooler air and soft light.
Wear shoes with grip for the slippery polished stone paths.
Stay for sunset to watch light fade on the Acropolis.
Bring water; there is little shade near the summit.
Walk the Pikionis paths slowly to notice their stone patterns.
