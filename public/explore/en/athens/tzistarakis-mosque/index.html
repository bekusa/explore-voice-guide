The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the paving of Monastiraki Square. Underfoot, the stone is worn smooth by countless shoes. Around you, the air carries the smell of grilled meat, roasted nuts, and warm metal from the trains below. Street musicians play near the metro steps. And just to one side, a low building with a pale dome and a shaded porch sits quietly above the noise. This is the Tzistarakis Mosque — one of the few Ottoman mosques still standing in the city centre.

First, a little context. For nearly four centuries, Athens was part of the Ottoman Empire. That is the vast state, ruled from Istanbul, that controlled much of southeastern Europe and the Middle East. During that long period, the town looked very different. It was smaller, with narrow lanes, bazaars, churches, and mosques side by side. Most of that Ottoman layer was cleared away after Greek independence. This building is one of the survivors.

The mosque was built in 1759. It takes its name from the man who ordered it, Mustafa Agha Tzistarakis. He was the voivode of Athens — the local Ottoman governor, responsible for taxes and order in the town. His mosque was placed at a busy point in the old market. Even then, this square was a meeting place for traders, farmers, and travellers coming in from the countryside.

Now comes the part of the story that most visitors never hear. To make lime plaster for his new mosque, the governor needed limestone or marble to burn. According to the most common account, he took a column from the Temple of Olympian Zeus, the great ancient temple to the southeast of here. The marble went into the kilns. For many Athenians, both Greek and Turkish, this was a deep offence against the city's past.

What happened next is where history and folklore blend together. Locals say that the destruction of the column brought a curse on the town. Not long afterwards, an outbreak of plague struck Athens, and many people connected the two events. Popular tradition also says the governor was later removed from his post for his actions. Treat these stories as legend rather than firm record. But they show how strongly people here felt about their ancient stones, even long before modern archaeology.

Look now at the building itself. The design follows a classic Ottoman pattern. There is a square prayer hall, crowned by a single dome. In front of it runs a covered porch with columns and arches. This porch gave worshippers shade and a place to gather before prayer. The walls are built from stone and brick, laid in bands. Notice how simple and balanced the whole form is — no heavy decoration, just clear geometry.

Something important is missing, though. Every mosque of this kind once had a minaret, the slender tower from which the call to prayer was sung. Here, the minaret is gone. It was taken down in the nineteenth century, after Greek independence. Without it, the building can be easy to miss. Many people walk past and assume it is a church or a small museum. In a sense, they are half right.

After independence in the nineteenth century, the mosque lost its religious role. The new Greek state used it for very practical purposes. Over the decades, it served at different times as a meeting hall, a barracks, a prison, and a storehouse. Each of these uses left the building a little more worn. Then, in the early twentieth century, it found a quieter purpose. It became a home for collections of Greek folk art.

Today, the interior holds the ceramics collection of the Museum of Greek Folk Art. Inside, you can see pottery from across Greece — jugs, plates, storage jars, and decorated pieces made by village workshops. The setting is part of the experience. Ordinary household pots sit under the curve of an Ottoman dome. It is a small, calm room, and a welcome pause from the crowds outside. Check locally whether it is open during your visit, as access can vary.

Turn your attention to the neighbours, because this square holds many layers in a very small space. Right behind the mosque stand the columns of Hadrian's Library. The Roman emperor Hadrian built it in the second century as a grand complex with a library, courtyards, and gardens. Close by is the small Byzantine church that gave Monastiraki its name — the word means little monastery. Roman, Byzantine, Ottoman, and modern Greek Athens all meet within a few steps.

The building has also survived natural shocks. Athens sits in an earthquake zone, and the mosque was damaged by a strong quake in 1981. Restoration work followed, and the structure was repaired and stabilised. When you look at the walls, you can see the result of this care. Some stones look older and rougher, others more even. That mix is typical of buildings that have been patched and protected over many generations.

Before you move on, lift your eyes above the dome. On a clear day, the rock of the Acropolis rises behind the square, with the Parthenon at the top. From this spot, you can hold three eras in one view — an ancient temple, a Roman library, and an Ottoman mosque. It is a quiet reminder that the city was never built once. It was built again and again, by many hands and many faiths.

So take a last look at that pale dome and the shaded arches of the porch. Listen to the trains below, the sellers calling, and the music near the metro. Then, as you walk into the flea market lanes or up towards Plaka, notice how often old stones appear in new walls. Athens keeps its past close. This small mosque, without its tower, is one of the clearest places to see it.
