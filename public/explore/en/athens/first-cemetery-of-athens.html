The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Athens is only a few streets away, yet here the loudest sound is often gravel under your shoes. Tall cypress trees stand in dark green columns along the paths. Their shade falls across white marble, and on warm days the air carries a dry, resinous smell. Somewhere a bird calls from a rooftop of carved stone. This is the First Cemetery of Athens, and it feels like a small city of its own.

The cemetery sits just south of the ancient heart of the city, behind the Temple of Olympian Zeus and close to the Panathenaic Stadium. The street that leads to the gate has a fitting name. It is called Anapafseos, which means repose, or rest, in Greek. The surrounding neighbourhood, known as Mets, is a quiet district of older houses, small cafés, and stone-carving workshops. Many of those workshops exist because of this place.

To understand why this cemetery matters, picture Athens in the 1830s. Greece had only recently won its independence from the Ottoman Empire. Athens became the capital of the new kingdom, but it was still a modest town. The young state needed institutions, and among them was a proper public burial ground. The First Cemetery opened in 1837. As its name suggests, it was the first official cemetery of modern Athens.

Over the following decades, this ground became the resting place of the nation's leaders, thinkers, and artists. Prime ministers, poets, scientists, and actors lie here, often only a few steps apart. Walking these paths, you move through the whole story of modern Greece. There are heroes of the independence struggle, nineteenth-century merchants, twentieth-century politicians, and performers whose names are still known in every Greek household.

The style of the older tombs tells you a great deal about that early period. The new Greek state looked back to the classical age for its identity. So the wealthy families of Athens built their tombs to look like small ancient temples. You will see columns, pediments, and carved reliefs of figures saying farewell, in the manner of ancient grave markers. This revival of ancient forms is called neoclassicism, and here it covers almost every hillside path.

Now for one of the most famous sculptures in Greece. Look for a young woman lying on a marble bed, one arm resting near her head, her body relaxed as if in light sleep. This is the Sleeping Maiden, made by the sculptor Yannoulis Chalepas for the tomb of a young woman named Sofia Afentaki, who died very young. Visitors often stand beside her in silence. The marble looks soft, almost like fabric.

The story of the sculptor is part of the power of this work. Chalepas came from the island of Tinos, a place with a long tradition of marble carving. Soon after he finished the Sleeping Maiden, he suffered a severe mental illness. For many years he could not work as he once had. Much later in life, he returned to sculpture. Many Greeks see the calm face of the Maiden as a moment of peace before that long darkness.

A very different tomb stands on higher ground. It looks like a small Greek temple raised on a tall base, and it belongs to Heinrich Schliemann. He was the German businessman and archaeologist who searched for the city of Troy and excavated at Mycenae. He made Athens his home. His mausoleum was designed by the architect Ernst Ziller, who shaped much of nineteenth-century Athens. Around the base runs a carved frieze with scenes from the Trojan War and from Schliemann's own excavations.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. On that frieze, Schliemann himself appears among the figures, together with his workers and his Greek wife, Sophia. The archaeologist placed his own story beside the heroes of Homer. You can read it as ambition, or as love for the myths that shaped his life. Either way, the frieze is worth a slow walk all the way around. Ziller, the architect, is buried in this cemetery too.

Modern Greece is here as well. One of the most visited graves belongs to Melina Mercouri. She was an actress who became an international film star, and later she served as Greece's Minister of Culture. She spent years campaigning for the return of the Parthenon sculptures from London to Athens. Fresh flowers often lie on her tomb. That small detail shows how close these names still feel to the people of the city.

As you walk, notice that this is not a museum. It is still a working cemetery. You may see families tending graves, lighting small oil lamps, or leaving flowers. In Greek Orthodox tradition, relatives often visit regularly and keep a small flame burning beside a photograph of the person they lost. You may also pass a funeral. If you do, step aside quietly, lower your voice, and keep your camera down.

The second surprise is about time. In many older family tombs, you will see dates that span more than a century. Grandparents from the nineteenth century share the same marble with grandchildren from recent decades. A single family name can carry the story of wars, emigration, and return. Read a few inscriptions slowly. Even if you cannot read Greek letters, the dates alone tell a quiet story of the generations that kept this city alive.

The craftsmanship also rewards close attention. Look at the folds of carved robes, the fine lettering, and the small marble angels with weathered wings. Some stones are bright and clean. Others are grey and spotted with lichen, softened by rain and long summers. Many of the carvers trained in Tinos or in workshops nearby. The marble yards you passed on the way in still continue that work today.

Before you leave, stop under one of the cypress trees and look back along the path. Notice the rows of white stone, the dark trees, and the pale Athenian sky above them. Beyond the walls, the city keeps moving. In here, the pace is slower. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again for the gravel under your feet... and for the moment when the noise of Athens returns.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the nearby Temple of Olympian Zeus.
Come in the morning when cypress shade cools the paths.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven marble and gravel walkways.
Step aside quietly and lower cameras if a funeral passes.
Dress modestly out of respect for mourning families.
