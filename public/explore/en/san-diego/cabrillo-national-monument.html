The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves through low, grey-green shrubs. Somewhere below you, the Pacific breaks against rock in a slow, steady rhythm. The air smells of salt and dry sage. You are at the southern tip of Point Loma, a long finger of land that curls around the entrance to San Diego Bay. On one side lies open ocean. On the other, a sheltered harbour full of ships. This narrow headland has watched every arrival into the bay for centuries.

This place is Cabrillo National Monument. It remembers a single landing. In 1542, a small Spanish expedition sailed north along the coast of what is now Mexico. It was led by a navigator named Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo. That autumn, his ships entered this bay and anchored here. It was the first time a European expedition set foot on the land that would later become the West Coast of the United States. Cabrillo called the harbour a very good, closed port.

But Cabrillo did not arrive in an empty land. The Kumeyaay people had lived around this bay for thousands of years. They fished these waters, gathered shellfish from the rocks, and harvested plants from the hillsides around you. The written accounts of the voyage describe meetings with local people on shore. Those meetings were cautious at first. Today, the park tells both sides of that encounter. As you walk, try to picture the bay through two pairs of eyes — those on the ships, and those watching from the beach.

Now turn toward the statue. A tall stone figure of Cabrillo stands on the plaza, looking out over the water. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The statue was a gift from Portugal, not Spain. Portugal has long claimed Cabrillo as one of its own, under the Portuguese form of his name, João Rodrigues Cabrilho. Some historians argue he was born in Spain instead. The question is still debated. So the man in stone carries two nations' pride on his shoulders.

Cabrillo never saw the end of his journey. After leaving this bay, his ships continued north along the California coast. During the winter, he was injured, and he died on one of the offshore islands. His crew sailed on without him and later returned to New Spain, the Spanish colony centred on Mexico. His exact grave has never been found. That quiet absence makes this monument feel more personal. It is a memorial to a man whose resting place remains unknown.

The monument itself is old by American standards. President Woodrow Wilson set it aside by proclamation in 1913. At first it was tiny, little more than a small plot of ground. Over the decades it grew to include the cliffs, trails, and shoreline you see today. For much of that time, the surrounding land belonged to the military. Even now, much of Point Loma remains a working naval area. You drove past signs of that on the road in, including rows of white headstones at the national cemetery.

Walk up the path to the small white lighthouse. This is the Old Point Loma Lighthouse, first lit in 1855. It was one of the earliest lighthouses on the Pacific coast. It looks modest — a simple house with a lantern tower rising from its roof. The builders placed it high on the ridge so ships could see it from far away. On paper, that was a clever choice. In practice, it created a serious problem, and you can probably guess what it was.

San Diego is famous for its low marine clouds and fog, especially in late spring and early summer. Locals call it May Gray and June Gloom. Up here, the lighthouse often sat inside the cloud, or above it. Sailors below could not see the light at all. After less than forty years of service, the old light was switched off in 1891. A new lighthouse was built much lower, closer to the water. Look down toward the shore and you may spot it. It still guides ships today.

Inside the old lighthouse, the rooms are furnished as a keeper's family home. Imagine life here before the city grew. Water and supplies had to come a long way. The wind pressed against the windows at night. The keeper climbed the stairs again and again to trim the wick and clean the glass. Children grew up with the whole Pacific as their front yard. It was a lonely post, but an honest one. Every clear night, that small flame meant safety for someone out at sea.

Now look outward. On a clear day, the view runs a long way. Across the bay you can see Coronado and the naval air station on North Island. Behind it rises the downtown skyline. Far to the south, low shapes sit on the horizon. Those are the Coronado Islands, which belong to Mexico. Navy ships and submarines sometimes slide through the channel below you. Sailboats and fishing boats follow the same route that Cabrillo's ships took nearly five centuries ago.

If you visit in winter, keep watching the open ocean. Between roughly December and March, gray whales pass this point on a long migration. They travel from cold Arctic waters to the warm lagoons of Baja California, where they give birth. Look for a brief puff of spray, called a blow, rising above the waves. Then look for a dark back curving through the water. The park has a whale overlook for this purpose. Patience matters here. Some days bring many sightings, others bring none.

The hillsides hold their own story. The plants around you are coastal sage scrub, a tough, fragrant community of shrubs. It once covered much of Southern California, and most of it has been lost to development. Here it survives. Walk the Bayside Trail and you will pass old concrete structures as well. These are remains of coastal defences from the World Wars, including searchlight shelters and gun positions. The military used this high ground to guard the harbour entrance during those years.

On the western side of the point, the land drops to rocky tidepools. At low tide, the sea pulls back and shows a hidden world. You may find sea anemones, small crabs, snails, and sea stars clinging to the rocks. Move slowly and watch where you place your feet, because the rocks are slippery. Touch gently, if at all, and leave everything where you found it. In winter, the lowest tides often fall during daylight, which makes that season a good time to explore.

Before you leave, stand at the edge once more. Feel the wind on your face. Listen to the water below. Look at the bay — the ships, the city, the low line of hills beyond. Every one of those things arrived after that first small expedition dropped anchor here. Yet the rhythm of the waves has not changed. As you walk back to the car, notice the sage brushing your legs. Breathe in. That scent is one of the oldest things on this point.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the tide tables and visit the tidepools at low tide.
Come between December and March to watch for gray whales.
Bring layers because the wind on the point stays cool.
Wear shoes with good grip for the slippery tidepool rocks.
Arrive on a clear morning for the longest views south.
