The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Footsteps on warm paving stones. A fountain splashing somewhere ahead. The faint sound of a street musician tuning a guitar under an arcade. And above it all, the smell of eucalyptus mixed with something sweeter, maybe jasmine or orange blossom. You are standing in Balboa Park, and the air here feels different from the city just a few streets away. It is softer, greener, and somehow slower. Let's walk through it together.

This park is enormous. It covers roughly 1,200 acres, which makes it one of the largest urban cultural parks in the United States. Inside its borders you will find gardens, theatres, and more than a dozen museums. You will also find canyons, playgrounds, and the San Diego Zoo. But here is the first surprise. When this land was first set aside, there was almost nothing here at all. No trees. No buildings. Just dry, scrubby hills.

In 1868, San Diego's city leaders reserved this large stretch of mesa as public parkland. It was a bold decision for a small frontier town. For decades, though, the land stayed mostly empty and brown. People called it simply City Park. It took one determined woman, and later one grand celebration, to turn these hills into the green place you see around you now.

That woman was Kate Sessions, a horticulturist, meaning an expert in growing plants. In the early 1890s, she made an unusual deal with the city. She could use part of the park for a plant nursery. In return, she promised to plant hundreds of trees every year, in the park and around the city. Many of the tall trees that shade these paths today come from her work. Locals still call her the Mother of Balboa Park.

In 1910, the park received its current name. It honours Vasco Núñez de Balboa, a Spanish explorer. In the early sixteenth century, he became the first European to see the Pacific Ocean from the Americas. The name was chosen with a purpose. San Diego was preparing a world's fair to celebrate the opening of the Panama Canal. The canal would link the Atlantic and the Pacific. And San Diego wanted ships to stop here first.

That fair was the Panama-California Exposition, which opened in 1915. It shaped almost everything you are looking at now. The planners chose a style called Spanish Colonial Revival. It borrows from the churches and palaces of Spain and colonial Mexico. Look at the carved doorways, the arched walkways, the red tile roofs, and the bright towers. The architect Bertram Goodhue led much of the design. He wanted visitors to feel they had walked into a dream of old Spain.

Here is the second surprise. Most of these buildings were never meant to last. They were built for the fair, often from plaster over wooden frames. Over the years, many began to crack and crumble. So the city made a choice. Instead of letting them disappear, it rebuilt several of them as careful copies, using stronger materials. Some of the elegant facades around you are faithful reconstructions, not the originals. Yet they keep the spirit of 1915 alive.

If you look toward the western edge of the park, you may see the California Building. Its tall tower and its dome are covered in coloured tiles, blue and yellow, that shine in the sun. This building is one of the few that was built to be permanent. Today it holds the Museum of Us, which focuses on human cultures. To reach it, many visitors cross the Cabrillo Bridge, a long arched bridge over a deep canyon. From there, the tower appears as a gateway into the park.

Now walk along El Prado, the main path through the centre of the park. Soon you reach the Botanical Building. It is made from thin strips of wood called lath. This lattice lets light and air pass through while it shades the plants inside. In front of it lies a long, still pool called the Lily Pond. On calm days, the curved building appears again upside down in the water. Many people stop here simply to sit and watch the reflection.

In the mid-1930s, San Diego held a second world's fair here, the California Pacific International Exposition. It added new buildings and new gardens. The Old Globe Theatre comes from this period. It was modelled on Shakespeare's Globe Theatre in London. After a fire in the late 1970s, it was rebuilt. Today it is still a working theatre. Nearby, the Alcazar Garden copies the tiled gardens of the Alcazar palace in Seville, Spain, with low hedges and bright fountains.

Another sound might reach you as you walk. It could be music from the Spreckels Organ Pavilion. This is a large outdoor pipe organ, built for the 1915 fair. It sits under a curved, decorated shell. Outdoor pipe organs like this are rare, because rain and sea air are hard on the instruments. San Diego's mild climate helps. The organ still plays regular public concerts, and people bring blankets and sit on the benches in the sun.

The park has also served the city in harder times. During the Second World War, the United States Navy used parts of Balboa Park. Some museum buildings became hospital wards and training spaces. Soldiers and sailors recovered in rooms that once held art and exhibits. After the war, the museums returned, but the park kept a quiet connection to San Diego's long naval history.

And then there are the small, human details. Painters work in their studios in the Spanish Village Art Center, with doors open onto a colourful courtyard. Families gather near the large Moreton Bay fig tree, with its long, twisting roots spread across the ground. Joggers run past museum steps. Dogs pull their owners toward the shade. This park is not a museum piece. It is the city's shared living room.

Before you move on, pause and look around once more. Notice the light on the tiled domes. Notice the old trees, many of them planted because one woman kept her promise. Notice the people, sitting, walking, talking. The fair ended more than a century ago, but the celebration never really stopped. As you walk away, listen for that fountain again, and let it carry you back into the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cross the Cabrillo Bridge on foot for the best approach.
Bring a blanket for an outdoor organ concert.
Visit the Lily Pond early for calm reflections.
Wear comfortable shoes; the park is large and hilly.
Check museum schedules online before planning your route.
