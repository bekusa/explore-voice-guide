The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere nearby, water slaps against a concrete edge. Gulls cry over Mission Bay. A low hum of pumps runs under everything, the sound of millions of litres of seawater moving through pipes you will never see. The air smells of salt, sunscreen, and fried food. This is SeaWorld San Diego, a park built on the edge of a calm, shallow bay, and almost everything here depends on water.

The story begins in the early 1960s with four graduates of the University of California, Los Angeles. Their names were George Millay, Milton Shedd, David DeMotte, and Ken Norris. Their first idea was modest — an underwater restaurant with a marine show attached. The plan grew. When SeaWorld opened in 1964, it had a handful of attractions, a few dolphins and sea lions, and a small staff. Few people imagined it would become one of the best-known theme parks in the United States.

Here is something many visitors miss. The ground under your feet is not private land. SeaWorld sits inside Mission Bay Park, a large public park owned by the City of San Diego. The company leases the land from the city. Mission Bay itself was once a marshy river mouth. In the twentieth century, engineers dredged it and reshaped it into the lagoons and islands you see today. So this park stands on land that people built.

Of the four founders, Ken Norris deserves a second mention. He was a marine biologist. Later, he became one of the leading researchers on how dolphins use sound to find their way. That scientific thread has run through the park from the start. Entertainment and research have always shared the same water here — sometimes comfortably, sometimes not.

The name most people link with SeaWorld is Shamu. The name first belonged to one orca in the 1960s. Later, it became a stage name used for many different whales across the company's parks. For decades, the large orca stadium was the center of the visit. Trainers rode on the whales, and crowds in the front rows came out soaked. For many families, that splash was the whole point of the trip.

Then the public mood changed. In 2013, a documentary called Blackfish raised hard questions about keeping orcas in captivity. Attendance fell. Criticism grew loud. In 2016, the company announced that it would end its orca breeding program. The theatrical orca shows in San Diego ended soon after. A new presentation replaced them, with more focus on natural behavior and less on tricks. Whatever your view, this park sits at the center of one of the major debates about animals and entertainment in modern times.

There is another side of the operation that many guests never see. SeaWorld runs an animal rescue program along the California coast. Over the years, teams have rescued sea lions, seals, sea turtles, and seabirds that were stranded, sick, or injured. Many of these animals were treated and returned to the ocean. In difficult years, when warm water pushes young sea lions onto beaches, the rescue team can be very busy. So when you see a sea lion here, it may have a story you would not guess.

Now look around at the rides. Over the last two decades, the park has added more thrill rides. Manta is a coaster themed around the graceful rays. Electric Eel launches riders forward and backward. Emperor, named after the emperor penguin, is a dive coaster. It holds riders over a steep drop before it lets them fall. The roar of these coasters now mixes with the sounds of the animal habitats. It is a strange and very modern combination.

The ownership has changed too. In 1989, the brewing company Anheuser-Busch bought SeaWorld. That is why older visitors may remember beer gardens and the famous Clydesdale horses. Later, the parks became their own company. In 2024, that company took a new corporate name, United Parks and Resorts. However, the SeaWorld name stayed on the gates. So the park has passed through several hands while it kept its identity.

One of the quieter spaces here is the penguin habitat. Step inside, and the temperature drops sharply. Artificial snow falls over rocks. Penguins waddle, dive, and shoot through the water like small torpedoes. The cold air is a surprise on a warm San Diego afternoon. It is also a reminder of how much energy goes into keeping polar animals alive in a sunny southern California city.

If you want a wider view, look up. The Skytower rises above the park, and its cabin turns slowly as it climbs. From the top, you can see Mission Bay, the Pacific Ocean, and on clear days the hills inland. Nearby, the Bayside Skyride carries small gondolas over a channel of the bay. Below, you may see kayaks, paddleboards, and sailboats. These rides give you the best sense of where the park sits — on a thin strip between city and sea.

For families with young children, there is a gentler corner themed around Sesame Street. Familiar characters wave, small rides turn in slow circles, and water play areas let children cool off. It is louder and more colorful than the rest of the park. But it also shows how far SeaWorld has moved from that first small marine show in 1964. Today the park tries to serve thrill seekers, toddlers, and animal lovers all at once.

So, as you walk on, take a slow breath. Notice the mix of sounds — a coaster rumbling overhead, a sea lion barking, a child laughing somewhere near the water. Look past the fences toward the open bay, where wild birds come and go as they please. This place started as an idea for an underwater restaurant. It became something much bigger and much more debated. What you make of it is your own conclusion to reach, here, at the edge of the water.
