The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. Up here, it pushes against your jacket and tugs at loose sleeves, and it carries the clean, salty smell of the open Adriatic. Below you, the water is a deep, clear blue, so clear that on a calm day you can follow the shadows of rocks far beneath the surface. Two great pale curves of concrete arc across the channel ahead. They do not look heavy. From a distance, they look almost drawn in the air with a single stroke of a pencil.

This is the Krk Bridge. It links the Croatian mainland, a short drive south of Rijeka, to the island of Krk. Krk is one of the two largest islands in the Adriatic Sea. Before the bridge, the only way across was by ferry. Islanders planned their lives around timetables, queues and the weather. When the crossing opened in 1980, an island became, in a practical sense, part of the mainland. Everything from tourism to the regional airport changed because of it.

Now look closely at the shape. The bridge is not one long span. It is two separate arches, and they meet in the middle on a small rocky islet called Saint Mark. The islet works like a natural stepping stone. Engineers used it to break the crossing into two parts. The shorter arch covers the narrower channel. The longer arch reaches across the wider stretch of sea. Together, the whole structure runs well over a kilometre from shore to shore.

Here is the first surprise. When it opened, the larger arch was the longest concrete arch span in the world, at about 390 metres between its supports. It held that record for well over a decade. Concrete is strong when you squeeze it but weak when you stretch it. An arch is the oldest answer to that problem. It turns the weight of the road into pressure that flows down into the rock on each side. The Romans built aqueducts on the same principle. Here, the idea simply grew to a scale the Romans never imagined.

The bridge was built in the second half of the 1970s, in what was then Yugoslavia. At its opening it carried a different name. It was called Tito's Bridge, after Josip Broz Tito, the long-time leader of the Yugoslav state. After Croatia became independent in the early 1990s, the name changed. Today most people call it simply the Krk Bridge. Some older locals still use the old name out of habit, without any politics in it at all.

Building something like this over open sea was a serious challenge. The arches could not simply be poured in place from the water. Workers built them piece by piece, reaching out from each side, with temporary supports holding the growing curves until the two halves met. Imagine the moment the gap finally closed, high above the channel, with the wind moving through the work. Every segment had to line up exactly, or the arch would not carry its load.

That wind is the second thing every local will tell you about. This coast is famous for the bura, a cold, dry wind that pours down from the mountains towards the sea. It can arrive suddenly and blow in violent gusts. On the bridge it can be strong enough to push a car sideways. When the bura is fierce, authorities restrict traffic or close the crossing completely. High-sided vehicles are usually the first to be stopped. For islanders, a bura closure is still a reminder of the old ferry days.

If you are standing on the bridge or near it, look at the colour of the concrete. It has weathered to a soft grey, with darker streaks where rain has run down the curves for decades. Salt air is hard on any structure. Over the years the bridge has needed repair and reinforcement to protect it from corrosion. The pale patches and slightly different tones you may notice are part of that long, quiet work of maintenance.

Now turn towards the mainland side. The rocky hills drop steeply to the water, covered in low, tough plants that can survive both summer heat and winter wind. Nearby lies the small town of Kraljevica, with a long history of shipbuilding. On the island side, the land is lower and greener. Not far away is the village of Omišalj, an old settlement on a hill above the sea, and the industrial port area below it.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The airport that serves Rijeka is not in Rijeka at all. It sits on the island of Krk, just a short distance from the bridge. If you flew into the region, you probably crossed this bridge without thinking about it. In a quiet way, this concrete arch is the front door of the whole area. Planes landing on the island and cars crossing the channel both depend on the same narrow link.

In summer, the traffic tells another story. Long lines of cars, camper vans and coaches roll onto the island, heading for beaches, campsites and the old town of Krk. Number plates come from across Europe. The bridge is a toll crossing, so expect to slow down at the toll point. In the evening, the flow reverses as day visitors head back towards the mainland. The rhythm of the island's summer is written in this traffic, morning and night.

From the shore, the best view is often from below, where you can see both arches at once against the sky. In the late afternoon, the low sun warms the grey concrete and throws long shadows across the curves. Boats pass underneath, small fishing boats and sailing yachts, and suddenly you understand the height. A tall mast slides under the larger arch with plenty of room to spare. Only then does the scale become real.

So take a moment before you move on. Listen to the wind in your ears and the hum of tyres on the road deck. Watch the light on the water under the arches. Two curves of concrete, resting on a rock in the sea, have carried an island's daily life for more than forty years. As you leave, look back once more. From a distance, the bridge looks light again, almost like a line drawn across the channel.

Practical tips

Check for bura wind closures before driving across.
Expect a toll stop when crossing the bridge.
View both arches together from the shoreline below.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on concrete.
Cross early on summer weekends to avoid long queues.
