The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The sand under your boots is soft and pale, and it shifts with every step. Around you, Scots pines rise straight and tall, their bark glowing copper where the light touches it. The air smells of resin and dry needles. Somewhere a woodpecker taps, then stops. It is hard to believe, but the edge of Warsaw, a capital city of nearly two million people, lies only a short drive behind you. Welcome to Kampinos National Park.

This forest is called Puszcza Kampinoska in Polish. The word puszcza means a vast, old, wild woodland, the kind that once covered much of Central Europe. The forest takes its name from the village of Kampinos on its western side. Today the park covers close to four hundred square kilometres of pine woods, wet meadows, marshes and sand. It was declared a national park in 1959, and it stands as one of the largest protected areas in Poland.

Here is the first surprise. The ground you are walking on is a field of dunes. These are not beach dunes. They are inland dunes, shaped by wind at the end of the last Ice Age, when the land here was cold, open and dry. Some rise many metres high, and many form long curved shapes, like horseshoes pointing into the wind. Scientists consider them among the best-preserved inland dune systems in Europe. Pines now hold the sand in place.

The landscape follows a simple, striking pattern. Long belts of dry dunes run roughly east to west, parallel to the old valley of the Vistula River. Between them lie belts of low, wet ground — marshes, alder swamps and damp meadows. Walk across the park from north to south, and you move from sand to bog to sand again. In spring the low belts fill with water and frog calls. In summer the dune ridges grow hot and still.

This pattern of dry and wet ground gives the park its wide variety of life. Thousands of plant and animal species have been recorded here. Cranes nest in the marshes, and their trumpet calls carry far across the open meadows. Black storks, shy relatives of the familiar white stork, hunt quietly in the forest streams. In the wetter corners, beavers build dams and fell trees. Look for gnawed stumps with pointed, pencil-like tips near the water.

The animal that most visitors hope to see is the elk, known in North America as the moose. Elk had disappeared from this area, and in 1951 a small group was brought back. The animals settled well. Today the elk is the symbol of the park, and you will see its outline on signs and boards. Beavers also returned with human help, and later the lynx, a wild forest cat, was released here too.

A practical note about the elk. They are large, heavy animals, and they are most active at dawn and dusk. If you meet one, stay calm, keep your distance and let it move away. Never step between a mother and her calf. You are far more likely to find their tracks — long, split hoofprints pressed into wet sand — than the animals themselves. Patience and quiet feet help a great deal.

For centuries this forest belonged to the rulers of Poland and Masovia. It served as a royal hunting ground, and small villages grew up in its clearings. People cut timber, gathered resin, grazed animals and farmed the poor sandy soil. Some of those villages still exist inside or along the park borders. Others faded away, and the forest slowly took back their fields. In places you can still find old fruit trees or lines of shrubs that once marked a farm.

The forest also carries a heavy layer of war history. Its dense cover has sheltered fighters many times. Insurgents moved through it during the January Uprising of 1863, a revolt against Russian rule. In September 1939, Polish soldiers fought their way through the forest toward besieged Warsaw after the Battle of the Bzura. In 1944, during the Warsaw Uprising, a Home Army unit known as the Kampinos Group operated from these woods and sent help toward the city.

The darkest chapter is at Palmiry, on the park's edge. Between 1939 and 1941, German occupation forces brought people from Warsaw here in secret and shot them in the forest. The victims included politicians, writers, and athletes, among them the Olympic champion runner Janusz Kusociński. After the war, the graves were found and a cemetery was created. Rows of simple crosses and markers stand among the pines today, beside a museum. Visitors usually lower their voices here.

The idea of protecting this forest began long before 1959. Polish naturalists, and especially the botanist Roman Kobendza, studied the woods in the early twentieth century and argued that they deserved protection. Their work was interrupted by war, but it laid the foundation for the national park. Later, in 2000, UNESCO recognised the forest and its surroundings as a biosphere reserve — an area where nature protection and human life are meant to exist side by side.

That balance is visible everywhere. Some parts of the park are strictly protected, and people may not enter them at all. Elsewhere, hundreds of kilometres of marked walking and cycling trails cross the forest. Colour-coded stripes on tree trunks show the routes. On weekends, Warsaw families arrive by car, bike and bus. On weekday mornings, you may walk for an hour and hear only wind in the pines and the crunch of your own steps.

The seasons change the park completely. In spring, the marshes fill with water and birdsong, and the young birch leaves shine bright green. Summer brings heat on the dunes, the smell of warm resin, and plenty of mosquitoes in the wet belts. Autumn turns the birches gold and covers the forest floor with mushrooms. In winter, snow on the dunes makes animal tracks easy to read, and the forest becomes very quiet.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Feel the sand under your feet — sand that wind carried here at the end of the Ice Age. Look up at the pines, and then down at the low, damp ground between the ridges. Think of the hunters, farmers, soldiers and naturalists who passed this way. As you walk on, watch the path ahead. A fresh hoofprint, a gnawed stump, a crane calling overhead... the forest is still writing its own story.

Practical tips

Come at dawn or dusk for the best wildlife chances.
Bring insect repellent for the wet marsh belts.
Follow coloured trail stripes painted on tree trunks.
Keep a wide distance from any elk you meet.
Visit on weekday mornings for quieter trails.
