The guide

The story

Listen first. Reeds rustle at the edge of the lake. A wooden gate creaks above you, and underfoot the path turns from earth to timber. Logs lie side by side, worn smooth by visitors' boots. The air smells of wet wood, lake water, and sometimes woodsmoke from a hearth. You are standing at the entrance to a town that people first built nearly three thousand years ago.

This is Biskupin, in the gentle lake country of central Poland, not far from the town of Żnin. What you see around you is a reconstruction. But it stands on the site of a real fortified settlement from the early Iron Age. People lived here on a small peninsula in the lake around the eighth century before Christ. That is older than the founding of Rome, according to the traditional date.

The story of its rediscovery is surprisingly modest. In the early 1930s, the level of the lake dropped. A local schoolteacher, Walenty Szwajcer, noticed rows of old wooden stakes rising out of the shallow water. He thought they were unusual and reported them. That small act of curiosity led to one of the most important archaeological excavations in Central Europe.

Archaeologists from Poznań, led by Professor Józef Kostrzewski, began digging in the mid-1930s. Soon they understood the scale of what they had found. This was not a scattered farmstead. It was a planned town, with streets, rows of houses, and a defensive wall. Excavations here were also pioneering in method. The team even used aerial photographs taken from a balloon to map the site from above.

Why did so much survive? The answer is water. The settlement sat in wet, peaty ground, and the lake level later rose. Oxygen could not reach the timber, so wood that would normally rot away was preserved for millennia. Here is a surprise most visitors miss. Because the original logs survived, scientists could count their tree rings. That method, called dendrochronology, dates the felling of the trees to a short window in the eighth century before Christ.

That precise dating shows something remarkable. The town was not built slowly over generations. It went up in a short burst of organised work. Someone planned the layout in advance and coordinated a large community to fell timber, haul it, and raise the walls. For a society without writing, that level of planning is striking. It tells us about leadership, cooperation, and shared purpose.

The people who built Biskupin belonged to what archaeologists call the Lusatian culture. That name describes a group of related communities across Central Europe in the Bronze and early Iron Ages. They did not leave us their own name for themselves. We know them through their pottery, their tools, their burial customs, and places like this one. They farmed, kept animals, fished in the lake, and worked bronze and iron.

Now look at the layout. Inside the walls, the houses stood in neat parallel rows, roughly a hundred of them by most estimates. Each row faced the same direction, and log-paved streets ran between them. A ring road followed the inside of the defensive wall. The houses were similar in size and plan. Many researchers read this as a sign of a fairly equal community, though that interpretation is still discussed.

Step inside one of the reconstructed houses if you can. The space is dim and low. There is usually a front area and a larger main room with a stone hearth. Families likely shared this room for cooking, working, and sleeping. Notice how close everything feels. Smoke from the fire would have filled the roof space and slowly escaped. Life here was warm, crowded, and shared with neighbours just a few steps away.

The defences were impressive. The wall was built from timber boxes filled with earth and sand, forming a thick rampart. Outside it, in the water, thousands of wooden stakes were driven into the lakebed at a slant. They worked as a breakwater, protecting the shore from ice and waves. They may also have slowed any attackers coming by boat. Those were the stakes Szwajcer first saw sticking out of the lake.

Biskupin also carries a darker modern history. In the 1930s, some scholars claimed the settlement was early Slavic, and the idea became a source of national pride. During the Second World War, German occupiers took over the site, and an SS research organisation carried out its own digging here, pushing a German interpretation instead. Both claims served politics more than evidence. Today, archaeologists reject linking the Lusatian culture to any single modern nation.

After the war, Polish archaeologists returned, and excavation and research continued for decades. Today the site is an open-air archaeological museum with reconstructed gates, ramparts, and houses. Every year, usually in September, it hosts a large archaeological festival. Re-enactors demonstrate ancient crafts like weaving, pottery, and metalwork. Here is another surprise: you can arrive the slow way, by a historic narrow-gauge railway that runs through the countryside from Żnin.

Before you leave, walk back toward the lakeshore. Look at the water and the reeds, and picture the stakes standing in long rows beneath the surface. Then turn and look at the timber gate. The wood in front of you is new, but the plan is ancient. A teacher noticed something odd in the water, and because of that, you are standing inside a town that waited nearly three thousand years to be seen again.

Bring insect repellent in summer near the reedy lakeshore.
