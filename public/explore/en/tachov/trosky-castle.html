The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the wind has nothing to stop it. It moves across the open ground and pushes against two dark towers of rock. Under your feet, the path is rough and uneven. It is part stone, part packed earth, worn smooth in places by many boots. Look up, and the silhouette above you looks unlikely. It looks like two stone fingers raised against the sky.

This is Trosky Castle. In Czech, the word trosky means ruins or wreckage. The name fits. What you see today is a ruin, and it has been one for centuries. But it is no ordinary ruin. It stands on two separate peaks of volcanic rock, with the remains of a fortress stretched between them. From a distance, the profile is so distinctive that people recognise it from many kilometres away.

Start with the ground itself, because the story begins long before any castle. These two peaks are what remains of ancient volcanic activity. Molten rock rose and cooled underground, and it hardened into basalt. Basalt is a dark, dense, heavy stone. Over a very long time, the softer land around it wore away. The harder basalt stayed behind. What was once hidden underground now stands in the open air.

The two peaks carry two towers, and each tower has a nickname. Locals call them Baba and Panna. Baba means old woman, or grandmother. Panna means maiden, or virgin. The maiden is the taller and slimmer of the two. The old woman is lower and broader. As you walk around the site, try to tell them apart from different angles. The two shapes shift against each other as you move.

Now the people. Historians generally credit the founding of the castle to Čeněk of Vartenberk, a member of the Bohemian nobility, in the late fourteenth century. Bohemia was then a powerful kingdom at the heart of central Europe. Building on top of two volcanic peaks was a bold choice. The rock gave natural protection on almost every side. But every stone, every beam, and every bucket of water had to be hauled up a steep slope.

Think about what that meant in practice. There were no roads up here as we know them. Workers used their own muscle, along with carts and animals, and the hill was steep. The towers had to be built straight onto uneven rock. That is part of what makes this place surprising. The castle does not sit on the hill. It grows out of the hill, as if the builders simply continued the stone upward.

In the early fifteenth century, Bohemia went through the Hussite Wars. These wars followed the execution of the religious reformer Jan Hus, and they divided the country along lines of faith. The Hussites wanted to reform the church. Their opponents stayed loyal to Rome. Trosky came into the hands of lords on the Catholic side. The castle's position made it extremely hard to attack. According to the usual account, the Hussite forces never managed to take it by storm.

The later fifteenth century was a restless time in this part of Bohemia. Some accounts describe armed men operating from the castle, raiding the surrounding area in a period of weak central control. Treat the more colourful versions of these stories with some caution. They have been retold many times, and details tend to grow with each telling. What is certain is that a fortress this hard to reach was valuable to whoever held it.

The castle's life as a working fortress ended in the seventeenth century, during the Thirty Years' War. That long conflict swept across much of central Europe and left many castles damaged or abandoned. Trosky was among them. After that, nobody rebuilt it as a home or a stronghold. Wind, rain, and frost did the rest. The fortress slowly became the ruin whose name it now carries.

Here is a twist that many visitors miss. Ruin was not the end of Trosky's story. It was a new beginning. In the nineteenth century, the Romantic movement swept through European art and literature. Romantic artists loved wild landscapes, broken towers, and the feeling of lost ages. Trosky fitted that taste perfectly. Painters and writers came here, and the ruin became a symbol of the Czech landscape. A broken castle became more famous than many intact ones.

So when you look at these towers, you are seeing two things at once. You see a medieval fortress, built for defence and control. You also see a picture that later generations chose to admire. Much of the care given to the site in modern times has been about keeping it safe to visit while preserving that ruined character. Nobody wants it rebuilt. The broken outline is the point.

Take a moment with the view if the weather is clear. From the castle grounds, the land opens out on every side. You can see farmland, woods, villages, and gentle hills. On a hazy day, the distance turns soft and blue. This is also why the castle was built here. Whoever stood on these rocks could watch the roads and valleys for a long way. The same view that pleases you today once served as an early warning system.

As you begin to leave, turn around one more time. Look back at the two towers. Notice how the dark basalt changes colour with the light. In sun it looks grey and warm, and under cloud it looks almost black. Listen again to the wind moving over the hilltop. The maiden and the old woman have stood here together for centuries. They will still be standing when you reach the bottom of the path.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, uneven paths.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed hilltop.
Walk around the base to see the towers realign.
Come on a clear day for the widest views.
Photograph the profile from below before climbing up.
