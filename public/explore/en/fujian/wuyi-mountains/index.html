The guide

The story

Listen first. Bamboo poles knock against stone, water slides beneath a raft, and somewhere above, a bird calls from a cliff you cannot quite see. The air carries the smell of wet moss and, on warm days, a faint roasted sweetness from the tea fields. Under your feet, the ground is often red. Welcome to the Wuyi Mountains, in the northwest corner of Fujian province, in southeastern China.

That red colour is the first thing to understand. These peaks are made largely of red sandstone and conglomerate, a type of scenery geologists call Danxia landform. Over a very long time, rain, rivers and wind cut the rock into flat-topped towers, sheer walls and deep, narrow gorges. The result is a landscape that looks carved rather than grown. Many cliffs rise straight from the water, with streaks of black and grey running down their faces where rain has stained the stone.

In 1999, UNESCO listed Wuyi as a World Heritage Site. It was listed for both its natural and its cultural value, which is rarer than you might think. Only a small number of places on Earth carry that double recognition. The protected area covers scenic peaks, a large nature reserve to the north, and archaeological sites that go back to the earliest kingdoms of this region. So as you walk, you are moving through geology, forest, philosophy and tea, all at once.

The heart of the scenery is the Nine Bend River. It winds through the peaks for nearly ten kilometres, turning back on itself again and again. Most visitors travel it on bamboo rafts, which drift downstream from the Ninth Bend toward the First. Two boatmen stand at either end, steering with long poles. They push off the riverbed, and in the shallows you can hear the raft scrape gently over pebbles. Many boatmen also tell stories and jokes along the way, naming each peak as it slides past.

Look up from the raft, and you may notice dark gaps high in the cliffs. Some of these crevices once held wooden coffins, shaped a little like boats. Archaeologists link them to ancient peoples who lived here long before this area became part of a unified Chinese empire. They are thought to be thousands of years old. How people lifted heavy coffins into such places is still debated. Nobody has a single, settled answer — and that mystery is part of their pull.

One of the best-known peaks along the river is Jade Girl Peak, a smooth, rounded column of rock. Legend has it that a jade maiden came down from heaven, fell in love with a local man, and was turned to stone for it. Across the water stands another peak, said to be her lover. The river runs between them, keeping them apart forever. It is a folk tale, not history. But locals still point the two peaks out with real affection.

Now, the mountains are also famous for something you can taste. Wuyi is the home of rock tea, a family of dark, roasted oolong teas. The bushes grow in narrow valleys and on ledges between the cliffs. The rocky soil, the mist and the minerals in the stone all shape the flavour. Tea lovers describe it as having a deep, mineral finish, sometimes called rock rhyme. When you pass small tea houses here, you will often smell charcoal roasting.

The most celebrated of these teas is Da Hong Pao, which means Big Red Robe. A few old mother bushes grow on a cliff face, protected and much photographed. According to one popular tale, a scholar was cured by this tea, and later draped a red robe over the bushes in gratitude. Treat that as legend. What is true is that leaves from those original bushes are no longer picked. Today's Da Hong Pao comes from plants propagated from them.

Here is something many visitors miss. Black tea, as much of the world drinks it, has roots in these mountains too. Lapsang souchong, the smoky black tea dried over pine wood, comes from the Wuyi area. It was one of the earliest Chinese teas to travel to Europe in large amounts. So a cup of black tea in London or Amsterdam may share its story with this quiet valley.

The mountains also hold an important place in Chinese thought. In the twelfth century, the philosopher Zhu Xi lived and taught here. He shaped Neo-Confucianism, a school of thinking that joined classical Confucian ethics with deeper questions about nature and the mind. His ideas later became central to the imperial examinations for centuries. The academy he founded near the river attracted students, and the area became a quiet centre of learning. Ruins and restored halls still mark that time.

As you explore, watch the cliffs for writing. Over many centuries, scholars, officials and travellers carved inscriptions into the rock. Some are poems. Some are single large characters. Some record visits, or praise the view. The carvings are often painted red, so they stand out against the grey and brown stone. Even if you cannot read Chinese, you can see how many hands passed here and wanted to leave a mark.

If you climb, Heavenly Tour Peak gives one of the classic views over the river. The path is steep and stepped, with handrails cut close to the rock. From the top, the Nine Bend River curls below like a ribbon, and on misty mornings the lower peaks rise out of white cloud. Further north lies a large nature reserve, home to rich forests and many rare plants and animals. It protects one of the most important wild areas in southeastern China.

Before you leave, pause for a moment where you are standing. Notice the colour of the rock near you, the sound of the water, and how the light changes as clouds move across the peaks. Perhaps you can smell tea again, drifting from a roasting room. These mountains have been home to ancient kingdoms, philosophers, boatmen and tea farmers. Now, for a little while, they are home to you too. Walk on slowly — there is always another bend.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the bamboo raft downstream for the classic river views.
Climb Heavenly Tour Peak early to catch morning mist.
Wear shoes with good grip for steep, damp stone steps.
Book rafts ahead in busy holiday periods.
Visit a local tea house to taste roasted rock tea.
