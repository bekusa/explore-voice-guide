The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the wall. The stone is pale and rough, warm if the sun has been on it, cool and slightly damp if it has not. Somewhere behind you, water moves against the quay. Rigging taps against masts. A gull calls from the top of a tower. You are standing at the western tip of Trogir, at the edge of a small island town, beside the fortress that once guarded it. This is Kamerlengo Castle.

Before we go inside, look at where you are. Trogir sits on a tiny island, squeezed between the Croatian mainland and the larger island of Čiovo. Narrow channels of sea surround the old town on every side. Bridges link it to land. That position made Trogir easy to defend, but also worth fighting over. Whoever held this corner of the island could watch every boat coming in from the west... and decide what happened next.

The town is very old. Greek colonists settled here in the centuries before Christ, and they called it Tragurion. Romans, Byzantines, Croatian kings and Hungarian rulers all passed through in turn. But the castle in front of you belongs to one particular chapter. In the early fifteenth century, after a long struggle along the Adriatic coast, Trogir came under the rule of Venice. The Venetians stayed for nearly four hundred years.

Venice did not trust the towns it took. It wanted a strong point that it controlled directly, a place for its garrison and its officials. So, during the fifteenth century, the Venetians built this fortress. They did not always start from nothing. Sources describe an older tower already standing on this spot, and the new walls were built around and onto earlier work. As you look along the stonework, you may notice that not every section matches perfectly.

Now, the name. It sounds romantic, but it comes from paperwork. A camerlengo was a Venetian official — roughly, a chamberlain or treasurer. He handled the money, the taxes and the accounts of the town. This castle became his seat. So the fortress is named not after a hero or a saint, but after the man who kept the books. That tells you a great deal about how Venice ran its empire.

Walk through the gate and into the courtyard. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Inside, the castle feels much more open than its outer walls suggest. The courtyard is a wide, enclosed space of stone and sky. There are no grand halls to explore, no painted rooms. What survives is the shell — the walls, the towers, the walkways. And that shell is strong enough to hold a whole small community of soldiers.

Look at the shape of the walls. The fortress is not a neat square. Its sides follow the edge of the island, and towers stand at the corners where attackers would come closest. The tallest tower rises above the rest. From up there, a lookout could see the channel, the mainland coast and the approach from the open sea. Defence here was about distance. The earlier you saw a sail, the more time you had.

If the stairs are open, climb the main tower. The steps are steep and worn smooth in places, so take your time. At the top, the town opens below you. You see red tile roofs packed tightly together, and the bell tower of the Cathedral of Saint Lawrence rising above them. Across the water lies Čiovo, with its houses climbing the hillside. Below you, boats line the waterfront promenade that locals call the Riva.

From the walls, look east along the open ground beside the castle. At the far end stands a second, smaller fortification — the Tower of Saint Mark. Its name is a clear signal. Saint Mark was the patron saint of Venice, and his winged lion was the symbol of Venetian power. The two structures worked together. Between them, the Venetians could cover this whole western edge of the island with their guns and their soldiers.

Here is a second detail that many people walk past. The open ground between the castle and Saint Mark's tower is now a sports field, where local teams play. Near it stands a small classical pavilion with columns. Locals link it to the early nineteenth century, when Napoleon's France briefly ruled this coast after the fall of Venice in 1797. A French marshal named Marmont is remembered for it. So in one short walk you pass Venetian walls, a French pavilion and a modern football pitch.

After the soldiers left, the castle slowly found a new purpose. Today, on summer evenings, the courtyard becomes an open-air stage. Concerts, theatre and film screenings take place inside these walls. Imagine the scene. The sky turns dark blue above the battlements. Swallows move over the towers. Music fills a space that was built for guards and cannons. The same walls that once kept people out now hold an audience in.

It also helps to see the castle as part of something larger. The whole historic core of Trogir is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. The reason is unusual. Here, a medieval street plan has survived almost complete, with buildings from many different centuries standing side by side. Romanesque churches — built in the heavy, round-arched style of the early Middle Ages — sit near Renaissance palaces. The castle is the town's armour, at its outer edge.

Before you leave, come back down to the waterfront and turn around. Look at the castle from outside again. Notice how low and solid it sits, how its pale stone matches the stone of the town behind it. Notice the light on the walls, and how the colour changes as clouds pass. Listen to the water against the quay, the voices on the Riva, the boats arriving. Five centuries ago, someone stood on that tower and watched this same channel. Now, for a moment, you are watching it too.
