The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, far below, a river moves through the bottom of a canyon so deep that its sound arrives only as a faint, steady hush. The air up here is thin and cool. It smells of wet grass and stone. Somewhere above, a large bird may be turning slow circles on the wind. You have arrived at Choquequirao, and you have earned every step of it.

This is an Inca site in the Vilcabamba mountains of southern Peru, in the Cusco region. It sits on a ridge high above the Apurímac River, a little over three thousand metres above sea level. The name comes from Quechua, the language of the Inca and still spoken across these mountains today. It is usually translated as cradle of gold. No one is certain how the Inca themselves named it.

People often call Choquequirao the sister of Machu Picchu. The comparison makes sense. Both are stone cities on mountain ridges, both are surrounded by cloud and cliff, and both use the same fine Inca building traditions. But there is one large difference, and you feel it right now. There is no train here and no road. Almost everyone who stands where you stand has walked for days to reach it.

The most common route starts in the small village of Cachora, on the Apurímac side of the canyon. From there, the trail drops more than a thousand metres to the river, crosses it, and then climbs straight back up the other side. Many hikers take two days to arrive and two days to return. That effort is the reason this place feels so quiet. On many days, you may share these terraces with only a handful of other people.

So who built it? Most historians link Choquequirao to the fifteenth century, the period when the Inca state expanded rapidly out of Cusco. It is often associated with the ruler Pachacuti or with his son, Topa Inca Yupanqui. Scholars still debate the exact patron. What is clear is the purpose of the design. This was not a simple farming village. It combined ceremony, administration, and a commanding view over the valley.

Take a moment to look at the walls closest to you. The stones here are mostly rough field stone, set in mud mortar, rather than the perfectly fitted granite blocks you may have seen in Cusco. That is not a sign of lower status. Builders used what the mountain offered. Look at the doorways and niches, though. Many of them have the trapezoid shape, wider at the base than at the top, that marks Inca architecture across the Andes.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. Only part of Choquequirao has been cleared. Archaeologists believe that a large share of the site still lies under forest and soil on the surrounding slopes. As you walk, you may notice low lines of stone vanishing into the undergrowth. Each one could be a wall, a terrace, or a building that no one has studied yet. The city is larger than what you can see.

Now, the surprise that makes this place unlike any other. On one steep slope, a series of terraces carries pictures made of stone. Pale, almost white rocks are set into the darker walls to form the shapes of llamas, walking in a line across the hillside. There are many of them. These llama figures were uncovered by excavation work in the early 2000s. Nothing quite like them is known from any other Inca site.

Why llamas? For the Inca, llamas were far more than animals. They carried goods along mountain roads. They gave wool for cloth and meat for feasts. They also played a role in religious offerings. Placing a herd of stone llamas on the mountainside may have honoured that relationship. It may also have marked a sacred route. Researchers offer several explanations, and the true meaning remains uncertain.

Turn your attention to the water. The Inca were skilled hydraulic engineers, and Choquequirao shows it. Stone channels bring water down from higher ground and carry it through the settlement, into fountains and across the terraces. Some of these channels still guide water after heavy rain. Follow one with your eyes. You will see how carefully the builders measured the slope so the water would run steadily, without flooding and without drying out.

Choquequirao also belongs to a later and sadder chapter. After the Spanish arrived in the 1530s, members of the Inca royal family retreated into the Vilcabamba mountains. They held out there for decades as an independent state. Choquequirao is usually connected to this region of resistance. The explorer Hiram Bingham visited the site in 1909, two years before he reached Machu Picchu. Even then, Choquequirao was already known to local people and to earlier travellers.

For many years, there has been talk of building a cable car across the canyon to bring more visitors here. Plans have been announced, delayed, and discussed again. For now, the trail remains the way in. That means the experience you are having is fragile. Walk only on marked paths. Do not climb on the walls or the terraces. The mud mortar between these stones is softer than it looks, and every step on a wall wears it down.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot near the main plaza and look outward. The ridges fold away in every direction, green and grey, and the river is just a thin silver line far below. If you are lucky, an Andean condor will glide past, almost without moving its wings. Breathe the cool air. Feel the uneven stone under your boots. Then, as you start the long walk down, look back once... and watch the city disappear again into the mountain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan at least four days for the round-trip trek.
Start the canyon descent early to avoid the midday heat.
Carry plenty of water for the long climb from the river.
Bring layers because the ridge turns cold after sunset.
Stay on marked paths and never climb the stone walls.
