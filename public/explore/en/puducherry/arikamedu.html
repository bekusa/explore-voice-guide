The guide

The story

Listen first. A breeze moves through coconut palms. Somewhere near the water, a fishing boat's engine coughs and fades. The ground underfoot is soft and sandy, scattered with dry fronds. Nothing here looks like a port. And yet this quiet bend of river, a short ride south of Puducherry's French Quarter, is one of the most important archaeological sites on the whole coast of India. You are standing where people once traded with Rome.

This is Arikamedu. The site sits on the bank of the Ariyankuppam River, close to where it meets the Bay of Bengal. Today the river is calm and brown, lined with mangroves and small boats. Two thousand years ago, it carried cargo. Merchants unloaded jars of wine and oil here. They packed beads, cloth and perhaps spices for the long journey west. The river was the reason this place existed at all.

For centuries, nobody remembered. Local people found old beads and pottery fragments in the soil. Children picked them up after the rains. In the early twentieth century, French scholars in Pondicherry began to take an interest. They noticed that some of the pottery did not look Indian at all. It looked Mediterranean. That small observation started one of the great detective stories of Indian archaeology.

In 1945, the British archaeologist Sir Mortimer Wheeler came to dig. He was then Director General of the Archaeological Survey of India. Wheeler found a fine, glossy red pottery known as Arretine ware. This tableware was made in and around Arezzo, a town in central Italy. Here was physical proof, buried in a riverbank in Tamil country, that Roman goods had travelled all the way to the eastern coast of India.

That is the first surprise for many visitors. We often imagine Roman trade with India happening only on the western coast, in places like Kerala. But Arikamedu shows that the network reached right around the tip of the subcontinent. Ships, or goods passed from ship to ship, came this far east. Some scholars link the site with a port called Poduke, named in ancient Greek sailing texts. That identification is widely discussed, though not certain.

Wheeler's team uncovered more than pottery. They found the remains of a long brick structure near the river, which he read as a warehouse. They found tanks and drains that may have been used for dyeing cloth. They also found pieces of amphorae. These were tall clay jars that Romans used to carry wine, olive oil and fish sauce. Imagine those smells drifting through the heat of a Tamil afternoon.

Later excavations changed the picture. French archaeologist Jean-Marie Casal dug here in the late 1940s. Then, from 1989 into the early 1990s, an American team led by Vimala Begley returned with newer methods. Their work suggested that Arikamedu was active long before the Romans arrived, and long after. It was not simply a Roman trading post. It was a local town, run by local people, that welcomed foreign traders.

This matters. Older histories sometimes told the story as if Rome came and built something here. The evidence points the other way. Tamil potters, bead-makers and merchants were already working on this riverbank. Indian rouletted ware, a fine grey or black pottery decorated with tiny repeated marks, appears in large numbers. The Mediterranean goods arrived into an existing world. Arikamedu was a meeting place, not a colony.

Here is the second surprise. Arikamedu was a major centre for making beads. Workshops produced glass beads in many colours, and beads of semi-precious stone such as carnelian and agate. Beads of this kind have been found at sites across the Indian Ocean world, and scholars connect many of them to this coast. Look closely at the sandy soil after rain. Visitors still sometimes spot tiny fragments of glass or pottery. Please leave them exactly where they lie.

As you walk, you will see a ruined building with thick walls and empty window openings. This is not Roman. It is much later, from the colonial era, and is generally associated with a French Jesuit mission. Vines grow over the brick. Light falls through the broken roof. It is a reminder that this riverbank has been chosen again and again, by different people, for different reasons, across many centuries.

Be honest with yourself about what you will see. Most of the ancient town lies under the ground, or was washed away by the shifting river. The excavation trenches have long been filled in. What remains is a protected landscape of low mounds, palms, scrub and water. The real treasures, the Arretine sherds, the amphora pieces and the beads, are kept in museum collections, including the Puducherry Museum in the old town.

So use your imagination here. Picture the river busy with small boats moving between the shore and larger ships waiting offshore. Picture potters at their wheels and bead-makers at their furnaces. Picture traders speaking Tamil, Greek and perhaps other languages, bargaining under this same sky. The monsoon winds that once carried their ships still blow across this coast every year. That rhythm has not changed.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the river for a moment. Watch the water move slowly toward the sea. Listen to the palms, the birds, perhaps a distant motorbike on the village road. It is easy to walk past this place and see only an overgrown field. Now you know what lies beneath it. As you head back toward the town, every bead in a market stall may look a little different.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Puducherry Museum to see excavated finds.
Come early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Wear closed shoes for sandy, uneven, overgrown ground.
Carry water and insect repellent near the riverbank.
Leave any pottery or bead fragments where you find them.
