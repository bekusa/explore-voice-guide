The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the foot of the hill and look up. The light hits the central dome first. On a clear day it does not look painted or polished. It looks lit from inside, like warm metal held near a flame. Around you, the lawns slope gently downward toward the river and the city. Above you, five domes rise against the sky. Four of them are small, dark, and quiet, and one is very bright. This is the Iowa State Capitol, and it was built to be seen from far away.

Start with the number five. Most American capitols carry a single dome, a design that echoes the United States Capitol in Washington. Iowa chose something different. One large central dome rises over the building, and a smaller dome sits at each corner. This makes it the only state capitol in the country with five domes. From a distance the arrangement looks almost like a crown. Up close, the four small domes frame the large one, so your eye keeps returning to the centre.

The gold is real. The central dome is covered in thin sheets of gold leaf, a metal hammered so fine that a breath of wind can lift it. Workers lay it by hand, piece by piece, over the curved surface. Weather slowly wears the gold away, so the dome has been regilded more than once since the building opened. Each time, the work takes many months on scaffolding high above the city. That shine you see today is the result of careful, repeated labour, not a single gesture made long ago.

The building took a long time to finish. Construction began in the early 1870s, and the capitol was not fully completed until the mid-1880s. That is roughly fifteen years of quarrying, carving, and debate. Iowa was still a young state then, only a few decades removed from frontier settlement. Lawmakers wanted a statehouse that announced permanence. They wanted visitors from the East to arrive and understand that Iowa was not temporary. It was here to stay, and it intended to govern itself with dignity.

The design is credited mainly to two architects, John Cochrane and Alfred Piquenard. They had already worked together on the Illinois capitol, so they brought experience with large public buildings. Piquenard died before the work was complete, and others carried the project forward. The style draws on Renaissance and classical ideas. That means columns, symmetry, carved stone, and a strong central axis, all borrowed from the grand buildings of Europe. On the Iowa prairie, those borrowed forms became a statement of ambition.

Now step inside, and notice the change in sound. The noise of the street fades. Footsteps ring on hard stone floors. Voices rise and soften under high ceilings. The interior uses many kinds of marble, more than two dozen varieties by most accounts, gathered from quarries in America and abroad. Look at how the colours change from wall to column to floor. Some stone is creamy, some is streaked with grey, and some is deep red or green. Together the colours turn the halls into a quiet catalogue of stone.

Walk toward the centre and look straight up into the rotunda. This is the inside of the great dome. The space pulls your gaze upward through rings of decoration, painted detail, and light. Many visitors spend a long minute here with their heads tilted back, saying nothing. It is worth doing. From below, the dome feels almost endless. From outside, it looked solid and bright. From inside, it feels open, like a sky built by hand.

Near the grand staircase you will find a large mural called Westward, painted by the American artist Edwin Blashfield. It shows pioneers moving across the land with a covered wagon, surrounded by allegorical figures. Those figures are symbols rather than real people, representing ideas like progress and civilization. The painting reflects how many Americans of that era told the story of the West. Today, visitors often look at it with fresh questions about whose land was being settled, and whose stories the mural leaves out. Both readings are worth holding at once.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Tucked inside the building is the law library, and it may be the most beautiful room in the capitol. Several levels of balconies rise around a tall open space. Decorative iron railings wrap each level, and spiral staircases climb in the corners. Rows of old leather-bound books line the shelves. The room looks less like a government office and more like a scene from an old novel. Stand at the bottom, look up through the tiers, and listen to how quiet it is.

Another surprise waits in the halls. A large, detailed model of the battleship USS Iowa is on display inside the capitol. The ship carried the state's name through several decades of naval history. The model is long, intricate, and full of tiny fittings, from gun turrets to deck rails. Children often press close to the glass to study it. Adults tend to do the same, a little more quietly. It is an unexpected sight in a legislative building, and it connects a landlocked state to the open ocean.

The building has also survived damage. In 1904 a fire broke out in the north wing, where the House of Representatives met. The flames and the water used to fight them harmed parts of the chamber. The state repaired and restored the damaged rooms, and the legislature continued its work. When you walk through the chambers today, you are seeing spaces shaped partly by that recovery. The capitol is not a frozen museum. It is a working building that has been mended, cleaned, and cared for over many generations.

And it still works. The Iowa legislature meets here, and the governor keeps an office in the building. During the legislative session, the halls fill with lawmakers, staff, reporters, and citizens who have come to speak about issues that matter to them. On quieter days, school groups and travellers move through the same corridors. Guided tours are offered, and some include a climb partway up into the dome. That climb is narrow and steep, but the view across Des Moines rewards the effort.

When you step back outside, walk a little way across the grounds and turn around. The hill gives the building its stage. To the west, the downtown skyline rises beyond the river. Below, the East Village spreads out with its shops, cafés, and older brick buildings. The capitol was placed on this rise on purpose, so that the seat of government would stand above the city it serves. From many streets in Des Moines, you can still catch that gold flash between buildings.

Before you leave, take one last look at the central dome. Notice how its colour shifts as clouds move, from pale gold to deep amber and back again. Listen to the wind across the lawns, the traffic below, and perhaps a few voices on the steps. Generations of Iowans have climbed these same steps to argue, to celebrate, and simply to visit. Now you have climbed them too. As you walk away, glance back once more. The gold will still be catching the light.
