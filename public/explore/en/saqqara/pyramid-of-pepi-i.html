The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, south of the busier parts of Saqqara, the loudest sound is often the wind moving sand across the ground. Your shoes press into a loose crust of grit and broken limestone. Ahead of you rises a low, rounded mound of rubble, pale in the sun. It does not look like a pyramid from a postcard. But this worn heap was once the tomb of a king, and it carries one of the most important names in Egyptian history.

This is the Pyramid of Pepi I. Pepi was a king of the Sixth Dynasty, near the end of the period historians call the Old Kingdom. That was the age of the great pyramid builders, centuries after Djoser's Step Pyramid and the giants at Giza. By Pepi's time, royal pyramids were smaller and built faster. Their cores were often made of rougher stone, with fine limestone only on the outside. That choice explains much of what you see today.

Here is the first surprise. Egyptian kings gave their pyramid complexes names. Pepi's was called Men-nefer-Pepi, which roughly means that the perfection of Pepi is enduring. Over time, the name Men-nefer spread beyond the tomb. It came to mean the nearby royal city itself. When Greek speakers arrived many centuries later, they said it as Memphis. So the name of Egypt's ancient capital may trace back to this modest pile of stone.

Why does the pyramid look so broken? The answer is simple and a little sad. The fine outer casing was cut from good white limestone. In later centuries, people treated these monuments as quarries. Stone was carried away for new buildings, and some was burned to make lime. What remains is mostly the rough inner core. The perfect shape has gone, but the structure underneath has stayed.

The real treasure of this monument was never on the outside. It was hidden underground, in the corridors and chambers beneath the mound. Their walls were carved with long columns of hieroglyphs. Scholars call these the Pyramid Texts. They are spells and prayers meant to help the dead king rise, join the gods, and travel among the stars. They are among the oldest large collections of religious writing anywhere in the world.

Pepi was not the first king to use these texts. They first appear a little earlier, in the pyramid of a king named Unas, also here at Saqqara. But Pepi's chambers held a large and important version. When explorers entered the tomb in the late nineteenth century, around 1880, they found these carved walls. The discovery helped show that pyramids were not silent tombs. They were, in a sense, written machines for eternity.

The second surprise is about what happened after that discovery. Many of the inscribed wall blocks had cracked and fallen. Thousands of fragments lay scattered in the chambers. Over many decades, a French archaeological mission worked here with great patience. Team members studied the broken pieces and matched them, one by one, like a giant puzzle with no picture on the box. Slowly, long passages of text returned to their places on paper and, in part, on the walls.

That same French mission also changed how we understand the ground around you. Their excavations uncovered a group of smaller pyramids nearby. These belonged to royal women, queens of Pepi's household. Some of their burial chambers also carried inscriptions. Together, the king's pyramid and the queens' tombs form a small royal cemetery. So as you look around, remember that the low bumps in the desert may also be tombs.

Take a moment to picture the full complex as it once stood. A pyramid was never alone. On its eastern side stood a mortuary temple, where priests made offerings for the dead king. A causeway led down toward a valley temple closer to the green fields. Walls enclosed the sacred area. Today you see only traces of all this. But the layout followed a plan that Egyptian builders had refined over generations.

Now look at the stone itself. Pick out the difference between the rough blocks of the core and any smoother, whiter pieces lying nearby. The smooth pieces show the kind of finish the pyramid once had. Notice too the colour of the light. In the middle of the day it flattens everything into white. Early or late, the rubble turns golden, and the shadows give the mound back some of its shape.

Turn slowly and look at the horizon. Saqqara is a vast burial ground, used for thousands of years. To the north, on a clear day, you may pick out the stepped outline of Djoser's pyramid. Other pyramids of the Old Kingdom stand scattered across this plateau, some sharp, some ruined like this one. Beyond the edge of the desert, the green of the Nile valley begins. The ancient capital lay down there, close to the river.

It is worth thinking about the gap between ambition and survival. Pepi's builders called his pyramid enduring. The stone casing did not endure. The shape did not endure. And yet the words survived, cut into the walls below your feet. The name survived too, carried through the centuries as Memphis. In the end, the writing outlasted the building. That is a quiet lesson for any traveller who stands here.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Feel the heat rising from the ground and the grit under your shoes. Look once more at the mound, and imagine the white pyramid that once stood here, catching the morning sun. Then, as you walk away, glance back over your shoulder. From a little distance, the ruin softens into a simple, patient shape against the sky.
