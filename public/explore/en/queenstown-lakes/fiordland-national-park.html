The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the hum of the wind there is water, and it is everywhere. It drips from moss and slides down bare granite. It falls in white threads from cliffs that rise straight out of the sea. The air feels cool and damp on your skin, and it smells of wet leaves and stone. This is Fiordland. It is a place made by ice and kept alive by rain, and it is one of the wildest corners of Aotearoa New Zealand.

Fiordland National Park was created in 1952, and it is the largest national park in the country. It covers more than twelve thousand square kilometres of mountains, lakes, rainforest and sea inlets. Most of that land has no roads at all. Many people reach it from Queenstown, but the park itself lies to the southwest, in the Southland region. Te Anau is the small town at its edge, where most journeys into the park really begin.

In 1990, the park became part of a larger World Heritage Area called Te Wāhipounamu. The name is Māori, and it means the place of greenstone. Greenstone, or pounamu, is a hard green jade that is deeply valued in Māori culture. The World Heritage Area joins Fiordland with several other parks across the southwest of the South Island. Together they protect one of the least changed landscapes left in the temperate world.

Here is the first surprise. Milford Sound, Doubtful Sound and the other long inlets are called sounds, but that name is technically wrong. A sound is a valley flooded by the sea after a river cuts it. These inlets were carved by glaciers during the ice ages, so geologists call them fiords. The early European settlers used the wrong word, and it stayed. Look at the sides of the valleys. They are steep and smooth, shaped like the letter U. That shape is the mark of moving ice.

The Māori people of the south, Ngāi Tahu, have their own story of how this coast was made. According to their tradition, an ancestor named Tū Te Raki Whanoa shaped the fiords with his adze, a tool like a broad axe. He worked from the south to the north. His skill grew as he went, so the northern fiords are said to be the finest. Milford Sound, the most northern of them all, has the Māori name Piopiotahi.

Now think about the rain. Fiordland is one of the wettest places in New Zealand, and the Milford area receives several metres of rain each year. When a heavy storm passes, hundreds of temporary waterfalls appear on the cliffs within minutes. Many visitors hope for blue sky, but locals often say that a rainy day is the best day to come. The rock walls turn silver with falling water, and the sound of it fills the valleys.

That rain creates the second surprise, and it happens below the surface. Fresh rainwater runs off the forest and stains brown with tannins from the leaves. It floats on top of the heavier seawater and blocks much of the sunlight. In this dim water, creatures that normally live very deep in the ocean can grow close to the surface. Black coral is one of them. Divers can find it here at depths where it would be impossible almost anywhere else.

The forest itself is old and thick. Beech trees cling to rock with almost no soil, and their roots hold onto each other and onto layers of moss. Sometimes a whole slope of trees becomes too heavy after rain and slides into the water. These events are called tree avalanches. Over time, the bare rock is slowly covered by moss, then ferns, then small trees, and the long cycle starts again.

The birds of Fiordland have their own stories. In 1948, a doctor named Geoffrey Orbell found living takahē in the Murchison Mountains, west of Lake Te Anau. Takahē are large, blue and green flightless birds that many people believed were extinct. That discovery changed conservation in New Zealand. Today you may also meet the kea, a clever mountain parrot with olive feathers and bright orange under its wings. Keep a close eye on your bag, because kea are very curious.

European history here is short. Captain James Cook sailed along this coast in 1770. He saw the entrance to one inlet but did not sail in, because he was not sure the winds would let his ship come back out. He called it Doubtful Harbour, and today we know it as Doubtful Sound. On a later voyage, his crew spent several weeks in Dusky Sound, further south, repairing their ship and recording the plants and birds.

For much of the twentieth century, Milford Sound could only be reached on foot or by sea. The road changed that. Workers spent years cutting the Homer Tunnel through solid granite, often in snow and avalanche danger. It finally opened to traffic in the 1950s. Today the road from Te Anau to Milford passes through beech forest, open valleys and high mountain walls. Many travellers say the drive is as memorable as the destination.

Walkers know Fiordland for its great tracks. The Milford Track, the Kepler Track and part of the Routeburn Track all pass through the park. They cross alpine passes, follow rivers and wind through rainforest. One small creature joins every walker. Sandflies are tiny biting insects, and they love still, damp air. Māori legend says the goddess of death released them here, so that people would not stay too long in a place this beautiful.

So take a moment where you are. Notice how the light changes as clouds move across the peaks. Notice the cold, clean taste of the air and the steady sound of water somewhere close by. This land was shaped over thousands of years, and it is still changing with every storm. As you move on, look back once. The mountains will look a little different than they did when you arrived.

Brown tannin-stained water on the fiord surface.
Before you go

Practical tips

Base yourself in Te Anau to shorten trips into the park.
Pack strong insect repellent for sandflies near the water.
Bring a waterproof jacket even on clear mornings.
Check road conditions before driving to Milford Sound.
Book Great Walk huts well ahead of your trip.
