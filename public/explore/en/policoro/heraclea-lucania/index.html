The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the loudest sound is often the wind moving through dry grass and olive leaves. Underfoot, the ground is pale and dusty. Low walls of stone and fired brick run in straight lines through the field. They are only knee-high now. Yet these modest ridges once marked the streets and houses of a Greek city on the southern coast of Italy.

This is Heraclea, in the ancient region of Lucania, at the edge of modern Policoro. The Ionian Sea is only a short drive away. On a clear day you can feel the coastal plain open out around you. Two rivers frame this land. The Agri runs to the north and the Sinni to the south. In antiquity, people knew the Sinni as the Siris, and the whole plain carried that name.

The city was founded in the 430s before Christ. Its founders were Greeks from Taras, the powerful colony we now call Taranto. They named their new city after Herakles, the hero known to the Romans as Hercules. The name was a statement. Taras looked to Herakles as a patron, and the new city carried his name. Before Heraclea, an older Greek settlement called Siris stood somewhere in this area. Heraclea grew up in its shadow, on land that Greeks had fought over for generations.

Look at the layout around you. The streets do not wander. They cross at regular angles in a grid. Greek planners in southern Italy often divided a new city into long, even blocks, with houses packed side by side. Walk slowly along one of these lines and imagine doorways opening onto the street. Picture courtyards, cisterns for water, and small rooms for storage and sleep. The scale is human. These were family houses, not palaces.

The city had two faces. The upper area, on the higher ground, served as the acropolis — the fortified heart of a Greek city. Down the slopes stood sanctuaries to the gods. One of the most important was a sanctuary of Demeter, the goddess of grain and the harvest. Archaeologists recovered thousands of small offerings there. Many were clay figurines and little vases, left by worshippers asking for good crops, healthy children, or safe passage through life.

Here is something many visitors miss. Heraclea was also a town of craftsmen. Excavations found a district of kilns, where potters fired their wares. Some of the earliest red-figure vases made in this region came from local workshops. Red-figure means the figures stay the natural orange colour of the clay, while the background is painted black. Scholars named one early vase painter after this very place. They call him the Policoro Painter, because his best work turned up in a tomb here.

Now for the moment that made Heraclea famous far beyond southern Italy. In 280 BC, the plain near this city became a battlefield. On one side stood Pyrrhus, king of Epirus, a Greek kingdom across the Adriatic Sea. Taras had invited him to fight the rising power of Rome. On the other side stood a Roman army. Pyrrhus brought war elephants. Most Roman soldiers had never seen such animals before.

Pyrrhus won the battle. But his losses were heavy. Ancient writers describe how his victories cost him so many experienced men that he could not replace them. From Pyrrhus, we get a phrase still used today. A Pyrrhic victory is a win that costs so much it feels like defeat. So the next time you hear that phrase, you can think of this quiet plain, and of elephants moving through the dust.

Rome did not stay defeated. Within a few years, Heraclea made a treaty with Rome. The Roman orator Cicero later described this agreement as unusually favourable to the city. Heraclea kept a special status for a long time. That status matters in one surprising story. Cicero once defended a Greek poet named Archias in a Roman court. Part of his argument was simple. Archias had been enrolled as a citizen of Heraclea, and that gave him a claim to Roman citizenship.

The second great treasure from Heraclea is not here. In the eighteenth century, people found large bronze tablets in this area. They are known as the Tables of Heraclea. One side carries Greek text about the lands belonging to temples of Dionysus and Athena. The other side, added later, carries Latin text with Roman laws about how towns should be run. A single sheet of bronze holds two languages and two worlds. Today the tablets are in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples.

Much of what came out of this ground is closer, though. Next to the archaeological area stands the National Museum of the Siritide. It holds finds from Heraclea and from the wider plain. Here you can see the painted vases, the figurines from the sanctuary, and objects from local tombs. Many visitors walk the ruins first and the museum second. Try it the other way around. The objects in the cases help you picture the people who lived along these streets.

As Rome grew, Heraclea slowly faded. The coast changed. Rivers carried silt, the land shifted, and malaria made the low plain dangerous for long periods. Over centuries, the population moved and the stones were reused or buried. The modern town of Policoro grew up much later, around a castle on the nearby ridge. For a long time, this land was farmland. Only in the twentieth century did large-scale excavations bring the old city back into view.

Stand still once more and look toward the sea. The distance between you and the shore was a lifeline for the people of Heraclea. Ships brought wine, oil, pottery, and news from across the Greek world. Today the view is wider and quieter. Notice the colour of the stone, the faint lines of the streets, and the way the light falls flat across the plain at midday and turns warm near evening.

As you walk away, glance back at those low walls one last time. They do not look like much at first. But they once held a city of potters, farmers, priests, and soldiers. They watched elephants on the plain and lawyers argue over citizenship in Rome. Listen again for the wind in the grass. It is the same sound the people of Heraclea heard, on an ordinary afternoon, more than two thousand years ago.

Practical tips

Visit the Siritide museum first to picture the ruins.
Bring water and a hat; the site has little shade.
Come in late afternoon for softer, warmer light.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, dusty ground.
