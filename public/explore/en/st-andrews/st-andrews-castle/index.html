The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the broken walls, the North Sea moves against the rocks — a slow, steady wash, then a sharper slap as a wave hits the cliff. The wind here carries salt and the cry of gulls. Underfoot, the grass gives way to worn stone. Ahead of you stands a ruin of pale, weathered sandstone, open to the sky on one side and to the sea on the other. This is Saint Andrews Castle, and very little about its story has been quiet.

The castle stands on a rocky headland at the edge of the town, a short walk from the cathedral ruins. That closeness matters. For most of the Middle Ages, Saint Andrews was the religious centre of Scotland. The castle was not built for a king or a warlord. It was the home of the town's bishops, and later its archbishops — the most powerful churchmen in the country. It was a palace, a fortress, and a prison all at once.

The first castle here dates to around 1200, when a bishop of Saint Andrews chose this headland for his residence. The site made sense. The sea guarded the north and east. Steep rock made attack difficult from below. Only the landward side needed strong defences, and that is where you came in today. Over the next few centuries, the building was damaged, rebuilt, and changed many times. What you see now is a layered structure, with work from different periods standing side by side.

During the Wars of Scottish Independence, in the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the castle changed hands between Scottish and English forces more than once. It was captured, slighted, and repaired again. To slight a castle means to damage it on purpose, so an enemy cannot use it. Look at the stonework as you walk around, and you will notice that not all of it matches. Different colours, different sizes of block, different styles of masonry — these are the marks of repeated repair.

Now to the most dramatic chapter. In the 1540s, Scotland was divided by religion. The new Protestant ideas of the Reformation were spreading, and the Catholic Church pushed back hard. At the centre of that struggle stood Cardinal David Beaton, Archbishop of Saint Andrews. In 1546, he ordered the Protestant preacher George Wishart to be burned at the stake, just outside the castle walls. Tradition holds that Beaton watched the execution from a window of the castle.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. Outside the castle entrance, set into the road surface, are the initials G W. They mark the place associated with Wishart's burning. People often stand on them without noticing. Before you leave, take a moment to find them. It is a small mark on an ordinary street, but it points to the event that set off one of the most violent episodes in this castle's history.

Only a few months after Wishart died, a group of Protestant men entered the castle in disguise, early in the morning. They killed Cardinal Beaton inside his own residence. According to accounts from the time, they then displayed his body from the castle walls for the town to see. The attackers stayed inside and held the castle. Other supporters joined them. What began as a murder turned into a long siege.

That siege left the castle with something extraordinary. Government forces tried to break in by digging a mine — a tunnel cut through solid rock, meant to reach under the walls. The defenders inside heard the work and dug a countermine to intercept them. The two tunnels met underground. Today you can go down into these passages yourself. They are among the best surviving examples of medieval siege tunnels anywhere in Europe — a rare chance to stand inside a battle that was fought in the dark.

The passages are low, narrow, and damp. In places you will need to stoop, and in the countermine you may need to crouch or crawl. The rock walls still carry the marks of picks and hand tools. It is easy to imagine the sound of digging echoing through the stone, and the fear on both sides. One group could not see the other. They could only listen... and keep cutting.

One of the people who joined the garrison during the siege was a preacher named John Knox. He later became the leading figure of the Scottish Reformation. In 1547, a French fleet arrived and bombarded the castle from the sea and the land. The garrison surrendered. Knox and others were taken away, and Knox spent time as a prisoner rowing in the French galleys. So this damp headland played a part in shaping the man who later shaped Scotland's church.

Not far from the tunnels is the bottle dungeon, cut down into the rock beneath one of the towers. It takes its name from its shape — a narrow neck at the top that opens into a wider chamber below, like the inside of a bottle. Prisoners were lowered in through the opening. There was no door and no easy way out. Look down into it and think about how little light reached the bottom, and how loud the sea must have sounded.

After the siege, Archbishop John Hamilton rebuilt much of the damaged castle. The grand front you see on the entrance side comes largely from his work, and his coat of arms appears on the stonework. But the Reformation soon swept away the power of the archbishops. Without its purpose, the castle fell into decay. Over time, local people took its stone for other building work, and the sea slowly ate away at the cliff below.

Today the castle is cared for by Historic Environment Scotland, with a visitor centre where you can see carved stones and learn more about its past. Below the walls lies a small beach, often called Castle Sands. From here, you can follow the coast path toward the cathedral and the harbour. Walking that line, you are moving between the two buildings that once controlled the religious life of a whole nation.

Before you go, stand by the seaward wall for a moment. Feel the wind coming off the water. Look at how the stone has softened at its edges, rounded by centuries of salt and rain. Beneath your feet run the tunnels. Behind you, in the road, two quiet letters mark a burning. The castle is broken now, and open to the sky. But as you walk away, listen once more — the sea is still working at the rock, as it always has.
