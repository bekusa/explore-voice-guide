The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here in the countryside of eastern Navarre, the loudest sounds are often the wind in the trees and the crunch of gravel under your shoes. Ahead of you rises a cluster of honey-coloured stone towers, square and solid, with a drawbridge and narrow windows. It looks almost like a castle from a child's drawing. But this is a real fortress, and it holds one of the most remarkable stories in Spanish history. Welcome to the Castle of Xavier.

The name itself carries a story. Most scholars trace "Xavier" to the Basque language, where "etxeberri" means something like "new house". Basque, by the way, is the ancient language of this corner of Europe, unrelated to Spanish or any other language around it. Over the centuries, the word changed shape in many mouths. Today it lives on as Javier in Spanish, Xavier in English and French, and Saverio in Italian. Millions of people around the world carry this name. Many of them have no idea it comes from this small fortress.

That is because of one man who was born here in 1506. His name was Francisco de Jasso y Javier, and the world knows him as Saint Francis Xavier. He was the youngest son of a noble Navarrese family. His father served the kings of Navarre, which was then an independent kingdom squeezed between France and Castile. So the boy grew up inside these walls, in a family close to power. Very soon, however, that power would collapse around them.

In 1512, Castilian forces conquered Navarre. Xavier's family had supported the losing side, the old Navarrese royal house. And that loyalty had a price. A few years later, by order of the Castilian authorities, parts of this castle were deliberately torn down. The outer walls and defensive towers were destroyed so that the family could never use the fortress against the new rulers again. Imagine being a child, around ten years old, watching soldiers pull apart your home stone by stone.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Much of what you see today is not the original medieval castle. After the demolition, the fortress lay damaged for centuries. Then, in the late nineteenth century, a major restoration rebuilt large parts of it. At the same time, a church in a revival style was built right beside it — the basilica you can see attached to the castle. So you are looking at a mix: genuine old stone at the core, and careful reconstruction around it. Look at the masonry and see if you can spot the difference.

Now back to the boy. As a young man, Francis left Navarre to study at the University of Paris, one of the great centres of learning in Europe. There he shared lodgings with another Basque-speaking student, a former soldier named Ignatius of Loyola. Ignatius was older and intense. At first, Francis was not very interested in his religious ideas. But slowly, Ignatius won him over. Together with a small group of friends, they founded the Society of Jesus, the religious order better known as the Jesuits.

What happened next is almost hard to believe. In 1541, Francis sailed from Portugal toward Asia. He reached Goa, on the western coast of India, the following year. From there he travelled through southern India, to the Malay Peninsula, to the islands of what is now Indonesia, and then to Japan in 1549. He was among the first Europeans to bring Christianity to Japan. All of this was done by sailing ship, on foot, and by small boat, in an age without maps for much of that world.

He never came home. In 1552, Francis died on a small island off the coast of southern China, while waiting for a chance to enter the mainland. He was in his mid-forties. His body was eventually carried back to Goa, where it still lies in a church today. In 1622, the Catholic Church declared him a saint, on the same day as his old friend Ignatius. And a man from this quiet Navarrese valley became one of the best-known missionaries in history.

Inside the castle, the rooms take you through his life, from these walls to the shores of Asia. But the most remarkable space is a small chapel in one of the towers, often called the Tower of the Holy Christ. Here you will find a medieval wall painting known as the Dance of Death. It shows skeletons smiling and leading people away. This was a common theme in late medieval Europe. It reminded everyone, rich or poor, that death comes for all. Few visitors expect to find it here.

The chapel also holds a wooden crucifix with a famous legend attached to it. Locals say that in the year Francis died, far away in Asia, this figure of Christ began to sweat blood on Fridays. It is a tale of faith, not a documented historical event. But it shows how strongly the people of Navarre felt connected to the man born in this house. Stand quietly in the chapel for a moment. Notice how close the walls feel, and how little light enters.

That connection is still alive today. Saint Francis Xavier is the patron saint of Navarre. Every year, in early March, thousands of people walk here on a pilgrimage known as the Javierada. Many set out from Pamplona, the capital of Navarre, and walk for a day or more along country roads. Some walk for faith, some for family tradition, some simply for the challenge. If you come in that season, you will see this quiet place filled with tired, happy walkers.

On an ordinary day, though, the setting is peaceful. Look around at the landscape. Low hills, farm fields, and patches of woodland surround the castle. You are close to the border with the neighbouring region of Aragon. Not far from here are a large reservoir and an old monastery in the hills. In the Middle Ages, this was frontier country, where kingdoms met and sometimes fought. A castle here was not decoration. It was a guard post on a contested edge.

Walk slowly around the outside of the walls. Notice the thickness of the stone, the small, high windows, and the way the towers stand close together. Everything here was designed for defence, not comfort. Then imagine a curious boy running across this courtyard five centuries ago. He could not have known that he would one day stand on beaches in India and Japan. Or that schools, churches, and children on several continents would carry the name of his home.

As you leave, turn around once more and look back at the towers. The light on the stone changes through the day, from pale gold in the morning to warmer tones near evening. Listen again to the wind and the gravel underfoot. It is the same quiet that Francis knew as a child. A long road began at this door — and it ran to the far side of the world.

Before you go

Practical tips

Expect crowds during the Javierada pilgrimage in early March.
Visit on a weekday for a quieter walk through rooms.
Walk the outer walls before entering the castle.
Combine with nearby monasteries and the reservoir by car.
Wear comfortable shoes for stairs and uneven stone floors.
