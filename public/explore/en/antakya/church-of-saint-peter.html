The guide

The story

Cool air meets you first. You step out of the Antakya sun and into a cave, and the temperature drops at once. The rock overhead is rough and dark, slightly damp to the touch. Somewhere at the back, water drips slowly onto stone. It is a small sound, but in this enclosed space it carries. Before you think about saints or centuries, notice that simple fact: you are standing inside a mountain.

This is the Church of Saint Peter, cut into the western slope of Mount Starius, above the old city of Antakya. For many centuries the city had a different name, Antioch. Christian tradition holds that this cave was one of the earliest places where followers of Jesus met in secret to pray. For that reason, many people call it one of the oldest churches in the world. It is not a grand building. It is a hollow in the hillside where people gathered quietly.

To understand why this cave matters, picture the city below as it once was. Antioch was founded around 300 BC by Seleucus, one of the generals who divided the empire of Alexander the Great. Under the Romans it became one of the largest cities in the eastern Mediterranean. Merchants, soldiers, philosophers and preachers passed through its streets. Greek, Aramaic and Latin were all spoken here. It was exactly the kind of crowded crossroads where a new idea could spread fast.

And one did. The Book of Acts in the New Testament says something remarkable about this city. It says that the followers of Jesus were called Christians first in Antioch. Think about that for a moment. The very word that billions of people use today is linked, by that text, to the streets you can see from this hillside. Acts also describes the apostles Paul and Barnabas teaching here, and Antioch became a base for their long journeys.

Saint Peter's connection to Antioch comes from early Christian tradition. Writers in the first centuries of the Church described Peter as the first leader of the Christian community here, before his later life in Rome. Whether Peter himself ever prayed inside this particular cave cannot be proven. Locals and pilgrims believe that he did, and that belief has shaped this place for a very long time. It is worth holding both ideas together: deep tradition, and honest uncertainty.

Now look at the front of the church. The carved stone facade with its arched doorways is much younger than the cave behind it. Crusader knights, who held Antioch in the late eleventh and twelfth centuries, are believed to have extended the cave and given it a built front. The facade you see today is younger still. It was rebuilt in 1863 by Capuchin friars, a Catholic order, with support from the French emperor Napoleon the Third.

This is the first surprise for many visitors. From the outside, the church looks like a neat nineteenth century building. But it is really a thin stone mask over something far older. Walk from the doorway toward the back, and you are walking backward through time. The cut stone gives way to raw rock. The ordered architecture gives way to the shape the mountain made for itself.

Look down as you move inside. On parts of the floor, there are fragments of old mosaic, small pieces of coloured stone set into patterns. They are worn and incomplete, and they are easy to miss if you only look at the altar. Scholars generally date these fragments to the late Roman or early Byzantine period, roughly the fourth to sixth centuries. Some faint traces of wall painting have also been noted on the rock, though very little survives.

Then return to that sound of dripping water. Water seeps through the rock in one part of the cave. Many visitors over the centuries have believed this water had healing power, and some still touch it or collect a little. Treat this as faith and local custom, not as a medical claim. But it is a living tradition, and it connects today's visitors with very old habits of pilgrimage.

Here is the second surprise. Toward the back of the cave, there is a narrow passage leading into the mountain. According to local tradition, this tunnel was an escape route. If soldiers or hostile authorities came to the door, the worshippers could slip away through the rock and disappear onto the hillside. Whether every story about it is true is hard to say. Still, the idea fits the early history of a small, often persecuted community.

In the twentieth century, the Catholic Church formally recognised this cave as a site of pilgrimage. Each year around the twenty-ninth of June, the feast day of Saint Peter and Saint Paul, Christians from Antakya and beyond come here for a special service. The city has long been a place where Muslim, Christian and Jewish communities lived side by side. This church is one small piece of that shared story, and local people of many backgrounds feel attached to it.

That story was badly shaken in February 2023. Two powerful earthquakes struck southern Turkey and northern Syria, and Antakya was among the worst-affected cities. Large parts of the old town were destroyed, and many lives were lost. Much of the city below you is still being cleared and rebuilt. Access to the church, and its condition, may change while repair work continues. If you are here today, you are seeing a city in the middle of recovery.

Before you leave, look up the slope above the church if you can. Higher on Mount Starius, a large figure is carved directly into the rock. It is known as the Charonion. Legend says it was carved in ancient times to protect the city from a plague. Its true purpose is debated. Either way, it shows that people were cutting meaning into this mountain long before Christian worshippers arrived.

Now step back out into the light. Feel the warmth return to your face. Below you, the valley of the Orontes River opens out, with the old city and the new construction side by side. Listen for traffic, voices, perhaps the sound of building work. Behind you, the cave keeps its own cool silence, and the water keeps dripping. As you walk down the hill, notice how quickly that quiet place disappears into the rock.

Practical tips

Check current access first; post-earthquake repairs may limit entry.
Wear shoes with good grip for the uneven, damp cave floor.
Come early to see the cave quietly before groups arrive.
Dress modestly; this cave is still an active place of worship.
Bring water for the warm uphill walk from the city.
