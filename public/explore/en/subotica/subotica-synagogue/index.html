The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. The walls in front of you do not look like stone at all. They look painted, almost stitched, with flowing floral patterns. Glazed tiles catch the light along the roofline. The shapes curve and bloom like embroidery on a festive shirt. This is the Subotica Synagogue, and it is one of the most unusual religious buildings you will see anywhere in Europe.

The building belongs to a style called Hungarian Secession. That is the Hungarian branch of Art Nouveau, the decorative movement that spread across Europe around 1900. Art Nouveau rejected straight classical columns and heavy symmetry. It preferred curves, plants, and natural forms. The Hungarian version added something of its own: the patterns of village folk art. Here those patterns cover a house of worship.

The synagogue was completed in 1902. It was designed by two architects from Budapest, Marcell Komor and Dezső Jakab. They worked as a team for many years and left buildings across the old Kingdom of Hungary. Their partnership mattered a great deal to Subotica. If you walk a few minutes into the centre, you will find the City Hall, also their work. The two buildings were designed by the same pair of architects, and they share the same decorative spirit.

To understand why a building like this appeared here, picture Subotica at the turn of the twentieth century. The town lay in the southern part of the Kingdom of Hungary, within the Austro-Hungarian Empire. It was a market town on the fertile Pannonian Plain, the flat farmland that stretches across this part of Central Europe. Trade in grain and livestock brought money. The Jewish community grew prosperous, and it wanted a synagogue that would show its place in the modern city.

The choice of style says a lot. In those years, Hungarian Secession was a statement of national identity. Using folk motifs meant saying: this is Hungarian, this is ours. For the Jewish community of Subotica, building in that style was a way of belonging. The synagogue was not hidden on a side street. It was large, confident, and decorated in the language of the country around it.

Now look at the shape of the building. A large dome rises over the centre. Around it, smaller roof forms and gables step down towards the street. From some angles the whole structure seems to grow upward like a plant. The surfaces are not flat either. Bands of colour, ceramic ornament, and carved details break up every wall. Your eye keeps moving, and there is always another pattern to find.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Much of the colourful ceramic work on Hungarian Secession buildings came from the Zsolnay factory in the city of Pécs, in southern Hungary. Zsolnay was known for frost-resistant glazed ceramics with a shimmering surface. These tiles could survive harsh winters and still keep their colour. When the sun moves across the roof, watch how some tiles seem to change tone. That shimmer is part of what made the material so popular.

Inside, the decoration continues. The interior is a single large space beneath the dome. Light enters through tall windows filled with coloured glass. The same folk-inspired flowers and curling lines appear on the walls and ironwork. The design creates the feeling of standing inside a decorated garden. Many people say the interior feels warmer and more intimate than the size of the building suggests.

The second surprise is about scale. The Subotica Synagogue is often described as one of the largest synagogues in Europe. That fact alone tells you how big and how ambitious this community once was. A building of this size was meant to hold many worshippers for the great festivals of the Jewish year. Standing in front of it today, it can be hard to imagine those crowds.

That is because the story of this building has a dark chapter. During the Second World War, the region came under Hungarian rule again. In 1944, the Jews of Subotica were deported. Most of them were murdered at Auschwitz, the Nazi death camp in occupied Poland. A community that had built this confident monument was almost destroyed within a few months. Only a small number survived and returned after the war.

After the war, the synagogue had too few people left to fill it. For decades, the building slowly decayed. Water came through the roof. Paint faded and ornaments cracked. At times, it was used for other purposes, including as a theatre space. For many years, scaffolding and worry were part of its appearance. Locals and preservationists argued for its rescue, but the work was large and expensive.

A major restoration finally brought the building back. It was carried out with support from both the Serbian and Hungarian governments, and the synagogue reopened in 2018. The restorers worked to return the colours and ornament to their original character. Today the building serves both as a place of memory and as a cultural venue, hosting concerts and events. It is a reminder of the people who built it, and of what was lost.

As you stand here, think about the city around you. Subotica is a place of several languages and traditions. You may hear Serbian, Hungarian, and Croatian in the same street. The synagogue sits within that mix. Its folk flowers came from village embroidery. Its builders came from Budapest. Its community was Jewish. Its restoration came from two states working together. Few buildings carry so many threads at once.

Before you move on, take one last slow look. Follow a single painted line from the base of a wall up towards the dome. Notice how it curves, splits, and turns into a flower. Then turn and walk towards the city centre. Keep your eyes on the rooftops and facades along the way. You will start to see the same curving language on other buildings — and you will know where it comes from.
