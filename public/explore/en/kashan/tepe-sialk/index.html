The guide

The story

The ground under your feet here is not quite soil and not quite stone. It is old mud brick, broken down by sun and wind into a pale, crumbly earth that turns to fine dust on your shoes. A dry breeze comes off the plain. To the west, the mountains rise in soft brown folds. And in front of you, a low, eroded hill climbs out of the flat land on the edge of Kashan. It does not look like much at first. That is part of its secret.

This is Tepe Sialk. In Persian, a tepe is a mound, and this one is not natural. It was built up slowly by people living, building, and rebuilding on the same spot for thousands of years. Each generation levelled the ruins of the last and raised new homes on top. Over time, the village lifted itself off the plain, layer by layer. What you see is a stack of human lives, compressed into earth.

There are actually two mounds here, a northern one and a southern one, a short walk apart. The northern mound holds the oldest traces. Archaeologists date its earliest settlement to roughly the sixth millennium before the Common Era. That is around seven or eight thousand years ago, long before writing, long before the pyramids of Egypt. The people who first settled here were farmers. They lived in simple houses, and they chose this spot for one very practical reason... water.

Look toward the hills to the southwest. Not far from here, springs flow out of the foothills near the village of Fin. Those same waters later fed the famous Persian garden at Fin, with its channels and fountains. Seven thousand years ago, they made life possible on this dry plain. A steady water source, fertile land, and a route between the mountains and the desert. Few early farmers in this region could have asked for more.

The modern story of Sialk begins in the 1930s. A French archaeologist named Roman Ghirshman led excavations here over several seasons in that decade. His team dug deep trenches into the mounds and recorded the sequence of layers. Many of the objects they found travelled to the Louvre in Paris. Others stayed in Iran and are now in the National Museum in Tehran. So pieces of this quiet hill sit in display cases in two capitals.

What made Sialk famous among archaeologists was its pottery. The early potters here painted their vessels with dark designs on a pale or reddish surface. Over the centuries, their style grew bolder. Rows of birds appear. Leaping ibex, a wild mountain goat with long curved horns, run around the rims. Some designs are almost abstract — zigzags, chequers, and wavy lines. For many experts, these painted pots are one of the clearest records of how early Iranian art developed.

Here is something many visitors miss. Sialk was also an early place of metalworking. Excavations found evidence that people here worked copper at a very early date. At first they hammered it cold. Later, they learned to heat and shape it. This was a quiet revolution. The step from stone tools to metal ones changed farming, building, and warfare across the ancient world. And a small piece of that story happened on this mound.

Now turn your attention to the southern mound. Its most striking feature is a large stepped structure of mud brick, often described as a ziggurat. A ziggurat is a terraced platform, rising in levels, like a giant staircase. The best-known examples are in ancient Mesopotamia, in today's Iraq. The Sialk structure is usually dated to around the third millennium before the Common Era. Scholars still debate its exact purpose, but many believe it held a temple or an important building on top.

Take a moment to look closely at the walls. The bricks are made of sun-dried mud, mixed with straw. You may be able to see the joints between them, and the soft, rounded edges where rain has worn them down. Some areas are protected with modern coverings or fresh mud plaster. This is not decoration. Mud brick melts slowly in the rain. Without care, this ancient platform would wash back into the plain.

Another surprise lies in the clay tablets found here. Excavators uncovered tablets carrying a very early form of writing, known as Proto-Elamite. The Elamites were an ancient people of southwestern Iran, neighbours and rivals of Mesopotamia. Their early script has never been fully deciphered. So somewhere in the record of this mound are messages, probably accounts and records, that nobody alive can fully read. The people of Sialk were counting and writing, and we still cannot read their words.

Much later, in the Iron Age, the area near Sialk served as a burial ground. Archaeologists found two cemeteries here. One of them held graves with distinctive clay vessels that have long, beak-like spouts, a little like a teapot. These spouted jars are some of the most recognisable objects from the site. They show a world very different from the early farming village — new people, new customs, and new ideas about death and the afterlife.

In the early 2000s, Iranian archaeologists returned to Sialk with a new research project. They used modern methods to re-examine the mounds and refine the dates of the earlier work. Their excavations added detail to the story, and they also brought new attention to protecting the site. Kashan has grown outward over the past century. Today, roads and houses come close to the mounds. That makes the work of conservation more urgent every year.

As you walk, notice how quiet this place is compared with the busy lanes of old Kashan. Listen to the wind moving across the open ground. Watch how the light changes the colour of the mud brick, from pale grey in the midday glare to warm honey in the late afternoon. There are no towers or domes here. Instead, there is a slow, patient kind of history, built from mud, water, and many ordinary lives.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look down at the earth by your feet. Somewhere below you, perhaps only a few metres deep, are floors where children played and fires burned seven thousand years ago. The mountains on the horizon are the same ones those farmers saw. The springs of Fin still run toward the city. As you walk back toward Kashan, you are walking the same route that people have taken from this hill for thousands of years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid strong heat.
Bring water, sun hat, and sturdy closed shoes.
Combine your visit with nearby Fin Garden.
Stay on marked paths to protect fragile mud brick.
See Sialk pottery at the National Museum in Tehran.
