The guide

The story

Listen first. Out on Independence Square, traffic runs wide and steady. Here, at the foot of these red brick walls, the sound softens. The brick is dark and warm, the colour of baked clay, and it holds the afternoon light long after the pale buildings around it have turned grey. Run your eyes up the tall tower. Then look back across the square. Few places in Minsk show such a sharp contrast in a single glance.

This is the Church of Saints Simon and Helena. Almost everyone in Minsk simply calls it the Red Church. It is a Roman Catholic church, and it rose in the first decade of the twentieth century, at a time when this land was part of the Russian Empire. The square in front of you did not yet exist in its present form. The church was here first, and the Soviet city grew up around it.

The story behind it is a family story. The church was paid for by Edward Woyniłłowicz, a wealthy landowner and a leading figure among the Catholic gentry of the region. He and his wife lost two of their children young. Their son was named Simon. Their daughter was named Helena. The church carries their names. So when you stand here, you stand at a memorial built by grieving parents, not at a monument built by a state.

There is a tale that locals often repeat. According to the popular story, Helena, before her death, described a beautiful church she had seen in a dream. Her parents later searched for a design that matched her words. It is a lovely legend, and it is told inside the parish too. But treat it as legend. What we can say with confidence is simpler. The building is a father's tribute, and its names were chosen with deep care.

Now look at the style. The architects worked in a Neo-Romanesque manner. Romanesque is the heavy, round-arched style of European churches from roughly a thousand years ago, before the pointed arches of the Gothic age. Here that old language is rebuilt in modern brick. Notice the rounded windows, the thick walls, the solid, almost fortress-like mass. It looks back to medieval Poland and to the churches of Central Europe. In Minsk, it stands out against Orthodox domes and Soviet stone.

Look at the tower again. One tall bell tower rises over the entrance, and smaller towers stand beside it. The tallest point is sharp and narrow, and it pulls the eye straight upward. The red brick does most of the decorative work. Look closely and you will see bands, recesses, and patterns made only by setting the bricks at different depths. There is very little carved stone. The craftsmen built ornament out of shadow.

Here is the first surprise. For decades, this was not a church at all. In the Soviet period, religious life was pushed aside, and the parish was closed in the early 1930s. The building passed through several uses. Later, it became a cinema and a home for the Belarusian film world, known as the House of Cinema. People came here to watch films under the vaults where the Mass had once been said. The altar space became a place for a screen.

The building also survived the Second World War. Minsk was devastated in those years. Much of the old city was destroyed, and the centre you see today was largely rebuilt in the postwar decades. The Red Church is one of the few large buildings here that carries the look of the city before that destruction. That alone makes it precious to many people in Minsk, whatever their faith.

In 1990, as the Soviet Union came apart, the building was returned to the Catholic Church. Restoration began, and worship returned. Today it is a working parish again, with services in Belarusian, Polish, and other languages. Step inside if a service is not under way. The interior is calmer and simpler than the outside suggests. Look up at the stained glass. Much of it was made after the return, so the windows are newer than the walls around them.

The second surprise waits outside, in the churchyard. Look for a bronze statue of the Archangel Michael, his spear driven down into a dragon. In Christian tradition, Michael is the protector who defeats evil. Nearby hangs a bell connected with the Japanese city of Nagasaki. It links the memory of the atomic bombing with the memory of the Chernobyl disaster, which hit Belarus very hard. Two catastrophes, half a world apart, remembered together beside one church.

The founder's own story came full circle here. Edward Woyniłłowicz left this land after the revolution and died in exile in Poland. For many years his remains lay far from Minsk. In the early twenty-first century, they were brought back and reburied at the church he built. Think about that for a moment. The father who built a memorial for his children now rests in it himself.

Turn around and look across the square. Facing you is the long, severe Government House, built in the Soviet era, with a statue of Lenin in front of it. The two buildings face each other across open stone. One speaks of private faith and family loss. The other speaks of state power. They do not argue. They simply stand there, and the square between them tells much of Minsk's twentieth century without a single word.

Before you go, step back to the edge of the churchyard. Let the whole front fit into your view. Notice how the red brick changes as clouds pass, from deep rust to almost brown. Notice the bell tower against the sky, and the people crossing the square without looking up. As you walk away, glance back once more. A grieving father wanted his children remembered. More than a century later, the whole city still says their names.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit late afternoon when low sun warms the red brick.
Dress modestly and stay quiet if a service is under way.
Cross to the far side of the square for the full facade.
Combine your visit with a walk along Independence Avenue.
Ask before photographing worshippers inside the church.
