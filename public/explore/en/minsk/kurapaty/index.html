The guide

The story

Listen first. Pine needles shift under your shoes. Wind moves through the tops of the trees, and somewhere beyond them you can hear the low, steady rush of traffic on the ring road. The light here falls in narrow bands between the trunks. The ground is soft and uneven, and in places it dips into shallow hollows. Those hollows are the reason you are standing here.

This is Kurapaty, a stretch of forest on the northern edge of Minsk. For most of the year it looks like any other Belarusian woodland: pine, birch, sandy soil, a few paths worn by walkers. But between the late 1930s and the start of the Second World War, this forest was used by the Soviet secret police, the NKVD, as a place of mass execution and burial. Thousands of people were brought here and shot.

The years at the centre of this story are 1937 and 1938. Historians call that period the Great Terror. Across the Soviet Union, ordinary people were arrested on invented charges. Teachers, farmers, priests, railway workers, writers and party members were all taken. Many were accused of spying or sabotage. Very few had any real trial. In Belarus, the terror struck especially hard at the educated class, including many poets and writers who worked in the Belarusian language.

How many people lie in this forest? The honest answer is that nobody knows. Estimates vary enormously. Some official investigations named a figure of several thousand. Many researchers believe the true number is in the tens of thousands, and some estimates go far higher. The NKVD did not leave public records of this place. That silence is part of the history too. The numbers are uncertain because the killings were meant to stay hidden.

For about half a century, they did stay hidden — at least officially. People in nearby villages remembered trucks arriving at night, and the sound of shots from the woods. But speaking about it was dangerous. Then, in 1988, during the period of openness under Mikhail Gorbachev, the archaeologist Zianon Pazniak and a colleague published an article about this forest. It named Kurapaty as a site of Stalinist killings, and it shocked the country.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Kurapaty did not only become a memorial. It became a starting point for a political movement. The public reaction to the discovery helped bring together the Belarusian Popular Front, a movement for national revival and independence. In the late 1980s, large memorial marches walked from the city to this forest. Grief for the dead and hope for a different future were carried along the same road.

Excavations in those years found human remains in burial pits, along with personal belongings: shoes, glasses, combs, small everyday objects. Investigators also found bullets and cartridge cases. These details helped confirm who had carried out the shootings. Later Soviet and Belarusian investigations formally acknowledged that the NKVD was responsible. You will not see the pits opened today. The hollows in the ground mark where the soil has settled over them.

Now look around at the crosses. They are the strongest feature of this place. Most of them were not put up by the state. They were raised by volunteers, families, churches and civic groups. Some are tall and carved from dark wood. Others are small, simple, and roughly made. Many carry names, short prayers, or dates. Some are tied with embroidered cloth, the traditional Belarusian ritual towel, which is often decorated with red patterns.

The story of these crosses has not been calm. Over the years, some have been damaged or pulled down by vandals. In 2019, authorities removed dozens of crosses that volunteers had placed near the edge of the forest, and an official memorial stone was installed instead. Many people protested the removal. So when you walk here, you are walking through a place where the question of how to remember is still very much alive.

There is also a small international chapter. In 1994, the American president Bill Clinton visited Kurapaty during a trip to Belarus. A memorial bench was later placed here as a gift from the American people, in memory of the victims. It has been damaged more than once. Like the crosses, it shows how this forest has drawn strong feelings from many sides.

And the name? Locals say that Kurapaty comes from a small white or pale flower that once grew here in spring, known in the local dialect by a similar word. Treat that as a local tale rather than settled fact. Still, it is a striking thought — that a forest of such dark memory may carry a name taken from something as gentle as a field flower.

As you move through the trees, notice how close the city is. Apartment blocks stand not far away. The ring road runs along one side, and traffic never quite goes quiet. Minsk grew right up to the edge of the forest. Life goes on next to the graves. Some visitors find that painful. Others find it fitting: the dead are not pushed far away, but kept beside the living.

This is a place to move slowly. Speak softly. Stay on the paths, because the ground itself is a burial site. Many people stop at a cross that has no name, because most of the victims still do not have one. Researchers and families continue to search archives, trying to connect names to this forest. Every name that is found turns a number back into a person.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen again to the needles underfoot, the wind in the pines, the distant road. Look at the nearest cross and at the cloth tied around it, faded by rain and sun. Someone came here and tied that cloth by hand. As you walk back toward the city, you carry a small part of that act of remembering with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stay on marked paths; the ground covers burial pits.
Keep voices low and treat the forest as a cemetery.
Wear sturdy shoes for sandy, uneven forest ground.
Bring flowers or a candle if you wish to pay respects.
Visit on a weekday morning for a quieter experience.
