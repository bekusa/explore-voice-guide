The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see a single ride, you hear the park. A low rumble rolls through the pine trees. Then comes a rising chorus of screams, and then a burst of laughter as a train of riders slows to a stop. The air smells of fresh waffles, sun cream, and warm resin from the forest. Underfoot, the paths are smooth and wide, made for strollers and running feet. This is Tusenfryd, the largest amusement park in Norway. It sits on a wooded hillside at Vinterbro, in Ås Municipality, a short drive south of Oslo.

Start with the name, because most visitors never stop to think about it. In Norwegian, tusen means a thousand, and fryd means joy or delight. So the park promises a thousand joys. There is a second meaning too. Tusenfryd is also the everyday Norwegian word for the common daisy, the small white flower with a yellow centre that grows in lawns across the country. One word holds both ideas. It is a simple, childlike flower, and it is also a big promise of fun.

The park opened in 1988, and its setting shapes everything about it. Look at the land around you. This is not a flat field on the edge of a motorway. It is a rolling forest landscape, with granite outcrops and tall spruce and pine trees. Large highways meet nearby, which makes the park easy to reach from Oslo and from the towns to the south. Inside the gates, though, the trees close in. Rides appear and disappear behind the trunks, so you often hear an attraction long before you find it.

The hills are not a problem for the designers. They are a tool. Roller coasters need height and drops, and here the ground already rises and falls. Tracks can dive into dips, swing along slopes, and climb out again. As you walk, notice how often a coaster track follows the shape of the land. Some sections run close to the treetops, while others drop low between them. Riders feel the forest rush past at close range, and that makes the speed feel even greater.

The most recognisable silhouette in the park belongs to ThunderCoaster, a large wooden roller coaster that opened in 2001. Wooden coasters feel very different from steel ones. The structure is a dense lattice of timber beams, and the ride is famous for its rattle and roar. When a train passes, you can hear the wood answer it, a deep drumming sound that carries across the park. Stand near the structure for a moment and watch it. Wooden coasters move slightly as trains run over them. That flexibility is part of their design.

Across the park, the other great thrill is SpeedMonster, a launched steel coaster that opened in 2006. Instead of climbing slowly up a hill, the train is fired forward from a standing start. The acceleration is sudden. One moment riders sit still, and a few seconds later they are racing through loops and turns. Watch the start of the track, and you will see faces change in an instant. Even from the ground, the contrast with the old wooden coaster is clear. One ride booms and shakes. The other hisses and is gone.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Several rides at a park like this are older than they look, and some have had earlier lives. Amusement rides are often bought, moved, and rebuilt in a new place. A coaster can travel between countries in pieces and then stand again on fresh foundations. So when you look at a classic looping coaster here, remember that its steel may hold memories of other crowds. Parks grow in layers, and each new section sits beside the parks of earlier decades.

That layering shows in the way the park is arranged. Tusenfryd is divided into themed areas, each with its own look and its own mix of rides. Some zones are designed for small children, with gentle rides, bright colours, and short queues. Others belong to the teenagers who want the biggest drops in Norway. Between them are food stands, games, and quiet benches under the trees. Families often split up for an hour, then meet again in the middle. The layout makes that easy, with clear paths that loop back on themselves.

Water is part of the experience too. In the warm months, a water park section called BadeFryd opens beside the main rides. The name works the same way as the park's own name. Bade means to bathe or swim, so BadeFryd is bathing joy. On a hot day, listen for the slap of water on slides and the shouts from the pools. Many Norwegian families treat a summer visit here as a full day out. They ride the coasters in the morning and then cool off in the water after lunch.

It helps to remember where you are in the world. Norway has long, dark winters and short, bright summers. An outdoor amusement park in this climate works with the seasons. It comes alive when the days are long and the evenings stay light. In early summer, the sun sets late, and the sky can stay pale long after dinner. The park also hosts special seasonal events, including an autumn Halloween season, when the paths fill with costumes, fog, and theatrical scares. Check the park's own calendar before you plan.

Now look at the people around you. Tusenfryd is a place of first times. For many Norwegians, this was the park of their childhood, the first big coaster, the first school trip without parents close by. You will see grandparents watching from the benches while grandchildren run ahead. You will hear Norwegian, Swedish, English, and many other languages in the queues. Listen to the conversations at the exit of a big ride. People repeat the same few words, often half laughing, half shaking. Joy here is loud and very public.

Take a moment to notice the smaller pleasures too, because the name promises a thousand of them, not just ten big ones. There is the smell of sugar from a sweet stall. There is the sound of a carousel organ, playing the same cheerful tune again and again. There is the shade of a tall pine on a hot afternoon, and the cool air near the water. A day at an amusement park is built from these small moments, as much as from the famous drops and loops.

Before you move on, find a spot where you can see both the forest and a coaster track at once. Look at the line where the timber or steel meets the dark green of the trees. Wait for the next train. You will hear it first, then see it, then hear the riders. When it passes, the forest becomes quiet again for a few seconds. That quiet is part of this place. As you walk on, keep an eye out for daisies in the grass. They are small, white, and easy to miss... the first thousand joys of Tusenfryd.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the biggest coasters early, before queues build up.
Bring swimwear on warm days for the BadeFryd water area.
Check the park calendar for seasonal events before visiting.
Agree on a meeting point if your group splits up.
Pack a rain layer, since Norwegian summer weather changes fast.
