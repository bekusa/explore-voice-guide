The guide

The story

Listen first. Out past the earthworks, the wind comes in flat and steady off the Bohai Sea. It carries salt, and the faint, muddy smell of the river mouth at low tide. Under your feet the ground is firm, packed earth rather than stone. The colours here are muted: grey water, brown banks, a pale sky. It is a quiet place now. For a few hard decades of the nineteenth century, it was one of the most contested shorelines in all of China.

You are standing at the mouth of the Hai River, where it empties into the sea. The Chinese name for this place is Dagu. Western sailors and diplomats wrote it as Taku, and that spelling stayed in English history books. The location explains everything. Upriver lies the city of Tianjin. Beyond Tianjin lies Beijing, the old imperial capital. Whoever held this river mouth held the sea road to the capital.

Defences at this spot go back a long way. Fortifications were first raised here under the Ming dynasty, when coastal raiders threatened the northern shore. The Qing dynasty, which ruled China from the mid-seventeenth century, later expanded them. By the nineteenth century there was a line of forts on both banks of the river. Each one was a raised platform of earth and masonry, with cannon pointing out across the mudflats toward the open sea.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The main forts were given names, and each name was a single Chinese character. Together the characters are usually given as wei, zhen, hai, men and gao. Roughly translated, they mean awe, shake, sea, gate and high. Read as a set, they carried a message to anyone arriving by water. This was the high gate of the sea, and it was meant to shake an enemy with fear.

That message was tested during the Second Opium War. This was a conflict between the Qing court and Britain and France over trade, diplomacy and access to China. In 1858, an Anglo-French naval force attacked the forts and captured them. The defeat pushed the Qing government into negotiations. The result was a set of agreements known as the Treaties of Tianjin, signed in the city just upriver from where you stand.

The next year brought a surprise that still appears in naval histories. In 1859, British ships returned to escort diplomats toward Beijing. This time the defenders were ready. They had rebuilt and strengthened the forts, and they had blocked the river channel. The British attack failed badly, with heavy losses of ships and men. For a moment, the high gate of the sea held. It was one of the rare Qing victories against a Western navy in that era.

The success did not last. In 1860, a larger Anglo-French force came back. This time it landed troops along the coast and attacked the forts from the land side, where the defences were weaker. The forts fell again. The allied army then marched inland. That campaign ended in Beijing, and it included the burning of the Old Summer Palace, a vast imperial garden complex outside the capital.

Four decades later, the forts were at the centre of another war. In 1900, the Boxer Uprising spread across northern China. The Boxers were a popular movement opposed to foreign influence and Christian missions. An alliance of eight foreign powers sent forces to the region. In June of that year, their warships attacked the Dagu forts and captured them. That attack opened the road for foreign troops to move on Tianjin and then Beijing.

The peace settlement that followed, signed in 1901, was harsh. Among its terms, it required China to demolish the forts here. That is why so little of the original complex survives. What you see today is a fragment of a much larger system. Look at the scale of the remaining mound and try to imagine it repeated along both banks. The emptiness around it is part of the story.

Today the site is preserved as a museum and memorial. In China, it is presented as a place to remember a period of foreign invasion and national humiliation. School groups come here, and the tone of the displays is serious. You will find historic cannon set out on the ramparts, along with exhibitions about the battles. Take your time reading the panels. They give the Chinese side of a history that Western accounts often tell very differently.

Walk up onto the earthworks if the path allows. Notice how low and wide the structure is. These forts were not tall castles. They were built to absorb cannon fire, with thick walls of earth that could swallow an iron ball. Many accounts describe the use of lime, sand and earth packed together, a mix that hardened as it dried. Up close, the surface looks plain. That plainness was the point.

Now turn toward the water. The coastline here has changed a great deal since the nineteenth century. Land has been reclaimed, ports have grown, and modern Tianjin has spread toward the sea. Cranes, tankers and container ships may fill part of the horizon. It is worth holding both pictures at once. Wooden warships once anchored offshore, waiting for the tide. Today, much of China's northern trade passes close to this same river mouth.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near one of the old guns. Feel the wind again, the same wind the gunners felt. Watch how the light sits on the mudflats and the slow brown river. The forts are mostly gone, but the reason they were built is still right in front of you. This is where the river meets the sea, and where the road to the capital began.

Before you go

Practical tips

Read the museum panels before climbing the earthworks.
Bring a windproof layer for the exposed coastal site.
Check the tide to see the mudflats fully exposed.
Allow extra travel time from central Tianjin to the coast.
Visit on a weekday to avoid large school groups.
