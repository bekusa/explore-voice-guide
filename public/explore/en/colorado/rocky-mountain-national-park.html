The guide

The story

Breathe in slowly. The air up here is thin, cool, and sharp, and it carries the smell of pine resin and sun-warmed granite. Listen for wind moving through the trees with a long, steady hiss. Somewhere a stream runs over stones. If you are on high ground, you may hear a short, squeaky whistle from the rocks. That is a pika, a small round relative of the rabbit, and it is probably watching you.

Welcome to Rocky Mountain National Park, in the northern Front Range of Colorado. This is a park measured in height as much as in distance. Its valleys already sit high above sea level, and its ridges climb far beyond the line where trees can grow. In a single drive, you can move from meadows of grass and wildflowers to a landscape that looks closer to the Arctic. That vertical journey is the real story of this place.

The park was established in 1915, when President Woodrow Wilson signed the act that created it. Much of the credit goes to Enos Mills, a naturalist and writer who lived near Longs Peak. Mills guided visitors through these mountains for years. He wrote, lectured, and campaigned tirelessly for protection. Many people today call him the father of the park. His old homestead area sits near the road to the peak he loved.

Long before any act of Congress, people knew these mountains well. Ute and Arapaho peoples travelled, hunted, and camped across this high country for generations. They followed old routes over the passes, moving between seasons and valleys. Some of the paths visitors walk today follow those same natural corridors. Locals often say that the name of the Never Summer Mountains comes from an Arapaho description. Whatever its exact origin, the name fits — snow lingers on those peaks deep into the warm months.

Now look for the long ridge of mountains that divides the sky. The Continental Divide runs straight through the park. Rain that falls on one side drains toward the Atlantic Ocean. Rain that falls on the other side drains toward the Pacific. Here is a surprise that many visitors miss — on the western side, in the Kawuneeche Valley, a small stream begins its journey as the Colorado River. The same river that later cuts the Grand Canyon starts here as water you could almost step across.

The road that ties the two sides together is Trail Ridge Road. It is the highest continuous paved road in the United States, and it climbs above 12,000 feet, or roughly 3,700 metres. Drivers pass viewpoints with long views over ranges and valleys. The road connects the town of Estes Park on the east with Grand Lake on the west. Heavy snow closes the high section each winter. When it reopens in late spring, crews often clear walls of snow taller than a car.

There is an older way up as well. Old Fall River Road is a narrow, unpaved route that predates Trail Ridge Road. It runs one way, uphill, with tight switchbacks and slow, careful driving. It follows a creek past waterfalls and through dense forest before it climbs into open tundra. Many visitors never notice the turnoff. For those who take it, the slower pace changes everything — you hear the water, you smell the trees, and the mountain arrives gradually.

Above the treeline, the world changes. A large share of this park is alpine tundra, a landscape too cold and windy for trees. The plants here grow low and tight against the ground. Some tiny flowers may take years to bloom. A single footstep off the path can damage plants that need decades to recover. That is why rangers ask everyone to stay on the trails. Look closely, and you will see cushions of moss, small lichens, and flowers barely taller than your thumb.

Just below that line, watch the trees. At the edge of the forest, spruce and fir grow twisted, short, and bent away from the wind. This stunted form is called krummholz, a German word meaning crooked wood. Some of these trees are much older than they look. They grow only on the side protected from the wind, and their branches stretch out like flags. Each one is a clear record of the direction the weather usually blows.

The animals are the part many people remember most. Elk graze in the open meadows, especially in the morning and evening. In autumn, the bulls gather females and fill the valleys with a high, eerie call known as bugling. Bighorn sheep appear on rocky slopes. Yellow-bellied marmots sun themselves on boulders. Moose are more common on the western side, near willows and water. Please keep your distance. These are wild animals, and an elk in autumn can be dangerous.

One mountain dominates the eastern skyline — Longs Peak. Its flat-topped summit rises above 14,000 feet, and it is the highest point in the park. Climbers call peaks of this height fourteeners, and Colorado has dozens of them. Longs Peak is the only one inside these park boundaries. Reaching the top is a long, serious day, often starting before dawn. Even if you never climb it, its broad granite face is a constant reference point as you move through the park.

For many visitors, the easiest window into this world is Bear Lake. A short, level path circles the water, with the peaks reflected on calm days. From there, trails lead to other lakes higher up the valleys, each one framed by rock and forest. In early morning, the water is often still and the light is soft. By midday in summer, clouds can build quickly over the summits. Afternoon thunderstorms are common, and lightning is a real danger on exposed ground.

Wherever you stand right now, take a moment before you move on. Notice how the air feels on your skin, and how quickly the light shifts as clouds pass. Look at the ground under your feet — granite, needles, grass, or tundra. These mountains were here long before the road and the park signs, and they will be here long after. As you walk away, listen once more for wind in the trees, for water, and maybe for that small whistle from the rocks.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check whether a timed entry reservation is needed before arriving.
Drink extra water to cope with the high altitude.
Start hikes early to avoid afternoon thunderstorms.
Bring warm layers even on summer days.
Watch elk from a safe distance during autumn.
