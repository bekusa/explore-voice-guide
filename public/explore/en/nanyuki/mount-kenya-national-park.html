The guide

The story

The air up here feels thin and cool, even at the equator. Morning mist hangs in the forest, and the ground underfoot is soft with fallen leaves and red volcanic soil. Somewhere above the trees, hidden for now behind cloud, a ring of dark rock peaks holds some of the last ice in tropical Africa. Listen for a moment. You may hear the low, rattling call of a colobus monkey, or the drip of water from moss-covered branches. Welcome to Mount Kenya.

This is the second-highest mountain in Africa. Only Kilimanjaro, far to the south in Tanzania, stands taller. The highest summit here is called Batian, at 5,199 metres. Its twin, Nelion, is only a few metres lower. A third peak, Point Lenana, is the one most trekkers reach. It rises to just under five thousand metres, and you can climb it without ropes or technical skill. The two higher peaks are serious rock climbs.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The country took its name from this mountain, and not the other way around. When European travellers arrived in the nineteenth century, they heard local names for the peak and turned them into the word Kenya. Later, the colony and then the independent nation carried that name. So when you say the name of the country, you are really saying the name of the mountain in front of you.

To the Kikuyu people, who live on the fertile slopes to the south and west, the mountain is Kirinyaga. In Kikuyu tradition, it is the home of Ngai, the creator. Families traditionally built their homes with the doors facing the peaks, and prayers were offered toward the summit. Other communities around the mountain, including the Embu and the Meru, also hold it as a sacred place. For many people here, this is not only a landscape. It is a place of faith.

Mount Kenya is an old volcano, now long extinct. Millions of years ago, it may have stood even higher than it does today. Ice and weather have worn it down over time. The sharp peaks you see are the hard rock that once filled the volcano's central vent. Everything softer around them has eroded away. That is why the summit looks like a jagged crown of stone, not a smooth cone like Kilimanjaro.

The park is a protected area, set aside in 1949. In 1997, UNESCO listed Mount Kenya as a World Heritage Site. Later, the listing was extended to include the Lewa Wildlife Conservancy and the Ngare Ndare Forest to the north. Together they form a corridor that lets elephants move between the high forest and the dry lowland plains. This movement matters, because elephants have walked these routes for a very long time.

Now think about the climb itself, because the mountain changes as you go up. Its slopes rise through a series of belts, almost like floors in a building. At the bottom is thick montane forest, full of tall trees, ferns and vines. Above that comes a band of bamboo, dense and green, where the light turns soft and filtered. Higher still is open moorland, with heather and tussock grass. Finally, near the top, there is bare rock, scree and ice.

The moorland is the strangest part, and it surprises almost everyone. Here grow giant groundsels, plants that look like something from another planet. They have thick trunks and heavy rosettes of leaves, and some stand taller than a person. Nearby you will see giant lobelias, tall spikes covered in feathery leaves. These plants survive nights of frost and days of strong equatorial sun. Their leaves close up at night, like a coat, to protect the growing bud inside.

The forest belt is home to much of the park's wildlife. Elephants and buffalo move through it, often unseen. Black-and-white colobus monkeys leap between the branches, their long white fur swinging behind them. Higher up, on the rocks, you may meet the rock hyrax. It looks a little like a large guinea pig, but its closest living relatives are thought to be elephants. Keep a careful distance from buffalo, which can be unpredictable.

The mountain also has a story of human daring. In 1899, the British geographer Halford Mackinder led the first recorded ascent of Batian, with mountain guides from the Alps. Then, in 1943, during the Second World War, three Italian prisoners escaped from a camp near Nanyuki. They had no proper gear and a map drawn from a label on a tin of food. They climbed high on the mountain, raised a homemade Italian flag on Point Lenana, and then returned to the camp by choice.

One of those prisoners, Felice Benuzzi, later wrote a book about the adventure, called No Picnic on Mount Kenya. It is still read by climbers today. It is a reminder that people have always looked at these peaks and felt pulled toward them. Even prisoners of war, far from home, wanted to stand on top and see the view.

The ice is the part of this mountain that is changing fastest. Mount Kenya once held many glaciers. Today, most of them are much smaller, and some have gone completely. The Lewis Glacier, one of the best studied in the tropics, has shrunk dramatically over the past century. Scientists use it to track how a warming climate affects high mountains near the equator. If you see ice up there, know that future visitors may not.

Nanyuki, the town at the foot of the mountain, sits very close to the equator. Many trekkers start from here, on routes such as Sirimon to the north and Naro Moru to the west. Another popular route, Chogoria, climbs the eastern side through lakes and deep valleys. Each route gives you a different face of the same mountain. Whichever you choose, go slowly. Altitude is the real challenge here, much more than distance.

Take a final moment where you stand. Look up toward the peaks, if the clouds allow it, usually early in the morning. Notice the cool air on your skin, so close to the equator. Notice the colour of the soil and the shape of the trees. This mountain gave a nation its name and a people their sacred centre. As you walk on, carry that thought with you, and keep looking back over your shoulder.

Rock hyraxes sunning themselves on boulders near huts.
A dense band of bamboo between forest and moorland.
Before you go

Practical tips

Look for the peaks early morning, before clouds build up.
Allow extra days on the climb to adjust to altitude.
Pack warm layers; nights near the summit drop below freezing.
Hire a registered local guide for any multi-day trek.
Keep a safe distance from buffalo in the forest belt.
