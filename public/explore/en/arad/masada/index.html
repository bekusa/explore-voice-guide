The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the wind is often the loudest thing on the summit. It moves across a flat plateau of pale, sun-baked rock. Below you, the desert drops away in folds of tan and grey, down to the flat, milky blue of the Dead Sea. The air is dry enough to feel on your lips. The stone underfoot is warm, even early in the day. This is Masada, a mountain fortress in the Judean Desert, and few places on earth carry a story this heavy.

If you came from Arad, you most likely arrived by the western approach. That matters, because this side of the mountain holds the most dramatic physical evidence of what happened here. Most visitors come up from the Dead Sea on the eastern side, by cable car or by the long, winding Snake Path. The western route is quieter and the climb is shorter. And it follows the ramp built by a Roman army almost two thousand years ago.

Before the Romans, there was a king. Herod the Great ruled Judea in the first century before Christ as a client of Rome. He was a brilliant builder and a deeply suspicious man. He chose this isolated rock as a refuge, a place he could flee to if his enemies rose against him. On its cliffs and summit he built palaces, bathhouses, storerooms and huge water cisterns. He made a desert mountain into a place where a king could live in comfort.

The most daring of these buildings is the Northern Palace. It does not sit on the summit at all. It hangs down the northern cliff face on three stepped terraces, one below the other. From the upper level you can look down at the lower terraces, where painted walls and columns still survive. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of those columns were not carved from fine marble. They were built from local stone and coated with plaster, then painted to look like something far more expensive.

Water was the real luxury here. Rain falls rarely in this desert, but when it does, it can flood the dry riverbeds in minutes. Herod's engineers caught that flash flood water in channels and stored it in enormous cisterns cut into the rock. With that water, the king could enjoy a bathhouse in the Roman style. You can still see small stacks of stone that once held up a heated floor. Hot air flowed beneath it, the same way a Roman bath worked in Italy.

After Herod died, the fortress passed through other hands. Then, in the first century after Christ, the Jewish population of Judea revolted against Rome. Jerusalem fell, and its great Temple was destroyed. A group of rebels held out here on the summit. Many of them were families, not just fighters. They lived inside Herod's old buildings, and they changed them to fit their needs. Archaeologists found ritual baths and a room that most scholars identify as a synagogue, one of the oldest known.

Rome did not leave this last outpost alone. A Roman governor named Flavius Silva brought a legion and many auxiliary troops to the mountain. They built a wall around the entire base of Masada, so no one could escape. They set up a ring of military camps. Then they began the ramp. Thousands of workers, many of them likely Jewish prisoners, piled earth and stone against the western side. When you walk up from the Arad side, you are walking on that siege ramp.

What happened at the end comes to us mainly from one man. Flavius Josephus was a Jewish commander who changed sides and later wrote history under Roman patronage. According to his account, the rebel leader Eleazar ben Yair gave a final speech. He urged his people to die free rather than live as slaves. Josephus writes that the defenders took their own lives before the Romans broke in. He says only a few women and children survived by hiding, and later told the story.

It is worth knowing that historians still debate this account. Josephus was not here at the end, and he wrote with his own purposes. Archaeology has not confirmed every detail of his story. But it has found things that are hard to forget. Among them were small pottery shards with names written on them. Some scholars link them to the lots Josephus describes, drawn to decide who would die last. One shard carries the name ben Yair.

Much of what you see today was uncovered in the 1960s. A large excavation was led by the Israeli archaeologist Yigael Yadin, with volunteers who came from many countries. As you walk, look closely at the walls around you. A painted black line runs along many of them. Everything below that line is original ancient stone. Everything above it was rebuilt by restorers. It is a quiet, honest way to show the difference between the past and our repair of it.

Masada also carries meaning in modern Israel. For many Israelis, the story became a symbol of resistance and refusal to surrender. Some soldiers have held ceremonies on the summit, and the phrase that Masada shall not fall again became widely known. Others read the story with more doubt, and ask what it really means to choose death over defeat. In 2001, the site was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list, the global register of places with outstanding cultural value.

Now walk to the edge of the western wall and look down. On a clear day, you can trace the outline of the Roman camps below. They look like square shapes drawn in stone on the desert floor. Because almost no one has built here since, the siege works are unusually complete. Few places in the Roman world show a military campaign so clearly. You are seeing the plan of an army, still lying on the ground where it was laid.

Take one more slow turn before you leave. To the east, the Dead Sea shines below, with the mountains of Jordan beyond it. To the west, the desert climbs back toward Arad. Notice the small details as you go. A swift cutting past the cliff. A lizard on a sunlit wall. The black line on the stones beside the path. Then walk back down the ramp, and remember that every step rests on earth carried here by human hands.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the western access from Arad for a shorter climb.
Start at dawn to avoid the strongest midday desert heat.
Carry plenty of water, a hat and sun protection.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose gravel on the ramp path.
Leave time to stand at the western wall overlooking the camps.
