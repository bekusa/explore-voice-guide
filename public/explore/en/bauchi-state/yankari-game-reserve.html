The guide

The story

Listen first. The air near the Wikki spring carries the dry rustle of grass and the low murmur of moving water. Somewhere in the trees, a baboon barks. The sandy ground under your feet is warm, pale, and soft. Then, between the trees, the water comes into view. It is so clear that you can count the pebbles on the bottom. Welcome to Yankari, in Bauchi State, in the northeast of Nigeria.

Yankari covers more than two thousand square kilometres of rolling savanna. Savanna is open grassland broken by scattered trees and woodland. Here the land lies in a transition zone between the drier north and the wetter south. In the rainy season the grass grows tall and green. In the dry months it turns gold and brittle. Then the animals gather close to the rivers, and they become much easier to see.

The reserve took shape in the 1950s, when the colonial government set the area aside to protect its wildlife. It opened to visitors in the early 1960s, around the time of Nigerian independence. Later it was raised to national park status. After that, management returned to Bauchi State. The name changed over the decades. The idea did not change. This is a protected piece of wild Nigeria where people can still meet the animals of the savanna.

Most visitors begin at Wikki Camp, and most visitors head straight for the spring. Here is the first surprise. The water stays warm all year — roughly thirty-one degrees Celsius — by day and by night, in the dry season and in the rains. The spring rises from under a sandstone ridge and feeds a broad, shallow pool. Locals and travellers have bathed here for generations. Stepping in feels like entering a bath someone prepared just for you.

Wikki is not alone. The reserve holds several other warm springs, among them Dimmil, Gwan, and Nawulgo. Wikki is the easiest to reach, and it is the one most people swim in. The water flows away from the pool and joins the Gaji River. This steady supply of water explains much of what lives here. In a land where the dry season is long and hard, permanent water draws life from far around.

And that life is large. Yankari is best known for its elephants. The reserve holds one of the largest surviving elephant populations in West Africa. Elsewhere in the region, many herds have shrunk or vanished. In the dry season, elephants come down to the Gaji River to drink and to bathe. Seeing a family group cross the sand is the moment many visitors remember for years.

The elephants share the river with other animals. Hippos rest in the deeper pools and surface with a slow, heavy snort. Buffalo graze along the banks. Antelope move through the grass, including waterbuck, with their shaggy grey-brown coats, and hartebeest, with their long faces and curved horns. Crocodiles sometimes lie on the mud. Lions were once more common here. Today they are very rare, and a sighting would be extraordinary.

The animals you will almost certainly meet are the baboons. Here is the second surprise. In Yankari, the boldest wildlife encounter often happens at camp, not on safari. Baboons and other monkeys wander between the chalets. They watch the picnic tables with great interest. They know what a bag looks like, and they know that bags often hold food. Keep yours closed and close to your body.

The reserve also tells a human story, and it is much older than the park. In the sandstone hills, people carved out a series of caves. Some of these caves were probably used as shelter, and some may have served for storage or defence. Elsewhere in Yankari, archaeologists have studied old wells and the remains of iron smelting. Smelting is the process of heating rock in furnaces to draw out metal. These traces show that communities lived and worked in this landscape long before it became a reserve.

Today, most visitors explore by open safari vehicle with a guide. Early morning is often the best time. The light is soft, the air is cool, and the animals are moving. As the day warms, much of the wildlife retreats into shade. The late afternoon brings a second chance, when the heat breaks and animals return to the water. Between drives, the spring offers a welcome rest.

Take a moment to notice the trees. Along the river, the woodland grows thicker and greener, because the roots reach permanent water. Further away, the trees stand farther apart, and the grass takes over. You can read the land by its colour. Green means water is near. Gold means the soil is dry. The animals read the same signs, and they follow the green.

Yankari also faces real pressures. Poaching has long threatened its elephants. The reserve sits close to farmland and growing towns. Rangers and conservation groups have worked for years to protect the herds and the habitat. Every visitor who comes here helps in a small way. Tourism gives the reserve value to the people around it, and that value supports the effort to keep it wild.

Now look around you again. Notice the pale sand, the clear water, the tall trees along the bank. Listen for the birds, and perhaps for the bark of a baboon nearby. As you walk away from the spring, the air will grow drier and warmer. Somewhere out in that gold grass, an elephant may be walking toward the river at this very moment. Take your time. Yankari rewards those who move slowly.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book an early morning safari drive for the best sightings.
Visit in the dry season when animals gather near water.
Keep bags closed and food hidden from camp baboons.
Bring swimwear for a dip in Wikki Warm Spring.
Carry binoculars to watch elephants along the Gaji River.
