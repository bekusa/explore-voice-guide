The guide

The story

Cool air meets you at the mouth of the cave. Outside, the sun bakes the reddish limestone cliffs of the gorge. Inside, the temperature drops and the light fades to a soft grey. Below, the Arpa River runs over its stones, and its sound follows you into the rock. The floor is dry and dusty underfoot. That dryness matters. It is the reason this ordinary-looking cave became one of the most important archaeological sites in the Caucasus.

This is Areni-1, a cave near the village of Areni in Armenia's Vayots Dzor Province. Locals also call it the Birds' Cave, after the birds that nest in the cliffs around it. For a long time it was simply a shelter in the rock. People walked past it on the road south. Then, in the early twenty-first century, a team of Armenian and international archaeologists began to dig carefully into its layers of soil.

What they found changed the story of wine. Deep in the cave, they uncovered a shallow clay basin set into the floor. It sloped toward a large clay jar buried beside it. Around these lay grape seeds, pressed grape skins, withered vines, and fragments of drinking vessels. Together, these objects form a full production system. Scientists date it to roughly six thousand years ago, in the Copper Age, the period when people first began to work metal.

Here is how the system probably worked. People spread the grapes in the clay basin and crushed them with their feet. The juice ran down into the buried jar. There, in the cool and steady air of the cave, it fermented into wine. Chemical tests on the clay found traces of a red pigment that comes from grapes. This suggests the wine here was red. It is the oldest known winery that archaeologists have found anywhere in the world.

People made wine in other places before this, and older traces exist elsewhere in the region. What makes Areni-1 different is scale and completeness. This was not one pot with a stain inside. It was a working installation, built to produce wine in quantity. That means someone planned it. Someone grew the grapes, harvested them, and organised the work. In other words, a community here was investing serious effort in wine long before the first cities rose in Mesopotamia.

Why make wine in a cave, and not in a village? The answer may be ritual. Archaeologists found human burials in the same cave. Some skulls lay inside clay jars. One of them, from a child, still held preserved traces of brain tissue, because the dry air protected it. Many researchers think the wine was tied to ceremonies for the dead. So this was probably not a tavern. It was more likely a sacred space.

Now for the second surprise. In another part of the cave, the team found a shoe. It is a single leather shoe, made from one piece of cowhide and laced with a leather cord. Someone had stuffed it with grass, perhaps to hold its shape or to keep the foot warm. It is about five and a half thousand years old. At the time of its discovery, it was the oldest known leather shoe in the world.

The shoe is small, and it looks strangely modern. If you saw it in a shop window, you might not look twice. It is similar to the soft leather shoes that country people in parts of Europe and the Caucasus wore until quite recently. That is the strange thing about Areni-1. Objects from six thousand years ago feel close and familiar. The original shoe is not kept here. It is held in Yerevan, the Armenian capital.

Look around the cave again. The dry floor, the stable temperature, and the layers of dung from animals that sheltered here over centuries created a natural seal. Organic material usually rots quickly. Here, grapes, straw, leather, and rope survived for thousands of years. This is why the cave can tell a story that most sites cannot. Excavation work has continued in stages, and parts of the cave are still being studied.

Step back outside and look at the landscape. The gorge narrows here, with steep cliffs on both sides. The river brings water through a dry, sunny valley. Those conditions suit grapevines very well. Hot days, cool nights, and stony soil have shaped wine in this valley for a very long time. The grape most linked with the area today is called Areni, a dark-skinned variety that takes its name from the nearby village.

That village is only a short drive away. Its roadside stalls sell homemade wine in reused plastic bottles, alongside dried fruit and fruit leather called sujukh. Several modern wineries also work in the area. Every autumn, Areni holds a wine festival that brings visitors from across Armenia. So the tradition did not stop in the cave. It moved into village cellars and family courtyards, and it continues there today.

Many visitors combine this cave with Noravank, a medieval monastery a little further up a side gorge. The two places make a good pair. In the cave, you see where people made wine six thousand years ago. At the monastery, you see carved stone and church walls from a much later Christian Armenia. Between them runs the same red rock, the same river, and the same vineyards.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the cave entrance. Feel the cool air on your face from inside, and the warm air from the valley behind you. Listen to the river below. Somewhere in the dust under your feet, people once crushed grapes with their bare feet and poured the juice into clay. As you walk back down toward the road, look at the vineyards along the valley. They grow in the same soil, under the same sun.
