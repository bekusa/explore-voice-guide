The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind comes straight off the Sea of Japan, and it carries salt and the cry of gulls. Above you, white cables run up into the sky like the strings of a giant harp. On a foggy morning their tops vanish completely. The steel seems to climb into nothing at all. This is the Russky Bridge, and it is one of the longest bridges of its kind anywhere on Earth.

The water below has a borrowed name. Locals call this strait the Eastern Bosphorus, after the famous strait in Istanbul. The bay that cuts into the heart of Vladivostok is called the Golden Horn, another name taken from Istanbul. Russian sailors in the nineteenth century saw a likeness between the two harbours. The names stayed. So you are standing on a bridge between two Bosphorus straits, half a world apart.

Now look at the shape of the structure. This is a cable-stayed bridge. That means the road deck does not hang from two great curving cables, as on a classic suspension bridge. Instead, straight cables run directly from the towers down to the deck, fanning out on each side. Engineers like this design for very long spans. It is stiff and strong, and it suits a windy, exposed place like this one.

Here is the number that matters. The main span, the open stretch between the two towers, measures 1,104 metres. When the bridge opened in 2012, that was the longest cable-stayed span in the world. It overtook the previous record holder in China by only a small margin. Think about that for a moment... more than a kilometre of road deck, held up only by cables, high above open sea water.

The two towers carry the whole load. Each one rises roughly 320 metres, which makes them taller than many skyscrapers. They are shaped like a giant letter A, with two legs that lean together and meet near the top. That shape gives them stability against the wind. From a distance, the pair looks almost delicate. Up close, the concrete legs are enormous, grey and smooth, and they stand on foundations sunk deep into the seabed.

Why build such a bridge here at all? For most of its history, Russky Island was a closed military zone. It guarded the sea approaches to the city, and it was reached only by boat. Then Vladivostok was chosen to host the summit of APEC, the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum, in 2012. The meetings were held on the island itself. A permanent link to the mainland suddenly became essential.

The bridge was part of a much larger building effort. In the same years, the city gained a second large cable-stayed bridge, the Golden Bridge, across the Golden Horn bay in the centre. New roads were cut through the hills. On the island, a large modern campus rose for the summit. After the leaders went home, that campus became the main home of the Far Eastern Federal University. Thousands of students now cross this bridge as part of ordinary life.

Here is something many visitors miss. The engineers had to plan for very harsh weather. Winters here are cold and dry, with strong winds from the continent. In some winters the strait freezes, and ice can press against the tower foundations. Summers bring heavy sea fog and damp heat. The bridge has to handle all of this, year after year. So the clean white lines you see are the result of very careful work against a difficult climate.

And here is a second surprise. In winter, when the ice is thick, you may see small dark figures far out on the frozen strait. These are ice fishermen. They sit for hours beside holes in the ice, bundled against the cold, often within sight of the towers. It is a long local tradition in Vladivostok. The contrast is striking — a quiet, old way of fishing beneath one of the most modern structures in the region.

The island on the far side has its own layers. Russky Island was once part of a wide ring of coastal defences around the city. Old forts and gun batteries still sit among the trees and on the headlands. Some can be visited today. Today the island also holds the large Primorsky Aquarium, the university campus, and long stretches of rocky shore. Many people cross the bridge simply to walk the coastline and look back at the city.

The bridge changes with the light, and that is part of its appeal. In clear weather the sea turns a deep blue, and the white cables stand out sharply. At sunset the towers can glow pink and gold. After dark, the bridge is lit, and the lines of the cables trace patterns in the night. In fog, which is common in early summer, the whole structure seems to float. Each visit can look quite different.

For the best view, many people go to the mainland shore or the hills nearby. Look for a spot where you can see both towers and the full sweep of the deck at once. From side angles, the cables seem to cross and overlap, and the bridge looks like a woven fan. From straight on, the towers line up, and the scale becomes easier to feel. Take a moment to try more than one angle.

Before you go, look up once more. Follow a single cable from the deck all the way to the top of a tower. Notice how far your eye has to travel. Listen to the traffic passing overhead, and to the wind moving across the strait. A few years ago, this water could only be crossed by boat. Now it is a daily route for students, families and fishermen. As you walk away, turn back once — and watch the towers grow quietly smaller against the sea.

Practical tips

Come after dark to see the bridge lit up.
