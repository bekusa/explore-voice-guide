The guide

The story

Listen first. The dry teak leaves crackle under every footstep, like paper being folded. Somewhere below, water moves over stone. The air smells of dust and warm bark. Then, from across a ravine, a sharp bark breaks the quiet. It is a sambar deer, sounding an alarm. In this forest, that sound often means one thing. Something with stripes is walking nearby. Welcome to Panna National Park, in the north of Madhya Pradesh, in the heart of central India.

This park sits in the Bundelkhand region, a land of flat-topped hills, deep gorges and long, hot summers. The ground here is built from ancient Vindhyan sandstone, laid down in layers that you can see in the cliffs. The landscape rises in steps. There are high plateaus, then sudden drops, then quiet valleys far below. From a high point, the forest looks like a rumpled green and brown blanket, cut through by one long, shining line of water.

That line is the Ken River. It runs roughly north through the park, on its way to join the Yamuna. The Ken carves gorges through the rock, and it feeds pools that stay wet long after the rains have gone. In the dry season, almost every animal in the park depends on it. Watch the riverbanks. You may see mugger crocodiles resting on the rocks, and in some stretches of this river, the long, thin-snouted gharial, a rare fish-eating crocodile.

The forest itself is mostly dry deciduous. That means many trees drop their leaves in the hot months to save water. Teak grows here, along with a slender tree that locals call kardhai, which clings to the rocky slopes. In summer, the canopy thins and the light turns gold and hard. After the monsoon, the whole place turns green in a matter of weeks. The same hillside can look like two different countries, depending on when you stand in front of it.

Long before it was a park, much of this land served as a hunting ground for the rulers of local princely states, including Panna itself. The forest was protected, in its way, because royalty wanted game to hunt. After Indian independence, those old rules faded. Panna became a national park in 1981, and it was declared a tiger reserve in 1994. On paper, the tigers were safe.

Here is the part of the story that many visitors do not know. By 2009, Panna had lost all of its tigers. Poaching had emptied the forest, quietly, over several years. Officials and scientists had to admit that a tiger reserve no longer held a single wild tiger. It was a national embarrassment, and a warning for conservation across India. Many people believed the park would never recover.

Then came an experiment. Beginning in 2009, tigers were brought here from other reserves in Madhya Pradesh, including Bandhavgarh and Kanha. Female tigers came first, and then a male. The animals wore radio collars, and teams tracked them day and night. The newcomers had to learn a strange forest. They had to find water, prey and territory. And then, slowly, cubs were born. The forest began to fill again.

One of the best-known chapters involves the first male tiger. Soon after his release, he walked out of the park and kept going, heading back in the direction of the land he came from. Forest staff followed him on foot for weeks, through villages and fields, before he was finally brought back. He stayed, and he fathered cubs. Today the Panna recovery is studied as one of the clearest examples of a tiger population brought back from zero.

Tigers are not the only residents. Leopards move through the rocky slopes, often harder to spot than the tigers. Sloth bears shuffle along at dusk, searching for termites and fruit. Nilgai, India's largest antelope, stand in open grassland. Look for chinkara, a small gazelle, and the four-horned antelope, a shy animal found almost only in South Asia. On the cliffs above the Ken, several species of vulture nest, perched on narrow ledges high above the water.

There is another surprise under your feet. The Panna district is known across India for diamonds. A large diamond mine operates not far from the park, and for generations, local people have searched the gravels of this region for stones. So this is a place where two very different kinds of value sit side by side. One is buried in the ground. The other walks through the forest on four padded feet.

The park also carries old human traces. In the rocky shelters of the region, there are prehistoric paintings, made with red and white pigments. Nearby, Pandav Falls drops into a deep green pool beside a set of caves. According to local legend, the five Pandava brothers from the Mahabharata epic sheltered here during their exile. Historians cannot confirm the story, but the name has stayed, and so has the feeling of the place, cool and shaded on a hot day.

Panna is also part of a larger protected landscape. In 2020, UNESCO added the Panna Biosphere Reserve to its world network. This recognises that people, villages, rivers and wildlife share this land together. That balance is not always easy. A major river-linking project has been planned on the Ken, and it has raised concerns about the future of parts of the park. For now, the river still runs as it has for a very long time.

So pause for a moment where you are. Notice the colour of the rock, the angle of the light on the leaves, the distance of the next bird call. Each set of pugmarks in the dust, each alarm call from a deer, belongs to a forest that was almost empty not long ago. As you move on, keep listening. The quiet here is not silence. It is a forest that came back, and it is still telling you where everything is.
