The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across a wide, flat plain, and somewhere a tractor hums in a distant field. Under your feet the ground rises gently into a low, grassy mound. It does not look like much at first... a swelling in the farmland, with mountains standing along the horizon. But you are standing on the remains of a royal city. Nearly three thousand years ago, this was Samʾal, the capital of a small and ambitious kingdom.

Today the mound is known by its Turkish name, Zincirli Höyük. A höyük is a settlement mound — a hill built up slowly by people living, building, and rebuilding in the same place over many centuries. The name Zincirli means something like "with chains" in Turkish. The ancient name, Samʾal, is much older. It appears in Assyrian records and in inscriptions carved by the city's own kings, so we know how the people here named their home.

Look around at the setting. To the west rise the Amanus Mountains, which local people call the Nur Mountains. The plain around İslahiye is a natural corridor between the Mediterranean coast and the interior of Anatolia and Syria. Armies, traders, and herders all passed through this valley. A city here could control movement along that route. That position gave Samʾal its wealth — and also made it a target for powerful neighbours.

Samʾal flourished in the Iron Age, roughly the tenth to the seventh centuries before the Common Era. Historians describe it as one of the Syro-Hittite or Neo-Hittite states. These were small kingdoms that grew up after the great Hittite Empire of central Anatolia collapsed. Their rulers mixed several traditions. At Samʾal, Luwian, Aramaean, and Phoenician influences all met. The Aramaeans were a people of the Syrian region whose language, Aramaic, later spread across much of the Middle East.

The shape of the city is its first surprise. Samʾal was almost perfectly round. A double ring of fortification walls enclosed the lower town, with many towers set along them and gates leading in from outside. At the centre, on higher ground, stood the citadel with its palaces. If you walk the edges of the site, try to picture that circle. Few ancient cities were planned with such clear geometry, and the plan is still visible from the air.

The gates were not plain openings. Their walls were lined with carved stone slabs, called orthostats, standing upright like panels. They showed hunters, warriors, musicians, mythical creatures, and gods. Great stone lions guarded some entrances. For a traveller arriving here, these images announced the power of the king before any official spoke a word. Much of this sculpture now lives in museums, but the gate positions can still be understood on the ground.

The modern story of the site begins in the late nineteenth century. German expeditions excavated here in several seasons between the 1880s and the early 1900s. The physician and anthropologist Felix von Luschan was a central figure, working with the architect Robert Koldewey, who later became famous for his work at Babylon. They uncovered the gates, the city walls, and the citadel palaces. Many of their finds travelled to Berlin, and others went to Istanbul.

The kings of Samʾal left their own words in stone. One ruler, Kilamuwa, commissioned an inscription in the ninth century before the Common Era. In it, he boasts that earlier kings achieved little, while he brought prosperity to his people. He claims that men who had never owned a sheep now owned flocks. Another king, Bar-Rakib, presented himself as a loyal servant of Assyria. These texts show a local dynasty trying to survive between larger powers.

That survival did not last forever. In the eighth century before the Common Era, Assyria grew into the dominant empire of the region. Samʾal first became a vassal, paying tribute, and later it was absorbed as an Assyrian province. One famous find from this site is a victory stele of the Assyrian king Esarhaddon. It shows the king towering over small kneeling captives. It was carved to celebrate his campaigns, including against Egypt, and it was set up here as a message.

Now the second surprise, and it is a recent one. Since 2006, a team from the University of Chicago has excavated at Zincirli. In 2008 they found a small carved stone in the lower town. It belonged to a royal official named Katumuwa. The inscription asks that offerings be made for his soul, which it says lives in this stone. Scholars found this remarkable. It suggests a belief in a soul separate from the body, at a time and place where many expected otherwise.

The Katumuwa stele shows the official seated at a table with food and drink, ready for a feast. Researchers think a small room once held the stone, and that family members gathered there to eat and remember him. Think about that for a moment. Somewhere beneath this soil, people shared meals with the memory of a man they had loved. This private, domestic detail makes the ancient city feel close and human.

The excavations also show that people lived here long before the Iron Age kings. Deeper layers reach back into the Bronze Age, so the mound holds many periods stacked on top of one another. Each layer represents generations of houses, streets, and rubbish. Archaeologists read these layers like pages, one on top of another. As you walk, you are moving across the top page of a very thick book that is still being studied.

So much of Samʾal is no longer here in physical form. The lions and carved slabs stand in museum halls far away. What remains on site is subtler: the curve of the ground, the shape of the citadel, the trenches where teams have worked, and the same wide view the kings once had. That honesty is part of the experience. You see a place, not a display, and you supply the walls with your imagination.

Before you leave, turn slowly and follow the line of the mountains. Notice the fields, the roads to İslahiye, the farm sounds carrying over the plain. This valley still serves as a passage, as it did when caravans came through the city gates. As you walk back down the slope, listen to the soil under your shoes. Somewhere below, the stones of Samʾal are still waiting to be read.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and open trenches.
Bring water and sun protection; the mound has little shade.
Visit museums in Gaziantep, Istanbul, or Berlin for the sculptures.
Walk the mound's edge to sense the city's round plan.
Respect fenced excavation areas and do not climb into trenches.
