The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the open sea here with nothing to stop it. It carries salt, and the cry of gulls, and the dull thump of waves against bare rock. Under your feet the tarmac curves and climbs. Ahead of you a ribbon of concrete rises steeply into the grey-blue sky. From certain angles it seems to end in mid-air. This is Storseisundet Bridge, the tallest and longest crossing on the Atlantic Ocean Road.

The road links the island of Averøy with the mainland. It hops from islet to islet across a scatter of low skerries. Skerries are small rocky islands, often barely higher than the tide. The route runs for about eight kilometres. Several bridges carry it over the gaps between these rocks. Most of them are short and low. Storseisundet is different. It is the one that lifts the road high above the water, and it is the one people come to see.

Look at its shape. The bridge does not run flat. It climbs in a sharp arc, then twists slightly as it comes down. From the approach, the upper deck seems to drop away into nothing. That illusion has earned it a nickname among drivers — the drunk bridge. Up close, of course, the road is perfectly safe. But standing at the right spot, you can see why the curve unsettles people. Your eye simply cannot find where the road goes next.

The height has a practical purpose. Fishing boats and other vessels need to pass through the sound below. A low bridge would have blocked them. So the engineers raised the deck in a long cantilever design. In a cantilever bridge, the main sections reach out from strong supports, like arms stretched from the shoulders. They meet in the middle. This lets the span cross open water without a forest of pillars standing in the current.

For centuries, this coast was reached mainly by boat. The people of Averøy lived by the sea, and especially by fishing. Travel to the mainland meant crossing water, often in rough weather. A fixed road across the skerries had been discussed for a long time. It was a bold idea. This is one of the most exposed stretches of the Norwegian coast. Storms roll in straight from the Norwegian Sea.

Work began in the early 1980s. Accounts from the construction describe a hard fight with the weather. Builders reportedly faced a dozen fierce storms during those years. Waves and wind repeatedly interrupted the work. Imagine pouring concrete and setting heavy steel on rocks like these, with spray flying over you. The road finally opened to traffic in 1989. For the communities on Averøy, it changed daily life. A boat trip became a short drive.

Here is something many visitors do not know. The road was not built mainly for tourists. It was built for local people, for trade, and for fishing communities. Its fame came later. Photographs and filmed car advertisements spread images of the curving bridge around the world. Today it is part of Norway's National Tourist Routes, a programme of scenic roads. That label came long after the concrete set. The bridge was a working road first.

A second surprise is how the bridge behaves in different weather. On a calm summer day, it looks gentle, almost elegant. The water is pale green over the shallows, and the light stays soft for hours. In autumn and winter, the same place turns wild. Waves break over the low sections of road nearby. Some people drive here deliberately in storms, just to watch the sea throw itself at the rocks. Locals treat that as a kind of entertainment.

Now take a moment to look below the bridge. You may see people standing on the rocks, or along walkways, holding fishing rods. Fishing is popular along the Atlantic Ocean Road. The currents through these sounds bring fish close to the shore. Watch the water itself as well. Where the sea squeezes between the islets, it moves fast. You can often see swirls and lines on the surface, marking where the currents meet.

Look at the materials. The bridge is made of reinforced concrete. Its surface is plain and grey, with no decoration. That plainness is part of its character. Against the dark rock, the green sea, and the changing sky, the simple form stands out clearly. Notice the guardrails as you walk or drive across. They frame the view in thin lines. Beyond them, the horizon is often just open water, with nothing between you and the far north.

In 2005, Norwegians voted the Atlantic Ocean Road their construction of the century, according to a widely reported national poll. That says something about how people here see it. It is not a cathedral or a palace. It is a road across rocks. Yet it connected scattered communities to each other and to the wider country. For a coastal nation shaped by the sea, that kind of connection carries real meaning. The bridge became a symbol of that.

If you have time, explore beyond this single span. Nearby rest areas offer viewpoints, and some have paths or walkways over the rocks. From those points, you can see the whole sweep of the road at once. The bridges rise and fall like stitches across the water. Many travellers find that the best view of Storseisundet is not from the deck at all. It is from a distance, where its strange curve becomes clear.

The weather can change quickly on this coast. One moment there is sunshine on the water. The next, a squall moves in from the sea and the bridge fades into mist. Dress for wind and rain, even on a bright day. Take care near the edges of the rocks, which can be slippery with spray and seaweed. The sea here deserves respect. People who live along this coast have always known that.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and face the open water. Feel the wind on your face, and taste the salt on your lips. Behind you, cars climb the long arc of the bridge and vanish over its crest. Below, the current keeps running between the rocks, as it did long before the road. When you drive away, glance back once. From a distance, the bridge will seem to end in the sky again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack a windproof, waterproof jacket even on sunny days.
Use the nearby rest areas to view the full curve.
Visit in a storm only if you stay well back from waves.
Wear shoes with good grip on wet, seaweed-covered rocks.
Never stop your car on the bridge deck for photos.
