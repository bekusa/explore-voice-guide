The guide

The story

A single brick tower rises straight from the flat ground, dark and weathered, with the faint ghosts of carved figures still clinging to its worn sides. Around it lie open grass, low broken walls, and the still water of an old moat. This is one of the oldest places in all of Sukhothai, standing here long before the great city walls were ever raised.

Wat Phra Phai Luang sits in the northern zone of the historical park, well beyond the main old city. A shallow moat once circled the entire compound, marking it off as sacred ground. Today you cross the calm green water on a low causeway and step into a wide, quiet field scattered with the brick remains of buildings that have largely fallen.

The story of this temple reaches back before the Thai kingdom itself. The first shrine on this spot was built by the Khmer, the great empire centered far to the east at Angkor. It was raised during the reign of King Jayavarman the Seventh, around the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, when Khmer power still reached deep into this land.

The Khmer set up three tall towers, called prang, standing in a row side by side. They served as Hindu shrines at first, and later became places of Buddhist worship as belief slowly shifted across the region. Of the original three towers, only one still stands close to its full height today. The other two have crumbled down to their square brick bases.

Look closely at the surface of the standing prang. Patches of old stucco still cling to the brick, holding the shapes of figures and fine patterns that were pressed into the soft plaster long ago. A great deal has dissolved away under centuries of monsoon rain, but just enough survives to show how richly the whole tower was once decorated.

Before the main walled city grew up to the south, this northern area may well have been an early center of the whole settlement. That would help explain the care and labor spent on the site. For a time, Wat Phra Phai Luang was likely counted among the most important religious places in the entire region around the young capital.

In front of the towers lie the broken remains of a mondop, a square brick building that once held large Buddha images. The walls stand only waist high or lower now. Within and around them you can still make out seated and standing figures, some reduced to little more than rough brick cores where the smooth outer plaster has long since fallen away.

Scattered among the ruins are the traces of large stucco Buddhas in several different poses. The sculptors of Sukhothai were known across the region for a smooth, flowing style, with calm oval faces, arched brows, and soft rounded curves. Even in these worn fragments, that gentle, unhurried quality still comes clearly through in the surviving pieces.

The mix of styles is the real lesson of this quiet place. Heavy, blocky Khmer towers stand close beside the lighter, more graceful Thai forms that came after them. As you walk the grounds, you move physically between two great ages of art, watching one tradition give way to the next across a single open patch of land.

The setting itself is calm and wide open. Water lies flat and still in the old moat, sometimes dotted with lilies. Grass grows thick over the low brick walls and footings. Trees stand in loose ranks at the edges of the field. Because the temple sits well apart from the busiest part of the park, it is often almost completely empty of other people. The only sounds are the wind in the leaves and the occasional call of a bird crossing the field.

With no crowds to hurry you along, you can walk a slow, full circle around the standing tower. Study the worn stucco from every angle, run your eye along the square outlines of halls that no longer have walls or roofs, and follow the line of the old moat. The deep silence of the place helps you picture what once rose here.

Before you leave, look back once at the lone tower standing dark against the open sky. It has outlasted the powerful empire that first raised it and the proud kingdom that followed after. Wat Phra Phai Luang keeps its very long memory quietly now, holding it in weathered brick, fading stucco, and the still surface of old water.
