The guide

The story

Listen first. A light wind moves through the grass, and somewhere below you water laps against a quiet shore. Fields roll down towards a narrow fjord, with low hills of pine and smooth grey rock behind. It looks like ordinary Norwegian farmland. Yet more than a thousand years ago, this gentle slope held one of the earliest towns in Scandinavia. Here, ships came in from distant coasts, and people from many lands met to trade.

You are standing at Kaupang, a short drive from the small coastal town of Stavern, in the county of Vestfold. The name comes from the Old Norse word for a market place. Many towns in Norway once carried a similar name. This one, however, is special. Archaeologists consider it one of the very first urban settlements in the country. It belongs to the Viking Age, the period roughly between the late eighth and the eleventh centuries.

Here is the first surprise. The shoreline you see today is not the shoreline the Vikings knew. After the last Ice Age, the enormous weight of the glaciers lifted away. Since then, the land in this part of Norway has been slowly rising, little by little, century after century. In Viking times the sea came higher up these slopes. So the beach where traders pulled up their boats now lies on dry ground, some way back from the water's edge.

This place also appears in writing — and that is rare for Viking Scandinavia. In the late ninth century, a Norwegian traveller named Ohthere visited the court of King Alfred of England. He described his voyages, and his account was written down in Old English. He told of sailing south along the Norwegian coast to a market place he called Sciringes heal. Many historians connect that name with Skiringssal, the old name for this district around Kaupang.

Archaeology has filled in much of the picture. Researchers believe the town was founded around the year 800. It seems to have been planned, with plots of land laid out along the shore. Craftsmen worked here in small wooden houses. Over the following decades, merchants and visitors came and went with the sailing seasons. Then, sometime in the tenth century, the town faded away. Nobody knows the full reason, though changing trade routes and the rising land may both have played a part.

The finds tell a story of long distances. Excavations uncovered silver coins from the Islamic world, minted far away in the lands of the caliphs. Archaeologists also found glass beads, pieces of amber from the Baltic, and pottery from the continent. Many objects were cut and weighed, because silver was often valued by weight, not by its stamp. Picture a trader at a small balance scale, measuring a broken bracelet against tiny lead weights. That simple scene happened here, many times.

Local materials travelled outward too. Soapstone, a soft stone that is easy to carve, was shaped into cooking pots and moulds. Whetstones for sharpening knives and tools came from quarries in the Norwegian interior. Iron came from inland areas as well. Kaupang sat between these northern resources and the markets of Denmark, the Baltic, and beyond. In a way, this quiet slope was a meeting point between forest, mountain, and sea.

The second surprise lies around you in the landscape. The people of Kaupang did not only live here — many of them were buried here. Archaeologists estimate that the area held several hundred graves, perhaps around a thousand. Some were simple. Others contained boats, weapons, jewellery, and tools. Several burial grounds surround the old town site. A few low mounds can still be traced in the ground, if you look carefully at the shape of the land.

The modern study of Kaupang has a long story of its own. In the mid twentieth century, the archaeologist Charlotte Blindheim led many years of excavation here, especially among the graves. Later, around the turn of the millennium, a major research project from the University of Oslo returned to the site. That team dug into the town area itself. Their work confirmed the planned plots, the workshops, and the sheer variety of goods passing through.

Not far away stood a centre of power. On a nearby rise at Huseby, archaeologists found traces of a large hall from the same era. Many scholars think a chieftain or king ruled the district from there. The ruler would have protected the market and taken a share of its wealth. Close by, the area also had an important assembly place and probably a sacred site. Trade, law, and belief seem to have gathered together in this one corner of Vestfold.

Today, the visitor area helps you imagine this lost town. During the summer season, a small exhibition and reconstructed buildings show how people may have lived and worked. Sometimes you will see demonstrations of old crafts — weaving, wood carving, or work with metal. These are interpretations, not original buildings. Still, they help the eye. Walk slowly, and try to place the wooden houses along the old shoreline in your mind.

Notice the sounds and smells as you move. There is the salt air from the fjord and the scent of cut grass in summer. Seabirds call over the water. On a calm day, the surface of the fjord is almost still, and the reflections of the trees lie flat upon it. A thousand years ago, this same water carried smoke from hearths, the noise of hammers, and voices speaking many languages. The setting has changed less than you might expect.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look down towards the water. Trace the gentle line where the old beach once ran, higher than the shore today. Think of the boats drawn up there, the scales and silver, the long journeys behind each object. Then look at the fields again. They seem simple now. But under your feet, the ground still holds the outline of a town that helped connect Norway to the wider world.
