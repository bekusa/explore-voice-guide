The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the quay and listen. Halyards tap against masts. Gulls call over the old port. Under your feet the stone is worn smooth, and the air carries salt, diesel, and a little frying oil from the cafés nearby. In front of you a long, low building sits along the water like a ship tied up at its berth. This is the Aquarium of Genoa, and it begins right where the sea meets the city.

The building's shape is not an accident. It stretches along an old pier, with the open harbour on one side and the waterfront promenade on the other. Its lines echo the cargo ships and ferries that still move in and out of this port every day. From the outside it looks almost industrial. Inside, it holds thousands of living animals from oceans and rivers across the world, arranged across dozens of tanks.

To understand why an aquarium stands here, go back to 1992. That year marked five hundred years since Christopher Columbus crossed the Atlantic. Columbus was born in Genoa, and the city used the anniversary to hold a major international exhibition. The old port was in poor shape back then. Warehouses stood empty, and the waterfront was cut off from daily life. The exhibition gave Genoa a reason to open the harbour back up to its people.

The architect behind that transformation was Renzo Piano, a son of Genoa who went on to design buildings around the world. He reshaped this stretch of the old port, and the aquarium was a central piece of his plan. He worked on it together with an American team that specialised in aquariums. Look to your left and right as you arrive. Many of the structures you see along this waterfront came from that same moment of renewal.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. The aquarium was built to last only as long as the exhibition, at least in the eyes of some. Instead, it became permanent and grew. Today it is counted among the largest aquariums in Europe. A building that started life as one part of a festival now draws visitors to the port year after year — long after the exhibition pavilions closed.

Once you step inside, the light changes completely. The bright Ligurian sun disappears. In its place comes a soft blue glow from the tanks, and the noise of the harbour drops to a low hum of pumps and moving water. Your eyes need a moment to adjust. Take that moment. Many people rush ahead, but the slow walk through the first dim rooms sets the mood for everything that follows.

The route leads you through a series of habitats, each built to show a different corner of the water world. You move from the Mediterranean, the sea right outside these walls, to coral reefs, tropical rivers, and cold southern waters. Watch for the moments when a tank opens above you or wraps around you. The designers wanted you to feel close to the animals, not separated from them by a long distance of glass.

The Mediterranean tanks deserve more attention than they usually get. Visitors often hurry past them toward the sharks or the dolphins. But these are the fish and creatures that live just beyond the harbour wall. The groupers, the sea bream, the small darting fish among the rocks — Genoese fishermen have known them for centuries. Seeing them here is a quiet reminder that this city has always looked to the sea for food and work.

Then come the larger animals. Sharks glide in slow circles, their movement almost lazy until they turn. Rays drift along the bottom like grey blankets. Seals and penguins bring a different energy, with sudden bursts of speed and splashing. Further on, the jellyfish rooms feel almost like an art gallery. Their pale bodies pulse slowly in lit water, and children and adults alike tend to stop and fall silent here.

The second surprise sits at the far end of the building. In 2013, the aquarium opened a new pavilion for cetaceans — the family of dolphins and whales. Renzo Piano returned to design it, more than two decades after his first work here. The pavilion stands along the pier with open views toward the harbour. Inside, wide pools give the dolphins space, and you can watch them from viewing areas both above and below the water line.

Dolphins in aquariums are a subject people feel strongly about, and that debate exists here too. Modern aquariums present themselves as places for research, conservation, and education, and this one is no exception. You will see panels about protecting the sea and about the pressures facing marine life. Whatever you think, it is worth reading them. They help you see the animals as part of a wider ocean story.

When you come back out into daylight, take a look at the rest of the old port. Close by, a large glass sphere floats over the water. It holds a small tropical environment of plants and animals, and it is another Piano design. Nearby rises a tall white structure with arms that look like a ship's crane. These shapes all belong to the same waterfront plan, and together they tell the story of a port that reinvented itself.

So pause here for a moment before you move on. Behind you, the long body of the aquarium rests on its pier. Ahead, the old city climbs the hill in a tangle of narrow lanes and tall, faded houses. Listen again to the halyards and the gulls. The sea you just walked through, tank by tank, is right here — lapping at the stone, a few steps from your feet.
