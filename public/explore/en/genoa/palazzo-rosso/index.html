The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on Via Garibaldi and look up. The street is narrow, and the light falls in a thin band between tall façades of pale stone and painted plaster. Then your eye lands on one wall that glows a deep, rusty red. That colour gives this building its name. Palazzo Rosso simply means the Red Palace, and on a bright afternoon the walls almost seem warm to the touch.

The street under your feet has its own story. In the sixteenth century, Genoa's richest families laid out a new straight road above the tangled medieval lanes of the old port. They called it the Strada Nuova, the New Street. Today it carries the name of Garibaldi, the nineteenth-century hero of Italian unification. Back then, it was a showroom. Each family built a palace here to tell the world how much money and taste they had.

Palazzo Rosso came late to that showroom. It was built in the 1670s for two brothers of the Brignole-Sale family, Rodolfo and Gio. Francesco. The design is credited to the architect Pietro Antonio Corradi. By then, the street was already lined with older palaces. So the brothers needed something that would hold its own... and a bold red front, set against its quieter neighbours, did exactly that.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Genoa's great palaces were part of an official system called the Rolli. These were public lists of private homes, sorted by rank. When a pope, a king, or an ambassador visited the city, the government chose a house from the list. The family was obliged to host the guest. A palace on this street was not only a home — it was also a kind of state guesthouse. In 2006, UNESCO listed the Strade Nuove and the Rolli palaces as World Heritage.

Walk through the entrance and notice the shift in temperature. The air in the atrium is cooler, and sounds from the street fall away. Genoese palaces were built around this movement. You pass from the street, into a shaded hall, then up grand stairs toward the light. The owners wanted guests to feel the climb. Every step took you further from the noise of trade and closer to the private world of the family.

The upper floors hold the real surprise. Several rooms are covered in ceiling frescoes painted in the late seventeenth century by two leading Genoese artists, Gregorio De Ferrari and Domenico Piola. A fresco is a painting made on wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall itself. Look for the rooms of the four seasons. Spring and Summer are by De Ferrari, Autumn and Winter by Piola. Figures tumble across the ceilings, clouds open, and painted architecture seems to lift the roof away.

Those ceilings carry a hidden scar. During the Second World War, bombing badly damaged parts of the palace, and some frescoes were lost or broken. What you see today includes careful restoration. If you look closely in good light, you can sometimes notice small differences in texture or tone. They are quiet reminders that this building nearly did not survive the twentieth century.

Now to the paintings, because the Brignole-Sale family collected with real ambition. The best-known works here are portraits by Anthony van Dyck, the Flemish painter who worked in Genoa as a young man in the 1620s. He painted members of this family in full aristocratic splendour. Look for Anton Giulio Brignole-Sale on horseback, and for Paolina Adorno, his wife, in a gown so rich you can almost hear the silk move. Van Dyck later became court painter in England, but Genoa shaped his style.

The collection reaches well beyond Van Dyck. You will find works by Guido Reni and Guercino, two major painters of the Italian Baroque, the bold and dramatic style of the seventeenth century. There is a dramatic Judith by Paolo Veronese from Venice. And there is a portrait of a young man attributed to Albrecht Dürer, the great German artist. These pieces show a family that looked across all of Europe for art, not just across the street.

How did a private palace become a public museum? In 1874, Maria Brignole-Sale, the Duchess of Galliera, gave the palace and its collections to the city of Genoa. Her gift was generous and very deliberate. She wanted the family's treasures to stay together and stay in Genoa. Today the palace is part of the Strada Nuova Museums, together with Palazzo Bianco, the White Palace, across the street, and Palazzo Doria-Tursi next door.

Here is the second surprise. In the 1950s, the modernist architect Franco Albini reworked parts of the museum. Albini was known for clean lines, steel, and glass, a very different taste from Baroque gold and clouds. Yet his displays sit quietly beside the old rooms, and they still influence how museums are designed. On the upper floor, an apartment he designed for the museum's director shows this mix of old walls and mid-century furniture.

If you can, keep climbing. Stairs lead up toward the roof, where a small terrace opens over the city. From here the view changes completely. Genoa stacks itself up the hills in layers of slate roofs, church towers, and pastel walls. Below, the port spreads toward the sea, with cranes and ferries on the water. After the dense rooms below, the open air feels like a deep breath.

As you look out, think about the brothers who built this place. They could not see the future from this height, but they could see their city — its ships, its banks, its rival families. The palace was their message to all of it. Three and a half centuries later, the message still stands, now open to anyone who walks in from the street.

When you step back onto Via Garibaldi, turn once more toward the red façade. Notice how the colour changes as clouds pass or as the sun moves lower. Listen to the footsteps echoing off the stone and the voices drifting from the other palaces. You are standing in a street that was built to impress. Take a slow walk along it, and let each doorway show you a little more.

What to look for

The deep red façade glowing against paler neighbouring palaces.
Four seasons frescoes by De Ferrari and Piola overhead.
Van Dyck's equestrian portrait of Anton Giulio Brignole-Sale.
Subtle restoration patches in war-damaged ceiling frescoes.
Albini's mid-century furniture inside the director's apartment.
Before you go

Practical tips

Check for a combined Strada Nuova Museums ticket covering three palaces.
Visit Palazzo Bianco across the street on the same day.
Save energy for the climb to the rooftop terrace.
Come on a clear day for long views to the port.
Look up often — the best art is on the ceilings.
