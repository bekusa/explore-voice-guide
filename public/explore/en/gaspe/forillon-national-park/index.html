The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the Gulf of Saint Lawrence and moves through spruce and fir. Below the cliffs, waves break against grey stone. Somewhere out on the water, a gull calls. The air smells of salt, resin, and cold seaweed. This is Forillon National Park, at the far eastern tip of the Gaspé Peninsula in Quebec. Here the land simply runs out, and the sea takes over.

That idea of an ending is very old here. The name Gaspé is generally traced to a word in the language of the Mi'gmaq, the Indigenous people of this coast. It is usually translated as land's end. The Mi'gmaq fished, hunted, and travelled these shores long before any European ship arrived. When you stand at the last cliff and look out at open water, the name feels less like a label and more like a plain description.

Forillon was created in 1970. It was the first national park established in Quebec, and it is managed by Parks Canada. The park is not large by Canadian standards, but it holds a lot in a small space. There are sea cliffs, sheltered coves, pebble beaches, salt marshes, mountain forest, and long views over two bodies of water: Gaspé Bay on one side and the Gulf on the other.

The ground under your feet has its own story. The northern side of the park is lined with tall limestone cliffs. The rock formed from layers of sediment on an ancient sea floor, hundreds of millions of years ago. Later, the layers were tilted and pushed upward. Today you can see them as long, slanted bands in the cliff faces. Look closely at the pale stone and you may notice how each layer is a little different in colour and texture.

Here is something many visitors miss. Forillon is the end point of a hiking route that starts far to the south. The Appalachian Trail runs along the mountains of the eastern United States. An international extension continues that path north into Canada. Its last stretch crosses the Gaspé Peninsula and finishes here, near Cap Gaspé. Some hikers arrive at this headland after weeks, even months, on foot. For them, this quiet point is the very end of the mountains.

The trail to Cap Gaspé follows the edge of the peninsula through forest, with glimpses of the sea between the trees. At the end stands a small white lighthouse above the cliffs. It is not a dramatic tower. It is modest, practical, built to guide ships around a dangerous point. The real view is beyond it: water on three sides, and on a clear day, a long horizon with nothing between you and the open Gulf.

The sea is the reason people lived here, and the reason the park exists in its present form. For generations, this coast lived from cod. Families caught the fish in small boats, then salted and dried it on wooden racks along the shore. The dried cod was shipped far away, to markets in Europe and beyond. At the Grande-Grave heritage site, inside the park, restored buildings show what that life looked like.

One of those buildings is an old general store, kept as it was when the fishing trade was active. Shelves hold goods that families would have bought on credit against their next catch. Nearby, a restored fisherman's house sits above a small cove. Walk through it slowly. The rooms are simple, the wood is worn, and the windows face the water. Everything about the house tells you where the family's attention was every day: on the sea and the weather.

There is a harder side to this story, and it deserves to be told. When the park was created, the people who lived inside its boundaries were required to leave. Many families lost homes, land, and fishing grounds their ancestors had worked. The expropriation caused deep pain in the local communities, and it is still remembered today. In later years, Parks Canada formally acknowledged that hurt. The heritage buildings you see are, in part, a way of keeping those lives visible.

Forillon also carries a quieter chapter from the Second World War. During that war, German submarines entered the Gulf of Saint Lawrence and attacked ships, some within sight of the Quebec shore. Canada built coastal defences around Gaspé Bay in response. Inside the park, you can still find the remains of one of these defensive positions, with concrete structures facing the water. Standing beside them, it is strange to imagine war so close to such a calm place.

Now look at the water again, and give it time. These waters are known for marine life. Grey seals and harbour seals often rest on rocks near the shore. In the warmer months, several kinds of whales feed in the Gulf, and some come close to the coast. Watch for a sudden spout of mist, or a dark curved back rising and sinking. Northern gannets, large white seabirds with black wingtips, dive straight down into the sea like falling arrows.

The forest has its own residents. Black bears live in the park, as do foxes, moose, and porcupines. Porcupines are often seen near trails and roadsides, slow and unbothered. Bears are usually shy, but they are wild animals, so keep food packed away and give any animal plenty of space. In the early morning, the forest is at its quietest. You may hear woodpeckers, the creak of trees in the wind, and very little else.

Before you go, find a place to stand still for a moment. Feel the wind on your face. Notice the colour of the water, which can shift from dark blue to green to grey within an hour. Look at the layered cliffs, the low dark spruce, the line where land meets sea. As you walk away, listen once more for the waves below. This is land's end. And somehow, it never feels quite finished.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring windproof layers, even on warm summer days.
Come early morning for quieter trails and wildlife.
Carry binoculars for spotting whales and seals offshore.
Store all food securely because black bears live here.
Allow several hours for the round-trip walk to Cap Gaspé.
