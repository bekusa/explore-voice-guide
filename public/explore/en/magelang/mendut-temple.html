The guide

The story

Stop at the foot of the stairs for a moment. The stone under your hand is dark grey andesite, the volcanic rock of Java. It is rough and slightly warm, and in places it is spotted with pale lichen. Above you, the staircase climbs toward a narrow doorway. Through that doorway, in the shadow, three enormous figures are waiting. Most visitors walk past this temple on the way to Borobudur. You did not. That is a good decision.

This is Mendut, a Buddhist temple built in the early ninth century, about three kilometres east of Borobudur. Scholars link it to the Sailendra dynasty, the royal house that also built Borobudur. Mendut, the small temple called Pawon, and Borobudur stand roughly on one line across the landscape. Many historians believe the three were planned as a set. Pilgrims may have moved from one to the next, preparing themselves before the great climb at Borobudur.

That old route is still alive. Every year, on Vesak, the festival that marks the birth, enlightenment and death of the Buddha, monks and pilgrims gather here. The procession starts at Mendut, passes Pawon, and ends at Borobudur. Robes in saffron and maroon move slowly along the road. Candles, flowers and holy water travel with them. So this is not only a ruin. For many Buddhists, it is still the first step of a journey.

Now look at the building itself. The temple sits on a high, square base. A wide terrace runs around the main body, so you can walk all the way around the walls. The staircase faces northwest, which is unusual. Most temples in this region face east. Nobody knows the reason for certain. Some scholars connect it to the position of Borobudur, but treat that as a theory, not a fact.

The temple did not always look this tidy. For centuries it lay in ruins, hidden under earth and plants. Dutch officials recorded it in the 1830s, when it was still buried in the undergrowth. Restoration came slowly, in stages, during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Look up at the roof. The upper part is incomplete. Too many original stones were lost, and the restorers chose not to invent what they could not prove.

Before you go inside, look at the walls of the entrance porch. On one side you will find a carved woman surrounded by small children. She is often identified as Hariti. In Buddhist legend, Hariti was a demon who stole and ate children. The Buddha hid one of her own children, and she finally understood the pain of other mothers. She became a protector of children instead. Look closely, and you can see the little figures climbing over her.

On the opposite wall, many scholars identify the male figure as Atavaka, another fierce spirit who was tamed by the Buddha's teaching. The two panels work like guardians at the door. One protects children and families. The other represents wild power brought under control. Together they carry a simple message for anyone stepping inside: even the most frightening forces can change.

The stairway walls hold another surprise, and it is easy to miss. Here the carvings tell animal fables. These come from the Jataka tradition, stories of the Buddha's earlier lives, often with a moral lesson. One famous panel shows a tortoise carried through the air by two birds, holding a stick in its mouth. In the story, the tortoise cannot stay quiet. It opens its mouth to speak... and falls. The lesson is about talking too much.

Now walk through the doorway into the chamber. Give your eyes a moment to adjust to the dim light. In the centre sits a large Buddha, carved from one massive block of stone. He sits as if on a chair, with both feet on the ground. This posture is rare in Javanese sculpture. His hands are held at his chest in a gesture linked to teaching, as if he is turning the wheel of the law.

Two large figures sit on either side of him. They are usually identified as bodhisattvas. In Buddhism, a bodhisattva is an enlightened being who stays in the world to help others. The figure on the left is commonly named Avalokiteshvara, linked with compassion. The figure on the right is commonly named Vajrapani, linked with protective strength. Some scholars suggest other names, so the identifications are not completely settled.

Here is the detail that makes Mendut special. These statues are still in the place they were made for. Across Java, many temple sculptures were taken away, broken, or lost their heads over the centuries. Many ended up in museums or private collections. These three remained. When you stand in this chamber, you see the arrangement that ninth century worshippers saw. Few rooms of this age in Java can offer that.

Step back outside and walk slowly around the terrace. The outer walls carry large carved panels of bodhisattvas and other divine figures. They stand calmly in shallow niches, wearing jewellery and soft fabric. Notice how deep the carving is and how the light changes it. In the morning and late afternoon, low sun throws shadows into the folds of the cloth. At midday, the same figures look flatter and paler.

Just next to the temple grounds there is a working Buddhist monastery. It has gardens, statues and large shady trees. It is a place of practice, not a museum, so move quietly and keep your voice low. The contrast is worth noticing. On one side stands a temple more than a thousand years old. On the other side, people still meditate, study and pray today.

Before you leave, stand once more at the bottom of the stairs. Listen to the street, the motorbikes, the birds in the trees. Look up at the dark doorway and think of the three figures sitting inside in the cool shade. They have waited there through eruptions, centuries of jungle, and the arrival of restorers. When you continue toward Borobudur, you will be walking the same line that pilgrims have followed for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Mendut before Borobudur to follow the pilgrim order.
Let your eyes adjust before studying the dark inner chamber.
Come early morning for deeper shadows on the wall carvings.
Keep quiet near the neighbouring working Buddhist monastery.
Visit during Vesak to see the procession toward Borobudur.
