The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in Piazza Castello and look toward the long, sober façade. Your shoes rest on stone worn smooth by centuries of carriages, soldiers and street sellers. In front of you, a tall iron railing runs across the courtyard, dark against the pale walls. On either side of the gate, two bronze riders hold their horses. The traffic hums behind you, but the palace itself looks calm, almost severe... and that calm is the first clue to its story.

This is the Royal Palace of Turin, for centuries the main home of the House of Savoy. The Savoys began as a family of counts and dukes in the Alps. Over time they became kings, and in the end they became the first kings of a united Italy. Their journey from mountain lords to national monarchs passed through these rooms. So the building in front of you is a family home, a seat of government and a statement of ambition, all at once.

The story begins in the sixteenth century. In 1563, Duke Emmanuel Philibert moved his capital to Turin. Before that, the dukes had ruled from Chambéry, on the other side of the Alps, in what is now France. Turin was closer to the plains of Italy and easier to defend. The duke wanted a capital worthy of a rising power. The palace grew slowly from that decision, and the city grew around it.

Much of the building you see took shape in the seventeenth century. One key figure was Christine of France, a duchess who ruled as regent and was known as Madama Reale, the Royal Lady. Under her influence, the court architect Amedeo di Castellamonte shaped the palace into its current form. Notice how plain the exterior is. That plainness was deliberate. The Savoy rulers kept the outside restrained and saved the drama for the inside.

Now look again at the railing and the two bronze horsemen. They represent Castor and Pollux, twin heroes from Greek mythology. These figures were added in the nineteenth century. Ancient stories made the twins protectors, and here they guard the threshold between the public square and the royal courtyard. The railing marks a clear line. On this side, the city. On the other side, the court. For centuries, ordinary people could look through, but very few could walk through.

Inside, the mood changes completely. The state apartments are a run of rooms filled with gilded ceilings, painted panels, silk walls, mirrors and inlaid floors. Several generations of artists worked here, so the styles shift as you walk — from heavy seventeenth-century decoration to lighter eighteenth-century elegance and later nineteenth-century taste. Look up often. The ceilings carry some of the finest work, and many visitors pass beneath them without raising their heads.

In the eighteenth century, the Sicilian architect Filippo Juvarra worked for the Savoy court. He left his mark across Turin, and inside the palace too. One of his most famous creations here is a staircase called the Scala delle Forbici, which means the Scissors Staircase. Its flights cross and fold like the blades of a pair of scissors. It is a clever, almost theatrical piece of design, and it is easy to miss if you rush.

Juvarra also worked on a small room known as the Chinese Cabinet. In the eighteenth century, European courts were fascinated by objects and designs from East Asia. Lacquered panels, painted scenes and exotic motifs became a sign of taste and wealth. Rooms like this were not accurate copies of China. They were European dreams of a distant place. Look closely at the panels, and you will see how the court imagined a world it had rarely seen.

The palace also connects to one of Turin's most remarkable buildings, the Chapel of the Holy Shroud. It was designed in the seventeenth century by Guarino Guarini, a priest, mathematician and architect. Its dome rises in stacked layers of stone arches, creating a strange, spiralling effect. In 1997, a fire badly damaged the chapel. After many years of careful restoration, it reopened in 2018. The darker stone is original to the design, not a sign of the fire.

Here is a detail many visitors do not expect. The palace is part of a larger museum complex called the Royal Museums of Turin. It includes the Royal Armoury, with suits of armour, weapons and equestrian figures. It also includes the Royal Library. That library holds a famous red chalk drawing that is widely believed to be a self-portrait by Leonardo da Vinci. Because the drawing is fragile, it is shown only on special occasions.

Behind the palace lie the Royal Gardens. Their formal layout follows the French style, with straight paths, clipped hedges and careful geometry. This style was fashionable at courts across Europe in the seventeenth century. Today the gardens offer a quieter contrast to the gilded rooms. You can hear gravel underfoot, birds in the trees and the faint sound of the city beyond the walls. It is a good place to pause and let the interiors settle in your mind.

The palace played a role in the creation of modern Italy. In 1861, Italy was proclaimed a kingdom, and Victor Emmanuel the Second of the House of Savoy became its first king. Turin was the first capital of the new nation, though only for a few years. In 1865, the capital moved to Florence, and later to Rome. After that, Turin was no longer the political heart of Italy. But the palace remained a strong symbol of where the nation began.

In 1997, the palace became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing called the Residences of the Royal House of Savoy. This listing covers a whole network of palaces, villas and hunting lodges around Turin. Together, they show how one family shaped a region with architecture. As you leave, look back at the plain façade and the dark railing once more. Behind that quiet front lie centuries of ambition. And around you, the square still carries the shape the dukes once planned.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the combined Royal Museums route to include the Armoury.
Allow at least half a day for the full museum complex.
Walk the Royal Gardens after the interiors to rest.
Check ahead whether the Leonardo drawing is currently on display.
Visit early in the day to see the state rooms quietly.
