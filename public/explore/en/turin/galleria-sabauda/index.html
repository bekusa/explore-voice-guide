The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The rooms here are quiet in a particular way. Footsteps soften on the floor, voices drop to a murmur, and the light falls evenly across walls hung with canvas and gilded wood. Somewhere nearby a guard shifts his weight. You are inside the picture gallery of the House of Savoy, the family that ruled this corner of Europe for centuries. Their taste, their ambitions, and their marriages are all here, painted in oil.

The Galleria Sabauda takes its name from that dynasty. Sabauda simply means of Savoy. The Savoy dukes began as rulers of Alpine lands that straddled what is now France and Italy. Over time they moved their capital to Turin, became kings, and in the nineteenth century led the unification of Italy. Along the way they collected art. Some came as gifts between royal houses, some through purchase, and some through marriage into other ruling families.

For a long time these paintings lived on the walls of palaces and private apartments. They were for the court, not the public. That changed under King Charles Albert in the nineteenth century. He decided that the royal pictures should form a gallery that ordinary people could visit. It was opened in 1832, inside Palazzo Madama, the castle that still stands in the middle of Piazza Castello. At the time this was a modern idea. A king was sharing his private treasures with his subjects.

The gallery did not stay in one place. In 1865 it moved to the palace of the Academy of Sciences, a heavy brick building a few streets away. For well over a century, that was its home. Then, in 2014, the collection moved again, into the wing you are standing in now. This long building is part of the Royal Palace complex. Today the gallery sits inside the Royal Museums of Turin, alongside the armoury, the palace apartments, and the gardens.

That move matters more than it might seem. For the first time, the Savoy paintings hang beside the rooms where the Savoy family actually lived. The ballrooms, the throne room, and the private chapels are only a short walk away. So as you look at a portrait of a duke or a princess, remember that this person may once have walked the same corridors. The collection has come home, in a sense.

Now, a first surprise. Among the early Flemish paintings is a small work by Jan van Eyck, the fifteenth-century master from the Low Countries. It shows Saint Francis receiving the stigmata, the wounds of Christ, on a rocky hillside. It is tiny, and many visitors walk past it. Look closely. The rocks, the plants, and the distant town are painted with astonishing precision. Only one other version of this scene by van Eyck survives, and that one is in Philadelphia.

Stay with the northern painters a little longer. Nearby you can find the Scenes from the Passion by Hans Memling, another Flemish master of the fifteenth century. Instead of one moment, it shows a whole walled city. Inside that city, the story of Christ's last days unfolds in many small scenes at once. The entry into Jerusalem, the trial, the crucifixion, all on one panel. Your eye travels through streets and gateways, following the story like a map.

The Savoy family had a strong link to these northern artists. In the eighteenth century, they acquired a large part of the collection of Prince Eugene of Savoy. He was a famous military commander who served the Habsburg emperors in Vienna. He was also a passionate collector of Dutch and Flemish painting. When his heirs sold his pictures, many came to Turin. That is one reason this gallery is so strong in northern European art, which can surprise visitors expecting only Italian works.

Of course, there is plenty of Italian painting too. Look for Tobias and the Angel by the Pollaiolo brothers, Florentine artists of the fifteenth century. A young Tobias walks hand in hand with the archangel Raphael, who protects him on a journey. Their clothes flutter. A small dog trots at their feet. Behind them a river valley opens toward the horizon. It is a gentle, almost cheerful picture, and it shows the elegance of Renaissance Florence at its height.

Here is a second surprise. One of the most loved paintings in the gallery shows the children of King Charles the First of England. It was painted by Anthony van Dyck, the Flemish artist who became court painter in London. Why is it in Turin? The children's mother, Queen Henrietta Maria, was the sister of a Savoy duchess. The painting was sent here as a family gift. Look at the faces. These are royal children, but van Dyck makes them feel real and slightly restless.

As you move through the rooms, notice how the gallery is arranged. Paintings are grouped by school and period, so you can follow changes in style from one room to the next. Early gold-ground panels give way to oil portraits, then to large dramatic canvases of the seventeenth century. Among these later works is an Annunciation by Orazio Gentileschi, an Italian painter influenced by Caravaggio. The angel and the Virgin Mary meet in a quiet room, with a heavy red curtain above them.

Take a moment between paintings to look at the building itself. The rooms are long and tall, with large windows. On a bright day, Turin's clear northern light comes through and changes the mood of the walls. From some windows you can glimpse the palace gardens or the rooftops of the old city. Turin is a city of straight streets and long arcades, laid out in a grid. Even indoors, you can feel that sense of order.

There is a lot to see here, and it is easy to feel tired. Do not try to look at everything. Choose a few rooms and give each painting a little time. Sit on a bench if you find one. Notice details: a hand, a fold of fabric, a reflection in a glass. These paintings were made to be looked at slowly, often by people who lived with them every day for years.

When you leave, you will step back out into the wide spaces around Piazza Castello. Trams pass, cafés fill, and the Alps sometimes appear at the end of the streets on a clear day. Carry one image with you. Perhaps van Eyck's tiny rocks, or Memling's crowded city, or van Dyck's royal children. They have travelled from palace to castle to academy, and now back to the palace. And for a few minutes today, they were yours.
