The guide

The story

Look up. One thin spire rises above every roof in the city. It climbs higher and higher until it ends in a fine point against the sky. The walls are pale brick, smooth and bare. The shape is hard to name, part temple, part tower, part something dreamed. This is the Mole Antonelliana, and Turin has built its whole skyline around it.

The building carries the name of its architect, Alessandro Antonelli. Work began in 1863. At the start, this was never meant to be a monument. The Jewish community of the city paid for it. They had just been granted full civil rights. They wanted a synagogue and a school, a proud building to mark their new freedom.

But Antonelli could not stop. He kept changing the plans. He wanted the building taller, then taller again. The costs rose far past the budget. The community had asked for a place of worship, not a record-breaking tower. In the end they could not pay for his dream. They gave the unfinished structure to the city, and the synagogue was built elsewhere.

Turin took over the work. The building was finished in 1889. By then it stood about 167 meters tall. For a time it was the highest building made of brick and stone anywhere in Europe. Antonelli used no steel frame. He trusted shape and careful stonework alone. The walls grow thinner as they rise. Engineers still study how it holds itself up.

Walk slowly around the base. The lowest part looks like a Greek temple, ringed with columns. Above it sits a tall dome. The dome narrows into a square stage. From that stage a slim spire shoots upward, crowned by a small pointed temple. Each piece belongs to a different idea. By every rule they should clash. Somehow, together, they stand.

The spire has not always been steady. In 1953 a violent windstorm struck the city. The upper section cracked and fell to the ground. No one was killed, but the loss was great. The city rebuilt the peak and hid a stronger frame inside it. What you see today is partly a careful repair, shaped to match the original line.

Now step inside. The whole building opens into one tall hollow space. Your eyes are pulled straight upward into the empty dome. The height feels almost impossible seen from within. Sound drifts and echoes. Light falls softly from far above. For many people this vast empty column is more moving than any picture on a wall.

Since 2000 the building has held the National Museum of Cinema. It is one of the strangest and finest film museums in the world. The exhibits wind around the great central hall. Long red couches let visitors lie back and watch clips play on the walls. Old cameras, posters, and costumes trace the path of moving pictures from simple shadows to the modern screen.

A glass elevator runs straight up the middle of the open hall. It rises through the hollow heart of the dome with nothing around it but air. In about one minute it reaches a viewing platform near 85 meters. The ride is part of the visit. You float upward while the whole interior drops away beneath your feet.

From the platform the view spreads in every direction. Turin lies below in a neat grid of streets. Beyond the rooftops, on a clear day, the Alps form a white wall along the edge of the world. The river curves through the city. Far off, small and faint, you can pick out other landmarks one by one.

This shape means a great deal to the people who live here. It appears on the Italian two-cent coin. It stands for Turin the way certain towers stand for other great cities. Films, photographs, and postcards return to it again and again. When residents come home by train, this is the first form they search for on the skyline.

Think about how it all began. A house of prayer that became a tower with no clear use. A near ruin that became the symbol of a city. Antonelli died before the work was truly done. He never saw it serve any purpose at all. Yet his stubborn climb toward the sky gave Turin its center.

So stand here a moment longer. Let the height settle over you. Above you is a building that failed at everything it first set out to be, and then became something greater than its makers planned. Few things rise so far from such a tangled start. Look up one last time before you go.
