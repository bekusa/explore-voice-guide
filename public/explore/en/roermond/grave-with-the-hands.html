The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere behind the trees a bicycle bell rings on a Roermond street. The air here smells of damp leaves and old stone. Rows of headstones lean at slight angles, softened by moss and weather. And then, at a low brick wall, you see them... two stone hands, reaching across the top of the wall, holding each other. You are standing at one of the quietest love stories in the Netherlands.

This is the Grave with the Hands, known in Dutch as Het graf met de handjes. It sits in Roermond's old cemetery, a burial ground that grew up near the edge of the old town. At first glance it looks like two separate monuments. One stands on one side of the wall. One stands on the other. Only when you look up do you see what connects them. Take a few steps back. Let the whole scene settle in front of you.

To understand why there is a wall at all, you need to know how this cemetery was arranged. In the nineteenth century, many Dutch cemeteries were divided by religion. Catholics were buried in one section. Protestants were buried in another. A physical boundary often separated them, and here that boundary is the wall in front of you. For most families, this was simply the order of things. For one couple, it became a problem that lasted beyond their own lives.

The man was Colonel van Gorcum, an officer in the Dutch army and a Protestant. The woman was Josephina van Aefferden, from a Catholic noble family. They married in 1842. In a deeply Catholic town like Roermond, a marriage across the religious line was unusual. Local accounts say it was not welcomed by everyone around them. Whatever the reactions were, the two stayed together for close to four decades.

The colonel died in 1880. Under the rules of the time, he could not be buried in the Catholic section. So he was laid to rest on the Protestant side, close against the dividing wall. His wife lived for eight more years. When she died in 1888, she was not buried in a family vault elsewhere. Instead, her grave was placed directly opposite his, on the Catholic side of the same wall.

Now look at the two monuments more closely. Each one is a tall stone marker, raised high enough to rise above the top of the wall. From the top of each marker, a carved hand stretches out. The two hands meet in the space above the brickwork and hold on to each other. The wall stays in place. The rule of separation is respected. And yet the couple is joined anyway, in the simplest possible gesture.

Here is something many visitors miss. The monuments are not identical. Walk around to each side and compare them. Notice the shape of each hand, the way each wrist emerges from its marker, and how the carving reflects a man's hand and a woman's hand. Small differences like these remind you that two real people stood behind this idea. It was not a generic symbol bought from a catalogue. It was a personal answer to a personal situation.

There is a second surprise in the setting itself. Because the two graves belong to different sections, you cannot see both inscriptions from one spot. You have to move from one side of the wall to the other. In a small way, the cemetery makes you repeat the couple's situation. You stand with him, then you walk around and stand with her. Only the hands, high above, are visible from both sides at once.

Who came up with the design is one of those details that has softened over time. Some versions of the story say the wife planned it herself before her death. Others give the credit to her family or to the craftsmen who carried it out. Treat these as local tellings rather than settled fact. What is certain is the result in front of you, and the clear wish behind it: to stay together where the rules said they could not.

Take a moment to notice the materials. The stone has weathered unevenly. Rain has darkened some surfaces and left pale streaks on others. Lichen grows in the creases where water collects. The brick wall shows patches of repair, with newer mortar beside older joints. Over the years the monument has needed care to survive, and people in Roermond have kept it standing. A grave like this lasts not only because of stone, but because a town keeps choosing to look after it.

The story also says something about Roermond itself. This is a town in Limburg, the southern province where Catholic tradition shaped daily life for centuries. Church towers rise above the old centre, and religious feast days still mark the calendar. Against that background, a Protestant officer and a Catholic noblewoman sharing a life was a quiet act of independence. Today, the divided cemetery feels like a relic. But for them, it was the present, and they found a way around it.

You may notice other visitors lingering here, sometimes couples, sometimes people alone. Some take photographs of the hands against the sky. Others just stand still. Please keep in mind that this is still a cemetery, with other graves and other families nearby. Keep your voice low, stay on the paths, and avoid leaning on the wall or the monuments. The stone is old, and the hands have survived well over a century of weather.

Before you leave, look up one last time at the two hands meeting above the bricks. Notice how small they are compared with the wall and the trees around them. Then listen again... the gravel, the birds, the distant traffic of a working town. As you walk back towards the gate, you might glance at other walls and boundaries along the way. Some of them, like this one, matter far less than what reaches across them.
