The guide

The story

Listen first. Water drips somewhere in the cliff. Leaves move on the slope above. Under your feet the path is soft red sandstone, worn smooth by centuries of shoes. Then you look up, and the rock is full of faces. Hundreds of them. Some calm, some fierce, some laughing. The damp air of this hilly corner of Chongqing settles on the stone and keeps it cool, even when the day outside is warm.

These are the Dazu Rock Carvings, a large group of religious sculptures cut directly into the hillsides around the town of Dazu. Across the wider district there are many separate carving sites. Together they hold tens of thousands of figures. In 1999, UNESCO added five of the most important groups to its World Heritage List. Those five are Baodingshan, Beishan, Nanshan, Shizhuanshan and Shimenshan. The word shan simply means mountain or hill.

The story of the carvings covers several centuries. The earliest major work here began in the late ninth century, near the end of the Tang dynasty. The busiest period came later, under the Song dynasty, which ruled China from the tenth century into the thirteenth. During those years, sculptors, monks and wealthy donors turned plain cliffs into something close to open-air temples. Most of what you see around you comes from that long Song chapter.

Beishan, the North Hill, is usually counted as the oldest of the main sites. Its carving began under Wei Junjing, a military official of the late Tang period. Over the following centuries, other donors added their own niches beside his. The result is a long gallery of small and medium alcoves. Each one is like a separate room. Many of them hold figures of Guanyin, the bodhisattva of compassion, shown with a gentle face and elegant, flowing robes.

Baodingshan is different. Its carvings follow a single plan, and that plan is linked to a monk named Zhao Zhifeng. He spent decades in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries directing the work here. Instead of scattered niches, Baoding feels like one continuous sermon in stone. The carvings wrap around a horseshoe-shaped valley. As you walk, the scenes lead you from one teaching to the next, almost like the pages of an illustrated book.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Dazu is not only Buddhist. Some carvings show Taoist figures, connected to China's native tradition of harmony with nature. Others carry Confucian ideas about family duty and respect for parents. At Shizhuanshan, figures from all three traditions sit side by side in the same cliff. In much of the world, rival faiths built separate temples. Here, the three are carved into one wall of rock.

That family theme appears clearly at Baoding. One large panel shows the many kindnesses that parents give a child, from carrying the baby to worrying when the grown child travels far away. The message is simple: remember what your parents gave you. It is a very human scene. You see ordinary people, not only gods and saints, in everyday clothes and everyday poses. For a religious site, the mood is surprisingly warm.

Now look for the great reclining Buddha. This is the Buddha at the moment of his passing into nirvana, the final release from the cycle of rebirth. The figure is more than thirty metres long. And here is the second surprise. You only see the upper part of his body. His legs simply disappear into the cliff. Sculptors often explain this as a deliberate choice — the figure is so vast that the rock itself cannot contain it.

Nearby is the Thousand-Hand Guanyin, perhaps the most photographed carving in Dazu. Hundreds of carved hands spread out behind the figure like a fan of golden feathers. Each hand is said to hold an eye or an object, a symbol of help reaching every person in need. Weathering and damp damaged this carving over time. A long conservation project in the early twenty-first century cleaned and repaired it, and the gold leaf now glows again.

Not every scene is peaceful. Baoding also has carvings of hell, and they are vivid. Sinners suffer punishments matched to their misdeeds. Among these scenes is a figure of a woman feeding her chickens. She looks calm and ordinary. But in Buddhist teaching, raising animals for slaughter could bring bad karma. So her quiet chore is a warning. Many visitors walk straight past her. Once you know the story, she is hard to forget.

The carvers also understood water. This region is wet, and rain is the biggest enemy of soft sandstone. Look closely and you will see channels and drains cut into the rock to guide water away from the faces. In one Baoding scene, a natural spring feeds water through a carved dragon. The idea was clever: use the problem as part of the art. Engineering and devotion worked together here.

As you walk, notice the colour. Most of the stone now looks grey or warm brown. But traces of old paint remain in the folds of robes and in the corners of niches — reds, greens and blues. Once, much of this cliff was brightly painted. Notice also the small details: a playful expression, a carved musical instrument, an animal tucked into a corner. The sculptors left room for humour and daily life.

The carvings sit in green, terraced countryside, and the walk between scenes is part of the experience. Birds call from the trees. Moss gathers on the shaded ledges. Locals and pilgrims still come to light incense at some sites, so you may smell smoke drifting along the path. The pace here is slow. These figures were made to be visited step by step, not seen in a single glance.

Before you move on, stop for a moment and look at one face. Any face. Someone shaped it with a chisel, perhaps eight or nine hundred years ago, in the same damp air you are breathing now. The rock is still the same rock. As you walk back along the path, listen again for the drip of water. It is the same sound that the carvers heard, and the same sound the stone will hear long after we leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Prioritise Baodingshan if you only have time for one site.
Bring a light rain jacket; the area is often damp.
Wear shoes with grip for wet sandstone paths.
Visit early to see carvings before tour groups arrive.
Check photography rules at each site before shooting.
