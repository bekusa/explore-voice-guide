The guide

The story

Push open the heavy door and the noise of the city falls away almost at once. Outside, buses hum and shoppers cross the square. Inside, the air is cool and still, and it smells faintly of old stone and candle wax. Your footsteps sound louder here. Look up, and the Gothic vaults rise far above you, painted and ribbed, holding the light that drifts in through the high windows.

This is the Cathedral of Saint Paul, the seat of the bishops of Liège. But here is the first surprise. For most of its long life, this building was not a cathedral at all. It was a collegiate church — a church served by a community of canons, priests who lived and prayed together. The real cathedral of Liège stood a short walk away, and it was far larger. To understand why this church took its place, we need to go back a long way.

The story of Liège begins with a murder. Around the year 700, a bishop named Lambert was killed in this valley. Locals and pilgrims soon honoured him as a saint, and a town grew up around his shrine. Over the centuries, his successors became powerful rulers. They were prince-bishops — church leaders who also governed a territory, with courts, taxes and armies. For roughly eight hundred years, Liège was the capital of their small state.

The church you are standing in was founded in the tenth century, during that early age of growth. Almost nothing of the first building survives. What you see today is mostly Gothic, built and rebuilt over several centuries from the thirteenth century onward. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches, tall windows and stone ribs that carry the weight of the roof. Notice how the columns lead the eye upward, and how the walls seem thin compared to their height.

Now, the great cathedral. Saint Lambert's Cathedral stood on the square that still carries his name, just a few minutes from here. It was one of the largest churches in this part of Europe. Then came the Liège Revolution in the late eighteenth century. The citizens overthrew their prince-bishop. They saw the cathedral as the symbol of his power, and in the 1790s they began to tear it down, stone by stone.

So when the Church in Liège was reorganised in the early nineteenth century, it needed a new seat. Saint Paul was chosen. That is how a church of canons became a cathedral almost by accident of history. Today, where Saint Lambert's once stood, there is an open square and a busy transport hub. Archaeologists have uncovered its foundations below ground, and part of that buried site can be visited near the Place Saint-Lambert.

Take a moment to walk slowly down the nave, the long central space where the congregation sits. The tower outside was finished only in the nineteenth century, much later than the body of the church. Inside, many layers sit side by side. There is medieval stonework, Renaissance glass and nineteenth century furnishings. The building does not belong to one age. It carries traces of many generations who repaired it, extended it and changed it.

Head toward the choir, the area around the main altar, and look at the stained glass. Some of these windows date from the sixteenth century. On a bright day, blues and reds fall across the stone floor and shift slowly as the sun moves. If you have time, sit for a few minutes and watch the colours travel. It is one of the quiet pleasures of this place, and most visitors walk past it too quickly.

Now for the second surprise — and it is a strange one. Find the pulpit, the raised platform where sermons were preached. Below it sits a sculpture of a young man, half-seated, with a troubled, beautiful face. This is the Genius of Evil, a figure of Lucifer by the Belgian sculptor Guillaume Geefs, from the mid nineteenth century. The story behind it is often told here. An earlier version by his brother Joseph was rejected. Many say it was judged too attractive to sit in a church.

Whatever the full truth, look closely at the figure Guillaume made. The wings are folded. The chain lies at his feet. There is a tear-like sadness in his expression, and something human in his posture. He does not look like a monster. He looks like someone who has lost everything. People often stand here longer than they expected, unsure whether they feel fear or pity.

The cathedral's other great draw is its treasury, housed in rooms beside the cloister. It usually requires a separate visit. Among its pieces is a large silver reliquary bust of Saint Lambert, made in the early sixteenth century. A reliquary is a container for the remains of a saint. This one shows the bishop with his mitre, his tall pointed hat, and scenes from his life around the base.

The most famous object in the treasury has a darker story. In 1468, Charles the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, crushed a rebellion in Liège and had much of the city burned. A few years later, he gave the Church a reliquary made of gold. It shows Charles himself, kneeling, with Saint George standing behind him. Many historians read it as an act of penance — a powerful man asking forgiveness for what he had done to this city.

When you step back outside, pause for a moment in the cloister or on the square. The cloister is a covered walkway around a garden, once used by the canons for walking and prayer. Listen to how the sounds of the city return slowly, the traffic and the voices. Then look back at the tower. It has watched this city through revolution, war and rebuilding. It began as the second church of Liège... and it became the one that remained.
