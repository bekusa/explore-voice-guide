The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment in the square and look up. The pale limestone catches the light almost like bone. Two stone spires rise above the main façade, and they are not solid. They are carved into open lace, so you can see sky through them. On a windy day you may hear pigeons clapping off the ledges, and the soft echo of footsteps on the paving stones around you.

This is the Cathedral of Saint Mary of Burgos. Work on it began in 1221, under King Ferdinand the Third of Castile and Bishop Mauricio of Burgos. The bishop had travelled through France, and he came home with French ideas about Gothic building. Gothic is the style of pointed arches, tall windows and stone ribs that carry the weight of the roof. Here, in the heart of medieval Castile, those ideas took root and grew.

The early builders worked fast for their time. Within a few decades the main body of the church was standing. But a cathedral like this is never really finished in one generation. Over the next three centuries, new chapels, towers and doorways were added. So what you see today is not one single moment in history. It is many centuries of work, layered on top of each other, all in the same warm stone.

Look again at those openwork spires. They were added in the fifteenth century by a master builder known as Juan de Colonia, which means John of Cologne. He came from Germany, and he brought the delicate tracery of northern Europe with him. Tracery is the carved stone pattern you usually see inside a window frame. Here it climbs all the way up the towers. In 1984, UNESCO listed the cathedral as a World Heritage Site, and it was the only Spanish cathedral to receive that honour on its own.

Now step inside. The air changes at once. It is cooler, quieter, and it carries a faint smell of wax and old wood. Your eyes need a moment to adjust. Then the space opens upward, with columns rising into ribbed vaults. Many visitors expect one great empty hall. Instead, this cathedral is full of separate rooms, screens and chapels. It feels less like a single church and more like a small stone city under one roof.

Walk toward the centre, where the long nave meets the crossing. Stop here and look straight up. High above you is a great star-shaped lantern of carved stone, letting light fall into the church. Here is a surprise. The first lantern collapsed in 1539. The cathedral chapter did not give up. They rebuilt it, and the version above you dates from the sixteenth century. It is one of the most complex pieces of stonework in the whole building.

Now look down at the floor beneath that lantern. Under a simple slab lie the remains of Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar, the eleventh-century Castilian knight known to the world as El Cid. Beside him lies his wife, Jimena. El Cid was born near Burgos, and he became a hero of Spanish legend and poetry. His remains were brought here in the twentieth century. For many Spaniards, this quiet stone is the most meaningful spot in the cathedral.

Somewhere nearby, look for an old wooden chest hanging on a wall. It is often called the Chest of El Cid, and a famous story surrounds it. According to the medieval epic poem about the knight, El Cid filled a chest with sand. He then left it with two moneylenders as a pledge, saying it held gold. Whether this exact chest played any part in that tale is uncertain. Treat it as a legend, but a very good one.

Next, find the Golden Staircase. It sits in the north transept, the arm of the church to the left as you face the altar. A transept is one of the side arms that give a cathedral its cross shape. The staircase was designed in the early sixteenth century by Diego de Siloé, a sculptor and architect from Burgos. Its two flights climb toward a doorway set high in the wall, with gilded iron railings that shine in the lamplight.

Why build a grand staircase up to a door? The street outside on that side sits much higher than the cathedral floor. The builders needed a way to bring people down into the church. Siloé turned a practical problem into something theatrical. Many later palace staircases in Spain and beyond show the influence of this design. So you are looking at a solution to a difficult hill... that became a model of elegance.

Now make your way to the far end, behind the main altar. Here you will find the Constable's Chapel. It was built in the late fifteenth century for Pedro Fernández de Velasco, the Constable of Castile, a high military office, and his wife, Mencía de Mendoza. Their marble tomb figures lie in the centre. Look up at the vault. It is a star of open stonework, and light falls through it onto the floor. Many people call this chapel a cathedral within the cathedral.

Before you leave, look high on the wall of the nave for a small painted figure near a clock. Locals call him the Papamoscas, which means the flycatcher. When the clock strikes the hour, he moves his arm and opens his mouth wide. Children and adults gather below to watch. It is a small, cheerful detail in a very serious building, and it shows that people here have always enjoyed a little humour alongside their faith.

When you walk back out into the square, turn around one last time. Pilgrims on the Way of Saint James, the old walking route to Santiago de Compostela, have passed this façade for centuries. Some may be passing you now, with packs and walking poles. Listen for the hour from the clock, notice the pale stone warming in the light, and see the sky through those carved spires. The cathedral is still part of daily life here... and for a moment, so are you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stand in the square early morning for soft light on the façade.
Wait below the Papamoscas a few minutes before the hour.
Visit the Constable's Chapel last as a quiet finale.
Bring a light layer since the interior stays cool.
Allow at least ninety minutes to see the main chapels.
