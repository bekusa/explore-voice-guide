The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the slope under your feet. The ground here is not flat. It rises in a steep, grassy shoulder, shaped long ago by human hands rather than by nature alone. Wind moves through the trees around the hill. If you listen, the town of Anykščiai feels far away, though it is close. This is the kind of place where you notice your own breathing as you climb.

The hill is known as the Šeimyniškėliai hillfort. For many people in Lithuania, it carries another name, one that is spoken with real feeling: Voruta. That name belongs to one of the great unsolved puzzles of Lithuanian history. Nobody can say with certainty where Voruta stood. But some archaeologists believe that it stood right here, on the ground you are walking on now.

To understand why this matters, we need to go back to the thirteenth century. At that time, Lithuania was becoming a single state. The man at its centre was Mindaugas, the first and only crowned King of Lithuania. He was crowned in 1253, after accepting Christianity. Before and after that moment, he had to fight hard to hold power, against rival relatives, neighbouring princes and the military orders of crusading knights.

Voruta appears in the written record during those struggles. A medieval chronicle from Galicia-Volhynia, a powerful principality to the south in lands that are now part of Ukraine, mentions Mindaugas retreating to a castle called Voruta. This happened during a civil war in the early 1250s. His enemies came after him. According to the chronicle, Mindaugas held out, and his opponents could not take the stronghold. That short mention is almost everything the written sources tell us.

And here is the first surprise. Voruta is one of the most famous castles in Lithuanian memory, yet it has no confirmed location. Historians have proposed many sites across the country. Each theory has supporters. The chronicles do not give a map, and the words they use are few. So the search for Voruta has become a kind of national detective story, carried on over generations by scholars, local historians and curious visitors like you.

Why do some researchers point to this hill near Anykščiai? Archaeological excavations here found traces of a fortified settlement that was active in roughly the right period. The hillfort also has strong natural defences. Steep slopes protect it, and the shape of the summit made it easier to guard. For a ruler under pressure, a place like this offered safety. Still, it is important to be honest: the link between this hill and Voruta is a hypothesis, not a proven fact.

Look at the shape of the hill itself. A hillfort, in this part of Europe, is a natural rise that people reshaped into a fortress. They cut the slopes steeper. They dug ditches and raised earthen ramparts. On top, they built timber walls and wooden buildings, because stone castles came later to most of Lithuania. Wood does not survive well in the ground. That is one reason why so much of early Lithuanian history must be read from soil, ash and postholes rather than from ruins.

In recent years, a wooden fortification has been reconstructed on the hill. It is meant to give visitors an idea of how a stronghold of this kind may have looked. Treat it as an informed impression rather than an exact copy... No one has a drawing of the original. But the timber, the height of the palisade and the narrow lines of sight help you imagine life here — the guards on the walls, the smoke from cooking fires, the animals kept close in times of danger.

Now for the second surprise. The word Voruta itself may hold a clue. Some scholars connect it to an old word linked to gates or enclosures, though linguists debate this. Whatever its origin, the name has outlived the castle by centuries. In Lithuania, Voruta became a symbol of resistance and of the early state. Writers, painters and even beer brands and sports teams have borrowed it. The castle vanished, but the name stayed in everyday speech.

Think, too, about the people who lived around such a fortress. Most of them were not kings or warriors. They were farmers, craftsmen and families who worked the land in the valleys below. In peaceful times, the hillfort was a centre of trade and authority. In times of war, it was the place everyone ran to. The forests here gave timber, game and honey. The rivers gave fish and routes for travel. Life was shaped by the seasons as much as by politics.

The Anykščiai region has a strong place in Lithuanian culture. The surrounding pine forests and river valleys inspired well-known Lithuanian writers, and the area is full of local legends about stones, hills and lost treasure. Voruta fits naturally into that landscape of stories. Locals say that every hill around here could hold a secret. Whether or not Mindaugas once stood on this summit, the hill connects you to a very old way of reading the land.

As you walk around the top, take your time with the view. Notice how far you can see, and in which directions the approach is open or hidden. A defender would have read this landscape carefully. Notice the edges where the slope drops away sharply. Notice the softer ground where the ramparts once stood. In spring and summer, the grass is deep green and full of insects. In autumn, the trees turn gold and the air smells of wet leaves and wood.

Before you leave, stand once more at the edge of the summit. Look down the slope you climbed. Somewhere in this country, the real Voruta waits under the earth, perhaps here, perhaps far away. The question may never be fully answered. But as you walk back down, feel the steepness under your feet again. That slope was made by people who needed safety. You are walking on their work.

Practical tips

Visit after dry weather when the paths are less slippery.
Walk the full summit edge to understand the defences.

Bring water, as the climb can be tiring in summer.
