The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes in off the creek, flat and steady, and it carries salt. Under your shoes the ground crunches. That sound is not gravel. Look down, and you will see fragments of pottery everywhere — red, buff, some glazed in green or blue. You are walking on the broken household goods of a city that was busy here for more than a thousand years, and then went quiet.

This is Banbhore, an archaeological site in the Thatta district of Sindh, in southern Pakistan. It sits on the northern bank of Gharo Creek, roughly between Karachi and Thatta. Today the landscape is open and low, with scrub, mudflats and wide sky. It is hard to picture ships here. But for many centuries this creek was a working channel of the Indus delta, and Banbhore was a port where goods from the Arabian Sea met the river trade of the interior.

Archaeologists generally trace occupation here from around the first century before Christ to roughly the thirteenth century. That is a very long life for one town. Excavators describe three broad phases. The earliest is linked to the Scytho-Parthian period, when rulers with roots in Central Asia and Iran controlled parts of the region. A Hindu and Buddhist phase followed. The final phase is Islamic, and it left the remains that most visitors come to see.

Here is the question that has followed Banbhore for decades. Is this Debal? Debal was the port that Arab forces under Muhammad bin Qasim besieged and took in the early eighth century, an event often described as the arrival of Islam as a political power in South Asia. Many scholars identify Banbhore with Debal. Others are more cautious, because the written sources are vague about geography. The question is not fully settled, so treat it as a strong possibility, not a proven fact.

Walk toward the centre and you reach the walled citadel. The defensive wall follows the shape of the high ground, and at regular intervals it swells outward into rounded bastions — semicircular towers that let defenders cover the base of the wall. Much of what you see is stone, with later restoration in places. Notice how the wall line still reads clearly from a distance, even where the upper courses have fallen. The builders chose this rise for a reason: it is drier, higher, and easy to defend.

Inside the walls lies the reason Banbhore matters so much to historians of architecture. Excavators uncovered the foundations of a mosque here, and it is counted among the earliest known mosques in the whole of South Asia. You will see the outline of a square courtyard and a prayer hall. Look for the bases of columns in rows. They once held up a flat roof, probably of timber, over the space where people stood to pray.

The dating of the mosque comes from something unusual. Stone slabs carved in Kufic script were found at the site. Kufic is an early, angular style of Arabic writing, with strong straight lines and sharp corners. One inscription is widely read as giving a date in the early eighth century, only a few years after the Arab conquest. Think about that for a moment. Standing here, you are close to one of the first places where Islamic architecture took root on this side of the Arabian Sea.

But Banbhore was not only a place of faith. Outside the walls, excavators found areas used for industry and trade. There is evidence of workshops for pottery, metal and other crafts. Finds from the site include glass, beads, coins and glazed ceramics. Some of the ceramics came from far away, including pieces linked to the Persian Gulf and even Chinese wares. This was a town connected to the wider Indian Ocean world, where merchants, sailors and craftsmen met.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Earlier layers at the site produced objects connected with Hindu worship, including stone symbols associated with the god Shiva. So the ground here holds more than one religious story, stacked one above another. A port city like this rarely belonged to one community alone. Different peoples came and went with the trade winds, and each left something in the soil. The layers you cross today are a timeline you can stand on.

Why did Banbhore fall silent? No single answer is certain. The most common explanation points to the Indus itself. The river and its delta channels have shifted many times over the centuries. When a channel moves or silts up, a port can lose its connection to the sea and to the hinterland. Trade then moves elsewhere, and people follow it. By around the thirteenth century, occupation here seems to have faded, and the creek slowly took back the shore.

Systematic excavation began in the twentieth century. Pakistani archaeologists carried out major work here in the late 1950s and the 1960s, and a small site museum was built to display finds. In more recent years, international teams have returned to study the site with newer methods. Banbhore has also been placed on Pakistan's tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage status. That means it has been proposed for consideration, but it is not yet inscribed.

As you walk, pay attention to the light. In the early morning and late afternoon, the low sun throws long shadows from the wall stubs and column bases. The plan of the town becomes easier to read. At midday the light is flat and bright off the water, and the heat can be strong. The colours change too. The pale stone turns honey-coloured near sunset, and the creek shifts from silver to grey.

Before you leave, stop at the edge of the high ground and face the creek. Imagine the masts, the shouting of porters, the smell of tar and fish and spice. Then let the picture go, and look at what is really here. Mudflats. Birds working the shallows. Wind in the scrub. And under your feet, those small pieces of pottery, each one handled by someone who lived here. Walk gently. The city is still speaking through the things it left behind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid strong heat.
Bring water, a hat and sun protection for open ground.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, pottery-strewn walking surfaces.
See the site museum first to understand the finds.
Leave every pottery fragment where it lies on the ground.
