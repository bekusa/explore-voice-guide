The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves across this low ridge with almost nothing to stop it. Your shoes scrape on dry, pale ground, scattered with grit and broken stone. Around you, in every direction, stand tombs. Some are small and plain. Some are large stone pavilions. Some are brick domes covered in blue tile. The light here is hard and bright, and the yellow sandstone takes on a soft gold colour as the sun drops. This is Makli, one of the largest burial grounds in the world.

The necropolis sits on a long limestone ridge just outside the town of Thatta, in the southern Pakistani province of Sindh. For several centuries, Thatta was a major city. It stood near the Indus River and its delta, and it was a centre of trade, learning and political power. The rich and the powerful wanted to be buried close to it. So did scholars, saints, soldiers and ordinary families. Over time, this ridge filled with their graves.

Nobody knows the exact number of people buried here. By many estimates, the figure runs into the hundreds of thousands. The site stretches for several kilometres along the ridge. Most graves are simple mounds or modest stone markers. Among them rise the monumental tombs that most visitors come to see. In 1981, UNESCO listed the historical monuments at Makli as a World Heritage Site. That recognition covers centuries of building, from roughly the fourteenth century to the eighteenth.

Locals tell a story about the name. According to one popular tale, a holy man once stopped on this ridge and felt that the place was so blessed it was like a small Mecca. From that idea, the legend says, came the name Makli. It is folklore, not documented history. But it shows how people here have long understood the ridge — not only as a cemetery, but as sacred ground.

The tombs fall into rough groups, linked to the dynasties that ruled Sindh. The earliest major group belongs to the Samma rulers, a local Sindhi dynasty. Later came the Arghun and the Tarkhan rulers, families with roots in Central Asia. After them, Sindh came under the Mughal Empire, the great dynasty that also ruled from Delhi and Agra. Mughal governors were then posted to Thatta. Each group left its own style on this hill.

The most famous Samma monument is the tomb of Jam Nizamuddin the Second, a ruler who died in the early sixteenth century. Locals often call him Jam Nindo. His tomb is a square structure built from carved sandstone blocks. Look closely at its walls. Bands of carving run around the stone — flowers, geometric patterns, and lines of Arabic inscription. Some of the motifs, like round medallions and lotus-like flowers, also appear in Hindu temple carving. Here they sit beside Islamic calligraphy on the same wall.

That mixture is the first real surprise of Makli. Many visitors expect a sharp line between traditions. Instead, the stonemasons of Sindh used a shared vocabulary of ornament. They carved for Muslim patrons using skills and patterns developed over generations in the wider region. Gujarat, the coastal region to the southeast, had a strong stone-carving tradition, and scholars often point to links with it. The result is a style that feels local to this corner of the subcontinent.

Move on toward the Tarkhan and Mughal period tombs, and the architecture changes. The tomb of Isa Khan Tarkhan the Second is one of the grandest. It is a large stone building with two storeys and open galleries, raised on a platform. Carved stone screens and pillars give it a light, layered look. From the upper level, the wider necropolis opens up around you — a field of domes and platforms stretching toward the horizon.

The second surprise is colour. Not every tomb here is bare stone. Some of the later brick tombs were once covered in glazed tiles in shades of blue and turquoise, with touches of white. Thatta was well known for this tile work. In many places the tiles have fallen away, leaving bare brick. But where they survive, especially inside domes and around doorways, the blue still catches the light. Imagine the whole ridge when these surfaces were complete and bright.

Time and weather have been hard on Makli. Salt in the soil and air eats into the stone and brick. Strong sun, wind and occasional heavy rain all add to the damage. Some tombs lean. Some have lost their domes or their facing stones. Over the years, conservation teams have worked to stabilise the most important structures, and international bodies have raised concern about the site's condition. When you see scaffolding or fresh repair work, you are seeing part of that long effort.

Makli is also still a living place. It is not only a museum of stone. Some graves here belong to people honoured as saints, and families come to pray at their shrines. You may see small offerings, threads tied to railings, or people sitting quietly in the shade of a tomb. Be respectful in these moments. Keep your voice low near active shrines, and ask before you photograph anyone at prayer.

As you walk, look down as well as up. Many of the smaller graves have carved stone covers. Some show patterns of flowers and vines. Some carry inscriptions. On certain graves, the carving hints at who lay beneath — for example, details that local guides often explain as signs of a man or a woman, or of a warrior. These smaller stones are easy to pass by. They are also some of the most personal objects on the whole ridge.

If you can, stay until late afternoon. As the sun lowers, the shadows grow long across the platforms, and the sandstone warms to gold and amber. The carved lines on the walls become sharper in the side light. The heat eases a little. The ridge becomes quieter, and the scale of the place becomes easier to feel.

Take a moment now, wherever you are standing. Look at the nearest stone and the marks of the chisel on it. Someone cut those lines by hand, hundreds of years ago, for someone they wanted to remember. Around you are many thousands of such choices. As you walk back toward Thatta, notice how the tombs slowly thin out behind you — and how the wind keeps moving across the ridge, just as it did for the people who built it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come in late afternoon for softer light and cooler air.
Carry water and a hat; shade is scarce on the ridge.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, gritty ground.
Keep voices low near active shrines and praying families.
Ask permission before photographing people at prayer.
