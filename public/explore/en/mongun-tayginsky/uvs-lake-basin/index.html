The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, the wind is often the loudest thing you will hear. It moves across short grass and loose stone, and it carries a dry, clean smell of sage and cold rock. Above you, the sky feels enormous. Below you, the ground falls away in long folds of steppe and mountain. This is the edge of the Uvs Lake Basin. It is one of the most complete natural landscapes left in the heart of Asia.

Let us start with the shape of the place, because the shape is the whole story. The Uvs Lake Basin is a closed basin. Rivers flow into it, but no river flows out. Every stream that runs down from these mountains ends its journey inside the basin, in lakes and marshes and salty flats. Nothing drains away to any ocean. The water stays here, and so does everything it carries.

At the low centre of the basin lies Uvs Nuur, a large and salty lake on the Mongolian side of the border. It sits far from where you stand in Tuva. Yet these Tuvan mountains belong to the same system. The snow that melts on these slopes feeds the same network of rivers. So when you look at the high ground around you, you are looking at the water supply for a lake far to the south.

In 2003, UNESCO added the Uvs Lake Basin to its World Heritage List. The listing is unusual. It crosses an international border, shared between Russia and Mongolia. It is also made up of twelve separate protected areas, scattered across the basin like pieces of a map. The Mongun-Taiga area, here in the southwest of Tuva, is one of those pieces. You are standing inside a site that two countries protect together.

Why does this matter so much to scientists? Here is the first surprise. Because the basin is closed and mostly untouched by heavy industry, it works like a natural laboratory. Researchers can watch how climate changes affect water, ice, soil, and life, all inside one sealed system. A change in rainfall or temperature shows up here clearly, without the confusion of a river carrying effects away. This quiet corner of Asia helps the whole world read its own climate.

Now look at how many worlds sit inside this one basin. On the highest ground, there are glaciers and permanent snow. Below them, alpine tundra spreads out, with mosses, lichens, and small hardy flowers. Lower still, you can find patches of mountain forest. Then come the open steppe grasslands. And further down, toward the lake, there are semi-desert plains and even sand dunes. Few places on Earth pack so many landscapes into a single view.

The Mongun-Taiga massif rises above this district. Its highest summit is the tallest point in the whole Republic of Tuva, reaching close to four thousand metres. The name itself is worth knowing. In the Tuvan language, it is usually translated as the Silver Taiga or the Silver Mountain. On a clear day, you can see why. Snow and ice on the high ridges catch the light and shine a pale, metallic white.

This high, cold country is home to animals that most people never see. The snow leopard lives in these mountains, though it is shy and very rarely spotted. Argali, the great wild mountain sheep with heavy curled horns, graze on the slopes. Siberian ibex climb the rocky cliffs. Lower down, marmots whistle from their burrows, and birds of prey ride the wind above the steppe. Patience and a pair of binoculars are your best tools here.

Here is the second surprise. This landscape is wild, but it is not empty of human history. Across the wider basin, there are many thousands of ancient burial mounds and stone monuments. Archaeologists link many of them to the nomadic cultures of the Iron Age, including peoples often grouped under the name Scythian — horse-riding nomads who ranged across the steppes of Eurasia. Some sites also carry rock carvings and standing stones from later centuries. Every low mound of stones you pass may hold a story much older than any town.

The people who live here today keep a long nomadic tradition. Herding families still move with their animals between seasonal pastures — sheep, goats, horses, and yaks on the higher ground. You may see a white felt yurt standing alone on the grass, with a thin line of smoke rising from it. The district centre, the village of Mugur-Aksy, is small and quiet. Life here follows the seasons more than the clock.

This district is also known for something you hear rather than see. Mongun-Taiga has a strong reputation in Tuva for throat singing, called khoomei. In this style, a single singer produces two or more notes at once — a deep drone and a high, whistling melody above it. Many singers say the sound grew from listening to the land itself: wind, water, and the echo of mountains. Standing here, in this open silence, that idea is easy to believe.

A visit here asks for respect and preparation. The weather changes fast. A warm afternoon can turn to sleet within an hour, even in summer. Distances are long, roads are rough, and services are few. Because this is a protected area close to an international border, permits and local guidance are often needed. Treat the burial mounds with care. Do not move stones, and do not climb on them.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Look at the line where the steppe meets the mountains. Notice the colours — the gold and green of the grass, the grey of the rock, the white of the snow above. Listen again to the wind. The water melting on these peaks right now will travel slowly toward a lake you may never see. And this wide basin will keep holding it, as it has for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack warm layers, since mountain weather can change within an hour.
Arrange border-zone permits and a local guide before travelling.
Bring binoculars for spotting argali, ibex, and birds of prey.
Carry enough fuel, water, and food for long remote distances.
Ask herding families before photographing them or their yurts.
