The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here the wind moves almost constantly. It comes across the ridge, catches in the dry grass, and carries the faint sound of goat bells from the valley below. Under your shoes the ground is loose and gritty, a mix of crumbled stone and pale earth. Around you, the mountains rise in folds of ochre, rust and grey. This is Alamut, a broken fortress on a rock, high in the Alborz range of northern Iran.

Take a breath before we begin. You have climbed to get here, and the climb is part of the story. The castle sits on a narrow ridge of rock, with steep drops on several sides. From below, the summit can look like a ship's prow pushing out of the mountain. Some people say the name means something like the eagle's nest, or the eagle's teaching. That is a traditional explanation, and scholars still debate the true origin. Standing here, it is easy to see why the image has lasted.

Now for the history. In the late eleventh century, a man named Hasan-i Sabbah took control of this fortress. The date usually given is 1090. Hasan was a leader of the Nizari Ismailis, a branch of Shia Islam. At the time, the Nizaris were a minority under pressure from the powerful Seljuk Turks, who ruled much of the region. Hasan needed a place that was hard to reach and easy to defend. This rock gave him exactly that.

According to the medieval chronicles, Hasan took the castle without a major battle. The accounts say he entered quietly, gained supporters inside, and then the holder of the fortress was sent away. The details come from later writers, some of them hostile to the Ismailis, so treat the exact story with some care. What is clear is the result. Alamut became the centre of a Nizari state that held a network of mountain castles across Iran and Syria.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Hasan is remembered in popular culture as a shadowy warlord. But the sources also describe him as a strict scholar who lived a very disciplined life. The chronicles say he stayed at Alamut for decades and rarely, if ever, left. He spent his time reading, writing and directing the community from this rock. Look around at the small, exposed summit, and imagine choosing to live your whole later life up here.

The Nizaris became known to their enemies, and later to Europeans, as the Assassins. The word is usually linked to an Arabic term, hashishiyya, which was used as an insult. Modern historians argue about what it really meant. It was not necessarily a description of drug use. The Nizaris did carry out targeted killings of political and military leaders. For a small community facing large armies, this was a way to resist without fighting open wars.

Now we reach the legends. The Venetian traveller Marco Polo told a famous tale about this region. He described a secret garden of paradise, where young men were drugged and then shown delights, so they would obey any order. It is a vivid story, and it spread across Europe for centuries. But historians treat it as legend, not documented fact. Polo travelled through Persia after the castle had already fallen, and no reliable evidence of such a garden has been found here.

What the castle did really hold was a library. Alamut was known for its collection of books on religion, philosophy, astronomy and other subjects. Scholars spent time in the Ismaili fortresses. The well-known Persian thinker Nasir al-Din Tusi is linked with the Nizari castles in this period, before he later joined the Mongols. So this windy rock was not only a military base. It was also a place of learning, which surprises many people who arrive expecting only a story of daggers.

That world ended in 1256. The Mongol army under Hulagu, a grandson of Genghis Khan, moved into Iran and attacked the Ismaili castles one by one. The Nizari leader of the time, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, surrendered, and Alamut was handed over. The Persian historian Juvayni travelled with the Mongols. He wrote that he looked through the library, kept some works he valued, and had others burned. For many readers, that moment is the saddest part of the story.

After the Mongols, the fortress did not disappear completely. It was damaged, but parts of it continued to be used over the following centuries. Later rulers are reported to have used it as a prison, which makes sense, since getting out would be as hard as getting in. Over time, though, the walls slowly crumbled. Earthquakes, weather and people taking stone for building all played a part. What you see now is a ruin shaped by many centuries.

In recent decades, archaeologists have worked on the site to study and protect what remains. You may notice metal scaffolding, protective covers or marked paths. These are part of that work. They can look out of place at first, but they help keep the soft rock and old walls from collapsing further. Please stay on the paths. The edges here are real drops, and the ground can shift under your feet, especially near the outer walls.

As you walk, look closely at the surfaces. In places you can see where walls were cut into the living rock, or built up from rough stone and mortar on top of it. Look for channels and hollows in the rock. Water was always a problem on a summit like this, and the defenders needed to store rain and snowmelt to survive long sieges. Then look outward. Notice how much of the valley you can watch from here. Anyone approaching would be seen for hours.

Before you start back down, stand still once more. Feel the wind again, and the sun on the stone. Below you, the Alamut valley spreads out with its villages, fields and the line of the river. Almost a thousand years ago, someone stood where you are standing and watched the same slopes for riders. As you climb down the steps, look back once or twice. The rock will slowly close behind you, quiet, and very hard to reach.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes with grip for the long stair climb.
Bring water and sun protection; there is little shade above.
Visit in the morning for cooler air and clearer valley views.
Stay on marked paths; the summit edges are steep drops.
Bring a warm layer, since mountain wind can be strong.
