The story

Pass under the great arch on Ogrodowa Street, and the city opens into a wide plaza framed by walls of dark red brick. Hundreds of arched windows march along the facades. Towers and chimneys mark the sky, and the smell of coffee drifts where coal smoke once hung. A century ago this was one of the largest cotton factories in Europe. Today it is called Manufaktura.

Give yourself time to simply stand still at the entrance. The scale is hard to take in at first: the complex holds more than a dozen major buildings, and a walk around its outer edge takes the better part of an hour. Locals cross it as a shortcut, meet by the fountains, and treat the old factory yard as a public square.

Everything here was built by one man's will. Izrael Poznański was born in 1833 into a Jewish merchant family. He took over his father's small trading business and spent a lifetime turning it into a cotton empire. By the end of the nineteenth century, he stood among the richest industrialists in this part of Europe, and his name meant thread, cloth, and power.

The mills rose fast. The grandest of them, the main spinning mill, was completed in 1878: a long palace of industry, floor upon floor of brick and glass, built to impress as much as to produce. Raw cotton arrived by rail, and finished cloth left for markets across the Russian Empire.

At its height, the Poznański works formed a city within the city. Thousands of people worked here. The complex had its own workers' housing, fire brigade, warehouses, and rail sidings. The factory whistle divided the day, and whole families passed their lives inside its rhythm. The work was hard, the hours long, the wages small. Strikes and unrest returned again and again, and Reymont's novel The Promised Land fixed this world in Polish memory.

Cotton made this place, and cotton left it. After the Second World War the factory was nationalized and worked on under the name Poltex. It kept spinning through the communist decades. In the 1990s, when the old Eastern markets collapsed, the machines stopped. The gates closed, and for years the halls stood silent, home to pigeons, rust, and broken glass.

The rescue took nearly a decade. A French development company and Polish conservators took on the ruin together. Workers cleaned and repointed miles of brick wall, and new floors, glass, and steel went inside the old shells. In 2006 the complex reopened as Manufaktura, one of the largest revitalization projects Europe had seen. The site covers about twenty-seven hectares, the size of a small town district.

The market square at its heart is new, yet it already works like the city's living room. A long line of fountains crosses the paving. In summer, children run through the jets, concerts fill the evenings, and sand is trucked in for an artificial beach. In winter an ice rink takes over, ringed by lights. Around the square, cafes and restaurants fill the ground floors of the old mills.

Culture moved in beside commerce. In a former weaving hall, the ms2 gallery shows the Muzeum Sztuki collection of modern art, one of the oldest collections of the twentieth-century avant-garde in Europe. The Museum of the Factory tells the story of the Poznański works with original looms. When the demonstration machines run, the floor trembles, and you understand what a lifetime beside them meant.

The spinning mill of 1878 found a second life as a hotel. Architects set a swimming pool in a glass box on its roof, so guests now float in the air above halls where spinners once worked long shifts. It is an odd reversal, and the building carries it well.

Walk the edges of the square and read the details. Cast-iron columns carry brick vaults inside the shops. Old rails remain set in the paving. Hoist beams and iron wall anchors survive on the facades, quiet reminders that beauty here was once a by-product of work.

At the corner of the complex, facing the street, stands Izrael Poznański's own palace, a pile of domes and columns that now houses the city museum. Residence and factory stood wall against wall. The owner could watch his empire from his ballroom windows, and the workers could watch his chandeliers being lit.

Stand in the middle of the square at dusk, when floodlights warm the brick and the fountains fall quiet. The whistle no longer blows here. The walls that once closed thousands of workers in now draw the whole city inside, freely, for pleasure. Places, like people, sometimes get a second chance.

Key facts
