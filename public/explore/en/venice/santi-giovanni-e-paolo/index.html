The guide

The story

Listen first. Across the campo, a church bell sounds, and pigeons lift off the paving stones. Somewhere nearby, a boat motor knocks along a narrow canal. Ahead of you rises a wall of warm, rose-brown brick, plain and enormous, like a cliff made by human hands. The light moves across it slowly. In the afternoon, the brick turns the colour of terracotta and toast. This is Santi Giovanni e Paolo. For centuries, Venetians have shortened that long name into one quick word: Zanipolo.

That nickname comes from the Venetian dialect. In old Venetian speech, Giovanni becomes Zuane or Zan, and Paolo stays close to Polo. Run them together and you hear the sound of the street. And here is the first surprise. The saints in the name are not the apostles John and Paul. They are two Roman brothers, both martyrs of the early Christian church. Many visitors walk in and out without ever knowing that.

The church belongs to the Dominicans, an order of preaching friars founded in the thirteenth century. Their mission was to fill large spaces with large crowds and teach them. So they built big. Legend has it that a doge of Venice dreamed of this marshy ground covered in white flowers and doves, and an angel told him this was the place for the friars. Whatever the truth of the dream, the city granted the land, and construction began in the thirteenth century.

Building something this size on Venetian mud was slow work. The friars needed generations to finish it. The basilica was finally consecrated in 1430, long after its first stones were laid. Look at the facade and you will see that the story never quite ended. The lower part carries a carved marble doorway in an elegant early Renaissance style. The upper brickwork stays bare. The full marble skin that was planned for it never arrived.

Now step inside, and let your eyes adjust. The nave is tall and wide, held up by great columns and crossed high above by wooden tie-beams. Those beams are a Venetian solution. On soft ground, heavy stone vaults push walls outward, so builders tied the structure together like a barrel. The space feels open and calm. The floor is cool underfoot, and voices fall quiet almost by themselves. Light enters in long, soft shafts from the high windows.

This church is often called the pantheon of Venice. Around twenty-five doges, the elected rulers of the old Republic, are buried here. Their tombs line the walls like a long, silent procession. Some are modest. Others fill entire sections of wall, with carved figures, columns and painted marble. Walking the length of the nave is like walking through the story of Venetian power, written in stone, one ruler at a time.

One of the finest is the tomb of Doge Pietro Mocenigo, near the main entrance, on the inside wall of the facade. It is the work of the sculptor Pietro Lombardo and his workshop, in the late fifteenth century. Look closely and you will notice something unusual. The doge stands upright, not lying down in prayer. Around him are soldiers and scenes of victory. This is a tomb that celebrates a commander, not only a man of faith.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a dark one. Look along the right side of the nave for the monument to Marcantonio Bragadin. In 1571, he commanded the Venetian defence of Famagusta, on the island of Cyprus, against the Ottoman army. After the city surrendered, he was executed in a brutal way. He was skinned alive. Some years later, his skin was taken from Constantinople and brought home to Venice. Records hold that it rests here, in an urn high inside the monument.

Not everything here speaks of war. On the right side of the nave, find a painted altarpiece in several panels, made for Saint Vincent Ferrer, a Dominican preacher. It is widely attributed to the young Giovanni Bellini, one of the great painters of Renaissance Venice. Notice the clear light, the calm faces, the careful folds of fabric. Then turn to the south transept, where a vast stained-glass window glows. It was made in the fifteenth century with glass from the island of Murano.

On the left side of the church is the Chapel of the Rosary, and it carries a sad story. In 1867, a fire broke out there. Among the works destroyed was a famous altarpiece by Titian, showing the death of a Dominican saint, Peter Martyr. It had been moved into the chapel only for a short time. Today, the chapel has been restored, and paintings by Paolo Veronese, brought from elsewhere in the city, now fill its ceiling.

Back outside, look to your right. A bronze horseman rides high on a tall stone pedestal. This is Bartolomeo Colleoni, a mercenary commander who fought for Venice. The statue was designed by the Florentine artist Andrea del Verrocchio, and finished after his death by Alessandro Leopardi in the 1490s. Watch the horse in mid-stride and the rider twisting in the saddle. Even the face carries a hard, watchful frown.

The story behind the statue is a lesson in Venetian cleverness. Colleoni left money to the Republic on one condition: that his statue stand near San Marco. But Venice never allowed a personal monument in its main square. So the Senate chose a different San Marco. Next to the church stands the Scuola Grande di San Marco, a former confraternity hall. Its marble facade has carved lions and arches that seem to open deep into space, but they are flat panels, a clever optical trick. Today, the building serves as the entrance to the city hospital.

Before you leave, stand still in the middle of the campo for a moment. Feel the wide space around you, rare in a city of narrow lanes. Children run past the old well-head. People sit at café tables. The canal beside you carries boats and, sometimes, ambulances heading for the hospital doors. The great brick wall of Zanipolo stands behind all of it. As you walk away, look back once more and notice how the brick holds the last warm light of the day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cover shoulders and knees before entering the church.
Visit late afternoon when the brick facade glows warm.
Bring binoculars to study tombs high on the walls.
Walk the nave slowly along the walls, not the centre.
Pair the visit with a stroll through Castello's quiet lanes.
