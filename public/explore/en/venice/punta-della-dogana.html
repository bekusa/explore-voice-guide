The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the very tip of the point. On your right, the Grand Canal flows in. On your left, the wider Giudecca Canal opens toward the lagoon. The stone under your feet is worn smooth by centuries of shoes. Water slaps against the edge, and the wake of a passing vaporetto, the city's water bus, reaches you a few seconds after the boat has gone.

This narrow triangle of land is Punta della Dogana. The name means, roughly, the Customs Point. It is the eastern tip of Dorsoduro, one of the six historic districts of Venice. For a city that lived by trade, this was the front door. Ships arriving from the sea passed right here before they entered the Grand Canal, the main street of the Venetian Republic.

Think about what that meant. Venice was not built on farmland or mines. Its wealth came from moving goods: spices, silk, salt, glass, wine, timber. And every cargo that came in from the sea had to be declared, inspected, and taxed. The long, low building behind you was where that happened. It was the Dogana da Mar — the sea customs house. Land goods were handled elsewhere in the city.

The building you see today dates from the late seventeenth century. It was designed by the architect Giuseppe Benoni, who won a competition for the commission. Look at how plain the long walls are. These were warehouses, not palaces. Inside, the space was divided into long storage bays, where cargo could be stacked, counted, and checked by officials before the merchants paid their duties.

Now turn and look up at the tower at the tip of the point. This is the one theatrical gesture in an otherwise practical building. On top, two bronze figures of Atlas kneel and carry a golden globe on their shoulders. And on top of the globe stands a single figure — Fortune, holding a sail or banner in her hands. The sculptural group is attributed to Bernardo Falcone.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Fortune is not fixed in place. She is a weathervane. When the wind changes, she turns, and her banner swings to show which way the air is moving. For sailors and merchants, that was useful information. It was also a clear message. In Venice, fortune moved with the wind and the sea — and a fortune could be made or lost in one voyage.

Just behind the customs house rises the great white dome of Santa Maria della Salute. The church was built as a thanksgiving after a terrible plague in the seventeenth century, and it was designed by Baldassare Longhena. From the water, the dome and the customs tower form one composition. The church stands for faith and gratitude. The tower stands for trade and luck. Venice put both side by side, at the entrance to its main canal.

For a long time in the twentieth century, the customs house stood mostly empty and closed. The salt air and the high water, which Venetians call acqua alta, damaged the brick and the timber. Then, in the 2000s, the building found a new life. The French collector François Pinault and his foundation took it on, and it reopened in 2009 as a museum of contemporary art.

The restoration was led by the Japanese architect Tadao Ando. He is known for smooth, polished concrete and calm, controlled light. But here he did something careful. He kept the old brick walls and the heavy wooden roof trusses. He cleaned and repaired them, rather than hiding them. At the centre of the building, he placed a clean concrete space, like a quiet room inside the old warehouse. If you go inside, you will feel the contrast — rough old brick, and silky new concrete, next to each other.

Another small surprise is right at the tip of the point. In 2009, a large white sculpture of a boy holding a frog was installed here. It was a work by the American artist Charles Ray. Some people loved it. Others felt it did not belong on this historic corner. A few years later it was removed, and a traditional-style lamppost now stands in its place. So the spot you are standing on was once the centre of a real argument about old and new in Venice.

Now take in the view. Straight ahead, across the water, is the island of San Giorgio Maggiore, with its white church facade by Andrea Palladio. To the left, along the curving waterfront, you can see the pink and white Doge's Palace and the bell tower of Saint Mark's Square. To the right, the long island of Giudecca stretches away. Few places in the city give you this many landmarks in one turn of the head.

When you are ready to move on, you have two good walks. You can follow the Zattere, the long sunny promenade along the Giudecca Canal, where locals sit with a coffee or an ice cream. Or you can walk back past the Salute church into the quiet lanes of Dorsoduro, with small bridges, art galleries, and neighbourhood bars. Both routes show you a calmer side of the city.

Before you go, look once more at the water around the point. Notice how it changes colour — green near the stone, grey and blue further out. Listen for the boats, the gulls, the low voices of people stopping here just like you. For centuries, ships arrived at this corner carrying the wealth of the world. Today, what arrives here is mostly people, and the light. And up on the tower, Fortune is still turning with the wind.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the Zattere promenade for a calm waterfront stroll.
Visit the Salute church next door after the point.
Come early morning for a quieter tip of land.
Museum entry needs a separate ticket from the church.
Check for acqua alta flooding before visiting in autumn.
