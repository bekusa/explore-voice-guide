The guide

The story

Feel the stone under your feet first. It is pale and smooth, worn by millions of travellers dragging suitcases off their trains. Below you, the Grand Canal runs green and restless. Water taxis cut white lines across it, and the vaporetti, the city's water buses, rumble past with their engines low. The air smells of salt, diesel, and wet brick. This is often the first bridge a visitor crosses in Venice. Many cross it without looking up at all.

Welcome to the Ponte degli Scalzi. Its name means the Bridge of the Barefoot Ones. That sounds poetic, and it is, but it also points to a real church. Just beside the station stands the Church of the Scalzi, built for the Discalced Carmelites. Discalced simply means shoeless. The friars of this order went barefoot or wore simple sandals as a sign of humility. The bridge took its name from their neighbourhood.

Take a moment to orient yourself. On one side is Cannaregio, with the railway station of Santa Lucia and its long modern front. On the other side is Santa Croce, one of the old districts of the city. Look across the water to the green copper dome of San Simeon Piccolo. It sits on the Santa Croce bank, almost facing the station. For many arriving travellers, that dome is their first image of Venice.

Here is something many visitors do not know. For most of its long history, the Grand Canal had only one bridge. That was the Rialto, in the commercial heart of the city. Everyone else crossed by boat, often on the traghetto, a simple gondola ferry. Today there are only four bridges across the whole Grand Canal. You are standing on one of them, and it is the second youngest.

The story of this crossing begins with the railway. In the middle of the nineteenth century, Venice was ruled by the Austrian Empire. The Austrians connected the city to the mainland by rail, and the station brought a new flow of people. They needed a way across the canal. In 1858, an iron bridge opened at this spot. It was practical, industrial, and very different from the stone city around it.

That iron bridge did its job, but it had a problem. It sat low over the water. As steam-powered vaporetti arrived on the Grand Canal, the low iron span became an obstacle for boats passing underneath. Many Venetians also never warmed to its look. A metal structure in the middle of marble palaces felt foreign. By the early twentieth century, the city decided to replace it.

The new bridge was designed by the engineer Eugenio Miozzi and opened in 1934. Miozzi shaped a great deal of modern Venice. He also worked on the wooden Accademia Bridge further down the canal, and on the large car park at Piazzale Roma. His task here was difficult. He had to build something modern and strong that still looked as if it belonged in Venice.

His answer was a single arch of Istrian stone. Istrian stone is a dense white limestone from the coast across the Adriatic Sea. Venetian builders used it for centuries because salt water does not easily damage it. Look along the waterfronts and you will see it everywhere — on steps, window frames, and the edges of palaces. By choosing this stone, Miozzi tied his new bridge to the oldest building tradition in the city.

Now look at the shape. The arch is wide and rises high enough for boats to pass freely underneath. There are no supports standing in the water. That was the whole point. The canal stays open from bank to bank. The steps climb gently on both sides, and the rise at the centre gives you a natural lookout point over the water.

Step back toward the station side and turn to the Church of the Scalzi itself. Its façade is heavy with carved stone, columns, and statues, in the dramatic Baroque style of the seventeenth century. Baroque means architecture full of movement, curves, and decoration. Inside, the church once held a ceiling painted by Giambattista Tiepolo, one of the great Venetian painters. It was destroyed during the First World War, when a bomb struck the building.

That loss carries a small irony. The bomb came during a war against Austria, the same power that had built the first bridge here. Within a few decades, Venice saw both an Austrian iron bridge removed and an Austrian bomb destroy a masterpiece next to it. History in this city often sits this close together, on a single stretch of canal bank.

Before you move on, pause at the top of the arch. Look down the canal toward the heart of Venice. The palaces lean slightly, their foundations resting on wooden piles driven deep into the mud long ago. Gondolas, delivery boats, and water buses share the same water. Listen for the boatmen calling to each other. This is a working canal, not a museum, and it has been for centuries.

When you step off the bridge, notice the stone again under your feet. Notice how many people around you are arriving, and how many are leaving. This bridge sits at the border between the modern world of trains and the older world of boats and footpaths. Behind you is the railway line to the mainland. Ahead of you are the narrow streets. Take your first turn slowly. Venice rewards those who walk without hurry.

Pause at the top for views along the canal.
Keep right on the steps when carrying luggage.
Visit the Church of the Scalzi beside the station.
