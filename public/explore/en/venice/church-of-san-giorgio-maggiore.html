The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against the stone edge of the quay. A vaporetto engine idles, then pulls away. Across the open water, the domes and the bell tower of Saint Mark's sit low on the horizon, softened by haze. Here, on this small island, the air feels quieter than in the city you just left. In front of you rises a white facade, bright against the sky, so clean it can look almost unreal from a distance.

This is the Church of San Giorgio Maggiore, on the island of the same name. It faces the Doge's Palace directly across Saint Mark's Basin, the wide stretch of water at the mouth of the Grand Canal. That position is no accident. For centuries, anyone arriving in Venice by sea saw two great white buildings framing the view: the seat of government on one side, and this church on the other. Power and prayer, facing each other across the water.

The island was home to monks long before this church existed. A Benedictine monastery was founded here in the late tenth century, after a Venetian nobleman gave the land to the order. The Benedictines are a Catholic religious order that follows the rule of Saint Benedict, a life built around prayer and work. Over the following centuries, the monastery grew wealthy and influential. Its monks farmed, studied, kept a library, and hosted important guests. The church you see today replaced earlier buildings on this same ground.

The architect was Andrea Palladio, one of the most influential designers of the Renaissance. Work began in the 1560s. Palladio is famous for bringing the forms of ancient Roman temples into new buildings: columns, triangular pediments, and calm, balanced proportions. His villas in the countryside around Venice shaped country houses across Europe and later in America. But here, in a city of water and narrow lanes, he had a rare chance. He could design a great church with open space all around it.

Look closely at the facade, because it holds a clever puzzle. Palladio faced an old problem. A church has a tall central nave and lower side aisles, but a classical temple front is a single block. His answer was to lay one temple front over another. A tall front with four giant columns covers the nave. Behind it, a wider and lower front with smaller pilasters covers the aisles. The two triangles interlock. Once you notice it, you cannot stop seeing it.

Here is something many visitors do not know. Palladio died in 1580, and he never saw this facade built. The interior was largely complete by then, but the front was finished in the early seventeenth century by others who followed his design. The white stone is Istrian stone, quarried across the Adriatic in what is now Croatia and Slovenia. Venetians loved it because it resists salt water and stays pale. That is why so much of the city's trim shines white.

Now step inside. The first thing most people feel is light. The interior is white and grey, with plaster walls and pale stone columns. There is very little gold and very little colour on the walls themselves. Daylight falls through large windows and spreads evenly across the space. Palladio wanted harmony, not drama. Stand near the entrance and look straight ahead. The nave, the crossing under the dome, and the altar line up in one long, calm view.

The colour that is here comes from paintings. In the chancel, near the high altar, hang two large canvases by Tintoretto, one of the great Venetian painters. One shows the Last Supper. The other shows the Israelites gathering manna, the food that fell from heaven in the Old Testament story. These are late works, painted near the end of his life in the 1590s. The Last Supper is unusual. The table runs diagonally into the dark, and ghostly angels drift through smoky lamplight.

Behind the high altar is the monks' choir, where the community gathered to sing their daily prayers. The wooden choir stalls are carved with scenes from the life of Saint Benedict. They were made in the late sixteenth century by a Flemish woodcarver. If the choir is open, take a moment there. The wood is dark and warm, a strong contrast to the cool white church. The monks sat here, hidden from the public, while their voices carried into the nave.

The monastery once held another famous painting. Paolo Veronese painted an enormous Wedding at Cana for the monks' dining hall, the refectory, also designed by Palladio. In 1797, Napoleon's troops took the painting to Paris. It still hangs in the Louvre today. More than two centuries later, a full-size digital facsimile was made and installed in the original refectory. So the image has returned to the room it was made for, even if the original has not.

The island played a part in one more unusual moment. At the end of the eighteenth century, Rome was occupied by French forces, and a pope had died in exile. The cardinals could not meet in Rome to choose his successor. They gathered here instead, in the monastery, and after months of debate they elected Pope Pius the Seventh in 1800. It is one of the very few papal elections held outside Rome in modern times.

For the best view in Venice, many locals will point you to the bell tower here. The present tower replaced an earlier one that collapsed in the late eighteenth century. Today a lift carries visitors up, so there is no long climb. From the top, the whole city spreads out below you: Saint Mark's Square, the domes of the Salute church, the long island of the Giudecca, and on clear days, the mountains far to the north. Access to the tower usually needs a separate ticket.

The former monastery buildings now belong to the Giorgio Cini Foundation, a cultural institution founded in the twentieth century by an industrialist in memory of his son. It runs a library, research centres, and exhibitions in the cloisters. Most of that complex is open only for events or guided visits. Even so, the island feels very different from central Venice. There are no shops crowding the quay, only stone, water, and the slow traffic of boats on the basin.

Before you leave, walk back to the edge of the quay and turn around. Look at the facade once more, then turn and look across the water at the Doge's Palace. Painters stood near this spot for centuries, including Claude Monet, who painted this church at dusk. Watch how the white stone changes as clouds move overhead. Listen to the water against the steps. When your boat comes, keep looking back. The church will stay bright long after the island falls behind you.
