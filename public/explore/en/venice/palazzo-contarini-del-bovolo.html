The guide

The story

Listen first. The narrow lane behind you swallows the noise of the city, and the tap of footsteps on stone carries further than you expect. Then the courtyard opens, and there it is. A slender white tower curls upward in a spiral of arches, pale stone against warm red brick. It seems almost too delicate for the old wall it is attached to. Take a breath here. Most visitors walk past this corner of Venice without knowing it exists.

You are standing at Palazzo Contarini del Bovolo, in the San Marco district, a short walk from Campo Manin. The name holds the key to the whole place. In the Venetian dialect, a bovolo is a snail shell. Look up at the staircase, and the nickname makes sense at once. The steps wind around a central column, turn after turn, just like the coil of a snail. The building took its name from this one feature.

The Contarini were one of the oldest patrician families in Venice, the noble class that governed the Republic for centuries. The family was large, and it gave the city several doges, the elected rulers of Venice. Because there were so many Contarini households, each branch needed its own label. This branch became known simply by its staircase. Imagine being remembered across five hundred years for a single architectural idea.

The tower was added around the end of the fifteenth century. It is usually attributed to a builder named Giovanni Candi, although records from this period are not always complete. At that time the palace already stood here, built in the Gothic style. The family wanted something new and impressive. Instead of rebuilding the whole house, they added this external spiral, a vertical showpiece that connected the floors and caught every eye in the courtyard.

Now look closely at the design. Each level is a ring of rounded arches, and the arches climb in a diagonal line that follows the stairs inside. Above the spiral, the tower ends in a small domed lantern. Beside it, horizontal galleries of arches, called loggias, run across the facade. The style mixes ideas. There is Renaissance order in the repeated arches, a lingering Gothic taste for lightness, and a hint of the Byzantine East that shaped Venice for so long.

Here is something many people miss. In Venice, grand staircases were usually hidden inside buildings, or tucked into a courtyard behind the main front. The proud face of a palace looked toward the canal, because the water was the main street. This staircase faces an inner courtyard instead. It was a private display, meant for guests who had already been invited in. In a way, you are seeing the palace the way a welcomed visitor would.

The materials tell their own story. The white stone is Istrian stone, brought from the coast of what is now Croatia and Slovenia. Venetian builders loved it because it resists salt water and stays bright. The darker sections are brick, the everyday material of the lagoon city. Look at where the two meet. The contrast between the cool white columns and the warm brick gives the tower its sharp, graphic outline, even on a grey day.

Before you climb, glance around the small garden courtyard. Scattered here you will find several old stone wellheads. These carved stone rims once sat over cisterns all across Venice. Fresh water was precious in a city built on salt water, so rainwater was collected beneath squares and courtyards. Each wellhead is a reminder of daily life, of servants and neighbours drawing water long before taps and pipes reached the islands.

Over the centuries, the palace changed hands, and its fortunes rose and fell with the city. For a long time it belonged to a Venetian charitable institution. By the late twentieth century, the stonework needed serious care. A careful restoration was carried out, and the tower reopened to visitors in the 2010s. Today, the stone looks clean and sharp again, and you can climb the same spiral that the Contarini guests once walked.

Inside the tower, the climb is gentle and steady. The stairs turn constantly, so the view through the arches changes with every few steps. First you see the courtyard below, then neighbouring walls, then chimneys and rooftops. Take it slowly. The openings frame small pieces of the city, like windows in a moving picture. Children often enjoy counting the turns, while adults tend to stop at every arch.

At the top, the belvedere opens out. A belvedere is simply a viewing platform, and this one rewards the climb. Spread around you is a sea of terracotta roofs, chimney pots shaped like upturned funnels, and small rooftop terraces on wooden posts. Look for the tall bell tower of Saint Mark in the distance, and the curving domes of the basilica. From up here, Venice feels quiet and close, a maze seen from above.

Here is the second surprise. From ground level, Venice can feel crowded, all bridges, alleys, and people. From this height, you notice how dense and tightly woven the city really is. There are almost no open spaces. Courtyards like the one below are rare pockets of air between houses. That is part of why this tower feels so special. It was built to rise out of the tangle and let light and views in.

When you come back down, pause in the courtyard once more. Notice how the sound changes as you leave the stairs, how the walls hold the quiet. Look up at the spiral one last time, the arches climbing like the inside of a shell. As you step back into the narrow lane, listen again for your own footsteps on the stone. The snail stays behind you, coiled and patient, waiting for the next curious visitor.

Before you go

Practical tips

Follow signs from Campo Manin through the narrow lane.
Climb slowly and pause at each arch for changing views.
Bring a camera for the rooftop panorama at the top.
Visit early to share the small courtyard with fewer people.
Wear sturdy shoes for the winding stone steps.
