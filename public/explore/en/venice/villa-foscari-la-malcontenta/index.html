The guide

The story

Listen first. The water of the Brenta moves slowly here, brown and green, with barely a sound. A boat engine hums somewhere downstream. Then you turn, and a pale temple front rises above the grass. Its white columns stand high above the riverbank, as if the building has stepped up out of the water to look at you. This is Villa Foscari, known to almost everyone by a stranger name: La Malcontenta, the unhappy woman.

You are not in Venice itself, but you are very close. This stretch of the mainland lies between the lagoon and the city of Padua. For centuries, the Brenta Canal was a kind of private road for Venetian nobility. Families travelled upriver by boat from their palaces on the Grand Canal. Along the banks, they built country houses for summer, for farming, and for showing the world their wealth and taste. This villa sits near the very start of that journey.

The architect was Andrea Palladio, the great builder of the Veneto in the sixteenth century. He worked for two brothers of the powerful Foscari family, Nicolò and Alvise. The house took shape in the middle of the century, in the years around 1560. Palladio was proud of it. He included the design in his famous treatise, the Four Books of Architecture, published in 1570. That book later carried his ideas to England, Ireland, and eventually to the United States.

Look at the front again. Those six Ionic columns, with their scroll-shaped tops, hold up a triangular pediment like an ancient Roman temple. Palladio believed that the Romans had decorated their own houses this way. He was probably wrong about that. But his mistake changed the history of architecture. Because of villas like this one, countless country houses, banks, and even government buildings around the world now wear a temple front.

Here is something many visitors miss. Those columns are not solid marble. Palladio built mostly in brick, a cheap and local material in this region, which has very little good stone. The brick was then covered in plaster and finished to look like noble stone. Even the walls carry lines cut into the plaster, so that they look like large stone blocks. It is an elegant trick, and it allowed the villa to look grand without the cost of quarrying.

Notice also how high the house sits. The main floor rests on a tall base, and the portico is reached by staircases. These stairs do not run straight down the front. Instead, they descend sideways, to the left and to the right. This high platform had two purposes. It protected the living rooms from the damp and the floods of the riverbank. And it made the owners look down on the world, which was exactly the impression they wanted to give.

Now walk around to the back, toward the garden, if the visit allows. The rear facade feels completely different. There is no temple here. Instead, you see a huge semicircular window, divided into three parts by two vertical posts. This shape is called a thermal window, because Palladio copied it from the great public baths of ancient Rome. It pours light into the central hall inside, and it gives the plain back of the house a strong, almost modern face.

Inside, the plan is shaped like a cross. A tall central hall runs from front to back under a curved, barrel-shaped ceiling. Smaller rooms sit on each side in a careful, balanced order. Palladio cared deeply about proportion, the mathematical relationship between length, width, and height. He believed that rooms built on harmonious numbers would feel calm and right to the human body. Stand still in the hall for a moment, and see if you feel that quiet balance yourself.

The walls were painted with frescoes, which are paintings made directly into wet plaster. The main painter was Giovanni Battista Zelotti, and another artist, Battista Franco, worked here before his death. The scenes show classical gods, allegories, and stories from ancient myth. Over the centuries, many of these paintings faded, and some were covered or damaged. What you see today is the result of long and patient restoration, so look closely at the difference between strong colour and softer, worn areas.

And now, the name. Why would anyone call this calm house the unhappy woman? Legend has it that a Foscari wife was sent here as punishment, perhaps for an affair, and lived out her days in exile. Locals still enjoy telling this story. Historians, however, point to a more ordinary answer. The name seems to belong to the area itself, used for this stretch of land and water before the villa was famous. The legend is better, but the place name is probably older.

The villa has had difficult chapters. After the Republic of Venice fell at the end of the eighteenth century, many noble families lost their fortunes, and houses along the Brenta were abandoned or stripped. This one suffered too. In the twentieth century, an English-speaking admirer of Palladio, Bertie Landsberg, bought the neglected building and began to bring it back to life. Later, the Foscari family returned and took ownership of their ancestral house once more, a rare homecoming in Venetian history.

Today, the villa forms part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing that covers the city of Vicenza and the Palladian villas of the Veneto. It is also unusual among those villas. Many Palladian country houses have long, low side wings for farm work, storage, and animals. This one stands almost alone, a single clear block beside the water. That isolation gives it a special purity. Nothing distracts you from the shape itself, and from the reflection it throws onto the canal.

So before you leave, find a place on the bank where you can see the whole front at once. Watch how the light moves across the plaster, warmer in the evening, cooler in the morning. Listen to the water again, the same water that once carried Venetian boats to these steps. As you walk away, look along the canal at the other houses. You will start to notice the temple fronts, the high bases, the balanced windows. That is Palladio, still speaking quietly along the Brenta.

What to look for

Six Ionic columns with scroll-shaped capitals on the portico.
Side staircases descending left and right from the portico.
Plaster walls scored to imitate large stone blocks.
Large semicircular thermal window on the rear facade.
Barrel-vaulted central hall in a cross-shaped plan.
Before you go

Practical tips

View the canal facade from the opposite bank for full framing.
Arrive by Brenta river boat to approach as Venetians did.
Walk to the garden side to see the rear facade.
Check visiting days in advance, since this is a private home.
Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the portico.
