The guide

The story

Stand for a moment and listen to the water. Vaporetto engines throb and fade. Small waves slap against old stone. Here the Grand Canal makes a sharp turn, and the light opens up in both directions. Venetians call this bend the volta del canal. Right on that turn, its pale face catching the sun, rises Ca' Foscari, one of the finest Gothic palaces in the city.

First, the name. In Venice, Ca' is short for casa, which means house. For centuries, Venetians used this modest word even for grand palaces. The custom says something about the city's republican pride. A noble family lived in a house, not a palace. So Ca' Foscari simply means the house of the Foscari family. And that family produced one of the most powerful men in Venetian history.

His name was Francesco Foscari. He served as Doge, the elected head of the Venetian Republic, for more than thirty years in the fifteenth century. That was longer than any other Doge before or after him. During his rule, Venice pushed its power deep into the Italian mainland. These were expensive, exhausting wars. They made Venice a land power, but they also made Foscari many enemies.

In the middle of the fifteenth century, Foscari acquired an older building on this site and rebuilt it on a grand scale. He chose the spot carefully. A house on the bend of the canal could be seen from far away in two directions. Every boat that came up or down the waterway would pass beneath it. In a city where everyone moved by water, this was the best address you could buy.

Now look at the facade. The style is late Venetian Gothic, the decorative style that Venice made its own in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Notice the pointed arches, slender columns, and stone tracery. On the upper floors, look for the rows of quatrefoils, those four-lobed openings like stone clover leaves. If you have seen the Doge's Palace near Saint Mark's Square, the pattern will feel familiar. That resemblance was no accident.

The Doge was building a family home that echoed the seat of government. It was a statement, written in stone, about who held power. The large windows in the centre of each upper floor mark the main halls inside. In Venetian palaces, these long rooms ran from the canal front to the back of the building. They let light and air flow straight through the house. Count the floors, and you can read the old layout from the outside.

Here is the first surprise. This glorious house did not bring its builder a happy ending. Foscari's son Jacopo was accused of crimes and sent into exile more than once. The old Doge had to accept the judgements of the state against his own child. In 1457, the Republic's councils forced Francesco Foscari to give up his office. He died only days later. The story was so painful that it travelled far beyond Venice.

Centuries later, Lord Byron turned it into a verse drama called The Two Foscari. Then Giuseppe Verdi, the great Italian opera composer, made it into an opera with the same title. So a family tragedy that began in this house reached theatres across Europe. As you look at the calm, elegant windows, it is worth remembering the grief behind them. Venetian power was glamorous, but the Republic placed the state above any single family.

The house continued to impress visitors long after the Doge's death. In 1574, the young King Henry the Third of France passed through Venice. The Republic gave him a lavish welcome, and he stayed here at Ca' Foscari. Records describe festivities, banquets, and decorated boats on the canal. Imagine the water in front of you crowded with gilded barges, music, and torchlight. The bend of the canal became a stage.

And it still is. Every year, Venice holds the Regata Storica, a historic regatta on the Grand Canal. Rowers race in traditional boats, and a floating stand is set up near this bend for the finish. Crowds line the banks and lean from windows. The house of the Foscari stands at the centre of the celebration once again. Here is the second surprise. Today, this palace is not a museum, and it is not a private residence. It is a university.

Ca' Foscari University of Venice began here in 1868 as a school of commerce. It was among the first business schools in Italy. Its founders believed that a trading city needed modern training in economics, languages, and law. Over time, the school grew into a full university. It is especially known today for languages, including Asian and Middle Eastern studies. Students now climb the same stairs that once welcomed a French king.

Inside, the building mixes old and new. In the twentieth century, the Venetian architect Carlo Scarpa redesigned some of the interior spaces, including a well-known lecture hall. Scarpa was famous for combining modern design with Venetian craft, using wood, glass, and stone with great care. If you can step into the courtyard from the land side, you will feel the change of pace at once. The canal noise falls away. Students talk, bicycles are absent, and the stone feels cool.

Watch for one lively local tradition. When students graduate, friends crown them with laurel wreaths. Then they often read out a long, teasing poster, full of jokes and cartoons about the graduate's life. Sometimes there is singing, and sometimes the graduate is gently embarrassed in public. If you see a young person in a green wreath surrounded by laughing friends, you are watching a ritual that the whole city knows well.

Before you leave, look once more at the bend in the water. Boats slow down here to make the turn. The light shifts as clouds pass over the canal. A doge built this house to be seen, and five centuries later, it is still seen by everyone who passes. As you walk away, listen to the voices of students in the alleys nearby. The house of the Foscari is still full of life.

Before you go

Practical tips

View the facade from the opposite bank or a vaporetto.
Ride slowly past the bend on a Grand Canal vaporetto.
Enter the courtyard from the land side when the university allows.
Respect lectures and students; this is a working university.
Visit during the Regata Storica to see the finish here.
