The guide

The story

Gold glows in the half-dark above you. Even before your eyes adjust, the ceiling seems to float, lit from within. This is St Mark's Basilica, the great church of Venice. For most of its life it was not a cathedral. It was the private chapel of the doge, and the showcase of an empire built on the sea.

The story begins with a theft. In 828, Venetian merchants took the body of St Mark the Evangelist from Alexandria in Egypt. Two merchants, Buono of Malamocco and Rustico of Torcello, led the raid. Legend says they hid the relics under a layer of pork to keep inspectors away. True or not, the arrival changed Venice. The city now had a great patron, and it needed a great church.

The building you see is the third church on this spot. It was begun in the 1060s and consecrated in 1094. Its builders looked east, to Constantinople, and copied the plan of a Greek cross with five domes. That is why St Mark's looks unlike any other church in Italy.

For centuries, Venetian ships brought treasures home for the church. Columns, carvings, and colored marble arrived from ports across the Mediterranean. Tradition says returning merchants were required to donate materials for the decoration. The facade became a collage of the whole sea trade, piece by piece, century by century.

Before entering, study the facade mosaics above the doors. The oldest one, above the left door, dates from the 1260s. It shows this very church, with the body of St Mark being carried inside. Look closely and you can compare the mosaic's picture with the building in front of you.

Above the main door stand four bronze horses. They are replicas. The originals, ancient sculptures of gilded bronze, were taken from Constantinople when Venetian forces helped capture the city in 1204. Napoleon later carried them to Paris, but they returned in 1815. The real horses now stand inside the museum, safe from the salt air.

Step through the doors and let your eyes adjust. The ceilings and domes are covered with mosaics on a gold ground. Taken together, they spread over about 8,000 square meters, more than a full football pitch. Most were made between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries, with later additions. The mosaics tell stories in pictures. Near the entrance, scenes from the Old Testament cover the small domes of the porch. Inside, Christ rises in glory in the central dome. Apostles, prophets, and angels circle above the gold. For medieval visitors who could not read, this ceiling was the whole Bible, opened overhead.

Look down as well. The floor is a sea of marble patterns, laid in the twelfth century and worn by feet ever since. Circles, stars, and animals swirl across it. And it rolls like water. The ground beneath has settled unevenly for nine hundred years, so the pavement rises and dips underfoot. No two visitors walk quite the same line across it.

Behind the high altar stands the Pala d'Oro, the golden altarpiece. Goldsmiths in Constantinople began it in 976, and Venice enlarged it for centuries. Its panels hold nearly 2,000 gems and enamel pictures of saints. The remains of St Mark rest below the altar, under a simple stone.

The church was the stage of the state. Here new doges were presented to the people. Here Venice gave thanks for victories and prayed in times of plague. Choirs sang from twin galleries, and composers wrote music that echoed between the domes. Only in 1807 did the building become the cathedral of Venice.

Climb the steep stairs to the museum and the outdoor gallery. Up there you can study the original bronze horses face to face. In the museum you will also pass fragments of old mosaics rescued during past repairs. Then step onto the loggia above the main door. The whole of St Mark's Square opens below you, with the bell tower rising on your left and the lagoon shining beyond.

The sea that made the church also threatens it. At high tides, water still enters the porch, and mosaics near the floor carry salt scars. Restorers work constantly, replacing tiny stones by hand. Keeping this building alive is slow, patient work that never truly ends.

Find a quiet corner before you leave, and look up one more time. Candlelight and daylight move across the gold, and the figures seem to breathe. A thousand years of prayers, bargains, and thanks have risen into these domes. For a moment, add your own.

Practical tips

Pay the small extra fees for the Pala d'Oro and museum
Go early or late; midday brings the largest crowds
