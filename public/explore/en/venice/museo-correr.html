The guide

The story

Climb the wide stone staircase, and the noise of Saint Mark's Square starts to fade behind you. The pigeons, the café orchestras, the rolling suitcases... all of it drops away. At the top, the air is cooler and still. Polished floors catch the light from tall windows. Through the glass, the square spreads out below you, and for the first time you see it the way the people who lived in these rooms once did. You are no longer in the crowd. You are above it.

This is the Museo Correr, the museum of Venice itself. It sits at the western end of Piazza San Marco, directly across from the golden facade of the basilica. Most visitors walk past its arcades without noticing a museum is here at all. The entrance is easy to miss, tucked beneath the colonnade. Yet behind these windows lies one of the deepest collections of Venetian art and civic memory anywhere in the city.

The museum begins with one man. Teodoro Correr was a Venetian nobleman and a passionate collector. He lived through the fall of the Venetian Republic at the end of the eighteenth century, when centuries of independence ended almost overnight. Palaces were emptied. Paintings, documents and objects were scattered and sold. Correr gathered what he could. When he died in 1830, he left his entire collection to the city, so that Venice would keep a record of who it had been.

For almost a century, that collection lived elsewhere in the city. In 1922 it moved here, into the grand buildings that frame the square. Think about that for a moment. The memory of the old Republic now fills the very rooms built by the powers that ended it. That is the first quiet irony of this place, and it shapes everything you see inside.

The wing you entered through is called the Napoleonic Wing. After Napoleon took control of Venice, he wanted a royal palace worthy of the city. The square was missing one thing he required — a grand ballroom. To make space, a church at this end of the square was pulled down. That church, San Geminiano, had been rebuilt by the architect Jacopo Sansovino, one of the great builders of sixteenth-century Venice. Its loss still stings for many Venetians.

What replaced it is elegant and cool, in the neoclassical style — a look inspired by ancient Greece and Rome, with clean lines, columns and restrained ornament. Walk through the ballroom and the formal rooms, and you will see marble, gilding and painted ceilings. Later, under Austrian rule, members of the Habsburg family used these apartments. Among them was the Empress Elisabeth, often called Sisi, whose rooms have been restored and opened to visitors.

In the early rooms you will meet a young Antonio Canova. He was the most celebrated sculptor of his age, and he trained here in the Veneto. The Correr holds some of his early works, including a group showing Daedalus fixing wings to his son Icarus. Look at the contrast between the two figures. The father is weathered and focused. The boy is smooth and unaware. You already know how the story ends. He does not.

Now the museum shifts from empire to Republic. These rooms hold the objects of everyday power in old Venice. There are coins, ceremonial robes, banners and maps. Keep an eye out for chopines, the astonishingly tall platform shoes once worn by Venetian women. Some stand so high that the wearer needed a servant's arm to walk. They lifted fine fabrics above the mud, and they also made a very public statement about wealth.

One object stops many visitors in their tracks. It is a vast bird's-eye view of Venice, printed from woodblocks around the year 1500 and linked to the artist Jacopo de' Barbari. Every canal, church and campo is drawn as if seen from the sky, centuries before anyone could fly. Here is the surprise. The museum keeps not only the print, but also the original carved wooden blocks used to make it. Lean in, and try to find the square where you are standing.

Higher up, the picture gallery traces Venetian painting across several centuries. You will find works by Giovanni Bellini, whose soft light and calm faces helped define the city's style. There is also a moving Pietà by Antonello da Messina. Time has damaged it badly, and the paint is worn away in places. Yet the grief in the figures still comes through. Sometimes a damaged painting holds the eye more firmly than a perfect one.

Do not leave without finding Vittore Carpaccio's Two Venetian Ladies. Two women sit on a terrace with their dogs and birds, looking bored and a little distant. For a long time people called them courtesans. Today most scholars see them as respectable noblewomen. And here is the second secret. This panel is only the lower half of a larger picture. The top half, showing hunters on the lagoon, hangs in a museum in Los Angeles, thousands of kilometres away.

The Correr also connects to its neighbours. From inside, the route leads into the National Archaeological Museum and the monumental rooms of the Marciana Library. That library building is another work by Sansovino, the same architect whose church was lost to make room for the ballroom. So the man's work is both missing and present in this one visit. Under painted ceilings, you can stand in reading halls that once held the Republic's most precious books.

Before you go, find a window that faces the square once more. Below, the crowds still move in slow currents, the bell tower rises to your left, and the domes of the basilica glow at the far end. Everything you saw inside — the coins, the shoes, the map, the bored ladies on their terrace — came from that world below. When you step back down the staircase, the noise will return. Listen to it for a moment... it is the same square, still alive.

Before you go

Practical tips

Look for the entrance under the arcade opposite the basilica.
Ask about combined tickets with the Doge's Palace.
Save energy for the picture gallery on upper floors.
Follow the route into the Marciana Library rooms.
Pause at the windows for elevated views of the square.
