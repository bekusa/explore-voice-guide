The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps softly against stone steps, a vaporetto engine hums past, and the wake rolls in to lap at the base of the palace. Look up from the canal, and the façade rises in pale Istrian stone, a dense white limestone from across the Adriatic. It does not crumble the way brick does when salt water rises. Ca' Vendramin Calergi stands on the Grand Canal in Cannaregio, one of the six districts of Venice, and it has watched this water for more than five hundred years.

This palace sits at a turning point in Venetian taste. For centuries, the great houses along the canal were Gothic, with pointed arches and lacy stone tracery. Here, at the start of the sixteenth century, something new appears. The arches are round. The columns follow classical rules. The proportions are calm and balanced. This is the Renaissance, the revival of ancient Roman ideas of order, arriving on the Grand Canal in one confident statement.

The design is usually credited to Mauro Codussi. He was an architect from Lombardy who shaped much of early Renaissance Venice. Codussi died before the work was finished, and the palace was completed in the years after, around 1509. Builders from the workshop of the Lombardo family, a dynasty of sculptors and architects, are often linked to the final stages. So you are looking at one architect's vision, finished by other hands. That was common in Venice, where building on water took patience.

Now look at the windows on the upper floors. Each large arch holds two smaller arches, with a round opening above them. This pattern is a signature of Codussi's style. It lets in a great deal of light while it keeps a strong, readable rhythm across the front. Between the windows, columns stack in tiers, one order above another, the way Roman builders arranged them on theatres. Set into the stone are discs of coloured marble. They catch the sun like coins pressed into the wall.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. At the level of the water, carved into the façade, is a short Latin inscription: Non nobis, Domine, non nobis. It comes from a psalm and means, "Not to us, Lord, not to us." The full line gives the glory to God and not to people. It is a striking choice for a family that had just built one of the grandest houses in the city. Humility, written in stone, on a palace designed to impress.

The palace was built for the Loredan family, an old Venetian noble house. Over the following century, it changed hands. In the late sixteenth century, it passed to Vittore Calergi, from a wealthy family with roots in Crete, which was then a Venetian possession. Later, through marriage, the Calergi line joined with the Vendramin family. That is why the name you hear today joins both families. In Venice, a palace name often reads like a short family tree.

The building also carries a darker story. Locals tell how, in the seventeenth century, members of the Calergi family were involved in a killing. According to the popular account, the Republic punished them by ordering a newer wing of the palace pulled down. That wing was later rebuilt. Historians argue about the details, so treat this as a tale handed down rather than a settled record. Still, it shows how firmly the Venetian state could reach into the lives of its noble families.

In the nineteenth century, after the Venetian Republic had fallen, the palace belonged to royalty in exile. The Duchess of Berry, a member of the French Bourbon family, bought it in the 1840s and lived here for years. Venice at that time was full of grand houses and families who no longer had the money to keep them. Foreign aristocrats, writers, and artists moved in. The city became a place to retreat, to remember, and sometimes to finish one's life.

That brings us to the most famous resident. The composer Richard Wagner rented rooms here in his final months. He was the German creator of vast operas such as the Ring cycle and Parsifal. He came to Venice for the mild winter and the quiet. On 13 February 1883, he died inside this palace. Venetians later carried his body by gondola along the Grand Canal, the first stage of the journey back to Germany. Imagine that slow black boat on this same water.

Wagner's memory still lives in the building. Rooms connected to his stay are kept as a small museum, run by a Wagner association in Venice. Inside, you find letters, scores, photographs, and objects linked to the composer and his circle. The museum is small and personal. It feels less like a grand exhibition and more like a private visit. For music lovers, standing in the place where the composer spent his last days can be quietly moving.

Today, though, most people walk through these doors for another reason. Since the mid-twentieth century, the palace has housed the Casino of Venice. The casino claims descent from the Ridotto, a public gambling house the Republic opened in the seventeenth century. So the card tables continue a very old Venetian habit. Renaissance rooms, chandeliers, and roulette wheels share the same space. It is an unusual mix... and very Venetian, a city that has always lived between devotion and pleasure.

There are two ways to approach the palace, and both are worth trying. From the water, you see the façade as its builders intended, rising straight from the canal, with a water gate where boats once tied up. From land, you walk through the narrow streets of Cannaregio. This is a more residential district, with laundry lines, small bars, and neighbours greeting each other. The nearest waterbus stop is San Marcuola, just a short walk away.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Watch how the light shifts across the white stone as clouds pass, warming it, then cooling it again. Notice the water marks near the base, where the canal has touched the palace for five centuries. Behind this calm front, families rose and fell, a composer wrote his last letters, and gamblers still try their luck each night. As you walk away, look back once. The arches keep their rhythm, steady above the moving water.
