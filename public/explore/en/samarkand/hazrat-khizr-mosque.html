The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The ground here rises, and you feel it in your calves as you climb the steps from the road below. At the top, the air seems to open. Traffic noise falls away. Behind you, the turquoise domes of central Samarkand sit low on the horizon. In front of you, painted wooden columns hold up a shaded porch, and the light between them is soft and green-gold. This is the Hazrat Khizr Mosque, and it watches over the city from one of its oldest corners.

The hill under your feet matters as much as the building on it. Just beyond this mosque lies Afrasiab, the vast earthen plateau where ancient Samarkand stood for many centuries. Long before Timur, long before the blue domes, the city lived up there, behind mud-brick walls. This hilltop sat near one of its gates. So when you stand here, you stand at a threshold between the old city and the newer one that grew below.

Tradition holds that a mosque stood on this site very early, soon after Arab armies brought Islam to Central Asia in the eighth century. That would make this one of the first places of Muslim prayer in the city. The early building did not survive. It was destroyed during the Mongol invasion of the thirteenth century, when Genghis Khan's armies devastated Samarkand. For a long time afterwards, the hill had no great mosque at all.

The building you see today is much younger. It dates mainly from the nineteenth century, and parts of it, including the entrance portal and the slender minaret, were added later still. It has been restored more than once since then. So this is not a medieval monument in the way Bibi-Khanym or the Registan are. It is something different, a working neighbourhood mosque that carries a very old memory of the place where it stands.

Now, the name. Khizr, often written Khidr, is one of the most mysterious figures in Islamic tradition. He is sometimes called the Green One. Stories describe him as a wise, immortal wanderer who appears to travellers when they are lost and guides them on their way. Many traditions link him with the water of life. Across the Muslim world, people pray in his name before journeys. A traveller's saint for a city that grew rich on the Silk Road... it fits well.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look at the porch, the long shaded gallery that local people call an aivan. A row of carved wooden columns holds up its roof. These columns are not simple posts. Each one has a carved base and a capital, and the wood has darkened with time. On hot afternoons, this porch is where prayer happens in summer, open to the breeze that moves across the hilltop.

Then tilt your head back. The ceiling of the aivan is painted in dense patterns of red, blue, green and gold. Geometric shapes fit inside each other, and flowers repeat in careful rows. This kind of painted wooden ceiling is a long tradition in Uzbek craft. The painters worked in small panels, so if you follow one line of colour with your eye, you can see how the whole pattern is built from small repeating units.

The outside walls tell a quieter story. Compare the plain brickwork with the tiled and decorated surfaces near the entrance. The contrast is deliberate. Builders in this region often kept the main walls simple and saved their colour for the places where a visitor's eye naturally goes: the portal, the doorway, the frame of a window. Notice how the tilework shows a later hand, with brighter, cleaner colours where restorers have worked.

And now the second surprise. Right beside the mosque, across a small garden, stands a newer domed building. This is the mausoleum of Islam Karimov, the first president of independent Uzbekistan, who died in 2016 and was buried here in his home city. Because of this, the hill now draws a steady flow of Uzbek visitors as well as foreign travellers. You may see families walking quietly through, and people pausing to offer a short prayer.

That mix is part of the character of this place. A mosque for daily prayer, a site of national memory, and a viewpoint over the old city, all on one small hill. Try to move slowly here. Speak softly near the prayer areas. If you enter the mosque itself, dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered, and women may want a scarf. Follow the lead of local people, and if you are unsure, simply ask.

Walk to the edge of the terrace and look out. From here you can see the great dome of Bibi-Khanym Mosque rising above the rooftops. Below it lies the Siab Bazaar, where traders sell bread, dried fruit, nuts and spices. Turn a little, and the ridge of Afrasiab stretches away, grassy and brown. Somewhere along that slope runs the long street of tombs called Shah-i-Zinda. The whole story of Samarkand spreads out in one view.

The light changes a lot over the day. In the morning, the sun comes from behind you and lights up the domes across the way. Late in the afternoon, the painted ceiling glows warmer, and the wooden columns cast long shadows across the floor. If you can, come back at a different hour from your first visit. The same porch can feel like two different rooms.

As you stand here, think about the name once more. Khizr is the guide who appears to travellers on the road. For centuries, caravans arrived in Samarkand after weeks of desert and mountain. Many of them passed close to this hill. Whether or not the old stories are true, the idea of a guardian for people on a journey belongs naturally to a city that so many people have reached from far away.

Before you go down the steps, take one more look upward at the painted ceiling, and one more look outward at the domes. Listen for the call to prayer if it is near the hour, or for the sound of the bazaar rising from below. Then go down slowly. At the bottom of the hill, the city is noisy and full again... and you carry a little of this quiet height with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cover shoulders and knees before entering the prayer areas.
Bring a scarf if you are a woman visiting the mosque.
Combine your visit with nearby Siab Bazaar and Bibi-Khanym.
Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the ceiling.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers if possible.
