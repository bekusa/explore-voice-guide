The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. Outside this museum, the earth rises into low, rolling hills of pale, dusty loess, cut by gullies and patched with dry grass. In summer the soil smells of heat and dust. It looks like empty land. It is not. Under these mounds lie the streets, houses and palaces of the oldest Samarkand, a city that stood here for well over a thousand years before it moved south.

This plateau is called Afrasiab. The name comes from a legendary king of the Persian epic tradition, and locals have long attached it to these ruins. Historians usually connect the site with Marakanda, the city that Greek writers described when Alexander the Great arrived in 329 BCE. Long before him, and long after, this was the heart of Sogdiana, a land of farmers, craftsmen and merchants between two great rivers of Central Asia.

The Sogdians matter to everything you will see inside. They spoke an Iranian language, and they became some of the most important traders on the Silk Road, the network of caravan routes between China and the Mediterranean. Sogdian merchants settled in Chinese cities and worked in markets across the steppe. Silk, silver, horses and ideas moved through their hands. Samarkand, on this hill, was one of their great home cities.

The museum itself opened in 1970, when Samarkand celebrated its 2,500th anniversary. Look at the building as you approach. It is a Soviet-era design, with clean lines and a strong, simple shape. It is modest compared with the tiled domes and portals elsewhere in the city. But that suits its job. This building does not try to compete with the past. It works as a protective box for something fragile.

That fragile thing is the reason most people come here. In 1965, workers were building a road across the plateau. Their digging exposed the remains of a rich residence, and on its walls were paintings. Archaeologists uncovered a room decorated on all four sides with large scenes of people, animals and boats. The paintings date to the seventh century. Today they are known as the Ambassadors' Painting.

Walk into the central hall and pause before you look closely. The room is arranged to echo the original chamber, with paintings on each wall around you. You stand roughly where a visitor to the palace might have stood. Notice what is missing: the upper parts of the scenes are gone. When the walls collapsed and were buried, only their lower sections survived. Many figures end at the shoulders or the chest.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. On the robe of one figure on the western wall, there is a long inscription in Sogdian script. Scholars have read it as a statement about envoys who came before a ruler named Varkhuman. Chinese records of the period also mention a Samarkand ruler with this name. So the painting and the written history support each other — a rare match for a building from this distant time.

The western wall shows a line of foreign envoys bringing gifts. Look at their different clothes, hairstyles and objects. Some carry rolls of silk. Some lead animals. Researchers have tried to identify where each group came from, and they do not always agree. Two small figures stand out for many visitors. They wear headdresses with two feathers, and many scholars connect them with the Korean kingdom of that time. That is a very long journey from Korea to this hill.

Now turn to the southern wall. It shows a long procession. A large white elephant, horses, camels and riders move in one direction. Some figures ride, and some walk. Specialists often interpret this as a ceremonial journey connected with the ruler, possibly a visit to an important shrine. The exact meaning is still debated. Look for the elephant — it is one of the largest and clearest shapes in the room.

The northern wall holds a second surprise. Its style and subjects look Chinese. On one side, women sit in a boat on water filled with fish and plants. Many scholars think one of them is a Chinese empress. On the other side, riders hunt animals, and some identify a Chinese emperor among them. A Sogdian ruler chose to show China on his own wall. It tells you how closely these two worlds were linked.

The eastern wall is the most damaged, and its subject is the hardest to read. Some researchers suggest it showed India, but much has been lost. Take a moment with the colours that do survive. The blues, reds and creams have faded, yet they still hold. These are mineral pigments on clay plaster. Imagine them fresh, in lamp light, more than thirteen centuries ago.

The smaller galleries tell the longer story of the hill. You will find pottery, coins, tools, figurines and building fragments from many periods. Look for ossuaries, small clay boxes for human bones. They come from the Zoroastrian tradition, the ancient Iranian religion, where bones were collected after the flesh was gone. Later objects show the arrival of Islam after the Arab conquest of the early eighth century, with new styles of glazed ceramics and decoration.

The end of this city came in 1220, when Mongol armies under Genghis Khan took Samarkand. Life on the plateau never fully returned. The city grew again to the south, where the later monuments of the Timurid era now stand. Afrasiab became farmland, graves and empty hills. That is partly why so much survived underground. Nobody built a new city on top of it.

When you leave, walk a little way out and look across the mounds again. Somewhere beneath that grass are rooms no one has opened yet. On the slopes to the south, the blue domes of the Shah-i-Zinda necropolis rise against the sky, part of the younger city. Listen to the traffic, the wind, the birds. Then think of the envoys on that wall, still walking toward a king who has been gone for thirteen centuries.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the central mural hall first, before the smaller galleries.
Walk around all four walls in order to follow the scenes.
Combine your visit with nearby Shah-i-Zinda on the southern slopes.
Bring water and a hat for walking the open mounds.
Come early to view the murals before tour groups arrive.
