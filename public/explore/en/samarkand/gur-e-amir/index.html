The guide

The story

Start with the colour. Above you, a ribbed dome rises in a deep, saturated blue, the blue of a winter sky just after sunset. Up close, the walls are made of glazed tiles and brick. In the sun they shine like water. In the shade they turn darker, almost green. Underfoot, the stone paving is worn smooth by centuries of shoes and bare feet. The street noise of modern Samarkand fades as you walk toward the entrance. This is Gur-e-Amir, the resting place of Timur.

The name comes from Persian and means, roughly, the Tomb of the King, or the Tomb of the Commander. Many visitors know the man buried here by another name: Tamerlane. That name comes from the Persian phrase Timur-i Lang, which means Timur the Lame. In the late fourteenth century he built an empire that stretched from Central Asia toward Anatolia and India. Samarkand was his capital. He brought craftsmen here from the cities he conquered, and their skills shaped what you see today.

Here is the first surprise. This building was not originally meant for Timur at all. It began as a memorial complex for his favourite grandson and chosen heir, Muhammad Sultan, who died young in 1403. Timur was grief-stricken. Two years later, in 1405, Timur himself died on campaign, as he marched east toward China. He had prepared a simpler tomb for himself in his home town of Shahrisabz. But winter roads were closed, and his body was brought here instead.

So a grandfather lies in a tomb built for his grandson. Over the following decades, this place became the family mausoleum of the Timurid dynasty, the ruling family that descended from Timur. His sons Shah Rukh and Miran Shah are buried here. So is his grandson Ulugh Beg, the ruler and astronomer whose observatory once stood on a hill just outside the city. Timur's spiritual teacher, a sheikh named Mir Sayyid Baraka, also rests here. Family stories cross in this one room.

Look again at the dome before you go inside. Its surface is divided into deep vertical ribs, many of them, like the segments of a melon. This ribbed shape is a signature of Timurid building. It makes the dome look taller and catches the light differently on every side, so that the blue changes from rib to rib. Below it, the tall drum carries bands of large calligraphy in tilework. These are religious inscriptions, written in an angular script made from bricks of different colours.

The tile patterns around the walls repeat in stars and interlocking lines. This is a style of geometric ornament common across the Islamic world. The designs avoid images of people or animals and instead create order through mathematics. Craftsmen planned these patterns carefully, then set thousands of small glazed pieces into place. Some tiles are original. Many others were replaced during restoration work in the twentieth century. If you look closely, you can often see a slight difference in tone between old and new surfaces.

Now step inside. The light drops, and the air feels cooler. The interior of the burial chamber is covered in gold. Gilded patterns climb the walls and spread across the inside of the dome. Much of this gilding comes from Soviet-era restoration in the later twentieth century, which recreated the original decoration. Below the gold, panels of onyx and carved marble line the lower walls. The room is quiet. Voices drop naturally here, even among large tour groups.

In the centre stand the tombstones, surrounded by a low carved marble screen. One stone is very different from the others. It is a long block of very dark green jade, so dark it can look almost black. This is the stone of Timur. It is often described as one of the largest pieces of jade of its time. Look carefully, and you will see that it is broken. A clean crack runs through the stone.

How did it break? Here, history and legend mix. The popular story says that in the eighteenth century the Persian ruler Nadir Shah carried the jade away from Samarkand. According to the tale, the stone cracked during the journey, and the shah then suffered misfortune. He was advised to return it, and he did. Historians accept that the stone was taken and later returned. The link between the theft and the bad luck belongs to legend.

The second surprise is that you are not looking at the actual graves. These stones are cenotaphs, which means memorial markers placed above the real burials. The bodies lie in a crypt directly below, in the same layout as the stones above. The crypt is usually closed to the public. This arrangement was common in grand Islamic tombs. It kept the true graves protected while giving visitors a place to pray and to remember.

In June 1941, a team of Soviet scientists opened the crypt. Among them was the anthropologist Mikhail Gerasimov, who was known for rebuilding faces from skulls. His team confirmed that the skeleton had injuries to the right leg and arm. This matched the old name, Timur the Lame. They also found that the skeleton of Ulugh Beg had been beheaded. That matched written accounts of his murder in 1449, after his own son turned against him.

Then comes the most famous story of all. Locals say an inscription warned that whoever disturbed the tomb would release a terrible war. Within days of the opening, Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union. The timing is real. The curse is folklore, and scholars doubt the inscription ever existed in that form. Another popular tale says the war turned only after Timur was reburied with full Islamic rites in late 1942. Believe what you like. Many local guides tell it with a smile.

This building matters beyond Samarkand. Architectural historians often see it as an ancestor of later Mughal tombs in India, such as Humayun's Tomb in Delhi and the Taj Mahal in Agra. The Mughal emperors descended from Timur, and they remembered their roots. Their builders took ideas from Timurid tombs: the tall dome on a drum, the strong entrance portal, and the tomb set within a garden. As you walk out, notice the portal behind you, and the blue dome rising over the low rooftops. Six centuries later, it still marks the spot where a grandfather and his grandson lie together.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning for softer light on the dome.
Return after dark to see the facade lit up.
Dress modestly; this remains a place of reverence.
Keep voices low inside the burial chamber.
Combine with the Registan, a short walk away.
