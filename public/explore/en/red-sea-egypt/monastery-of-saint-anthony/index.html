The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves over the gravel and the stone, and it carries almost nothing with it: no traffic, no engines, only the dry hush of the Eastern Desert. Above you, a wall of pale limestone cliffs rises toward the sky. Below those cliffs sits a walled village of domes, towers, and palm trees. This is the Monastery of Saint Anthony. Monks have prayed in this valley for more than sixteen centuries.

To understand why anyone would live here, start with one man. Anthony was born in the Egyptian countryside in the third century, into a Christian family of farmers. As a young man, he gave away what he owned and went out into the desert to live a life of prayer. He moved farther and farther from the towns. In the end he settled in this remote place, near a spring, under these cliffs.

Anthony is often called the father of Christian monasticism, the way of life in which people withdraw from ordinary society to devote themselves to God. He did not invent the idea alone. But his example spread it across the Christian world. A biography of him, written by Athanasius, a bishop of Alexandria, was read widely in Greek and Latin. Through that book, his story travelled to Europe. It shaped the monasteries that later rose there.

Here is the first surprise. Anthony himself never built this monastery. He wanted solitude, not walls. Tradition holds that after his death in the fourth century, his followers gathered near the spring at the foot of the mountain. They formed a community around his memory. That community grew into the monastery you see now. It is often described as one of the oldest active Christian monasteries in the world.

Look at the walls around you. They are high, thick, and built for defence. For long periods, this monastery was a target. Raiders from the desert attacked it more than once, and the monks needed protection. For centuries, there was no ordinary gate at ground level. Visitors, supplies, and even monks were lifted over the wall in a basket or on a platform, pulled up by a wooden winch. Some older travellers described this ride with some nervousness.

Inside the walls, the monastery feels like a small town. Narrow lanes run between cells, churches, gardens, a mill, and a bakery. Palm trees and olive trees grow in the gardens. All of this life depends on water. A spring rises at the foot of the cliffs, and it has fed the community since Anthony's time. In a desert this dry, that spring is the reason the monastery exists at all.

The heart of the complex is the Church of Saint Anthony. It is an old building, low and solid, with thick walls and small openings. Step inside and let your eyes adjust to the dim light. Slowly, figures appear on the walls: saints, monks, warrior horsemen, and scenes from the Bible. These wall paintings date mainly to the thirteenth century. They are among the most important surviving examples of medieval Coptic art.

A word on that term. The Copts are the Christians of Egypt, and their church is one of the oldest in the world. Their tradition carries a language, a liturgy, and an art style of its own. In these paintings, you can see that style clearly. The faces are calm, with large eyes. The colours are strong reds, ochres, and blues. The figures look straight at you, as if they are waiting for you to notice them.

Here is the second surprise. For many years, these paintings were almost invisible. Centuries of smoke from candles and lamps, along with dust and later layers of paint, covered them in a dark film. In the 1990s, an international team of conservators worked here alongside the monastery. They cleaned and stabilised the walls with great care. When the grime came off, the original colours returned. Many visitors today see a brightness that earlier travellers never saw.

Now lift your eyes to the cliffs again. High above the monastery, there is a small cave. Tradition says Anthony lived there during the last years of his life, apart even from his own followers. A path with more than a thousand steps climbs the mountainside to reach it. The climb is steep and hot, and it takes real effort. At the top, the cave is narrow and plain. Many pilgrims say the plainness is the point.

From the path to the cave, the view opens across the desert valley toward the Red Sea. On a clear day, you may see the distant line of the water. Below, the monastery looks small and green inside its walls. From up there, it is easy to understand the choice Anthony made. The silence is wide. The horizon is empty. Nothing distracts the eye from the sky and the stone.

This monastery is not a museum. It is a living community. Monks still pray here every day, following the rhythm of the Coptic liturgy. They work in the gardens and receive pilgrims from across Egypt and beyond. On the other side of this same plateau stands the Monastery of Saint Paul, linked by tradition to another early hermit. The two communities have shared these mountains for many centuries.

As you walk through the lanes, notice the small details. A wooden door worn smooth by hands. A dome whitewashed against the sun. The sound of a bell, or a voice chanting in Coptic from a church doorway. Monks may pass you quietly in black robes. If someone offers you a cup of tea or a piece of bread, accept it. Hospitality to guests has been part of desert monastic life since the very beginning.

Before you leave, stand still once more near the walls. Feel the heat in the stone and the cooler air in the shade of the palms. Listen to the wind on the cliffs above. A young man walked into this silence long ago, looking for something he could not find in the towns. The people who followed him are still here. And for a few moments today, you have stood in that same silence too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the cave climb early before the desert heat builds.
Bring plenty of water for the long stair climb.
Wear modest clothing covering shoulders and knees.
Remove shoes when monks ask before entering churches.
Ask before photographing monks or the church interior.
