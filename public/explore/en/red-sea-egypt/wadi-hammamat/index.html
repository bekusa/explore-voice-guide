The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves along the floor of the valley, dry and steady, and carries the faint scrape of sand against rock. The air smells of hot stone and very little else. On either side, dark cliffs rise in folds of grey and green, close enough that sound comes back to you. This is Wadi Hammamat, a long, dry corridor through the mountains of Egypt's Eastern Desert. Travellers have passed through this valley for thousands of years. Many of them stopped here and cut their names into the rock.

A wadi is a desert valley that holds water only after rare rain. Most of the year, it stays dry. This one has a special position on the map. It runs between the Nile Valley, near the ancient town of Coptos, now called Qift, and the Red Sea coast near Quseir. For the ancient Egyptians, it was one of the most practical routes from the river to the sea. Today the modern road between Qift and Quseir follows almost the same line through the mountains.

Look at the cliffs again, and notice their colour. They are not the warm gold of the sandstone along the Nile. They are darker, often a deep greenish grey. This is the stone that brought people here. The Egyptians called it bekhen. Geologists usually describe it as greywacke or siltstone, a fine, hard sedimentary rock. It takes a smooth polish, and it holds sharp detail. That made it valuable for statues, sarcophagi, and small carved objects.

Here is the first surprise. This stone was in use long before the pyramids. In the late prehistoric period, Egyptians carved flat ceremonial palettes from this kind of rock. These palettes were used for grinding pigment, and some carry detailed scenes of animals and rulers. Later, sculptors used the same dark stone for royal statues. So when you see a smooth, dark-grey statue in a museum, there is a real chance that its stone came from cliffs like these.

Now move closer to the rock faces along the old quarry area. Here you can find hundreds of inscriptions and rock drawings. Some are very old drawings of animals and boats, made by people who used this route before written history. Others are formal hieroglyphic texts, carved with care for kings and officials. Many are simple names and titles. Together, they form a long record of human visits, written directly onto the mountain.

The inscriptions are mostly records of expeditions. A pharaoh would send a large group into the desert to bring back stone. That meant quarrymen, scribes, soldiers, and many porters. They needed food, water, tools, and a plan for moving heavy blocks across open desert to the Nile. When the work succeeded, the leaders often carved a text here. The text named the king, the officials, and sometimes the number of workers.

One group of texts is especially well known. They come from the Middle Kingdom, during the short reign of King Mentuhotep the Fourth. The expedition leader was a vizier, the king's chief minister, named Amenemhat. Many historians think this man later became King Amenemhat the First and founded a new dynasty. If that is true, a future king stood in this valley, organizing a quarry team.

The texts from that expedition describe two events that the workers saw as signs from the gods. In one, a pregnant gazelle walked up to a block of stone and gave birth on it. The team took this as a sign and chose that block for the lid of the royal sarcophagus. In another, rain fell, and the workers found a source of water. In this dry valley, that was an extraordinary gift.

The second surprise is a map. A papyrus from the time of the Ramesside kings, now kept in Turin in Italy, is often called one of the oldest surviving geological maps. Many scholars believe it shows this region. It marks roads, hills, and the location of stone and gold. So this valley was not only carved and quarried. It was also planned on paper, more than three thousand years ago.

The route stayed important after the pharaohs. Inscriptions show that officials serving the Persian kings, who ruled Egypt for a period in the first millennium before Christ, came here too. Later, under Greek and then Roman rule, the desert roads were organized with watering stations and small guard posts. These stops let caravans move goods between the Nile and the Red Sea ports. Trade from the Red Sea, and from lands further east, travelled along routes like this one.

Gold was part of the story as well. Not far from the quarry area, the hills contain old gold workings, and the remains of a mining settlement from late antiquity. The site is known today as Bir Umm Fawakhir. Its rough stone houses still stand in low rows. The ancient Egyptians valued this desert for many reasons. Stone, gold, and the route to the sea all came together in the same mountains.

As you walk, take your time with the rock faces. Some inscriptions are deep and clear. Others are shallow and appear only when the sun is low and casts a shadow across the carving. Please do not touch the inscriptions or step on fallen blocks. These marks have survived for thousands of years because the desert is dry and quiet. Hands, oils, and scratches can damage them faster than the wind ever has.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look along the line of the valley toward the east, where the road continues to the sea. Then look back toward the west, where the Nile lies beyond the mountains. People have walked between those two points for a very long time. They carried stone, gold, and goods, and they left their names on these cliffs. Today, your own footsteps join that long line of travellers.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning when low sun sharpens the carvings.
Carry plenty of water; shade is very scarce.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose stones near quarries.
Never touch the inscriptions or climb on carved rock.
Arrange a local driver familiar with the desert road.
