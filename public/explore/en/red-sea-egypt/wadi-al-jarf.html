The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes off the Red Sea in a steady push, and it carries salt and dust together. Underfoot, the ground is pale gravel and broken limestone. It crunches with every step. Behind you, low hills rise in shades of cream and ochre. In front of you, the water runs from turquoise to deep blue. There are no domes here, no columns, no grand gates. And yet this quiet stretch of coast holds one of the most important discoveries in Egyptian archaeology of recent decades.

This is Wadi al-Jarf. A wadi is a dry desert valley, cut by the floods of rare rainstorms. This one opens onto the western shore of the Gulf of Suez, far from any town. Archaeologists now count it among the oldest known harbours in the world. It dates to the reign of Khufu, the Fourth Dynasty king who built the Great Pyramid at Giza. That places it roughly 4,500 years in the past.

The site was not completely unknown before modern excavation. Explorers in the nineteenth century noticed strange openings cut into the hills here. Later visitors passed through too. But nobody understood what they were looking at. The real work began in 2011, when a French and Egyptian mission led by the archaeologist Pierre Tallet started systematic excavation. What they found changed how historians picture the Old Kingdom — the age of the great pyramids.

Look toward the hills behind the shore. Cut into the limestone is a line of long galleries — tunnels dug deep into the rock. There are around thirty of them. They were not tombs, and they were not homes. They were storerooms, and some of them held boats. Dismantled ships, ropes, tools, and jars were packed inside. Then the entrances were sealed with heavy limestone blocks, some marked with painted signs. The crews closed them up and left, planning to return.

Why store boats in a cave? Think about the setting. There was no timber growing on this coast. Every plank had to be carried across the desert from the Nile Valley. Wood was precious. So the expeditions built their ships, used them, took them apart, and locked them away for the next season. It is a very practical solution. It also tells us that sea travel here was organised, repeated, and planned by the state.

Now turn toward the water. Down at the shoreline, the excavators found the remains of a long stone jetty. It was built from heavy blocks and bent into an L shape to shelter boats from the wind and waves. Today it lies low and partly under the sea. Near it, the team recovered dozens of stone anchors. Some were found lying in rows on the seabed, almost exactly where the sailors left them.

So where were these boats going? Look east, across the gulf. On a clear day you can see the mountains of the Sinai Peninsula on the far side. Sinai held copper and turquoise, two materials the Egyptian court wanted badly. Copper made tools — including the chisels used to cut stone for the pyramids. Archaeologists have identified a matching site on the Sinai coast. Wadi al-Jarf was the departure point. The crossing was short, but the logistics behind it were enormous.

Here comes the first surprise. In 2013, the team found papyrus fragments wedged between the blocks at one of the gallery entrances. These are the oldest inscribed papyri ever discovered. They are written in hieratic, a fast, cursive form of the hieroglyphic script used for everyday records. For a long time, papyri from this early period were thought to be almost entirely lost. Here, in the dry air of the desert, some survived.

The most famous group is known as the Diary of Merer. Merer was an official, an inspector who led a team of around forty men. His logbook records their work, day by day, over many months. And the work was remarkable. His crew moved blocks of fine white limestone by boat, from the quarries at Tura, near modern Cairo, to Giza. That limestone was used for the outer casing of the Great Pyramid. In other words, this is a first-hand record from a man who helped build it.

That is the second surprise. The written account of the Great Pyramid's construction was found here, on a lonely beach far from Giza, not near the pyramid itself. Why? Merer's team seems to have been a mobile crew. They worked on the Nile when the river was high, then joined the expeditions to the Red Sea. When they left this harbour, their papers stayed behind. Someone used them, perhaps, as packing near the sealed galleries.

The diary also shows the human side of the project. It notes where the crew slept, which canals they used, and when they stopped for supplies. The men were not slaves in chains. They were organised, fed, and named in official records. Jars found here carry marks linked to work gangs, and some gangs had names that honoured the king. These details are small, but together they show a well-run system of labour and supply.

Today, Wadi al-Jarf remains an active archaeological area, not a polished visitor site. There is no museum on the spot, and very little has been reconstructed. Much of what was found has been studied and moved away for protection. What remains is the setting itself: the sealed rock, the line of the jetty, the open gulf. Treat it gently. Stay on the paths, and do not climb into the galleries or move any stone.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Face the sea and let the wind hit your face. Somewhere out on that water, more than four thousand years ago, small wooden boats pushed off toward the Sinai mountains. On the shore behind them, men like Merer counted jars and sealed the caves. As you walk back across the gravel, listen to it crunch. You are walking on the same ground they did.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrange site access in advance; it is an active excavation area.
Bring plenty of water; the coast has no shade or services.
Visit early morning before the desert heat and glare build.
Wear sturdy shoes for loose gravel and broken limestone.
Pair the visit with Merer's papyri in Cairo museum collections.
