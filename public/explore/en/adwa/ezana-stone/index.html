The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across dry farmland, and somewhere a goat bell clinks. Inside a small shelter, out of the hard Tigray sun, stands a slab of grey stone. Its surface is covered in tight rows of carved letters. Some are worn soft. Others are still sharp enough to catch the light. This is the Ezana Stone. It is one of the most important written records left by an ancient African kingdom.

First, a note on where you are. The stone does not stand in Adwa itself. It stands on the edge of Aksum, the old royal city a short drive west of Adwa. Many travellers visit both on the same trip. The two towns share a landscape of rocky hills and high, thin air. They also share a long history. Adwa sits in the shadow of jagged volcanic peaks. Aksum lies in a gentler valley, where a great kingdom once had its capital.

That kingdom was Aksum. Between roughly the first and seventh centuries of the common era, it was one of the great trading powers of the ancient world. Its merchants dealt in ivory, gold and incense. Goods moved through the Red Sea port of Adulis toward Egypt, Arabia and India. Aksum minted its own coins in gold, silver and bronze. Few kingdoms of that time did this. Writers in the Mediterranean world counted it among the great powers of its age.

The king named on this stone is Ezana. He ruled Aksum in the fourth century. He stands among the most important figures in Ethiopian history. His inscriptions describe military campaigns against peoples to the west and north. Some of these campaigns reached the region of the Nile in present-day Sudan. Kings across the ancient world liked to carve their victories in stone. Ezana did the same, and this slab carries his voice across about 1,700 years.

Now the first surprise. Look closely, and you may see that the stone is not written in one language. It is written in three. One is Ge'ez, the classical language of Ethiopia. A second is written in the old South Arabian script, from across the Red Sea. The third is Greek, the common language of trade in the eastern Mediterranean. Because of this, people often compare the Ezana Stone to the Rosetta Stone in Egypt.

Why three languages? The answer tells us what kind of place Aksum was. Ge'ez spoke to the king's own people. The South Arabian script reflects old ties with southern Arabia, close family links in culture and writing. Greek spoke to the merchants and envoys who sailed into Adulis. A single stone could address the home audience, the neighbours and the wider world at once. In a way, it was a public notice and a diplomatic message in one.

The second surprise is about faith. Ezana is remembered as the king who brought Christianity to Aksum. Tradition credits a Syrian Christian named Frumentius with teaching him. Ethiopians still honour Frumentius as Abba Salama, which means Father of Peace. Some of Ezana's earlier inscriptions call on older gods, including a war god named Mahrem. His later texts speak of a single God of heaven. Scholars read that change in wording as a record of conversion.

The king's coins tell the same story. Earlier Aksumite coins show a crescent and a disc, symbols of the older religion. During Ezana's reign, the cross appears on the coins instead. This makes Aksum one of the first states anywhere to place the cross on its money. Here in the north of Ethiopia, the Christian kingdom has deep roots. Its later churches, monasteries and festivals all trace back to this period.

Locals say that farmers found this stone while working the soil. That is a fitting origin for an object that now stands among fields. It was not taken away to a distant museum. It stays close to where it was found, under a simple roof that protects it from rain and sun. Standing here, you see it as many people once did. It is a message set beside a road, in a working landscape.

Take a moment with the stone itself. The slab is tall enough to look a person in the eye. The letters run in neat horizontal lines. Ge'ez letters often look like small houses with legs and hooks. Greek letters look more angular and familiar to many visitors. Run your eyes across the surface, but please do not touch it. Oils from the skin, even from many gentle fingers, slowly damage carved stone.

Think about the craft behind it. Someone planned the text and measured the lines. Then a skilled carver cut each letter with metal tools, one stroke at a time. A mistake could not be erased. In the low morning or late afternoon light, shadows fall into the grooves, and the letters stand out clearly. At midday, under a flat overhead sun, the surface can look almost blank.

The stone also connects you to the rest of Aksum. Not far away stand the great carved stelae, tall granite monuments that mark royal tombs. The town also holds old churches, ruined palaces and royal burial chambers. Together they form a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Ezana Stone gives these silent monuments a voice. The stelae show the power of the kingdom. The inscription tells you, in the king's own words, what he wanted remembered.

And Adwa carries its own place in the story of Ethiopian independence. In 1896, Ethiopian forces defeated an Italian army near Adwa. That victory kept Ethiopia free during the European scramble for Africa. Many Ethiopians see a long line running through this region. It runs from the ancient kings of Aksum to the defenders of Adwa. For local people, these places belong to a single living history.

Before you leave, step back from the shelter. Look past the stone to the fields and hills around it. The same sky covered King Ezana's messengers, his soldiers and his traders. Listen again for the wind and the goat bells. As you walk away, notice the dry earth under your feet. Somewhere beneath it, other stories from Aksum may still be waiting.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon when shadows sharpen letters.
Combine with Aksum's stelae fields on the same day.
Arrange transport from Adwa before setting out to Aksum.
Bring sun protection; the surrounding fields offer little shade.
Hire a local guide to help read the scripts.
