The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Gravel shifts underfoot, swallows call above the walls, and somewhere below the traffic of Trento hums softly. In front of you, pale stone and warm plaster rise in layers. Some walls look like a fortress, some like a palace, and some like something in between. Behind it all, the green slopes of the Alps close in on the valley. This is Buonconsiglio Castle, and it is really several buildings from several centuries, joined together over time.

For roughly five hundred years, from the thirteenth century until the start of the nineteenth, this hill was home to the prince-bishops of Trento. That title needs a short explanation. A prince-bishop was a churchman who also ruled as a secular lord. He led the faithful, and he also governed territory, collected taxes, and sat in the politics of the Holy Roman Empire. Trento sat on the main road between the German lands and Italy, so whoever held this castle watched one of the busiest crossings in Europe.

The oldest part is called the Castelvecchio, which simply means the old castle. Look for the tall round tower. It is the clearest trace of the medieval stronghold, built to defend first and to impress second. Over the centuries the bishops softened this hard shell. One fifteenth-century bishop added a graceful loggia in the Venetian Gothic style, with pointed arches that would look at home on a canal in Venice. Here, in the middle of an Alpine city, it is a small surprise.

Now turn your attention to the long Renaissance wing, the Magno Palazzo, or Great Palace. It was the project of Bernardo Cles, a powerful prince-bishop of the early sixteenth century. Cles was a diplomat and a cardinal, and he moved in the highest circles of the Habsburg court. He wanted a residence that showed Trento was no provincial outpost. So he brought in builders, sculptors, and painters, and he turned the old fortress into a true Renaissance palace.

The painters he hired were among the best in northern Italy. Girolamo Romanino from Brescia worked here, and so did the brothers Dosso and Battista Dossi from the court of Ferrara. Their frescoes cover ceilings and walls with gods, heroes, and allegories. The vault of the loggia that carries Romanino's name is especially lively. Figures lean out over painted balustrades and seem to look down at you. Take your time here. Tip your head back and let your eyes travel slowly across the ceiling.

There is a reason Cles worked so hard on this palace. Trento was being prepared for a great event. In the middle of the sixteenth century, the city hosted the Council of Trent, the long meeting at which the Catholic Church answered the Protestant Reformation. The sessions themselves took place mostly in the cathedral and in the church of Santa Maria Maggiore, down in the town. But the city needed to receive cardinals, envoys, and princes in style. The castle helped give Trento that confidence.

The third major section came later, in the seventeenth century. It is known as the Giunta Albertiana, named after a bishop of the Alberti family who extended the complex. It links the older medieval core with the Renaissance palace. Walking between these wings, you can feel the change in mood. Thick walls and narrow openings give way to wide windows and painted halls. The building is a timeline you can walk through, one doorway at a time.

Now, the treasure that many visitors miss. Along the walls stands the Torre Aquila, the Eagle Tower. Inside is a room painted around the year 1400 with the Cycle of the Months. It was commissioned by Bishop George of Liechtenstein. Month by month, the frescoes show nobles and peasants through the year: ploughing, harvesting, hunting, courting, making wine. It is one of the finest pictures of daily life from the late Middle Ages anywhere in Europe.

Look closely at January, if you get the chance. Elegant young aristocrats are having a snowball fight outside a castle. It is often described as one of the oldest known paintings of a snowball fight. Six centuries ago, people in this valley were doing exactly what children do in the snow today. Other panels show women gathering hay and men treading grapes. The colours are still bright, with deep blues, soft greens, and the red of fine clothes.

The castle also carries a much darker memory. After the bishops lost their power at the start of the nineteenth century, Trento came under Austrian rule, and the complex served for a long time as military barracks. During the First World War, Austro-Hungarian authorities held trials here. In 1916, three men from this region who had fought for Italy, Cesare Battisti, Fabio Filzi, and Damiano Chiesa, were executed in the castle's moat. Today Italians remember them as martyrs of the war.

You can visit the place in the moat where they died. It is quiet, grassy, and quite plain, which makes it more powerful. Battisti in particular is a national figure. He was a geographer, a journalist, and a member of the Austrian parliament who argued for Trento to join Italy. Standing in the moat, you are at the base of these great walls, looking up at the same stone the condemned men saw. Many visitors stay here longer than they expected.

Today the whole complex is a museum. It holds collections of paintings, sculpture, ceramics, coins, and archaeological finds from the Trentino region. The rooms change in scale and style as you move through them. Some are bare stone, some are covered with frescoes, and some have carved wooden ceilings. In warmer months the gardens and courtyards open up the visit. From the upper terraces you can look out over the rooftops of Trento toward the mountains.

Before you leave, pause in one of the courtyards and look around once more. Notice how the colours change from wall to wall, from grey medieval stone to warm Renaissance plaster. Listen for the bells of the town below. Bishops, painters, soldiers, and prisoners all crossed this same ground. As you walk back toward the old town, turn around once at the bottom of the hill. The castle will look different again — a single silhouette, holding five centuries together.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the Torre Aquila visit in advance when you arrive.
Bring binoculars or a zoom lens for high ceiling frescoes.
Visit the moat memorial at a slow, quiet pace.
Save energy for the upper terraces and their mountain views.
Allow at least two hours to see all three wings.
