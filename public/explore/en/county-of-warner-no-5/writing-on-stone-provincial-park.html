The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through dry prairie grass, and somewhere below you the Milk River runs low and brown over its gravel bed. The sandstone under your boots is warm and gritty, almost soft. Around you, pale rock rises in strange columns and caps, like a crowd of figures frozen mid-gesture. This is Writing-on-Stone Provincial Park, in the far south of Alberta, close to the border with Montana. For thousands of years, people have come to this valley to look at the stone, and to leave marks on it.

The Blackfoot people call this place Áísínai'pi. The name is often translated as "it is pictured" or "it is written." That idea sits at the centre of everything here. The Blackfoot Confederacy, a union of related Plains nations, holds this valley as a sacred landscape. It is not simply a park with old carvings. For many Blackfoot people today, it is a living place where the spirit world comes close to the human one. As you walk, it helps to remember that you are a guest on very old ground.

Those pale columns around you are called hoodoos. They are made of sandstone laid down long ago, when an ancient sea and its shorelines covered much of this region, in the age of the dinosaurs. Over a very long time, wind, rain, frost, and the river carved the softer rock away. Harder layers stayed on top, forming caps. The result is a maze of mushrooms, pillars, and hollows. Look closely at their surfaces. You can often see thin lines of rusty iron staining and tiny pockets where the rock has crumbled.

Now the surprise that many visitors do not expect. Writing-on-Stone holds the largest concentration of rock art on the North American Great Plains. There are thousands of images here, spread across the cliffs and hoodoos of the valley. Some are petroglyphs — figures carved or scratched into the stone. Others are pictographs — images painted onto the rock, usually with red ochre, a natural iron-rich pigment. In 2019, UNESCO inscribed the park as a World Heritage Site, recognising both the rock art and its deep importance to the Blackfoot.

What do the images show? People, often with shields. Animals such as bison, horses, and deer. Footprints, weapons, and shapes whose meaning is not easy for outsiders to read. Some of the oldest images appear to come from long before European contact. Others clearly show horses and guns, which tells you they were made after those arrived on the Plains. So the valley is not one moment in history. It is a long record, added to over many generations, like pages in a book that never closed.

One famous panel is often called the Battle Scene. It shows many figures, shields, and weapons in what appears to be a large conflict. Researchers have studied it closely, and different interpretations exist. It is a good example of how this rock art works. It can be a story, a record, a vision, or all three at once. Blackfoot knowledge keepers offer their own understanding, and that understanding is not always the same as an academic reading. Both deserve careful listening.

Here is something many first-time visitors miss. A large part of the rock art sits inside a protected archaeological preserve. You cannot simply wander in. Access is mainly through guided tours led by park staff. This rule exists for good reasons. Sandstone is fragile, and touching, chalking, or scratching the surface can destroy images that survived for centuries. Sadly, some panels already carry modern graffiti from earlier visitors. When you see it, you understand at once why the protections matter.

The Blackfoot also say that the spirits themselves add to the rock. According to traditional teaching, some images appear or change over time, made not by human hands but by spiritual beings. For this reason, people came here to seek visions and guidance. You do not need to share that belief to feel the effect of the place. Stand still for a minute in a quiet hollow between the hoodoos. Notice how the wind drops, how sound changes, how the rock seems to close around you.

Look across the valley to the south. On a clear day you can see three dark, rounded peaks rising from the flat plain. These are the Sweetgrass Hills, across the border in Montana. They are an important part of this sacred landscape too. In Blackfoot tradition, the hills and the valley are connected, and the view between them matters. Seeing them from here, you start to understand that the rock art was never meant to be read in isolation. It belongs to the whole horizon.

The valley also holds a later chapter. In the late nineteenth century, the North-West Mounted Police — the force that later became the Royal Canadian Mounted Police — set up an outpost here. Its job was to watch the border, and especially to stop whisky traders and smugglers moving between the United States and Canada. Today a reconstructed version of the post stands in the park. Its simple wooden buildings give a sense of how isolated and exposed life must have felt for the officers stationed here.

Nature here is rich and a little wild. The Milk River valley is home to prairie rattlesnakes, so watch where you step and where you put your hands. You may see deer moving along the river, hawks circling above the coulees, and swallows darting near the cliffs. In early summer, small cactus flowers can appear on the dry slopes. The river itself draws people in hot weather. Many visitors float or paddle gently downstream, watching the hoodoos drift past from the water.

Light changes everything in this valley. At midday, the sandstone looks flat and pale, almost white. In the early morning and late evening, the low sun turns the rock gold, then orange, and long shadows stretch between the hoodoos. That is when the carved lines show most clearly, because the angled light catches every groove. If you are staying at the campground, it is worth walking out at dusk. On clear nights, with little town light nearby, the stars can be very bright.

Before you leave, pause where you are standing now. Feel the grit of the sandstone, the dry wind, the warmth coming off the rock. Look at the nearest hoodoo and imagine the many hands that have passed this way — hunters, travellers, vision seekers, police officers, families on holiday. Each left something, even if only footprints in the dust. As you walk back, keep your eyes on the cliffs. A faint red mark or a thin carved line may still be waiting for someone patient enough to notice it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a guided tour to enter the archaeological preserve.
Never touch the rock art, even lightly.
Watch your step for prairie rattlesnakes on trails.
Bring water, a hat, and sun protection in summer.
Walk the Hoodoo Trail in early morning or evening.
