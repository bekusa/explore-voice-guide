The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the wall. It feels cool, dry, and slightly gritty, like packed sand that has set into stone. Up close the surface shows faint horizontal bands, one layer pressed on top of the next. Overhead, dark clay roof tiles curve out over the edge of the wall. Somewhere inside, a rooster calls, a television murmurs, and a wooden door knocks shut. You are standing outside a home that holds a whole village.

This is a tulou. The name means, quite simply, earthen building. Across the mountains of southwestern Fujian, families built these giant fortified homes over many centuries. Some are round, some are square, and a few take stranger shapes. In 2008, UNESCO added forty-six of them to the World Heritage List. Several of the best-known groups stand here in the hills of Zhangzhou, especially in Nanjing and Hua'an counties.

Who built them? Mostly two communities. The first are the Hakka. The name means guest people, and they are a Han Chinese group whose ancestors migrated south from the north over many generations. The second are the Minnan, the southern Fujian people whose language is also called Hokkien. Both groups faced the same problem in these remote valleys. Bandits, clan feuds, and wild animals were real dangers. A family was safer living together behind one thick wall.

So the tulou is first a fortress. Look at the outside again. The lower walls usually have no windows at all. The small openings start higher up, where a defender could watch the valley. There is often only one main gate, framed in stone and closed with heavy timber. In times of trouble, the whole clan could shut that gate. With a well inside and grain in storage, they could wait out a siege.

Now step through the gate, and the mood changes completely. The fortress outside becomes a shared home inside. In a round tulou, rings of wooden balconies circle an open courtyard. Each family traditionally had a vertical slice of the building. The kitchen sat on the ground floor. The second floor held grain and tools. The upper floors were for sleeping. Neighbours lived wall to wall, and the whole clan shared the same open sky.

In the centre of many courtyards you will find the ancestral hall. This is the heart of the building. Here the clan honoured its ancestors, held weddings and funerals, and gathered for festivals. The design places the ancestors in the middle, and the living families in rings around them. It is a floor plan that carries a social idea — everyone faces the same centre.

How did people build walls this strong from earth? The method is called rammed earth. Builders set up wooden frames and filled them with damp soil, often mixed with sand and lime. Then they pounded it down, layer after layer, until it became very hard. The walls are thickest at the base and thinner near the top. Wooden beams and pillars hold up the floors and the roof inside.

Locals like to tell you about secret ingredients. According to popular tradition, builders sometimes added sticky rice, brown sugar, or egg whites to the earth mix to make it stronger. It is a good story, and you will hear it often. Treat it as local lore rather than a proven recipe for every building. What is certain is the result. Many of these walls have stood through heavy rain, summer heat, and earthquakes in the region.

Here is a story many visitors enjoy. Legend has it that during the Cold War, foreign analysts looking at aerial photographs could not explain these huge circles in the mountains. One version says they were mistaken for missile silos. The tale is repeated widely, but it is hard to confirm. Still, it is easy to understand. From above, a cluster of round tulou does look like something engineered, not something lived in.

If you visit the Tianluokeng cluster in Nanjing County, you will see why. Four round buildings sit around one square building on the hillside. Local people have a cheerful nickname for the group. They call it four dishes and one soup, like a family meal laid out on a table. Climb to the viewing point above the village, and the shapes really do look like bowls set down on the green slope.

Not every tulou is round, and not every one is huge. Some are square, with corner rooms and long straight galleries. Some are small, built for a single extended family. Others grew very large, with hundreds of rooms around the courtyard. Each one shows the wealth, the needs, and the taste of the clan that built it. Carved wooden screens, painted beams, and stone inscriptions over the gates often carry the family's name and its hopes.

Today many tulou are still homes. Some families have moved to newer houses in town, and some rooms stand empty. But in many buildings, older residents still cook on the ground floor and dry vegetables in the courtyard. Small shops sell tea, dried bamboo shoots, and local snacks near the gates. Please remember that you are walking through someone's front yard. A quiet greeting goes a long way here.

Before you leave, stand in the courtyard for one more moment. Look up at the ring of balconies and the circle of sky above them. Listen to the sounds bouncing softly off the curved walls — a voice, a broom, a cup set down on wood. Then walk back out through the single gate. Turn around once, and notice how quickly the open home becomes a closed wall again.

Key facts

Forty-six Fujian tulou joined UNESCO's World Heritage List in 2008.
Tulou means earthen building in Chinese.
Built mainly by Hakka and Minnan clan communities.
Walls are made of rammed earth, thickest at the base.
Tianluokeng's layout is nicknamed four dishes and one soup.
Look closer

What to look for

Horizontal bands in the walls from each rammed layer.
Windowless lower walls with small openings high above.
The ancestral hall standing in the central courtyard.
Stone-framed main gate with heavy timber doors.
Carved inscriptions above the gate naming the clan.
Before you go

Practical tips

Climb to the Tianluokeng viewpoint for the overhead view.
Visit early morning, before tour buses fill the courtyards.
Ask residents before photographing them or their rooms.
Wear sturdy shoes for steep wooden stairs and uneven ground.
Allow a full day to see more than one cluster.
