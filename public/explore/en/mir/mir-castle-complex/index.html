The guide

The story

Listen first. On a still day the only sound here is the water. A wide pond lies beside the castle, and its surface holds a second, upside-down version of the towers. Wind breaks the reflection into red and white ripples, and then it settles again. Under your feet the gravel crunches softly. Ahead of you, five towers rise out of the flat Belarusian countryside. You are standing at Mir Castle, in a small town in the Grodno region of western Belarus.

Look at the walls before you think about dates. The castle is built mostly from deep red brick, laid in patterns, and the brick is set off with panels of white plaster. In some places pale stone blocks sit at the base of the walls. Up close, the surface is not flat at all. The brick steps in and out, forming niches and frames that catch shadow. The whole building looks dressed up — and that was the point. It was meant to impress as much as to defend.

The story begins in the early sixteenth century. At that time this land belonged to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, a large state that covered much of today's Belarus, Lithuania and parts of Ukraine. A nobleman named Yuri Ilyinich began building a fortress here. His family wanted a stronghold that matched their rising status. The first castle was a square of thick walls with towers at the corners and a gate tower in the middle of one side. That plan is still easy to read today.

The early work was in the Gothic style. Gothic is the medieval European style of pointed arches and vertical lines, the style of many old cathedrals. You can see it here in the tall, narrow proportions of the towers and in the brick decoration. But Mir did not stay purely Gothic for long. Here is the first surprise: this building is really several styles stacked on top of each other, because each new owner added a layer.

In the 1560s the castle passed to the Radziwiłł family. The Radziwiłłs were one of the most powerful noble families in this part of Europe, with huge estates and their own private armies. Under them, Mir changed. They added Renaissance features — the style that came from Italy, with calm proportions, larger windows and decorated palace rooms. A palace wing grew inside the walls. The fortress slowly became a residence. Walk into the courtyard and you can feel that shift from defence to comfort.

The Radziwiłłs also changed the landscape around the castle. Earthen ramparts and a moat were built outside the walls for extra protection. Later, an ornamental garden was laid out nearby, in the Italian manner, with ordered paths and planting. When you walk the grounds, notice how the ground rises and falls. Some of those slopes are not natural. They are the remains of old defensive earthworks, now covered in grass.

Peace did not last. Over the following centuries the castle was damaged in wars that swept across this region. During Napoleon's campaign against Russia in 1812, fighting took place around Mir, and the castle suffered. For decades afterwards it stood partly ruined. Roofs were missing. Rooms were open to the rain and snow. Many visitors who see the restored walls today do not realise how close this building came to disappearing completely.

In 1891 the estate was bought by Prince Nikolai Svyatopolk-Mirsky. His family began to repair parts of the castle and the grounds. The pond that you heard at the start of this walk dates from this later period of the estate. Nearby, the family built a chapel that also served as their burial vault. Look for it among the trees in the park. It is a quieter building, and it gives a sense of how the owners saw this place as home, not only as property.

The twentieth century brought the darkest chapter. During the Second World War, when German forces occupied the area, the castle was used as a ghetto for the Jewish people of Mir. The town had a large Jewish community before the war and was known for its yeshiva, a school of religious learning. Most of the Jews of Mir were murdered in 1942. A memorial in the area marks this history. It is worth a moment of silence as you move through the grounds.

After the war, people lived in parts of the castle for a time, and the building was again neglected. Serious restoration began later in the twentieth century and continued for many years. Craftsmen worked on the brick, the plaster and the roofs. Here is the second surprise: in 2000, the Mir Castle Complex was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List. UNESCO is the United Nations body that recognises places of outstanding value to all humanity. Mir was one of the first sites in Belarus to receive this status.

Inside, the castle now works as a museum. The rooms take you through different periods of its life. You can see restored halls, displays about the owners, and spaces that show how people lived and worked here. Stairways inside the towers are narrow and steep, so take your time. Climb where you can. From the upper levels, the windows frame the pond, the park and the red roofs of the small town beyond.

Step back outside and take in the whole complex once more. Notice how each tower is slightly different. Some have more decoration, some are plainer. The roofs, the window shapes and the plaster patterns change from one side to another. This is the honest record of a building that was started, damaged, extended and rebuilt over roughly five hundred years. Nothing here was designed in a single moment.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the pond again. Turn around and look back at the castle across the water. The red brick looks warmer from here, especially when the sun is low. Listen for the water against the bank, the birds in the park, perhaps voices from the courtyard. Families, soldiers, princes and prisoners all passed through these walls. Now it is your turn to walk away... and the towers will stay, reflected in the water, as they have for centuries.
