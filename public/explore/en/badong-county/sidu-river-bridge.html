The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere far below you, water moves over stone. The sound is faint, almost lost in the wind that pushes through this gorge. Above you, or perhaps in front of you, a thin pale line runs from one mountain to the other. That line is a road. It carries trucks, buses and cars across empty air, high above a river most of its travellers will never see.

This is the Sidu River Bridge, in Badong County, in the far west of Hubei province in central China. It is a suspension bridge, the kind that hangs its deck from two great cables slung between towers. It opened in 2009 as part of the expressway that links Shanghai on the coast with Chongqing deep in the interior. For a few years, many engineers counted it as the highest bridge in the world.

The height is the first thing that stops people. The deck sits roughly five hundred metres above the floor of the valley. Try to picture that distance. Stack a modern skyscraper on top of another one, and you are still not quite there. A stone dropped from the railing would fall for about ten seconds before it reached the water. Of course, nobody should try.

The second number is the span. The main span between the two towers measures around nine hundred metres. That means the road crosses almost a full kilometre with no support from below. There are no piers standing in the river. The gorge here is simply too deep and too steep for that. Everything hangs from the cables, and the cables hang from the towers on each side.

Now look at the landscape that forced this design. These are the mountains of western Hubei, a region of limestone ridges, narrow valleys and sudden drops. Rivers here have cut down through the rock for a very long time. The result is a series of deep, tight canyons. For centuries, travel through this country meant mule tracks, river boats and long detours around each ridge.

The area belongs to the Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture. The Tujia and the Miao are two of China's ethnic minorities, with their own languages, songs and building traditions. In the villages around the gorge, you may still see older houses made of timber, some raised on posts against the slope. They sit in sharp contrast to the steel and concrete overhead. Two very different ideas of how to live with these mountains share the same view.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Building a suspension bridge starts with a small problem. How do you get the very first line across the gap? Usually a boat or a helicopter carries it. Here, according to widely reported engineering accounts, the builders used a rocket. They fired it from one side of the gorge to the other, trailing a thin pilot line. Thicker cables were then pulled across, step by step, until the main cables could be spun.

That image is worth holding for a moment. A small rocket arcs over the valley, a thread behind it. From that thread grew the cables that now carry heavy trucks every day. It is a reminder that great structures often begin with something light and almost fragile. The strength comes later, layer by layer, strand by strand.

Notice how the road behaves at each end of the bridge. On this stretch of expressway, the mountains leave very little flat ground. Roads here often leave a bridge and enter a tunnel almost at once, then come out onto another bridge. For drivers, the crossing can feel strangely brief. One moment there is rock on every side, then suddenly open sky, then rock again. Many travellers cross without fully understanding how high they have been.

That is the quiet surprise of this place. The best way to understand the Sidu River Bridge is not to drive across it, but to see it from outside. From the valley floor, or from a viewpoint on the hillside, the deck looks impossibly thin. The towers seem small against the cliffs. Only then does the scale become clear. The bridge belongs to the mountains as much as to the road.

Take a moment to study the light. Mist often gathers in these gorges, especially in the cooler months and in the early morning. On those days, the deck can appear to float, with the river hidden completely below. When the air is clear, sunlight picks out the vertical hangers, the thin steel rods that drop from the main cables to the deck. They form a long, regular pattern, like the strings of an instrument.

This bridge was not built as a monument. It was built to move people and goods through one of the hardest landscapes in China. Before the expressway, a journey through this region could take many hours longer. The road has changed how villages here connect with the wider world. Markets, schools and hospitals in the larger towns are now much closer in time, if not in distance.

So as you stand here, let your eyes travel slowly. Start with the river, if you can see it, a narrow ribbon of green or grey far below. Rise up the rock walls, past the scrub and the small terraced fields. Find the towers, then follow the cables down to the deck. Somewhere up there, a truck is crossing right now, its driver perhaps thinking about lunch. And down here, the water keeps moving over the stones, just as it did long before the rocket flew.

Before you go

Practical tips

View the bridge from the valley or hillside, not the expressway.
Never stop a vehicle on the bridge deck to take photos.
Come early on cool mornings to see mist in the gorge.
Bring a zoom lens to capture the towers and hangers.
Wear sturdy shoes for steep, uneven paths around the gorge.
