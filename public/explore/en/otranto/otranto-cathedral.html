The guide

The story

Push open the door and the first thing you notice is the floor. It is not plain stone. It is a carpet of tiny tiles, cool underfoot and slightly uneven, worn smooth by centuries of shoes. The light inside is soft and amber. Your eyes need a moment to adjust after the white glare of the Salento sun. Look down before you look up. The real story of this building runs beneath your feet.

This is the Cathedral of Santa Maria Annunziata, the mother church of Otranto. It stands on the high ground of the old town, a short walk from the harbour and the castle. The building was consecrated in 1088. That was a time when Norman rulers held southern Italy. These knights came from northern France and took the region from Byzantine control. Their churches mixed the styles of every culture they conquered.

Now, back to the floor. The great mosaic was laid between 1163 and 1165. A monk named Pantaleone designed it, and an archbishop called Jonathan commissioned it. Both names appear in inscriptions set into the tiles themselves. Think about that for a moment. You are reading a signature that is more than eight hundred years old. It runs the full length of the nave, from the entrance to the altar.

The spine of the whole design is a single enormous tree. Its trunk rises from the entrance toward the altar, and its branches spread out across the floor. Scholars call it the Tree of Life. Along its branches, Pantaleone placed hundreds of figures. There are scenes from the Bible, creatures from legend, kings, monsters and ordinary people at work. It reads less like a sermon and more like an encyclopedia of the medieval mind.

Here is the first surprise. Among the biblical scenes, look for a crowned figure riding a strange beast, perhaps a goat. The inscription beside him names him as King Arthur. Yes, the Arthur of Camelot, here on a floor in the far south of Italy. This is one of the earliest known images of him anywhere. It shows how far the stories of Arthur had travelled with the Normans, all the way to the edge of the Adriatic.

Arthur is not alone. Nearby you can find Alexander the Great, the ancient Macedonian king. In one famous tale, he flies up to heaven carried by griffins. You can also find Noah building his ark, and Adam and Eve beside the forbidden tree. Look as well for the Tower of Babel, with workers on ladders. Elsewhere, roundels show the months of the year with the labours of each season. One man prunes, another harvests, another sits by the fire.

Take your time with the animals. There are elephants holding up the base of the tree, which Pantaleone probably never saw in real life. There are mermaids, dragons and creatures that are half one thing and half another. Some look comic to modern eyes. Visitors often laugh out loud. That reaction is fine. The medieval artist mixed the serious and the playful freely, and the floor still feels alive because of it.

Now lift your eyes. The nave is lined with columns of different stone and different shapes. Many were taken from older Roman or Greek buildings and reused here. This was a common practice, and it explains why no two seem to match. Above, the ceiling is a later addition, carved and gilded in a much more decorative style. The contrast is sharp. Below you, the twelfth century. Above you, a far later taste for gold.

To the right of the main altar is a chapel that changes the mood of the visit. This is the Chapel of the Martyrs. Behind glass, in tall wooden cases, you will see rows of human skulls and bones. They belong to the men of Otranto who died in 1480. Many visitors stop at the threshold. Some step back. It is a quiet, heavy place, and it deserves a slow approach.

The story behind it is the darkest chapter in the town's past. In 1480, an Ottoman fleet attacked Otranto and took the city after a siege. Historical accounts record that about 800 local men refused to give up their Christian faith. They were executed on a hill just outside the town. Their remains were later gathered and brought here. In 2013, Pope Francis declared them saints. Local tradition says the stone they died on is kept in this chapel.

The second surprise lies below. Steps lead down to the crypt, which is older in feeling than the church above. Here the ceiling is low, and a forest of slender columns holds up small vaults. Again, the columns do not match. Their capitals, the carved tops, show leaves, animals and simple geometric patterns. The air is cooler and smells faintly of damp stone. Sound changes too. Footsteps become soft, and voices drop without anyone asking.

Back outside, step away from the entrance to see the facade properly. Its most striking feature is the large rose window, a round window filled with stone tracery like the spokes of a wheel. The pale local limestone turns honey-coloured in late afternoon light. Around the square, the narrow lanes of the old town fold back on themselves. Laundry hangs from balconies. Somewhere, a moped echoes. The cathedral sits right inside daily life.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the doorway and look back along the nave. The great tree runs away from you toward the altar, its branches full of kings, beasts and labourers. Behind you is the brightness of the street. In front of you is the dim gold of the interior. Pantaleone made this floor for people who could not read. It still works the same way. Walk out slowly, and let the sunlight find you again.
