The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Just behind the hum of traffic in western Sydney, you may hear a softer sound: shoes being placed on a rack, a door easing shut, a low voice reciting in Arabic. Look up, and a broad grey dome rises above the rooftops of Auburn. Two slender minarets stand on either side like pencils against the sky. It is an Ottoman silhouette, the kind you might expect beside the Bosphorus in Istanbul. Instead, it stands in a suburb of brick homes, corner shops and wide Australian light.

This is the Auburn Gallipoli Mosque. It is a working place of prayer first, and a visitor attraction second. Keep that order in mind as you explore. Families come here for daily prayers, for Friday worship, for weddings and for funerals. The building you are looking at belongs to a living community. That is part of what makes standing here feel different from visiting a museum. The building has a history, but it is also in use today.

Start with the name, because it holds the key to the whole place. Gallipoli is a peninsula in Turkey. In 1915, during the First World War, Australian and New Zealand troops landed there and fought against Ottoman Turkish forces. Thousands died on both sides. For Australians, that campaign became the foundation of the ANZAC story, which is remembered every year on the twenty-fifth of April. For Turks, the same battle is a defining moment of national memory.

So here is the first surprise. A community of Turkish migrants chose to name their mosque after a battlefield where their grandparents' generation fought Australians. They did not do this to reopen a wound. The name was chosen as a gesture of shared remembrance. Enemies who once faced each other across the trenches became neighbours here. The mosque carries that idea in its name.

The people who built it arrived in Australia from Turkey during the second half of the twentieth century. Many came to work in factories and on building sites across western Sydney. At first, they prayed in modest spaces, including rented rooms and converted buildings. Over time, the community raised money to build something larger and more permanent. Construction stretched over many years. Much of the funding came from local families, often in small amounts, rather than from one wealthy patron.

Now let your eyes travel up the outside of the building. The design follows the classical Ottoman mosque tradition. That tradition is often linked to Mimar Sinan, the great sixteenth-century architect who shaped the skyline of Istanbul. Notice how the main dome sits above smaller half-domes and rounded shoulders. The shapes step down gently toward the ground, like a hill seen from a distance. The minarets are tall and narrow, with pointed caps. In the past, a caller would climb them to announce the time for prayer.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look at the forecourt and the arcade in front of the entrance. In a classic Ottoman mosque, this outer area works as a threshold. It is a place to slow down, wash, and prepare before stepping inside. Some worshippers perform their ritual washing, called wudu, at taps set aside for this purpose. Before prayer, they rinse their hands, face and feet. If you see someone doing this, you are watching a practice repeated in mosques around the world.

If the doors are open to visitors, remove your shoes before you enter. Then pause just inside. The first thing most people notice is the space. Without pews or chairs, the floor runs open beneath a carpet laid out in rows. Those rows guide worshippers to stand shoulder to shoulder, all facing the same direction. That direction points toward Mecca, in Saudi Arabia. From Sydney, it lies far to the west and slightly north.

Now raise your head slowly toward the dome. The interior is decorated with painted patterns and tilework inspired by the Ottoman style. Look for deep blues, turquoise, red and white. Flowers such as tulips and carnations appear often in this tradition. The tulip held a special place in Ottoman art and culture. You will not see human or animal figures in the decoration. Islamic religious art traditionally uses geometry, plants and calligraphy instead.

That calligraphy is worth a closer look. The large circular panels and flowing bands of script carry words in Arabic. Usually these are the names of God, the Prophet Muhammad, and early leaders of Islam, along with verses from the Quran. For many worshippers, the beauty of the writing is a form of devotion in itself. Even if you cannot read it, notice the rhythm of the letters. Some strokes stretch out long and low. Others rise tall and straight, almost like the minarets outside.

At the front of the prayer hall, find two important features. The first is the mihrab, a decorated niche set into the wall. It marks the direction of prayer. The second is the minbar, a tall staircase-like pulpit, usually set to its right. From the minbar, the imam gives the Friday sermon. The imam is the person who leads the prayer. Notice that the steps rise toward a small canopy at the top. This is a familiar form in Ottoman mosques.

Here is the second surprise. Many visitors expect a mosque to feel closed to outsiders. Yet this one has a long tradition of welcoming school groups, church groups and curious neighbours. Volunteers have often guided visitors and answered questions about faith and daily life. The welcome depends on the timing, and prayer always comes first. But the spirit of the place matches its name. It was built by migrants and named for a shared past, and it often shows that spirit through hospitality.

Step back outside and look at the surrounding streets. Auburn is one of the most culturally mixed suburbs in Sydney. Within a short walk, you may find Turkish bakeries and Lebanese sweet shops. You may also see Afghan grocers, Chinese restaurants and signs in many languages. The smell of fresh bread and grilled meat often drifts along the main roads. The mosque is a landmark, but it is also one part of a larger neighbourhood. Many communities have settled here and made it home.

Before you leave, turn around once more and look at the dome against the sky. You may hear a car door, a child calling, or the soft click of a gate. Look at the two minarets standing over the suburban rooftops. A distant battle from 1915 is remembered here in stone, tile and a single name. Many of the people who built this place arrived with very little. They left behind a building that invites the neighbourhood to come closer. Take that thought with you as you walk back into the streets of Auburn.
