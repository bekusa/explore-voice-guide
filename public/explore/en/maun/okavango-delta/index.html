The story

Water shines all the way to the horizon. Green islands drift on silver channels, and the air smells of wet grass and wild sage. From a small plane, the land below looks like a giant leaf, its veins filled with light. This is the Okavango Delta, one of the largest inland deltas on Earth, and by the ordinary rules of geography it should not exist at all.

Most rivers end in the sea. The Okavango does not. It begins as rain in the highlands of Angola and flows south for more than 1,000 kilometers. Then it meets the flat sands of the Kalahari and spreads out like an open hand. The water slows, divides into thousands of channels, and finally disappears into sand and sky. Almost none of it ever reaches an ocean.

The delta also keeps a strange calendar. Rain falls in Angola in the southern summer. The flood then creeps down the river so slowly that it arrives in Maun months later. Water levels peak between June and August, in the middle of the local dry season. The delta is fullest exactly when the land around it is at its driest.

That timing explains the animals. When pans and rivers fail across northern Maun, this water remains. Elephants walk in from every direction, and Maun holds the largest elephant population on Earth. Buffalo herds graze the floodplains. Hippos keep the channels open by pushing through the reeds, and crocodiles wait patiently in the deeper lagoons.

Some animals belong to the water itself. The red lechwe is an antelope with long, splayed hooves, built for running through flooded grass in loud silver splashes. The shy sitatunga spends its life hidden in papyrus beds. Even the lions have adapted. Prides here wade and swim between islands, something lions elsewhere almost never do.

Birds are constant company. More than 400 species have been recorded in the delta. African fish eagles throw their heads back and call from dead trees; many people describe that sound as the voice of the wild continent. Carmine bee-eaters flash pink and blue over the water. Herons, storks, and kingfishers hunt along every channel.

In 2014, the Okavango Delta was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list as the 1,000th site, a round number for a singular place. The listing honored not only the wildlife but the rare natural process behind it: a great river that ends in a desert and turns part of it green.

The classic way to travel here is the mokoro. It is a narrow canoe, once carved from a single tree trunk, now usually made of fiberglass to spare the old hardwood trees. A poler stands at the stern and pushes quietly through the shallows. You sit low, at the level of the water lilies. Reed frogs cling to stems beside your shoulder, and it is calm enough to hear dragonfly wings.

Look closely at the islands around you. Many begin as termite mounds. A mound rises above the flood, a seed lands on it, a tree takes root, and over many years an island forms. Thousands of islands in the delta grew this way. The largest, Chief's Island, stands on ground raised by an old fault line and was once a royal hunting ground.

People have lived with this water for many generations. The BaYei people are credited with bringing the mokoro tradition to the delta. Villages still stand along the outer channels, and fishing, reed cutting, and guiding remain part of daily life. The town of Maun, founded in 1915, is the gateway. Its airport hums with light aircraft flying to remote camps.

Every season offers a different delta. In high flood, boats and mokoros travel far across the plains. As the water drops, game drives take over, and predators follow the shrinking lagoons. The green summer brings storms, newborn animals, and birdsong; the dry winter brings clear skies and crowded waterholes. There is no wrong month, only different water.

A scenic flight is worth the cost if you can manage it. From above, the delta reveals its whole design: the fan of channels, the game trails crossing pale floodplains, elephant families moving like grey boats through the green. Pilots fly low enough that you can watch hippos scatter rings across a lagoon.

The water itself is astonishingly clear, filtered by sand and papyrus as it moves. Very little flows out; the sun and the plants take nearly all of it. Stay until dark if you can. Hippos leave the water to graze, reed frogs ring like small bells, and the Milky Way stretches unbroken overhead. Somewhere far to the north, new water is already traveling toward you, moving slowly toward a river that never finds the sea, and gives itself to the desert instead.
