The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is short, springy grass over hard rock, often damp, sometimes slick after rain. The wind comes up off the water below and pushes against the stone in front of you. Somewhere a sheep calls from the hillside. Above it all stands a single round tower, grey and weathered, on a rocky knoll that feels made for exactly this purpose... to look out, and to be seen.

You are not in Bangor itself. You have travelled inland from the coast to Llanberis, in the heart of Snowdonia, which the Welsh call Eryri. The castle sits on a narrow strip of land between two lakes. On one side lies Llyn Padarn, long and silver. On the other lies Llyn Peris, darker and tucked tight against the mountains. Behind you the land climbs toward Yr Wyddfa, the Welsh name for Snowdon, the highest mountain in Wales.

This tower belongs to an age before the English kings ruled here. It was built in the early thirteenth century by Llywelyn ab Iorwerth, a prince of Gwynedd, the powerful kingdom of north-west Wales. Later generations gave him a simple title — Llywelyn the Great. He was a ruler who held much of Wales together through war, marriage and sharp diplomacy. Castles like this one were part of how he did it. They were statements in stone, built on Welsh soil by a Welsh prince.

Why build here, of all places? Look along the valley to the south-east. That gap between the mountains is the Llanberis Pass, one of the old routes through the heart of Snowdonia. Whoever held this rock could watch the traffic moving between the coast and the uplands. The lakes added natural protection. Gwynedd's mountains were its fortress, and this tower worked like a gatekeeper at one of its doors.

Now look closely at the shape of the keep. It is round. That may seem ordinary, but here is the first surprise. Many castles built by the Welsh princes used rectangular towers, or towers shaped like the letter D. Round keeps were more typical of the Norman lords along the English border, the so-called Marcher lords. Llywelyn seems to have borrowed the idea from his rivals. A round tower has no corners for attackers to undermine, and it deflects missiles better than a flat wall.

The tower is built from local stone, rough pieces set in mortar, with thick walls that have survived centuries of mountain weather. Notice where you enter. The original doorway was not at ground level but higher up, at the first floor. Defenders would have reached it by a wooden or stone stair that could be held or removed in an emergency. Inside, the floors are gone. What remains is a tall, hollow drum open to the sky, and the sound of the wind moving around it.

The second surprise is a darker one. This castle is remembered not only as a fortress but as a prison. In the 1250s, Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, grandson of the builder, fought his own brother, Owain Goch, for control of Gwynedd. Llywelyn won. Owain was locked away, and according to the traditional account he was held here at Dolbadarn for around twenty years. Picture a man looking out at these same lakes and mountains, season after season, while his brother ruled outside.

The independence of Gwynedd did not last. In the early 1280s, the English king Edward the First launched his final campaign into north Wales. Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was killed in 1282. His brother Dafydd tried to fight on from the mountains, and Dolbadarn fell to the English in 1283. Accounts from the period suggest that timber from the castle was later taken away for Edward's great new fortress at Caernarfon, on the coast not far from here.

So the tower lost its purpose almost overnight. It was no longer a frontier post for a Welsh prince. It became an empty shell on a hill. Over the following centuries the outer buildings crumbled and the stone settled into the turf. Yet the keep itself stayed standing. That is part of what gives it such force today. It is not a polished palace. It is a survivor, plain and stubborn, still holding its rock.

In the late eighteenth century, a new kind of visitor arrived. These were travellers and artists in search of wild scenery. The painter J. M. W. Turner came to north Wales and made Dolbadarn the subject of a moody, dramatic painting, with the tower dark against a stormy sky. For many people across Britain, images like his turned this ruin into a symbol of the mountains themselves — remote, ancient, and a little melancholy.

Turn now and look across Llyn Peris to the far hillside. You will see huge stepped terraces cut into the mountain, grey and purple in the light. These are the old slate quarries, once among the largest in the world. For generations, thousands of local workers split slate here that roofed buildings across Britain and far beyond. The castle and the quarries face each other across the water — two very different chapters of the same valley.

As you walk around the knoll, watch the grass for low lines of stone. These are the remains of other buildings that once stood beside the keep, including a hall and smaller structures inside a defensive wall. They are easy to miss, but they show that this was once a small, busy site, not just a lonely tower. People cooked, slept, kept watch and kept animals on this rock. Try to picture smoke rising from a roof where now there is only sky.

The weather here changes quickly, and that is part of the experience. Cloud can drop over the mountains in minutes, and then lift again to show sharp, bright slopes. In soft evening light the stone of the tower turns warm and golden. In rain, it goes almost black. Each version tells you something true about the place. This was built by people who knew these conditions well, and lived with them every day.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment beside the tower. Feel the wind against the stone and look out along the lakes toward the open valley. The road below, the walkers, the distant quarry terraces — all of it sits inside the view that Welsh princes once guarded from this spot. As you walk back down, glance over your shoulder. The round keep will still be there, watching the pass, just as it has for eight hundred years.
