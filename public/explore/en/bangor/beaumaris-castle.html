The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against stone, and gulls call across the Menai Strait. Underfoot, the path is soft and level, almost too level for a castle. There is no rocky crag here and no dramatic cliff. Instead, a ring of grey walls rises from flat ground at the edge of the sea, with a moat of still water at its feet. You have crossed from Bangor and the mainland onto the Isle of Anglesey, and you are standing at one of the most carefully planned fortresses of medieval Europe.

The name gives you the first clue. Beaumaris comes from the Norman French words for a fair marsh. That is exactly what this place was. It was low, wet ground beside the shore. Other castles in North Wales cling to rock for defence. This one had nothing to cling to. So its builders did something different. They made geometry do the work that a mountain would normally do. Every wall, every tower, every angle you see was drawn with intention.

The man behind it was King Edward the First of England. In the late thirteenth century he set out to control Wales by force, and he built a chain of castles across the north to hold it. Caernarfon, Conwy and Harlech belong to that same chain. Beaumaris was the last of them. Work began in 1295, after a Welsh uprising led by Madog ap Llywelyn shook Edward's grip on the region. Anglesey mattered enormously. It fed North Wales with grain, and whoever held the island held a great deal of power.

The design came from Master James of Saint George, a mason from Savoy, a region in the Alps between modern France and Italy. He was the great castle architect of Edward's Welsh campaign. Here, on open ground, he could finally build without compromise. The result is what historians call a concentric castle. In simple terms, it is a castle inside a castle. An outer ring of walls surrounds an inner ring of taller walls. Look up and you will see how the inner walls tower above the outer ones.

Think about what that meant for an attacker. Suppose you crossed the moat and somehow broke through the outer wall. You would find yourself in a narrow strip of ground, trapped between two lines of stone. Defenders on the high inner wall could shoot down at you. Defenders on the outer wall could shoot too. There was nowhere to hide. Walk slowly along that gap between the walls today, and you can feel how exposed it is.

The entrances were planned with the same care. Each gateway forces anyone entering to turn, to pause, and to pass beneath openings in the ceiling. These are often called murder holes, and they let defenders drop missiles or pour water on anyone below. Doors, gates and portcullises, the heavy sliding grilles, could block the passage at several points. Even the outer gates are set so they do not line up with the inner ones. Nobody could simply charge straight through.

Now here is something many visitors miss. Beaumaris was never finished. The walls of the inner ward were meant to rise higher. The great gatehouses were planned to be taller and grander than they are now. But money ran short. Edward was also fighting wars in Scotland, and those campaigns drained his treasury. Building slowed within a few years and stopped for good in the early fourteenth century. Look at the tops of the gatehouses and towers. Some of them simply end, as though someone put down their tools and walked away.

That unfinished state is part of what makes the castle so readable. Because the design stayed on the ground, almost perfectly symmetrical, you can understand the whole plan in a single walk. Many castles grew piece by piece over centuries, with new towers added to old ones. This one shows one bold idea, frozen partway through. It is a little like looking at an architect's drawing turned into stone.

Find the small dock beside the outer wall, near the gate that faces the sea. In the medieval period the water came right up to the castle. Ships could sail in on the tide and unload supplies directly at the walls. That meant the garrison could be fed and armed from the sea, even if an enemy surrounded the castle on land. Today the shoreline has shifted, but the idea is still clear. This was a fortress built to be reached by water.

Inside the inner ward, the space opens up into a wide, grassy courtyard. Halls, kitchens and living rooms were planned around its edges, though much of that was never completed. Walk into the passages inside the thick walls. They are dark, cool and narrow, and they run for long distances around the castle. Look for the chapel in one of the towers on the eastern side. It is a small, quiet room with a vaulted stone ceiling, and it feels very different from the military spaces around it.

The castle did not stay peaceful. In the early fifteenth century, during the rising led by the Welsh leader Owain Glyndŵr, it was captured by his forces and later taken back by the English crown. Much later, in the seventeenth century, it was held for the king during the English Civil War, the conflict between King Charles the First and Parliament. After that, the castle slowly fell into ruin. Stone was taken away for other buildings, and grass and ivy crept over the walls.

Today the castle is cared for by Cadw, the Welsh government's historic environment service. Together with Caernarfon, Conwy and Harlech, it forms part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. That listing recognises these castles as some of the finest examples of military architecture from the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. Of the four, many specialists consider Beaumaris the most perfect in its design, precisely because it was planned from scratch on open ground.

Before you leave, walk out along the edge of the moat. Look across the water at the wall walks and the round towers reflected on the surface. Then turn around. Across the strait, you can see the mountains of Snowdonia rising beyond the mainland. Edward chose this site to watch over that land and that water. The same view is in front of you now. And as you walk back into the small town of Beaumaris, notice how the streets still sit close to the castle, just as they were laid out in its shadow more than seven hundred years ago.
