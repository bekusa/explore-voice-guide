The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Traffic hums beyond the walls, a dog barks somewhere in the housing estates, and then your shoes meet a raised walkway above pale, broken stone. The light here is softened by a wide canopy overhead. Below you, great slabs of honey-coloured limestone lean and stand in rows. Some are smooth from weathering. Others carry carved spirals. You are standing among buildings older than the pyramids of Egypt, in the middle of an ordinary Maltese suburb.

First, a small note on geography. These temples stand in the town of Tarxien, a short drive inland from Cospicua and the Grand Harbour. The Three Cities, with their fortifications and dockyards, are only a few kilometres away. That contrast is worth holding in your mind. Cospicua's walls belong to the age of the Knights and the British. The stones in front of you belong to a farming people who lived on these islands more than five thousand years ago.

The site lay hidden under fields for thousands of years. In 1914, local farmers kept striking large blocks of stone while working the soil. Word reached Themistocles Zammit, the director of the Valletta museum and a doctor by training. He led excavations here over the following years, through the period of the First World War. What his team uncovered was not one temple but a complex of several, built and rebuilt over many centuries.

Archaeologists generally describe four structures here. The oldest is a small, very ruined temple at the eastern edge. The others were built later, and they connect to each other in an unusual way. The builders did not simply add new temples beside old ones. They joined them, so that you can pass from one into another. The result feels less like a single building and more like a sacred quarter that grew over generations.

Most of the construction belongs to a phase that archaeologists call the Tarxien phase, roughly the late fourth and early third millennium before the common era. The temple builders had no metal tools. They shaped stone with harder stone, with flint, and with a great deal of patience. To move the biggest slabs, they likely used stone balls as rollers. Several of these spheres were found on site, and you may notice similar round stones near the walkway.

Now look at the layout. Maltese temples of this period follow a recognisable plan. A curved façade faces a forecourt. A central passage leads inside, with rounded chambers opening on each side, a shape scholars often call lobed or apsidal, meaning rounded like the end of a church. Seen from above, the plan looks a little like a clover. Some people say it resembles a human body, though that is an interpretation, not a proven intention.

Here is the detail most visitors miss. Tarxien is the most richly decorated of all the Maltese temple sites. Look along the lower stone blocks for carved spirals, some running in pairs like tendrils. Look too for the animal frieze. Goats, pigs, and a ram walk in a line across the stone, carved in relief. Many of the carvings you see today are careful replicas. The originals were moved indoors to the National Museum of Archaeology in Valletta to protect them from weather.

The second surprise is a pair of enormous legs. Near the entrance area stands the lower half of a large stone figure, a broad skirt above thick calves and small feet. Only the bottom part survives. Even so, the complete statue would have been well over the height of a person. Figures like this are often called fat ladies or fertility figures. Nobody truly knows whether they show a goddess, a priestess, or something else entirely. Again, the original is in Valletta.

The temples were not quiet places. Excavators found altars, stone bowls, and hollows that may have held offerings. Animal bones were recovered from several spots, which suggests sacrifice or ritual feasting. In one decorated altar, a flint knife was reportedly found along with bones. Some stone blocks were fitted with holes that may have held ropes or doors. Picture firelight, the smell of burning fat, and the low sound of voices inside these chambers.

Then, at some point around the middle of the third millennium, the temple culture of Malta came to an end. The reasons are still debated. Possible causes include drought, soil exhaustion, disease, or the arrival of new people. What is clear is what came next. A Bronze Age community later used the ruined temple as a cremation cemetery. They placed urns of ashes among the old stones, a reuse that gave its name to an entire archaeological period.

Modern Tarxien has grown right up to the edges of the site. Houses and roads press close on every side. That pressure is part of the reason for the canopy above you. Installed in the twenty-first century, it shields the soft limestone from rain and harsh sun. Some visitors find it changes the mood of the ruins. Others appreciate the shade. Either way, it is a reminder that keeping such old stone intact takes constant work.

If this place moves you, there is more within walking distance. A few hundred metres away lies the Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum, an underground burial complex carved from rock by the same culture. Entry there is strictly limited and must be booked well in advance. Together with the temples here and the finds in Valletta, it helps complete the picture. The Maltese temples, Tarxien among them, are recognised as a World Heritage Site by UNESCO.

Before you go, pause at one of the spiral carvings. Look at how the lines curl inward and then out again. Someone chose that shape, cut it slowly with stone, and expected others to see it. As you walk back toward the street, notice how quickly the modern sounds return... the scooters, the voices, the clink of a café. The ground under that ordinary town still holds older stories than almost anywhere in Europe.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Valletta's archaeology museum to see original carvings.
Book the nearby Hypogeum well ahead of your trip.
Bring water for summer heat on the walkway.
Stay on raised walkways to protect fragile limestone.
Read the site plan first to follow the four temples.
