The guide

The story

The air changes first. As you leave the street and go down, the warmth of the day falls away. Cool, still air settles on your skin, and the city noise softens to almost nothing. Under your feet, the walkway carries you over pale, honey-coloured rock that people shaped by hand thousands of years ago. You are now standing inside one of the oldest underground structures anywhere on Earth. Welcome to the Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum.

First, a small point of orientation. Many visitors arrive from Cospicua and the other harbour towns around the Grand Harbour. The Hypogeum itself sits a short distance inland, in the town of Paola. Above you is an ordinary residential street of terraced houses, front doors and parked cars. That ordinary street is part of the story. Nobody knew this place was here until the houses were already being built.

The discovery came in 1902. Workers were digging cisterns for new homes, and they broke through the rock into a hollow space below. The early part of the story is uncomfortable. Some of the material inside was reportedly disturbed or cleared before archaeologists could study it properly. A priest and scholar named Manuel Magri led the first excavations. After his death, the Maltese archaeologist Themistocles Zammit continued the work and published much of what we know today.

So what exactly is a hypogeum? The word comes from Greek and means, simply, 'underground'. This one is a complex of chambers, passages and halls cut downward into the rock on three levels. The deepest level lies roughly ten metres below the street. The builders did not have metal tools. They worked with stone, antler and bone, and they cut slowly through the soft local limestone that the Maltese call globigerina.

The Hypogeum was in use over a very long period, from roughly the fourth millennium before Christ into the third. That makes it older than the great pyramids of Egypt. It belongs to the same culture that raised the megalithic temples of Malta and Gozo — huge structures of stacked stone blocks above ground. Here, those same people did something different. Instead of building up, they carved down.

And this is the first surprise. Look closely at the walls in the main chambers. The carvers imitated the architecture of the temples above ground. You can see shapes that look like upright stones with a horizontal stone laid across the top, a form called a trilithon. Some ceilings are cut in steps, as if they were rows of corbelled stone. But nothing here was built. Every 'block' is a single piece of living rock, shaped to look like construction.

The main purpose of the site was burial. Excavators found the remains of a very large number of people. Estimates often run into the thousands, mixed together with pottery, beads, amulets and small carved figures. Bones were frequently found in a jumbled state, which suggests that burials happened over many generations. People seem to have returned here again and again to lay their dead in the same chambers.

The most famous object from the site is a small clay figure known as the Sleeping Lady. She lies on her side on a low couch, with a full, rounded body and one arm folded under her head. Nobody knows for certain what she represents. Some scholars connect her to sleep, death or dreaming. She is not displayed here. You can see her at the National Museum of Archaeology in Valletta, which makes a good companion visit.

Now look upward in the inner rooms, if your guide points it out. On some ceilings you will notice faint red patterns — curling spirals and branching lines painted in red ochre, a natural pigment made from iron-rich earth. These are among the very few prehistoric paintings known from the Maltese islands. They have lasted for thousands of years in this dark, damp stillness. That is exactly why the site is protected so carefully today.

Here is the second surprise. One small chamber is known as the Oracle Room. It has a niche cut into the wall at about head height. When a low voice speaks into this niche, the sound seems to resonate and travel through the surrounding chambers. Whether the carvers designed this effect on purpose is still debated. Locals and researchers alike have tested it for many years, and the deep hum is real. The interpretation is the open question.

Nearby is a chamber often called the Holy of Holies. It is one of the most carefully finished spaces in the complex, with a carved doorway framed by what looks like a trilithon. The name is modern. Archaeologists gave it to the room because of its elaborate form, not because any written record tells us its use. The people who carved it left no writing at all. Everything we know comes from stone, bone and clay.

The site carries scars from its modern history too. Building work above, changes in humidity, and early unrestricted visits all affected the fragile surfaces. The Hypogeum closed for many years of conservation in the 1990s. Today it is managed by Heritage Malta, with careful control of temperature and humidity. Only small groups enter at a time, and they walk on raised platforms. In 1980, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List.

Before you leave, take a moment to stand still on the walkway. Listen to the quiet. Notice the soft marks of ancient tools on the rock, and the way the light falls only where it is placed. Then climb back up into the street and its ordinary daylight. Look at the doorsteps, the balconies, the cars. Somewhere beneath them, the rock is still cool, and the oldest rooms in this town are still keeping their silence.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book weeks or months ahead because group sizes are small.
Leave cameras away since photography is not allowed inside.
Bring a light layer for the cool underground air.
Pair your visit with the Tarxien Temples a short walk away.
See the Sleeping Lady at Valletta's archaeology museum.
