The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water drips somewhere below you, and the air smells of wet earth, leaf mould and sweet, green rot. Overhead, the light comes down in patches, filtered through leaves that look too big for England. Underfoot, the path is soft and damp. This is Heligan, a few miles south of Saint Austell in Cornwall. For much of the twentieth century, almost nobody walked here at all.

Heligan was the home of the Tremayne family for around four centuries. Over generations they shaped this valley into a grand country estate. There were formal gardens for pleasure and productive gardens for food. In the Victorian era, Heligan worked like a small village. It had its own glasshouses, walled gardens and a large team of gardeners. Their job was to feed the big house and to impress its guests.

Then came 1914. When the First World War began, many of the estate's gardeners and outdoor staff left to serve. Some of them never came back. Without those hands, the work of keeping the gardens slowly stopped. Brambles and laurel crept over the paths. Ivy pulled at the walls. Fallen trees blocked the valleys. Over the decades that followed, the gardens disappeared under green, and Heligan became, quite literally, lost.

Here is the first surprise. Restorers later found a small room near the garden buildings, used as the gardeners' privy. People often call it the Thunderbox Room. On its lime-washed wall, workers had written their names in pencil, with a date in August 1914. That was the first month of the war. Those faint signatures are among the most moving traces at Heligan. They are a quiet roll call of the men who walked out of this garden and into history.

The gardens stayed buried for most of the century. Then, at the start of the 1990s, a restoration began. Tim Smit, who later created the nearby Eden Project, worked with John Willis, a descendant of the Tremayne family. With them came a small team of builders and gardeners. They cut through the undergrowth almost by hand, and slowly the old layout came back. A television series followed the work, and visitors began to arrive in large numbers.

Now let us walk to the productive gardens, the working heart of the estate. Inside the high walls, the air is warmer and calmer. You will see rows of vegetables, fruit trained flat against brick, and old glasshouses. The restorers chose to grow heritage varieties here, using methods that Victorian gardeners would recognise. So this is not just a pretty display. It is a working kitchen garden, still producing food in the old way.

The second surprise is the pineapple pit. Victorian gardeners grew pineapples here in Cornwall, long before supermarkets brought tropical fruit to every town. Their secret was horse manure. Fresh manure was packed around the growing beds. As it rotted, it gave off a steady heat that kept the plants warm through the cool months. A pineapple from your own estate was a powerful status symbol at a Victorian dinner. Today, the gardeners at Heligan still use this method.

From the walled gardens, the land drops away into the Jungle. This steep valley feels like another world. Tree ferns open their huge fronds above the path. Bamboo creaks in the wind. Giant gunnera, a plant from South America with leaves bigger than an umbrella, grows beside the ponds. The mild, wet Cornish climate allowed Victorian collectors to grow plants from warmer countries here, and many of them still thrive in this sheltered valley.

If you are feeling brave, look for the rope bridge that crosses part of the Jungle. It sways a little as you walk, and the view down through the canopy is worth the wobble. Down here, sound behaves differently. Voices soften. Running water fills the gaps. On a wet day, the whole valley steams gently, and the greens become almost unreal in their depth. Take your time on the boardwalks, and let your eyes adjust to the shade.

Heligan is also known for its sculptures, made from living plants and earth. The most famous is the Mud Maid, a sleeping woman shaped from rock, soil and plants, lying in the woodland. Moss and ivy cover her face, so she changes with the seasons. Nearby, the Giant's Head rises out of the ground, covered in green. Neither is old, but both suit the story of this place. They show a garden quietly reclaiming the shapes of people.

There is more to find in the pleasure grounds. Look for the Italian Garden, a small formal space with a pool, built for calm and order. Look for the Crystal Grotto, a cool little cave set with sparkling stones. In spring, the old rhododendrons and camellias put on a huge show of colour. Some of these shrubs were planted generations ago and have grown into the size of small trees, with twisted, peeling trunks.

Beyond the formal gardens, Heligan opens into wider estate land. Paths run through woods, wetlands and fields, down towards the Lost Valley. Here the feeling is less like a garden and more like countryside. You may see rare-breed farm animals, and the estate works to support wildlife such as birds, bats and insects. The big house itself is not part of the visit. It is private, so the story you follow here is the story of the land.

Before you leave, stop somewhere quiet. Perhaps by a pond, perhaps under a tree fern, perhaps beside a warm brick wall. Notice the moss on the stones, the smell of the soil, the sound of a blackbird in the hedge. Everything around you was once buried and forgotten. Now it grows again because people chose to clear it, one bramble at a time. As you walk back up the path, remember those pencil names on a wall from 1914.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy waterproof shoes for the steep, muddy Jungle paths.
Visit in spring to see the old rhododendrons flowering.
Allow at least half a day to explore all areas.
Pick up a site map to find the hidden sculptures.
Bring a light rain jacket even on bright Cornish days.
