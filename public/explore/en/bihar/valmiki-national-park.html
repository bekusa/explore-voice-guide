The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest reaches you through sound. Dry sal leaves crackle underfoot, broad and papery as old letters. Somewhere above, a peacock calls, harsh and sudden. Then comes a quieter layer: insects, wind in tall grass, and the distant rush of a river. The air smells of damp earth and leaf litter. Morning light falls in long gold bars between straight grey trunks. This is the far northwestern corner of Bihar, where the plains finally meet the hills.

You are standing in Valmiki National Park, the only national park in the state of Bihar. It sits in West Champaran district, pressed right up against the border with Nepal. The core of the park also forms Valmiki Tiger Reserve, part of India's long-running national programme to protect tigers. For a state known mostly for flat, crowded farmland and ancient cities, this is a surprise. Here the land rises, the forest closes in, and people become the visitors.

The name comes from Valmiki, the sage traditionally honoured as the author of the Ramayana, one of the two great epic poems of India. The Ramayana tells the story of Prince Rama, his wife Sita, and her rescue from the demon king Ravana. Local tradition holds that Valmiki had his hermitage in these forests. According to the popular tale, Sita sheltered here during her exile, and her twin sons were born and raised nearby. Pilgrims still visit a site known as Valmiki Ashram near the border.

Now think about the shape of the land around you. To the north rise the Someshwar hills, a low outer edge of the Himalayan foothills. To the west and south runs the Gandak, a wide river that flows down from Nepal into the Ganges basin. Between hills and river lies a mix of dense sal forest, open grassland, and wetland. Ecologists call this belt the Terai, a word for the moist, grassy lowland strip along the foot of the Himalaya.

Here is the first thing most visitors miss. This forest does not stop at the border. Across the line in Nepal lies Chitwan National Park, and beyond it other protected forests. Together they form one continuous landscape for wildlife. Tigers, leopards, and elephants do not carry passports. Animals move back and forth along these corridors. Occasionally even a greater one-horned rhinoceros wanders across from the Nepalese side, a rare and memorable sight on the Indian bank.

The tiger is the animal everyone hopes for, and honesty matters here. Tigers in this forest are shy, and most visitors never see one. What you are far more likely to find is evidence. Look for pugmarks, the soft rounded paw prints, pressed into sandy tracks. Look for long scratch marks on tree bark. Listen for alarm calls, because a sudden bark from a spotted deer often means a predator is moving nearby. Reading these signs is half the pleasure of the forest.

The other residents are easier to meet. Spotted deer graze at the forest edge, their coats dappled like the light itself. Sambar, the large dark deer of Indian forests, move more cautiously. Wild boar root through the leaf litter. Langur monkeys, grey with black faces, watch from the branches. The park is also home to sloth bears, leopards, and gaur, the massive wild cattle sometimes called Indian bison. Each animal keeps its own hours, and dawn is when many of them appear.

The second surprise lives in the water. The Gandak river is home to the gharial, a slender crocodile with a long, narrow snout built for catching fish. Gharials are critically endangered, and the Gandak is one of the few rivers where they still survive in the wild. The same river also shelters the Ganges river dolphin, which is almost blind and finds its way by sound. If you watch the surface patiently, you might catch the brief curve of a dolphin rising to breathe.

Birds fill the gaps between the larger stories. The mix of forest, grassland, and river draws a long list of species. Hornbills fly heavily overhead with loud, whooshing wingbeats. Kingfishers flash blue along the banks. Raptors circle above the clearings. In the cooler months, migratory waterbirds arrive on the river and the wetlands. Even if you know little about birds, a pair of binoculars turns a quiet hour here into a constant, changing show.

People have shaped this landscape too. The villages around the park are home to communities including the Tharu, an indigenous people of the Terai who have lived along these forests for generations. Their houses, crafts, and farming follow the rhythm of the seasons and the river. In recent years, local people have also become involved in guiding and homestays. Their knowledge of the forest, its paths and its moods, is often deeper than any map.

At Valmikinagar, near the edge of the park, you will find a large barrage across the Gandak. A barrage is a low dam with gates that control the river's flow for irrigation. It is a reminder that this water feeds farmland far to the south. Not far away, local tradition marks a sacred meeting point of rivers called Triveni, where pilgrims gather for festivals. Sacred sites, working infrastructure, and wild forest sit side by side here.

The forest changes sharply with the seasons. After the monsoon, the grass grows tall and green, and the rivers run full and brown. In the cool winter months, mist often lies low over the river in the early morning. As spring and summer arrive, the grass dries, the leaves fall, and visibility improves along the tracks. Each season gives a different forest. Rangers and guides can tell you which areas are open and safe at any moment.

A visit here rewards a slower pace. Safaris usually run in the early morning and late afternoon, when animals are most active. Wear muted colours, keep your voice low, and switch off your phone's sounds. Follow your guide's instructions, especially near water and in tall grass. Remember that this is a working reserve. The animals have priority, and the best moments often come when you simply stop, wait, and let the forest decide what to show you.

Before you move on, pause for a moment. Look at the straight trunks of the sal trees around you, and the light shifting through their leaves. Listen again for the river, and for any sudden call in the undergrowth. Somewhere in this forest, perhaps quite close, an animal is listening to you. As you walk back, watch the sandy ground at your feet. The tracks of the night are often still there, waiting to be read.
