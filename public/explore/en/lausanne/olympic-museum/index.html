The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves slap against the stone quay below, and the breeze off Lake Geneva carries a faint smell of fresh water and cut grass. Across the lake, the French mountains rise in layers of blue and grey. Here, on the shore at Ouchy in Lausanne, a pale, terraced building sits above a sloping park. This is the Olympic Museum, and before you go inside, the walk up through the gardens is already part of the visit.

Why is the museum here, in a quiet Swiss lakeside city, and not in Athens or Paris? The answer is one man. Pierre de Coubertin, the French educator who revived the Olympic Games in the 1890s, moved the International Olympic Committee to Lausanne in 1915. Europe was at war. Neutral Switzerland offered a safe, stable home. The committee never left, and over time the city became known as the Olympic Capital.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Coubertin lived and died in this region, and he is buried in Lausanne. But his heart is not here. At his request, his heart was taken to Olympia, in Greece, the site of the ancient Games. It rests there in a monument. So the founder of the modern Olympics is split between two places — the city that housed his dream, and the valley where the idea first began.

The museum itself opened in 1993. It was a project strongly supported by Juan Antonio Samaranch, who led the International Olympic Committee at that time. In the 2010s the building closed for a major renovation. It reopened in late 2016 with new displays, more screens, and a more interactive approach. So what you see inside today is a fairly recent presentation, set inside a building that has stood on this shore for more than thirty years.

Before you enter, take your time in the park. The gardens climb in terraces from the lakeside up to the museum doors. Along the way, sculptures of athletes stand among the trees and lawns — runners, jumpers, figures caught in the middle of an effort. Some are realistic, some are almost abstract. Watch how local families use this space. Children run along the paths, joggers pass by, and people sit on the benches simply to look at the water.

Near the entrance, you will usually see an Olympic flame burning in a basin. It is a quiet reference to the great ritual of the Games. Every modern Olympics begins with a flame lit from the sun's rays at Olympia, then carried across countries by thousands of hands. The flame here is smaller and calmer. But it reminds you that this museum is about symbols as much as sport.

Inside, the exhibition is spread across several levels, and it moves roughly from ideas to events to people. The first theme is the Olympic world itself — its origins in ancient Greece, the revival under Coubertin, and the meaning of its symbols. You will see the five rings, which Coubertin designed in the 1910s. The colours were chosen so that, together with the white background, every national flag of the time could be drawn from them.

One of the most popular displays is the collection of Olympic torches. Each host city designs its own torch, so the line of them reads like a history of industrial design. Some are slim and metallic. Others are sculptural, curved, or colourful. Look closely and you can trace changes in materials and taste across the decades. It is a quiet room, but people often stand here the longest, pointing out the torch from a Games they remember.

The next part of the visit turns to the Games themselves. Here you meet the objects of competition — shoes, skis, bicycles, costumes, and medals. Seen up close, these objects are surprisingly human. A pair of worn running shoes tells a story of training that no television broadcast shows. Older equipment often looks heavy and simple next to modern gear. That contrast says a great deal about how sport, technology, and the body have changed together.

The museum also gives space to the Winter Games, which began in the 1920s. Switzerland, of course, has deep ties to snow sports, and the Alps are visible from the windows on a clear day. Look for the evolution of skates, sledges, and skis. Many displays use film and sound, so you may hear the roar of a crowd or the hiss of blades on ice as you walk through these rooms.

The final theme focuses on the athletes and the experience of being an Olympian. Interactive stations invite you to test your balance, reactions, or memory. Displays show the Olympic Village, the training, the nerves, and the ceremonies. This is where the museum moves from objects to feelings. It asks a simple question — what does it take, and what does it cost, to reach this level? Families often linger here, because children can take part.

If you have time, go up to the top floor. There is a café with large windows and a terrace facing the lake. From here the view opens wide — the water, the boats, the mountains on the French side. It is one of the best places in Ouchy to sit and rest. The contrast is gentle and striking. Inside, you have seen speed, effort, and records. Outside, the lake moves slowly, as it has for thousands of years.

When you leave, walk back down through the gardens toward the shore. The promenade along the lake continues in both directions, lined with trees, hotels, and small piers. The Olympic Committee's modern headquarters sit a little farther west along this same shoreline. Pause for a moment by the water. Notice the joggers passing, the sculpted runners behind you, and the real ones in front. In Lausanne, the Olympic idea is not only kept in a museum — it is part of an ordinary afternoon by the lake.
