The guide

The story

Start with the bricks under your hand. They are warm, rough, and the colour of dried clay at sunset. Rest your palm against the wall and you may notice something odd. There is almost no visible mortar between them. The bricks seem to hold each other up by patience alone. Around you, incense smoke drifts up the steps, and from below comes the sound of motorbikes crossing the river.

You are standing on a low granite hill at the northern edge of Nha Trang, in Khánh Hòa province. This is Po Nagar, a temple complex built by the Cham people. The Cham were a seafaring kingdom that ruled much of what is now central Vietnam for well over a thousand years. Their language, their faith, and their architecture came partly from India, carried by traders across the sea. This hill was one of their most sacred places.

The name tells you who lives here. Po Nagar means, roughly, Lady of the Land or Mother of the Kingdom. She is a goddess of the earth, of rice, and of the people who farm and fish this coast. The Cham linked her to the great Hindu goddesses, the fierce and protective consorts of Shiva. Later, Vietnamese settlers took her into their own worship under a different name, Thiên Y A Na. So one goddess carries two names on this hill.

The story of the site goes back a long way. Stone inscriptions found here record that raiders from across the sea burned an earlier shrine in the late eighth century. That first temple was probably made of wood. The Cham rebuilt in brick, and over the following centuries different kings added towers, repaired others, and left more inscriptions. What you see today is several layers of building, from roughly the eighth century to the thirteenth.

Before you climb, look at the lower terrace. Here stand rows of thick brick pillars, open to the sky. They once held up a roof over a great hall where pilgrims gathered. Scholars think worshippers paused here to prepare themselves before going up to the goddess. Walk between the pillars slowly. Today they frame the towers above like a gateway with no walls. It is easy to rush past them, so give them a moment.

Then comes the stairway. It is steep, and the steps are uneven. That climb was part of the ritual. You leave the ordinary town behind you and rise toward the sacred. As you go up, turn and look back. The Cái River opens below, crowded with blue and red fishing boats. Beyond it lies the long curve of the bay. The Cham chose this spot with care, at the meeting of river, sea, and hill.

At the top, the largest tower rises ahead of you. It stands more than twenty metres tall, built in tiers that get smaller as they climb. Each tier repeats the shape of the one below it, like a mountain built of smaller mountains. That is no accident. In Hindu belief, the temple tower represents Mount Meru, the sacred peak at the centre of the universe. You are looking at a model of the cosmos, made from fired clay.

Now look above the main doorway. A sandstone panel shows a goddess with several arms, dancing. Musicians play on either side of her. Many visitors walk straight under it without looking up. Take a moment. Sandstone is softer than the brick, and centuries of monsoon rain have rounded its edges. Still, the movement of the figure comes through — one foot raised, the body swaying to music no one can hear anymore.

Inside the main tower, the air changes. It is dark, close, and heavy with smoke. When your eyes adjust, you will see the statue of the goddess. She sits on a high base, carved from dark stone, with ten arms. She is usually dressed in fabric robes offered by worshippers. This is not a museum piece. People come here to pray for health, for good harvests, for safe fishing trips, and for children. Move quietly, and let them have their space.

Here is something many visitors miss. The Cham brick technique is still not fully understood. The bricks fit so tightly that experts have argued for years about how they were joined. Some think a natural plant resin was used. Others suggest the bricks were rubbed together until their surfaces fused. There is no single agreed answer. So the wall you touched at the start still holds a small mystery, more than a thousand years later.

The smaller towers around the main one were dedicated to other gods and to members of the goddess's divine family. Each has its own altar and its own character. Walk around them slowly. On their outer walls, you can see false doors, decorative niches, and carved corners. Some carvings are crisp. Others have worn almost smooth. Weeds and moss grow in the cracks, and the brick changes colour from orange to grey to almost purple.

This is a living temple, and it has a yearly festival. Every spring, in the third month of the lunar calendar, the hill fills with pilgrims. Locals say the goddess returns to her people at this time. There are processions, offerings of fruit and flowers, and ritual bathing of the statue. Cham and Vietnamese worshippers come together. If you visit at another time of year, you may still see a small group of dancers performing on the terrace.

As you walk around, listen. You might hear temple bells, the murmur of prayers, the clatter of a gong. Below, the traffic crosses the bridge, and a fishing boat engine coughs into life. The two worlds sit very close together here. The Cham kingdom faded long ago, absorbed into Vietnam over several centuries. Yet the goddess kept her hill. People still climb these steps with incense in their hands.

Before you leave, stand at the edge of the upper terrace for one more minute. Put your hand on the brick again. Feel how the sun has warmed it. Then look out over the river mouth, where the water meets the sea. The Cham builders stood on this same hill and looked at this same view. As you walk back down the steps, notice the smoke rising behind you. Someone has just lit a new stick of incense.

Before you go

Practical tips

Cover shoulders and knees, as this is an active place of worship.
Remove your shoes before entering the main tower.
Come in early morning for softer light and fewer crowds.
Take the stairs slowly; the steps are steep and uneven.
Visit in the third lunar month to see the annual festival.
