The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes off the water in steady pushes and moves the dry thyme and the low scrub along the slope. Below you, the Aegean breaks against the rocks with a slow, heavy rhythm. Above you, white columns stand against an open sky. There is almost nothing between this headland and the horizon. You are standing at Cape Sounion, the southern tip of Attica, where the land around Athens finally runs out and the sea takes over.

The ancient Greeks felt the weight of this place too. In Homer's Odyssey, the poet calls it holy Sounion, the cape of Athens. Sailors coming home from the east watched for this point of land. When they saw it, they knew Athens was close. When they left, it was the last piece of home they saw. So a temple here was more than decoration. It was a landmark, a prayer, and a promise to anyone crossing these waters.

The temple belongs to Poseidon, god of the sea, of earthquakes, and of horses. For a seafaring city like Athens, he was a god you did not ignore. Storms could sink a fleet. The sea could feed a city or starve it. The building you see today dates from the middle of the fifth century before Christ. That is the same period, and the same burst of confidence, that produced the Parthenon on the Acropolis in Athens.

Here is something many visitors do not know. This is not the first temple on the spot. An earlier temple to Poseidon stood here and was still unfinished when the Persians invaded Greece in 480 BCE. The Persian army destroyed it. After the Greek victory, the Athenians came back and built again, on the same ground. So the columns in front of you are, in a sense, a statement of recovery after war.

Look at the columns themselves. They follow the Doric order, the plainest of the classical styles. Doric columns have no base, a simple cushion-shaped top, and vertical grooves called flutes. Count the flutes on one column if you can. Most Doric columns have twenty. These have sixteen. Fewer, wider grooves catch the strong sea light differently, and some scholars think the builders chose this on purpose for a site so exposed to sun and salt wind.

The stone is also local. The marble came from quarries nearby, not from the famous Pentelic quarries that supplied the Parthenon. It is a grey-white stone with a fine grain, and it has weathered in its own way over the centuries. Some scholars link the design to the same architect who built the Temple of Hephaestus in the Athenian Agora, because the proportions are close. His name is not known, so you may hear him described simply by the buildings he left behind.

Now look at the shape of the whole. The temple was a long rectangle, surrounded on all sides by a row of columns. Only part of that ring still stands, but enough remains for your eye to complete it. Walk slowly around the outside. Watch how the gaps between the columns frame the sea, then the sky, then the islands on the horizon. On a clear day, you can make out the shapes of islands floating in the haze.

Here is the second surprise, and it is carved into the stone. Lord Byron, the English Romantic poet, visited Greece in the early nineteenth century and loved this cape. Visitors have long pointed to his name, cut into the marble of the temple. He was not alone. Travellers of that era often scratched their names into ancient monuments. Today this would be vandalism, and the area is roped off, so please admire these old marks only from a distance.

There is also a famous legend tied to this cliff. According to the myth, King Aegeus of Athens waited here for his son Theseus to return from Crete, where Theseus had gone to fight the Minotaur. Theseus promised to raise white sails if he survived. He forgot. When Aegeus saw black sails, he believed his son was dead and threw himself into the sea. Legend says that is why the waters below are called the Aegean.

Sounion was not only sacred. It was strategic. Look along the coast and you will be looking toward Lavrio, where the ancient silver mines of Laurion lay. That silver helped Athens build the fleet that fought the Persians at sea. Ships carrying grain to Athens also passed this cape. During the long Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta, the Athenians fortified the headland to protect these routes. Traces of the fortification walls can still be found around the site.

Poseidon was not the only god honoured here. On a lower hill to the northeast, a short distance away, stood a sanctuary of Athena, the patron goddess of Athens. Little of it rises above the ground today, so many visitors walk past without noticing. Archaeologists working at Sounion also found large marble statues of young men, known as kouroi, from an earlier period. Those statues now stand in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.

If you stay until late afternoon, you will see why so many people make the drive along the coast road from Athens. As the sun drops toward the sea in the west, the marble turns warm, almost golden, and the shadows of the columns stretch across the ground. Crowds gather on the slopes and fall quiet. Even the wind seems less noticeable when everyone is watching the same slow light on the water.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the rough rock under your shoes and the salt in the air. The same headland watched Athenian triremes, Persian fleets, Roman travellers, and Romantic poets. Now it watches you. As you walk back down the path, turn once more and look up at the columns. From below, they stand exactly as sailors saw them for centuries... a white signal at the end of the land.

What to look for

Byron's name carved into the temple marble.
Wide, shallow flutes running up each Doric column.
Islands framed between columns on the horizon.
Low remains of Athena's sanctuary on the nearby hill.
Traces of the old fortification walls on the slopes.
Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive well before sunset to find a good viewing spot.
Bring a windproof layer; the headland is very exposed.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven rocky paths.
Carry water and sun protection; shade is scarce here.
Pair the visit with a stop in nearby Lavrio.
