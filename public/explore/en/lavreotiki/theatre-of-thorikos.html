The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the dry grass on the hillside, and somewhere below a car passes on the coast road. Underfoot the ground is pale and stony, and the rock is rough and grey. In front of you, rows of worn stone seats curve around a flat open floor. Then the curve straightens out. This theatre does not look quite like the ones you may know from postcards, and that is the first thing worth noticing.

You are standing at the Theatre of Thorikos, on the lower slope of a hill called Velatouri, close to the modern town of Lavrio. In ancient times Thorikos was a deme, a local community and district of Attica, the territory ruled from Athens. It sat near the sea, with a natural harbour nearby. This made it one of the busiest corners of the Athenian countryside. The theatre is the heart of what survives of that old town.

Now look at the shape again. Most Greek theatres you see in photographs form a neat half circle, like a fan opened around a round stage. Here the seating is long and irregular. The middle section runs almost straight, and only the ends bend inward. Archaeologists often describe it as elongated or oval. Scholars think the builders simply followed the natural shape of the slope. They worked with the hill, rather than cutting it into a perfect geometric form.

This matters because the theatre is very old. Many experts count it among the earliest surviving theatres in the Greek world. Its first phase is usually dated to around the late sixth century BCE, a time when Greek drama itself was still young. A larger rebuilding followed in the fourth century BCE, when more rows were added. So the odd shape is not a mistake. It may show us what theatres looked like before the classic semicircle became the standard design.

Take a moment to study the floor in front of the seats. This open space is the orchestra, from a Greek word for the place where the chorus danced. In later theatres the orchestra is usually round. Here it is closer to a rectangle, with flattened sides. Picture a chorus of local men moving across this beaten earth, singing in honour of a god. There was no tall stage building behind them in the early period, only the open landscape.

That god was Dionysus, the god of wine, theatre and religious ecstasy. Drama in ancient Attica grew out of festivals held in his honour. Look toward the western side of the orchestra and you can find the foundations of a small temple. Most scholars connect it with Dionysus. On the opposite side, the remains of an altar have also been identified. Religion and performance were not separate here. A play was, in part, an act of worship.

The theatre probably served more than drama. In a country deme like Thorikos, a large stone seating area was a rare public space. Many historians suggest that local citizens also gathered here for meetings and civic business. So imagine this slope filled with farmers, traders and mine workers, all neighbours. They watched plays, they sang, and they may also have argued about local affairs. The seats you see were a kind of town hall in the open air.

And here comes a surprise many visitors miss. The wealth of Thorikos came from underground. These hills belong to the Laurion district, famous in antiquity for its silver mines. That silver helped to fund the power of classical Athens, including its navy. On Velatouri hill itself, archaeologists have found ancient mine galleries cut into the rock. People were digging here long before this theatre was built. The ground beneath the seats is full of history you cannot see.

Near the theatre, excavators have also uncovered workshops and installations used to wash crushed ore. Water was precious in this dry landscape, so these systems were built with great care. Workers channelled and reused it to separate silver-bearing material from waste rock. Much of the hard labour in the Laurion mines was done by enslaved people. It is worth holding that thought as you look at the elegant stone rows. The festivals here rested on very hard, often brutal work nearby.

The story of the hill goes back even further. Higher up on Velatouri, archaeologists have found traces of much older settlement, including tombs from the Mycenaean age, the late Bronze Age world of Greece. Thorikos was already an important place long before classical Athens. The theatre is only one chapter of a very long timeline. From the seats, you can sense how the town climbed the hill, with houses, streets and workshops spread around this public centre.

The site was first explored by Greek archaeologists in the late nineteenth century. In the twentieth century, Belgian archaeologists began long-term excavations here, and their work continued for many decades. Much of what we know about the houses, the mines and the theatre comes from their patient digging. As you walk, notice the walls built from carefully fitted blocks. These retaining walls held back the earth and supported the seating. They are some of the strongest pieces of masonry on the whole site.

If you climb to the upper rows, turn around and look out. The view opens toward the low hills and the coast near Lavrio. In ancient times the audience would have looked across a working landscape of smoke, slag heaps and busy roads. Today it is quieter. Try sitting for a moment on the stone and speaking in a normal voice to a friend below. The sound carries further than you might expect, even with this unusual shape.

Before you leave, let your eyes travel once more along the long, flattened curve of the seats. Notice where the stone is smooth and where it is cracked and patched. Notice the small temple foundations and the open orchestra floor, empty now except for light and dust. People sat exactly where you are standing, more than two thousand years ago, waiting for a chorus to enter. As you walk back down the hill, listen to the wind again. It is the same sound they heard.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring water and sun protection; the hillside has little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and loose gravel.
Climb to the upper rows for views toward the coast.
Combine your visit with Lavrio's mining history sites.
Visit early or late in the day to avoid harsh heat.
