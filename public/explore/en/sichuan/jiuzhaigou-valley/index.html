The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, water is moving. It slides over pale stone, drips through moss, and drops in thin white threads into pools so clear they hardly look wet. The air is cool and smells of pine resin and damp earth. Underfoot, the wooden boardwalk gives a soft knock with every step. And in front of you, the water is a colour that seems unlikely: turquoise at the edges, deep sapphire in the middle, with fallen logs lying on the bottom.

Welcome to Jiuzhaigou, in the mountains of northern Sichuan. The name means Nine Village Valley. It comes from the Tibetan villages that have stood along these slopes for generations. You are in the Aba Tibetan and Qiang Autonomous Prefecture, a region where Chinese, Tibetan and Qiang cultures meet. So the prayer flags you may see on the ridges, and the small stupas by the road, are not decoration for visitors. They belong to people who live here and still worship here.

The valley has a simple shape once you picture it. It forms a rough letter Y. Three main branches meet near the middle: the Shuzheng valley at the base, and the Rize and Zechawa valleys reaching up into the high country. Along these branches lie more than a hundred lakes, linked by streams, shoals and waterfalls. Green park buses run between the main stops, and boardwalks connect the views. Most visitors ride up first, then walk down with the flow of the water.

So why is the water this colour? The answer is chemistry, not paint. The streams here carry a large amount of dissolved calcium carbonate, washed out of the limestone mountains. As the water slows and spreads, that mineral settles out and coats everything it touches. Over thousands of years it has built the pale terraces and dams you see, a stone called travertine. The light-coloured lake beds reflect the sky, while algae, mosses and minerals add greens, golds and blues.

Here is a detail many people miss. Look closely at the logs lying underwater in the clearer lakes. Many of them are not rotting the way wood normally does. The cold, mineral-rich water slows decay, and some trunks are slowly gaining a crust of calcium. In a sense, the lakes are turning old trees into stone, very slowly, in front of you. On a calm morning these sunken branches look sharp enough to touch, even when they lie several metres down.

Locals tell a different story about the lakes. According to a popular Tibetan legend, a mountain god named Dage gave a mirror to the goddess he loved, Semo. The mirror slipped from her hands and broke into many pieces. Each fragment, the tale says, became one of the lakes in this valley. It is folklore, not geology. But stand at the edge of a still pool on a windless day, and you will understand why people tell it.

The valley stayed largely unknown to the outside world until the second half of the twentieth century. Logging reached these forests, and concern for the landscape grew. Protection followed in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and the area opened to tourism soon after. In 1992, UNESCO added Jiuzhaigou to its World Heritage List for its natural beauty. A few years later it was also named a biosphere reserve, recognising the value of its forests and wildlife.

Those forests matter as much as the water. Spruce, fir and birch climb the slopes, with thickets of bamboo underneath. That bamboo is food for one of China's rarest residents — the giant panda. Pandas do live in this wider region, but almost nobody sees one here. They are shy, and they keep to dense, steep cover. You are far more likely to notice birds, squirrels and butterflies. Still, the names of places like Panda Lake and Arrow Bamboo Lake point to that hidden life.

Now, a turning point in the valley's recent story. In August 2017, a powerful earthquake struck this area. People lost their lives, and the park closed. The shaking damaged several famous sites. Sparkling Lake lost much of its water when its natural travertine dam broke, and Nuorilang Waterfall was also damaged. The park reopened in stages over the following years. As you walk, remember that some of what you see is a landscape still recovering and still changing.

That change is actually part of Jiuzhaigou's nature. These lakes were made by movement in the first place. Landslides, rockfalls and mineral dams have blocked and reshaped these streams for thousands of years. Waterfalls migrate. Pools fill and drain. The travertine keeps building, layer on layer, wherever the water slows. So the valley you are seeing today is one moment in a long process. Visitors who return years later often notice that a familiar view has quietly shifted.

At different points you will meet the most photographed places. Nuorilang Waterfall is wide rather than tall, a long curtain of water spilling over a travertine ledge through trees. Pearl Shoal is a broad, sloping bed of stone where water breaks into thousands of bright droplets. Five Flower Lake shows patches of blue, green and yellow side by side. And up at the head of one branch, Long Lake sits high and cold, the largest body of water in the valley.

The seasons change the colours completely. In autumn, the slopes turn red, orange and gold, and the lakes reflect them. That is when the boardwalks are most crowded. Winter brings snow, frozen waterfalls and a quieter valley. Spring and summer bring fresh green leaves and fuller streams. Light matters too. Early morning often gives the calmest water and the clearest reflections, before wind ruffles the surface. And remember the altitude. Walk slowly, drink water, and give yourself time.

In a moment, before you move on, pause at the rail. Look down past the surface to the stones and branches on the lake bed. Then look up to the trees, and further, to the ridgeline and the sky. The same water links all of it. It started as snow on those mountains. It passed through rock, gathered minerals, and settled here as colour. As you walk away, listen again for the sound of it — moving downhill, still quietly building this valley, one thin layer at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the park bus uphill first, then walk down.
Arrive early for calmer water and sharper reflections.
Pace yourself and drink water at high altitude.
Pack layers; mountain weather shifts quickly through the day.
Stay on the boardwalks to protect fragile travertine.
