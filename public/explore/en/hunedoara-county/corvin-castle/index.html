The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the bridge, a small river runs over stones, and the sound carries up through the timber under your feet. The planks give a little as you walk. Ahead, grey stone walls rise straight out of the rock, topped with steep roofs in red and dark tile. Pointed towers stand against the sky. You are on the long wooden bridge to Corvin Castle, in the town of Hunedoara, western Romania. And with every step, the entrance gate gets a little taller.

This bridge is the best place to understand the castle's shape. It sits on a rocky outcrop above a stream called the Zlaști. The rock was a natural defence long before anyone laid a stone here. The builders followed the line of the rock, so the walls bend and turn instead of forming a neat square. Look at the tall stone pillars that hold up this bridge. They lift you above the water, much as the castle lifts itself above the town.

The castle is tied to one family above all others — the Hunyadi. In the early fifteenth century, the estate here was granted to a nobleman named Voicu, whose roots were in Wallachia, the region to the south. His son became one of the most famous soldiers of his age. Romanians call him Iancu de Hunedoara. Hungarians know him as Hunyadi János. In English, he is often called John Hunyadi.

John Hunyadi rose to become a great military leader in the Kingdom of Hungary. At that time, Transylvania belonged to that kingdom. He spent much of his life fighting the expanding Ottoman Empire. His best-known victory came in 1456, when he helped defend Belgrade against an Ottoman siege. He died weeks later, of plague, in the army camp. Much of the castle you see took its Gothic form during his lifetime, as he turned an older fortress into a noble residence.

Gothic, for anyone unfamiliar with the word, means the late medieval style of pointed arches, ribbed ceilings, and tall, narrow windows. You will see it everywhere here. But look closely and you will also notice later touches. Some windows and loggias are wider and calmer, in the style of the Renaissance. The castle grew in layers over several centuries, and each owner added something of their own taste.

Now, a detail most visitors walk past. Watch for a black raven holding a gold ring in its beak. It appears in carvings and on the family coat of arms. The raven gave Hunyadi's son, Matthias, his famous surname — Corvinus, from the Latin word for raven. That is where the castle's name comes from too. Matthias became King of Hungary and is still remembered as one of its greatest rulers.

There is a story behind that raven, and it is a legend, not a record. According to one popular tale, a raven snatched a royal ring and flew off with it. A young man of the family chased the bird, shot it, and returned the ring. The grateful king then granted the raven as a family symbol. Historians treat this as folklore. But stand under one of these carvings and it is easy to see why people loved the story.

Inside the castle, the most impressive room is usually the Knights' Hall. It is a long, cool space divided by a row of stone columns. Above them, slim ribs of stone spread out across the vaulted ceiling. Take a moment in the middle of the room. Notice how sound behaves here — footsteps and voices bounce off the stone and linger. This was a place for gatherings and feasts, built to impress anyone who came to visit.

Now for one of the castle's best-known tales, and again, treat it as legend. In the courtyard there is a deep well. Locals say it was dug by Turkish prisoners, who were promised their freedom once they reached water. The story says it took them many years. When they finally struck water, the promise was broken. An old inscription near the well is often linked to this tale. Scholars still debate what it truly says, so enjoy the story, but hold it lightly.

In the seventeenth century, the castle passed to new owners. Among them was Gabriel Bethlen, a Prince of Transylvania. During this period, more residential rooms were added, and the castle became more of a palace and less of a fortress. Look for the softer architecture in some of these later wings. The walls are still thick, but the windows open more generously to the light and the hills beyond.

The castle you see today is also the result of long restoration. Fire and neglect damaged parts of the building over the centuries. Restorers in the nineteenth century and later worked to rebuild it. Some of their choices were romantic rather than strictly accurate — a few roofs and towers may look more fairy-tale than they did in the Middle Ages. That is worth knowing. What you see is partly medieval, and partly an idea of the Middle Ages.

Here is a second surprise. Step out onto a terrace or look through a window toward the town. Hunedoara grew into a major centre of iron and steel production, especially in the twentieth century. In some directions, you may glimpse traces of that industrial past. A Gothic castle facing old factory land is a strange view, and an honest one. It shows how this valley has always been about iron, ore, and work, not only about knights.

The castle has also found a second life on screen. It was used as a filming location for the horror film The Nun, and its dark corridors suit that kind of story. But you do not need a film to feel the mood. Walk slowly through the narrow passages. Notice how the light changes from bright courtyard sun to the dim cool of the stairwells. Touch the worn stone of a doorframe, smoothed by many hands.

Before you leave, go back to the bridge, and stop halfway across. Turn around and look up at the walls one more time. The red roofs, the grey stone, the towers on the rock — this is the same view travellers have had for centuries as they approached. Listen again to the river below. It was here before the castle, and it will keep running long after you have walked on into the town.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pause midway on the bridge for the full view of the walls.
Wear shoes with good grip for worn stone stairs.
Visit early in the day to enjoy quieter halls.
Walk the outer path below for views up at the towers.
Allow at least an hour to explore the courtyards and halls.
