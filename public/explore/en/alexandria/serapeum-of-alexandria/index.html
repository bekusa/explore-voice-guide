The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The ground here is not flat. It rises in a low, rough hill of pale limestone, dusty and warm in the sun. Around the edges, you can hear Alexandria going about its day — car horns, a call from a nearby mosque, children somewhere beyond the wall. And in the middle of it all, one enormous column of red granite rises straight into the sky. It is the only thing still standing tall on a hill that once held one of the most famous temples of the ancient world.

This is the Serapeum of Alexandria. For centuries it was a temple to Serapis, a god who did not exist before the city did. Today it is an open archaeological site of broken stone, underground passages, scattered fragments, and that single great pillar. It can feel quiet, even empty, at first glance. But almost everything you see here comes from a long story of power, belief, and loss. So let us walk through it slowly.

First, the god himself. When Alexander the Great died, his general Ptolemy took control of Egypt and founded a new ruling dynasty. The Ptolemies were Greek-speaking rulers of an Egyptian land. They needed a god that both peoples could share. Serapis was the answer. He combined Osiris, the Egyptian lord of the dead, and Apis, the sacred bull of Memphis, with the look of a bearded Greek god like Zeus. It was religion and politics working together.

Now look at where you are standing. This hill sits in the old quarter the Greeks called Rhakotis. It was the Egyptian part of the city, older than Alexander's grand new streets near the harbour. Placing the temple of the shared god here was a careful choice. It sat on high ground, visible from far away, in the neighbourhood of the native Egyptians. The temple faced both communities at once — and that was exactly the point.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Archaeologists working on this site found foundation deposits — small plaques buried under the corners of the temple when it was built. These plaques name King Ptolemy the Third, and the inscriptions appear in both Greek and Egyptian. So the temple itself carried the same double message as its god. Two languages, two traditions, one building. Copies and originals of finds from this hill are now held in the city's museums.

Ancient writers describe the Serapeum as one of the grandest buildings in Alexandria. They speak of a raised platform, colonnades, and halls around a great inner sanctuary. Inside stood a large statue of Serapis. According to one popular ancient tale, the statue was so impressive that people believed the roof would fall if anyone dared to touch it. And some ancient sources say that the temple also held a collection of books — a kind of daughter library to the famous Library of Alexandria near the palace quarter.

Now turn to the column. Its shaft is a single piece of red granite, brought down the Nile from the quarries at Aswan, far to the south. It rises more than twenty-five metres, with a heavy Corinthian capital on top — that is the style with carved leaves, like a basket of acanthus. Stand at its base and look up. The polish has worn away in places, and the stone shows a speckled mix of pink, grey, and black crystals.

And here is the second surprise. This column is called Pompey's Pillar, but it has nothing to do with Pompey. The Roman general Pompey was killed in Egypt, and in the Middle Ages travellers came to believe his head was kept in a vessel on top of the column. That was legend, not history. The Greek inscription on the base tells a different story. It honours the Roman emperor Diocletian, and it was set up near the end of the third century, long after the temple was first built.

Walk around the pillar and you will find two sphinxes carved from granite. They are smaller than the great sphinx near Cairo, and their faces are worn. They sit low to the ground, calm and patient, as if they are still guarding something. Many of the stone fragments on this site came from different periods and were gathered here over time. So when you look at them, you are looking at pieces of a long history, not one single moment.

Then comes the end of the temple. In the late fourth century, the Roman Empire was turning firmly toward Christianity. Around the year 391, under the bishop Theophilus, the Serapeum was attacked and destroyed. The great statue of Serapis was broken. Ancient Christian and pagan writers left very different accounts of that day, and historians still debate the details. But the outcome is clear. The temple that had stood for centuries above Alexandria was gone, and the age of the old gods in this city was closing.

Before you leave, look for the entrances to the underground galleries. Long passages are cut into the rock beneath the hill, with small niches in the walls. Scholars are still not fully sure what all of them were used for. Some have suggested storage, some religious use, and some connect them with the temple's books. Step inside if they are open. The air feels cooler at once, and the sound of the street drops away almost completely.

It helps to know that this hill is part of a wider ancient landscape. Not far from here are the Catacombs of Kom el Shoqafa, a deep burial complex that mixes Egyptian, Greek, and Roman styles in its carvings. If you visit both, you will see the same blending of cultures that created Serapis himself. This part of Alexandria is the place where the city's mixed identity is easiest to see, carved directly into the stone.

So take a moment now. Stand back from the column, perhaps near the edge of the hill where you can see the rooftops around you. The apartment blocks, the satellite dishes, the washing on balconies — they press close to the old site. Modern Alexandria lives right up against its ancient past. Very little of the great temple remains, but the shape of the hill is the same one that Ptolemaic priests walked across.

As you walk away, look back once more. The red column will stay in view for a long time, above the streets and traffic. It has outlasted the temple, the god, the emperor it honoured, and even the true meaning of its own name. Notice how the light changes on the granite as you move. It is one stone, standing in the same place, while the city around it keeps changing.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven limestone ground.
Bring water, as shade on the hill is limited.
Combine your visit with the nearby Catacombs of Kom el Shoqafa.
Stand back near the hill's edge for a full column view.
