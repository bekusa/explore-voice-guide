The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beyond the walls, Alexandria keeps moving: car horns, the rattle of a tram somewhere nearby, a shopkeeper calling to a friend. Inside the gate, the sound drops away. You may smell incense, sweet and smoky, drifting from the doors. The stone underfoot is cool, even when the street outside is hot. This is Saint Mark's Coptic Orthodox Cathedral, and it stands on ground that Egypt's Christians have treated as sacred for a very long time.

First, a quick word on the name. Coptic comes from an old Greek word for Egyptian. The Copts are Egypt's ancient Christian community, one of the oldest Christian churches in the world. Their liturgy still uses some Coptic, the last stage of the language the pharaohs' scribes once wrote. So when you hear a chant here, you may be hearing words with roots that reach back to the age of the temples.

The story of this place begins with one man. According to Coptic tradition, Mark the Evangelist, the author of one of the four Gospels, brought Christianity to Alexandria in the first century. Tradition says his first convert was a shoemaker who had injured his hand. Mark healed him, and the shoemaker opened his home to the new faith. From that small beginning, the Church of Alexandria grew.

Alexandria at that time was one of the great cities of the Roman world. It was a harbour city, a city of scholars, and home to the famous Library. Greek, Jewish and Egyptian communities lived side by side. That mix made it a powerful centre for early Christian thought. Theologians trained here argued about questions that shaped Christianity across three continents. The bishops of this city carried enormous weight in the ancient church.

The tradition about Mark has a dark ending. Coptic accounts say he was seized by a hostile crowd in Alexandria and dragged through the streets until he died. For Copts, he is not only the founder of their church but also its first martyr. That idea of martyrdom runs deep here. The Coptic calendar even counts its years from the era of the martyrs, starting under the Roman emperor Diocletian, whose persecution hit Egypt hard.

Now for something many visitors do not know. In the ninth century, merchants from Venice took the body believed to be Mark's and carried it across the sea. Legend has it they hid it under pork, so that inspectors would not search the cargo too closely. That is why Venice has its own great Basilica of Saint Mark, and why the winged lion, the symbol of the Evangelist, appears all over that city.

But the story does not end in Venice. Coptic tradition holds that the head of Saint Mark remained here in Alexandria, and that it is kept in this cathedral. For believers, that makes this building a living link to the founder himself. In the twentieth century, the Vatican returned some relics of Saint Mark to the Coptic Church, a gesture of friendship between the two churches. Those relics went to the newer cathedral in Cairo.

That brings us to a second surprise. The head of the Coptic Church is called the Pope of Alexandria. The bishops of this city were among the very first Christian leaders to carry the title Pope, long before it became linked mainly with Rome. Today the Coptic Pope often lives and works in Cairo. Still, this cathedral remains the historic seat of his office. The title still says Alexandria for a reason.

The building in front of you is not the original church. Over the centuries, churches on this site were damaged, destroyed and rebuilt many times, through invasions, fires and changing rulers. What you see is a modern rebuilding. Yet its location matters more than the age of its walls. Each new church was built to continue an unbroken line of worship in the place most closely tied to Mark.

Step inside, if the doors are open, and let your eyes adjust. Coptic churches usually have a carved wooden screen called an iconostasis. It separates the sanctuary, where the priests celebrate the liturgy, from the space where the people stand. Icons line the screen: painted images of Christ, Mary, the apostles and saints. Look at the faces. Coptic icons often have large, calm eyes, and that gaze is meant to meet yours.

This cathedral also carries recent pain. On Palm Sunday in 2017, a suicide bomber attacked the entrance while the Pope was leading a service inside. He survived, but many people were killed, including police officers guarding the gate. The same day, another church in the city of Tanta was attacked. That is why you will pass through security here. The checks are part of how this community protects the place it loves.

If you are here during a service, you will see Coptic life up close. Families arrive together. Women may cover their hair. People kiss icons and light candles. The chanting can go on for a long time, and some worshippers stand through most of it. Many Copts have a small cross tattooed on the inside of the right wrist, a quiet sign of identity. You may notice it on the hands around you.

Be respectful of that rhythm. Speak softly. Ask before you photograph anyone, and avoid using a flash during prayers. If you are unsure where you may stand, wait near the back. Someone will often guide you, sometimes a deacon, sometimes an older woman who has been coming here all her life. Visitors are generally welcome, but this is a working church first and a sight second.

Before you leave, pause once more near the gate. Look back at the crosses above the entrance and at the people moving through the doors. Two thousand years of tradition rest on this small piece of city ground, wrapped by ordinary streets and traffic. When you step back outside, the noise of Alexandria will return. Let it. The life of this church has always continued in the middle of the city.
