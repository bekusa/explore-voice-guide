The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here, southwest of Alexandria, the wind moves across the low desert and carries fine grit that settles on your sleeves. The ground underfoot is pale and crumbly, scattered with broken limestone and pottery shards. There are no tall towers on the horizon. There is only a wide, flat plain, a few low walls, and a quiet that feels almost heavy. And yet, for several centuries, this was one of the busiest pilgrimage places in the Christian world.

This is Abu Mena, the city of Saint Menas. The name means Father Menas, a title of respect in Egyptian Christian tradition. Today you see foundations, column bases, and the outlines of rooms traced in stone. But try to picture it full of people. Pilgrims arrived from across the Mediterranean, some after weeks of travel by sea and by land. They came to pray at a tomb, to drink holy water, and to carry a piece of this place home.

Who was Menas? According to tradition, he was an Egyptian soldier who served in the Roman army in the late third or early fourth century. He refused to give up his Christian faith during a period of persecution, and he was executed for it. Much of his life story comes from later accounts written by believers, so it is best heard as sacred biography rather than strict history. What is certain is that his memory became very powerful here.

The most famous story is a legend. Locals and Coptic Christians tell it this way. When the saint's body was being carried into the desert on a camel, the animal stopped at this spot and refused to move any further. The people took this as a sign and buried him here. Later, according to the tale, miraculous healings began at the grave. Whether or not you take the camel literally, the story explains why a great shrine rose in such an empty place.

Now, here is something many visitors miss. Look closely at images of this saint, and you will often see him standing between two camels. That picture comes straight from the legend. It appears on thousands of small clay flasks made here. Pilgrims filled these little flasks with holy water or oil and carried them home. They are often called Menas ampullae, an old word for a small flask with two handles.

Those flasks travelled remarkably far. Archaeologists have found Menas flasks in many parts of Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East. Each one is a small piece of evidence of a long journey made by an ordinary person. Think about that as you stand here. A clay bottle shaped in this desert workshop might have ended up on a shelf in a distant port town. This plain was once connected to the whole Mediterranean.

The heart of the site is the great church complex. Over time, builders added a large basilica, a church built on the Roman hall plan, with a long central space and side aisles. Beneath part of it lay the crypt with the saint's tomb. Next to it stood a baptistery, the building where new Christians were baptised. Walk slowly and look for the curves and the right angles in the stone. These shapes show where columns stood and where walls once rose high above your head.

Around the churches, a whole town grew up to serve the visitors. There were guesthouses for pilgrims, public baths, workshops, streets, and market spaces. Excavations found the remains of kilns where the clay flasks were fired. So this was not only a holy place. It was also an economy. Food, water, lodging, and souvenirs all had to be provided for crowds arriving season after season, far from any river or large city.

The site flourished most strongly from around the fifth century into the sixth and seventh centuries. After that, its fortunes faded. Political change, shifting trade routes, and the slow pull of the desert all played a part. Over the following centuries, the buildings fell into ruin, and sand covered much of what remained. For a long time, the exact location of this famous shrine was largely forgotten by the outside world.

In the early twentieth century, a German archaeologist named Carl Maria Kaufmann led excavations that brought the site back into view. Since then, other teams have continued the work. In 1979, UNESCO added Abu Mena to the World Heritage List. That status recognises it as a place of value to all of humanity, not only to Egypt or to Christians. And yet, being listed did not keep the ruins safe from a very modern problem.

That problem was water. This may surprise you, here in the desert. Farming projects in the surrounding area brought irrigation, and the groundwater level rose beneath the site. The clay soil softened, and some structures began to crack and collapse. In 2001, UNESCO placed Abu Mena on its list of World Heritage in Danger. Workers even filled some underground spaces with sand to stop them caving in. Drainage and conservation work has since tried to lower the water and protect what survives.

The story of the saint did not end with the ruins. Not far from here stands the modern Monastery of Saint Mina, a working Coptic Orthodox monastery. The Coptic Church is the ancient Christian church of Egypt. Monks and pilgrims still come to this area to honour the same saint. So the place is not only an archaeological site. For many Egyptians, it is still a living centre of faith, with prayer and worship continuing nearby.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the low stone outlines around you, and at the open sky above them. Notice how quiet it is now, compared with the crowds who once filled these courtyards. As you walk back across the gritty ground, watch for small fragments of pottery at your feet, and leave them where they lie. They belong to this place, just as the old stories do.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check access rules in advance, as entry can be restricted.
Bring plenty of water and sun protection for open desert.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, crumbly ground.
Combine the trip with the nearby Monastery of Saint Mina.
Dress modestly if you plan to visit the working monastery.
