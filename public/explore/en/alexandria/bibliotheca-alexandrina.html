The guide

The story

Stand on the Corniche and listen for a moment. Behind you, traffic rushes along the seafront and car horns rise and fall. In front of you, the Mediterranean moves against the harbour wall, grey-blue and restless. Between those two sounds sits something strange: a huge tilted disc of glass and metal that seems to slide into the ground. It looks like a sun rising out of the earth, or perhaps setting into it. This is the Bibliotheca Alexandrina, Alexandria's modern library.

Before we walk closer, it helps to know what this building is answering. More than two thousand years ago, this city held the most famous library of the ancient world. It was founded in the third century before Christ under the Ptolemies, the Greek-speaking dynasty that ruled Egypt after Alexander the Great. Their royal quarter lay close to this stretch of shoreline. So when you look at the modern library, you are looking at a building that knows exactly whose shadow it stands in.

The ancient Library was more than a room full of scrolls. It belonged to a wider institution called the Mouseion, a place devoted to the Muses, the Greek goddesses of learning and the arts. Scholars lived and worked there with royal support. Among them was Eratosthenes, who estimated the circumference of the Earth with remarkable accuracy, using shadows and geometry. Scholars here also worked to collect, copy and correct the texts of Homer. This was, in a real sense, a research centre.

The ancient sources also give us some colourful stories. According to one account, ships that docked in Alexandria were searched for books. The books were copied, and the owners often received the copies while the originals stayed in the Library. Whether this happened as often as the story suggests, nobody can say for certain. But it shows how hungry the Ptolemies were for knowledge, and how far they were willing to go to gather it.

And then — the Library disappeared. Here is a fact that surprises many visitors: no one knows exactly how it ended. One popular version blames a fire during Julius Caesar's war in Alexandria in 48 BCE. Other versions point to later conflicts, or to slow decline as funding and scholars faded away. Many historians now believe it was not one dramatic night of flames, but a long, quiet loss over centuries.

The idea of reviving the Library grew in the late twentieth century. Egypt worked with UNESCO, the United Nations agency for education and culture, to make it happen. An international architecture competition followed, and the winner was Snøhetta, a young firm from Norway. The new library opened in 2002. So the building in front of you is, by the standards of this city, very young. Alexandria counts its history in millennia.

Now look at the shape again. The main roof is a vast circle, tilted toward the sea. Part of it rises high above the street, and part of it sinks below ground level, into a pool of water. The architects wanted the disc to suggest the sun, an ancient Egyptian symbol of light and renewal. The roof panels are angled so that northern light enters the reading room, while the harsh direct sun stays out.

Walk around to the curved outer wall, and look closely at the stone. This is grey granite from Aswan, in the far south of Egypt — the same region that gave stone to the pharaohs. Carved across its surface are letters, signs and characters from many of the world's writing systems. You may spot Arabic, Greek, Chinese, hieroglyphs, and scripts you have never seen before. Touch the cuts if you can. The wall is a kind of alphabet of humanity.

Inside, the main reading room is the heart of the building. It descends in a series of wide terraces, like a gentle staircase of desks and shelves. Light falls from the glass roof above and spreads across the open floors. There are very few interior walls, so you can see readers on many levels at once. The space holds thousands of seats. It feels less like a closed library and more like a calm, open landscape of study.

The library is not only for books. The complex also includes museums, exhibition halls, research centres and a conference area. One museum shows antiquities, including objects found during excavations nearby. Another focuses on manuscripts and rare books. Outside, you may notice a dark sphere on the plaza — that is the planetarium. Here is a second surprise: for years, this library hosted a copy of the Internet Archive, a digital record of the early web.

Think about that for a moment. The ancient Library tried to collect every important text in the known world. Its modern successor kept a backup of the internet itself. The tools changed from papyrus scrolls to servers, but the ambition stayed the same. The loss of the first library also shaped the thinking behind the second. Knowledge can disappear, so you protect it by copying it and sharing it.

As you move through the plaza, notice the people around you. Students carry laptops and folders. Families take photographs by the water. Groups of school children gather near the entrance. This is a working public institution, not a monument kept behind glass. For many young Alexandrians, it is simply the place where they come to study, meet friends, and attend lectures and concerts.

Before you leave, step back toward the sea and look at the whole building once more. The tilted disc faces the harbour where ancient ships once arrived with their cargo of grain, oil and books. Behind you, the city carries on with its noise and movement. As you walk away along the Corniche, keep that carved granite wall in mind... thousands of letters, from hundreds of cultures, all waiting to be read.
