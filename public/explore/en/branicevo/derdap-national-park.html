The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Danube moves almost without sound. It is wide, heavy and green-grey, and it slides between limestone walls that rise straight from the water. A cool breath comes off the river, even on a warm day. It carries the smell of wet stone and the sharp, resin-like scent of the forest above. Somewhere a boat engine hums, then fades. This is the edge of Đerdap, the place where Europe's second-longest river pushes its way through the mountains.

Đerdap is the largest national park in Serbia. It runs along the right bank of the Danube, and the river itself forms the border with Romania. On the far shore, the hills you see belong to another country. The park was declared in 1974. It covers a long strip of land that starts near the town of Golubac, here in the Braničevo District, and continues downstream for many kilometres. At its heart lies a gorge that travellers have feared, admired and written about for thousands of years.

In English, this gorge is known as the Iron Gates. The name is a good one. For most of history, this was the most dangerous stretch of the entire Danube. The river ran fast and shallow over hidden rocks. Whirlpools formed in the narrow places. Boatmen needed local pilots to guide them through. Ships were wrecked here, and cargo was lost. The gorge was a door between the plains of Central Europe and the Black Sea, but it was a door that did not open easily.

The Iron Gates is not one single canyon. It is a chain of gorges joined by wider valleys, where the river relaxes for a while before the cliffs close in again. The most dramatic section is called Kazan, which means cauldron. There, the river narrows sharply and becomes very deep. The rock walls stand close on both sides. If you take a boat through Kazan, you will feel the light change. The air cools, and the sky shrinks to a band above your head.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not realise. The river you are looking at is not the river the ancient travellers saw. In the 1960s, Yugoslavia and Romania built a large dam and hydroelectric power station downstream, near the town of Kladovo. It was completed in the early 1970s. The dam raised the water level dramatically. The old rapids disappeared. Islands, villages and stretches of road went under the surface. The calm, deep water in front of you is, in part, a modern lake.

That rising water nearly swallowed one of the most important archaeological sites in Europe. Its name is Lepenski Vir. In the 1960s, before the flooding, archaeologists excavated a settlement on a terrace above the river. They found the remains of people who lived here in the Mesolithic period, the Middle Stone Age, roughly nine thousand years ago and more. These were hunters, gatherers and fishermen. They chose this bend in the river because the Danube gave them food all year.

What they left behind is extraordinary. Their houses had floors in a trapezoid shape, wide at the front and narrow at the back, laid out with great care. And they carved stone sculptures. Some show faces with wide mouths and round eyes, half human and half fish. Nobody knows exactly what these figures meant. Because the water was going to cover the site, the whole settlement was lifted and moved to higher ground. Today a museum protects it, a short drive downstream within the park.

The Romans came to this gorge too, and they left their mark in the rock itself. The Emperor Trajan, who ruled at the start of the second century, wanted to conquer Dacia, a kingdom on the far bank in what is now Romania. To move his army, his engineers cut a road into the cliffs along the river. A stone inscription celebrates this work. It is known as Trajan's Plaque. When the dam raised the water, the plaque was cut out and moved higher up the cliff, so it is still visible from the river.

Trajan also ordered a great bridge across the Danube, further downstream near Kladovo. It was one of the longest bridges in the ancient world. Only fragments of its stone piers survive today. On the Romanian side of the gorge, you may notice a large face carved into the cliff. It looks ancient, but it is not. It is a modern sculpture of Decebalus, the last Dacian king, the man who fought against Trajan. So both sides of the old war now face each other across the water.

Back at the upper end of the park, near Golubac, stands the scene most people photograph first. Golubac Fortress climbs a steep rock right at the entrance to the gorge. Its towers step up the hillside, joined by thick walls, like a staircase made of stone. The fortress is medieval. Over the centuries, Serbian rulers, Hungarian kings and the Ottoman Empire all fought to hold it, because whoever held this rock controlled traffic on the river. After a long restoration, it opened again to visitors in 2019.

Now look past the stone and the history, up at the slopes. Here is the second surprise. These forests are some of the oldest kinds of woodland in Europe. During the last Ice Age, much of the continent was cold and bare. But the deep, sheltered gorge kept a milder climate. Some plant species survived here when they disappeared elsewhere. Scientists call them relict species, survivors from a much older world. Among them is the Turkish hazel, a tall tree with a thick, cork-like bark.

The park is also part of a wider protected landscape. In 2020, the Đerdap area was named a UNESCO Global Geopark. That title recognises places where the rocks themselves tell a long story. In these cliffs, you can see layers of limestone, sandstone and older rocks, folded and broken by the slow movement of the earth. Caves, viewpoints and walking trails let you see this up close. On a clear day, the high viewpoints above the gorge look down on the river as it bends.

Most visitors explore Đerdap in a mix of ways. Some follow the road along the river, stopping at the fortress, the viewpoints and the museum at Lepenski Vir. Others take a boat into the gorge, which is the best way to feel its scale. Hikers climb into the forest for the long views. Whatever you choose, give yourself time. Distances here are longer than they look on a map, and the road follows every curve of the water.

Before you move on, stand still for one more moment. Watch the surface of the Danube. Notice how it barely seems to move, though it carries the water of half a continent. Look at the far bank, at the line where the forest meets the rock. Stone Age fishermen watched this same river. Roman soldiers marched along these cliffs. Today it is your turn. As you walk away, listen again for that quiet, steady sound of moving water.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take a boat into Kazan gorge to feel its full scale.
Start at Golubac Fortress, then drive downstream along the river.
Allow a full day; the riverside road winds slowly.
Bring a light jacket; the gorge air stays cool.
Visit high viewpoints early for clearer views over the river.
