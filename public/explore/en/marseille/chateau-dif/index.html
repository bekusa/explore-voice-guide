The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes off the open sea and hums across the bare white rock. Gulls call above you. The limestone under your feet is pale and rough, warm when the sun is out, cold when it is not. Behind you, across a stretch of blue water, Marseille climbs its hills. In front of you stands a square stone fortress with round towers. It looks small from the ferry. Up close, it feels heavy and silent.

This is the Château d'If, on the smallest island of the Frioul archipelago, just off the coast of Marseille. The island is little more than a rock. There is almost no soil, very little shade, and no fresh spring. For centuries, that was the point. Anyone placed here was cut off from the city they could clearly see. The fortress is close enough to shore to watch the boats come and go, but far enough that escape was very hard.

The story of the castle begins with a rhinoceros. This sounds like a legend, but it is well recorded. In 1516, a ship carrying a live Indian rhinoceros stopped near Marseille. The animal was a gift from the King of Portugal to the Pope in Rome. King Francis the First of France came to see it. Few people in Europe had ever seen such a creature. The German artist Albrecht Dürer later made a famous woodcut of this same animal, based only on descriptions.

During that visit, the king noticed something important. This small island sat right at the entrance to the harbour of Marseille. A fortress here could guard the approach from the sea. In the 1520s, work began on the castle you see now. It was built as a defence against attack by sea, at a time when France and the Holy Roman Empire were rivals in the Mediterranean. The rhinoceros, sadly, never reached Rome. Its ship was lost in a storm off the Italian coast.

Here is the first surprise. The fortress was built for war, but it never really fought one. No great siege ever took place here. Its main enemy was boredom and isolation. So, over time, the castle found a different use. Its thick walls and island position made it an ideal prison. From the late sixteenth century onward, the Château d'If held people the state wanted to keep out of sight.

Many of those prisoners were held for their beliefs. After King Louis the Fourteenth revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, French Protestants lost their legal protection. The Edict had given them limited religious freedom. Large numbers of Protestants were arrested, and many passed through this fortress. Some were held here before being sent to serve as rowers on the royal galleys, the long oared warships based at Marseille. It was a brutal fate, and many did not survive it.

Not every prisoner suffered in the same way. Inside the castle, your money decided your comfort. Prisoners who could pay were often given rooms on the upper levels. These rooms could have a window, a fireplace, and sometimes better food. Poor prisoners were placed in the dark cells at ground level. These cells had little light and little air. As you walk the corridors, notice how the cells change from floor to floor. The difference between rich and poor is built into the stone.

Some well-known names spent time here. The young Count of Mirabeau, who later became a leading voice of the French Revolution, was held here in the 1770s at his own family's request. In 1871, Gaston Crémieux, a leader of a short-lived revolutionary government in Marseille, was imprisoned here. He was later executed in the city. Look for plaques and names on the cell doors as you go. Each one marks a real life lived inside these walls.

But the most famous prisoner of the Château d'If never existed at all. In 1844, the writer Alexandre Dumas published The Count of Monte Cristo. In the novel, a young sailor named Edmond Dantès is falsely accused and locked away in this very castle. Here he meets an old prisoner, the Abbé Faria, who teaches him and tells him about a hidden treasure. Dantès escapes by taking the place of a dead body sewn into a sack, which the guards throw into the sea.

This is the second surprise. Today, visitors can see cells labelled for Dantès and for the Abbé Faria. There is even a rough tunnel opening between them. None of it is historical. The cells were given these names because so many readers came asking for them. A work of fiction changed how a real prison is shown to the world. The castle opened to the public in 1890, and the novel has drawn people here ever since.

There is one more tale worth knowing. Some stories claim that the mysterious Man in the Iron Mask was held here. He was a real prisoner of Louis the Fourteenth, whose identity was kept secret. But historians place him on other islands and in other prisons, not on If. Treat this one as legend. It shows how a place like this attracts stories. A lonely fortress on a rock is the perfect setting for secrets.

Now walk up to the terraces and the roof. From here, the view opens wide. You can see the old port of Marseille and the basilica of Notre-Dame de la Garde on its hill, topped by a golden statue. To one side lie the larger Frioul islands, Ratonneau and Pomègues, linked by a sea wall. The water around you changes colour with the light, from deep blue to pale green over the rocks. On a clear day, every detail of the city seems close enough to touch.

That closeness is the quiet cruelty of this place. For a prisoner, the city was always there. They could see the hills, the ships, perhaps even the lights at night. Yet the city may as well have been on another continent. Take a moment to stand still and look back toward the shore. Feel the wind, hear the waves hit the rocks below. Then, as you walk back down to the ferry, notice how small the island really is.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the ferry from Marseille's Old Port before you go.
Strong mistral winds can cancel boat crossings.
Bring water and a hat; the rock has little shade.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone steps and terraces.
Combine your trip with a walk on the Frioul islands.
