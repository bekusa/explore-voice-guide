The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is almost nothing to hear. A thin wind moves across the grass, a stream runs somewhere out of sight, and a marmot whistles once, sharp and high. The air feels light in your chest. Every breath seems to hold a little less than you expect. Around you, the land rolls away in soft green waves, and the sky sits very close overhead. You are standing on the Deosai Plains, one of the highest plateaus on Earth.

This open country lies in Gilgit-Baltistan, in the far north of Pakistan, between the Karakoram range and the western end of the Himalaya. The plateau sits at an average height of around four thousand metres above the sea. That is higher than most mountain summits in Europe. Yet here, the ground is not jagged or steep. It is wide, gentle, and covered in grass. That contrast surprises many visitors. They climb for hours from Skardu, expecting cliffs, and arrive on something closer to a rolling meadow.

The name carries a story. Locals often say that Deosai means "the land of giants" — a place where powerful spirits once lived. This is folklore, not history, but it is easy to understand. Standing here, the scale feels wrong for human beings. The hills look small until you try to walk to one. Distances stretch. A ridge that seems close can take an hour to reach. Shepherds and travellers have long treated this plateau with caution, and that respect still makes sense today.

For much of the year, Deosai is not green at all. Heavy snow covers the plateau through the long winter, and the roads close. Only in the short summer does the snow melt back. Then the ground fills with water. Streams braid across the grass, small lakes appear in hollows, and the soil becomes soft and spongy underfoot. Life here works on a tight schedule. Plants, animals, and herders all have just a few warm months before the cold returns.

In high summer, the plateau changes colour. Wildflowers open across the meadows in patches of yellow, purple, pink, and white. Some stretches look almost painted. Look closely at your feet, not only at the horizon. Many of these flowers are tiny, low to the ground, built to survive wind and frost. Bees and butterflies move between them in the brief warmth. Late in the day, when the sun drops low, the flowers catch the side light and the whole plain seems to glow.

Pakistan declared Deosai a national park in 1993. The main reason was one animal — the Himalayan brown bear. This large, heavy bear is rare across its range, and at that time its numbers on the plateau had fallen very low. Hunting and disturbance had pushed it close to disappearing from this place. Protection gave the bears a chance. Since then, conservation work has helped the population recover, and Deosai is now one of the most important strongholds for this bear anywhere.

Here is something most visitors miss. These bears spend much of their time eating plants and roots, and they also dig for food. If you see patches of turned earth, or deep holes torn into the turf, a bear may have been searching for marmots or roots. Seeing a bear is never guaranteed. They are shy, and the plateau is huge. But signs of their presence are often closer than people think. Always keep your distance, and never leave food behind.

The marmot is the animal you will almost certainly meet. Golden-brown and round, marmots sit upright beside their burrows like small guards. When they sense danger, they give a loud whistle and dive underground. Their colonies spread across the grass, and they are a key food for bears, foxes, and birds of prey. Above you, watch the sky. Large raptors ride the wind here, including golden eagles and the bearded vulture, a huge bird with long, narrow wings.

On the western side of the plateau lies Sheosar Lake. It is a wide, cold sheet of water set among low hills, and on calm days it holds a clear reflection of the sky. The surface can be deep blue in the morning and grey under passing cloud. Wind picks up quickly here, and the water roughens in minutes. Many travellers stop at the shore simply to sit and look. There are no buildings, no shops, only grass, stones, and water.

Near the centre of the park, a river crosses the plain at a place called Bara Pani, which means "big water". A bridge carries the track over the stream, and campers often stay nearby. The river runs fast and very cold, fed by melting snow. Trout live in these waters. In the evening, you may smell woodsmoke or diesel from camps and jeeps — small human traces in a very large, very empty space.

The weather on Deosai deserves real respect. At this height, the sun is strong, even when the air feels cool. Clouds can build quickly, and rain, hail, or even summer snow may arrive with little warning. Temperatures drop sharply after dark. Visitors who come only in light clothing often regret it. The thin air also matters. Walk slowly, drink water, and give your body time. Headaches and breathlessness are common signs that you need to rest.

The journey itself is part of the experience. From Skardu, the road climbs past Satpara Lake and up through rocky valleys before it opens onto the plateau. Tracks are rough, and most people travel in four-wheel-drive jeeps with local drivers. Those drivers know the routes, the river crossings, and the weather. Their knowledge keeps travellers safe. For many families in the Skardu area, the short summer season on Deosai is also an important source of work.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Turn slowly in a full circle. Notice how few straight lines there are — no roads you cannot see, no walls, no towers. Only the curve of hills, the shine of water, and the long shadows of clouds moving over grass. Listen again for the marmot's whistle, and for the wind. As you head back down toward Skardu, the air will thicken and the plateau will drop out of sight. But that quiet will stay with you for a while.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring warm layers even on sunny summer days.
Hire a local jeep driver who knows the tracks.
Walk slowly and drink water to handle the altitude.
Carry all rubbish out; never leave food behind.
Use sunscreen and sunglasses against strong high-altitude sun.
