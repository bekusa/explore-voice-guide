The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. It is warm, reddish sandstone, worn smooth by centuries of bare feet. Around you, low stone buildings rise from the village lanes, some fenced inside neat lawns, others pressed right against houses and cattle sheds. A goat may wander past a carved doorway. Somewhere a pressure cooker whistles. This is Aihole, a quiet village in the Bagalkot district of northern Karnataka, and it holds more than a hundred old temples within a short walk of each other.

Why so many, in such a small place? The answer is the Chalukyas. The Early Chalukya kings ruled much of the Deccan, the high plateau of central and southern India, from roughly the sixth to the eighth century. Their capital was nearby at Badami. Aihole seems to have served as a kind of workshop, a place where builders tried out ideas in stone. Historians often call it a cradle of Indian temple architecture. Walking here, you can see experiments side by side.

Before the stone, there is a story. Locals say that the mythical warrior-sage Parashurama came to the nearby Malaprabha river after a great slaughter. He washed his bloodied axe in the water, and the river turned red. A village woman saw it and cried out, "Ayyo, hole!" which means roughly "Oh no, the river!" in Kannada, the local language. Legend has it the village took its name from that cry. It is folklore, but people here still tell it with a grin.

The village has carried other names too. Old inscriptions call it Ayyavole and Aryapura. In the medieval period, a powerful merchant guild took the name of this place. Records refer to it as the Ayyavole Five Hundred. These traders moved goods across southern India and beyond, and their name travelled with them. So a dusty village you might pass in minutes once gave its name to one of the great commercial networks of the region.

Now turn to the building most visitors come to see first, the Durga temple. Look at its shape. Instead of a simple square, the back end is rounded, like a horseshoe. Scholars call this an apsidal plan, and some compare its curve to the back of an elephant. A covered walkway with carved pillars runs all the way around the outside. You can walk this gallery in a full circle, with the sanctuary always on one side.

Here is the first surprise. Despite its name, the Durga temple was probably never dedicated to the goddess Durga. Many historians think the name comes from "durg", a word for fort, because the temple stood close to old fortifications. It became the Durga temple by association, not by worship. The sculptures around the walkway show several Hindu gods, so take your time and look closely at each panel as you circle.

A few steps away stands the Lad Khan temple, and it looks almost nothing like a temple at all. It is low and broad, with a flat, gently sloping roof of stone slabs. Inside, heavy square pillars hold up the ceiling. To many eyes it resembles a village meeting hall more than a shrine. That may be exactly the point. Some scholars think the builders were adapting the familiar form of a wooden assembly hall into stone.

The second surprise lies in the name. Lad Khan was not a king or a builder. According to the commonly told account, he was a man who made the old temple his home much later, long after its original worship had faded. His name simply stuck. It is a small reminder that these buildings did not freeze in time. People lived in them, sheltered in them, and reused them over many generations.

Look at the stone walls and towers across the site and you may notice two different ideas of how a temple should rise. Some towers curve upward in a smooth, beehive-like shape. This is the northern style, called Nagara. Others climb in stepped tiers, like a small pyramid. This is the southern style, called Dravida. At Aihole, builders used both, sometimes very near each other. Few places in India show the two traditions growing up together so clearly.

For the most important words ever carved in Aihole, climb the small hill to the Meguti temple. It is a Jain temple, built for the followers of Jainism, an ancient Indian faith devoted to non-violence. On its wall is a famous Sanskrit inscription composed by a poet named Ravikirti. It praises the Chalukya king Pulakeshin the Second and records his victory over the northern emperor Harsha. The inscription is dated to 634 of the common era.

The Meguti inscription matters for another reason. In it, Ravikirti compares his own skill to two great poets, Kalidasa and Bharavi. That passing mention helps scholars date those earlier writers, because Kalidasa must have been famous by then. A single proud line on a temple wall became a key clue for the history of Indian literature. From the hilltop, you can also see the whole village spread below, temples and rooftops mixed together.

If you have time, look for the Ravana Phadi cave temple, cut directly into a rock face rather than built from blocks. It is dedicated to Shiva. Inside the cool, dim space, carved figures stand along the walls, and the stone still shows the marks of chisels. Near the Durga temple complex, a small archaeological museum holds sculptures and fragments gathered from around the site. Together, they help you picture how richly decorated these buildings once were.

Aihole is one corner of a triangle. Badami, the old Chalukya capital, lies nearby, with its cave temples and red cliffs. Pattadakal, a little further along the Malaprabha, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site where later and grander temples stand. Many visitors see all three together. Aihole is often the earliest chapter, where the ideas first take shape, and Pattadakal and Badami show where they led.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in one of the village lanes. Notice how the old stone sits beside painted walls, washing lines and tea stalls. Children may run between the pillars, and the light will slowly shift across the carvings as the day moves on. Run your hand along a weathered column if it is permitted. These stones have stood here for well over a thousand years, and today they are simply part of a living village.
