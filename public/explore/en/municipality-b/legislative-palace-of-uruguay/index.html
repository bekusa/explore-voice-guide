The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the steps for a moment and look up. The stone in front of you is pale and cool, streaked in places with soft greys and creams. Traffic moves around you in a slow loop, and the wind off the Río de la Plata carries the smell of the city. Above it all, a wide classical facade rises in columns and carved figures. This is the Legislative Palace of Uruguay, the home of the country's parliament.

The building sits in Montevideo, at the end of a long avenue that runs toward the old centre of the capital. Planners placed it here on purpose. Walk a few steps back and you can see how the avenue frames the palace from a distance, like a stage set. It was meant to be seen from far away. It was meant to tell anyone arriving that this young republic took its laws seriously.

Inside, the palace houses the General Assembly. That means both chambers of the Uruguayan parliament: the Chamber of Senators and the Chamber of Representatives. This is not a museum frozen in the past. Laws are still debated and voted on here. On some days you may see politicians, staff and journalists moving through the halls. The ceremony of the building and the ordinary work of government share the same corridors.

Now for the first surprise. The man who drew the original design never saw it built. He was Vittorio Meano, an Italian architect who worked across the river in Argentina. Meano also designed the Congress building in Buenos Aires. In 1904, before construction here had properly begun, he was killed in his home in Buenos Aires. His death left two great parliaments in two capitals without their architect.

The work passed to another Italian, Gaetano Moretti. Moretti did not simply copy Meano's plans. He reshaped many details and brought his own taste for rich decoration. So the palace you see today is a kind of conversation between two architects... one who imagined it, and one who finished it. Construction stretched across the early decades of the twentieth century. It was a long, slow project, as grand buildings usually are.

The opening date was chosen with care. The palace was inaugurated on 25 August 1925. That day marked exactly one hundred years since the Declaration of Independence of 1825, a founding moment for Uruguay. Uruguayans still celebrate the twenty-fifth of August as a national holiday. So the building is not only a parliament. It is also a centenary monument, a gift the nation gave itself on its hundredth birthday.

Look again at the stone. Here is the second surprise. Much of the marble and granite in this palace was quarried in Uruguay itself. The builders wanted a national building made from national material. Inside, you will find dozens of different stones, in shades of rose, green, black, white and honey. Each one came from the ground of the country. In a way, the palace is a geological map of Uruguay, laid out in walls and floors.

The style is neoclassical. That means it borrows the language of ancient Greece and Rome: columns, triangular pediments, symmetry, and carved figures standing for ideas. Republics around the world chose this style in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. They wanted to link their new governments to the old democracies of the Mediterranean. Here, that tradition meets South American ambition, and the result feels both familiar and local.

Many of the sculptures on and inside the building are by the Italian artist Giannino Castiglioni. His figures stand for themes like law, labour, justice and the nation. As you walk around the exterior, take your time with them. Some are calm and dignified. Others feel full of movement, with flowing robes and strong arms. Together they turn the facade into a long carved story about what a republic is supposed to be.

If you join a guided tour, the most famous room is the Hall of Lost Steps. The name comes from a European tradition. In many parliaments, this is the great hall where people wait, walk and talk outside the chambers. Here it is long and tall, with polished stone, gilded details and coloured glass overhead. Light falls across the floor in soft bands. Your footsteps echo, and voices carry further than you expect.

This hall also has a solemn role. On important occasions, the bodies of notable public figures have lain in state here, so that citizens could come and pay their respects. Imagine the room quiet and full of people moving slowly past. The same space that hosts tours and receptions has also held moments of national grief. That double life is part of what makes the palace feel like a living building.

The palace is also recognised as a National Historic Monument of Uruguay. That status protects it, but the building is not closed off behind glass. Tours take visitors through the main halls, and sometimes into the chambers themselves, when parliament is not sitting. Outside, the gardens and wide plaza give you room to circle the whole structure. Each side shows a slightly different face, with new carvings and new angles of light.

Before you leave, walk out a little way along the avenue and turn around. From here, the palace sits at the end of the long straight line, pale against the sky. Notice how the columns catch the light, and how small the people on the steps look beside them. Listen to the traffic, the birds, the voices of the city going about its day. A century after it opened, the building is still doing its quiet work... holding a nation's laws in stone.
