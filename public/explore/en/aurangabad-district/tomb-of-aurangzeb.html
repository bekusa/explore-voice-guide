The guide

The story

Take off your shoes, and feel the stone under your bare feet. It is cool in the shade and warm where the sun has reached it. Around you there are low arches, whitewashed walls, and the smell of incense and rose petals. Somewhere nearby a pigeon settles on a ledge. This is a shrine before it is a monument, and the quiet here is the quiet of people who come to pray.

You are in Khuldabad, a small town in the hills of the Aurangabad district in Maharashtra, in western India. The town sits a short drive from the Ellora caves. For centuries it has been known as a valley of saints, because many Sufi teachers lived and were buried here. Sufis are the mystics of Islam, teachers who seek a direct, personal closeness to God. And among their resting places lies one of the most powerful emperors India has ever known.

Aurangzeb ruled the Mughal Empire for close to half a century, from the middle of the seventeenth century into the early eighteenth. Under him, the empire reached its greatest size, stretching across most of the subcontinent. He spent the last decades of his life far from Delhi, here in the Deccan, the high plateau of central and southern India. He fought long, exhausting campaigns against the Marathas and the southern sultanates. He died in 1707, an old man, still on campaign.

Now look at the grave itself. After the domes of the Taj Mahal, built by his father Shah Jahan, this may surprise you. There is no great dome here. There is no marble mausoleum. There is a simple grave of bare earth, open to the sky. This was not an accident, and it was not neglect. It was what the emperor himself asked for.

Aurangzeb was known for his strict and personal religious practice. He asked to be buried simply, near the shrine of a saint he deeply respected. According to a well-known tradition, he paid for his own burial with money he earned by stitching prayer caps by hand. Accounts also say he copied the Quran in his own handwriting. How much of this story is exact detail and how much is pious memory is hard to say. But the simplicity in front of you is real.

The saint whose shrine shelters the grave is Shaikh Zainuddin Shirazi, a Sufi of the fourteenth century. Aurangzeb regarded him as a spiritual guide, even though the two men lived centuries apart. So the emperor chose to lie at the feet of a teacher, inside a teacher's courtyard. Think about that for a moment. The ruler of millions placed himself as a guest in someone else's sacred space.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look closely at the soil on the grave. Small green plants are often growing there. Keeping living greenery on a grave is a custom with deep roots in Muslim tradition, and caretakers tend it with care. The emperor who once commanded vast armies now rests under a small patch of herbs, watered by hand.

Now look at the white marble screen around the grave. This lattice was not part of the original wish. It was added in the early twentieth century, during the time of Lord Curzon, the British Viceroy of India. Curzon took a strong interest in preserving India's historic monuments. So the one decorative element at this famous grave came from a foreign administrator, about two centuries after the burial. That is the second surprise of this place.

It helps to know what came after. The name of this town, Khuldabad, is often linked to the emperor's posthumous title, Khuld-makan. That phrase is usually translated as one whose home is eternity. Before this, the town was widely known as Rauza, meaning a garden or a tomb. Today the old name still appears in local speech, and the shrine area remains a place of daily prayer.

If you have visited the city of Aurangabad, you may have seen Bibi Ka Maqbara. It is a white domed tomb that many people compare to the Taj Mahal. It was built for Aurangzeb's wife, Dilras Banu Begum, by their son Azam Shah. Hold the two places in your mind together. A grand mausoleum for the empress in the city, and a plain earth grave for the emperor in the hills. The contrast tells you a great deal about the man's own choices.

Aurangzeb remains one of the most debated figures in Indian history. Some people remember him as a devout and disciplined ruler. Many others remember the wars, the heavy taxes on non-Muslims, and the destruction of temples during his reign. These arguments are still alive in India today, and sometimes they become loud. You do not need to settle them here. But it is worth knowing that this small grave carries a large and contested memory.

As you move through the complex, take in the ordinary life around you. There are pilgrims tying threads, offering flowers, and sitting quietly by the shrines. Caretakers sweep the stone. Families walk between the arches and talk in low voices. Many come for the saints of Khuldabad, not for the emperor. Respectful dress, a covered head, and bare feet are part of visiting, and they help you feel the rhythm of the place.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the screen. Look at the open sky above the grave, and the plain earth below it. Notice the sound of your own feet on the stone, and the scent of the flowers in the air. When you walk back out into the street, past the stalls and the town traffic, carry that contrast with you. An empire once stretched from here to the far edges of India. And at its centre, in the end, lies a simple patch of ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Remove your shoes before entering the shrine courtyard.
Dress modestly and carry a scarf to cover your head.
Combine the visit with the nearby Ellora caves.
Visit Bibi Ka Maqbara in Aurangabad for a striking contrast.
Keep voices low; this is an active place of prayer.
