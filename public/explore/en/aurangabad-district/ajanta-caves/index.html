The guide

The story

Listen first. Water drips somewhere in the dark, and bare feet slap softly on cool stone. The air inside the rock smells of damp earth and old plaster. Outside, the cliff curves away from you like the inside of a cupped hand. A thin river runs far below. You are standing on a ledge cut by human hands, in front of doorways that open straight into a basalt wall. This is Ajanta, and almost everything around you was carved, not built.

The shape of the gorge matters. The Waghur River bends here in a deep horseshoe, and the caves follow that curve along the cliff face. There are around thirty of them, cut side by side into dark volcanic basalt. Some are simple rooms. Others are vast halls with rows of pillars and high, ribbed ceilings. Walk from one end to the other and you move through several centuries of work. The caves are numbered by position along the path, not by age.

That numbering hides a surprise. The oldest caves sit near the middle of the curve, not at the start. Monks began cutting here in the second century before the Common Era, perhaps a little earlier. Then, for reasons historians still debate, the work stopped. For several hundred years, the cliff was largely quiet. A second great burst of carving came in the fifth century of the Common Era. Most of the painted caves you will see today belong to that later phase.

That second phase is linked to the Vakataka dynasty, a royal house that ruled much of this region in the fifth century. Inscriptions in the caves name ministers and patrons connected to the court of King Harishena. Some scholars argue that the later caves were cut in a remarkably short time, perhaps within a few decades. If so, this cliff was once loud with chisels, ladders, and lamp smoke, and full of painters working shoulder to shoulder in the half-dark.

All of this was made for Buddhist monks. The caves come in two main types. A chaitya is a prayer hall, long and tall, with a rounded end that holds a stupa. A stupa is a dome-shaped shrine, a symbol of the Buddha's presence. A vihara is a monastery, a square hall with small cells cut into the walls where monks slept on stone beds. As you walk, try to spot which kind you are in. The ceiling usually tells you.

In the prayer halls, look up. The ceilings are carved into ribs that curve like the inside of a wooden roof. But there is no wood. The stone copies timber beams that builders once used in freestanding halls, now long gone. In the oldest halls, real wooden ribs were once fitted into the rock, and some traces have been noted there. It is a strange idea... a building carved to look like another building.

Now the paintings. These are the reason many people travel here. The artists did not paint on wet plaster, as in a true fresco. They laid layers of clay, mixed with plant fibre and other materials, over the rough rock. Then they smoothed it, coated it, and painted on the dry surface. Their colours came from the earth and minerals around them, such as red and yellow ochre, white, and black. A rare deep blue appears in places, made from a precious imported stone.

What do the walls show? Mostly stories. Many scenes come from the Jataka tales, stories of the Buddha's earlier lives, when he was born as a prince, a merchant, an elephant, or a monkey. In each life he shows kindness or sacrifice. But the painters filled these stories with their own world. You see palaces and markets, musicians and dancers, boats and horses, lovers and servants. The walls work almost like a window into court life fifteen centuries ago.

In Cave 1, one figure stops most visitors. It is a bodhisattva, an enlightened being who stays in the world to help others. He is usually identified as Padmapani, the one who holds the lotus. His head tilts slightly. His eyes look down, heavy-lidded and calm. He wears a tall crown, and a single blue lotus rests in his fingers. Stand still for a moment. The face seems to change as your eyes adjust to the dim light.

Here is a detail most people walk past. For centuries after the monks left, the forest grew back over the cliff. Then, in 1819, a British officer named John Smith came to the area on a tiger hunt. From the far side of the gorge, he saw the arch of a cave entrance through the trees. He climbed down, entered, and scratched his name and the date onto a pillar in Cave 10. That graffiti can still be seen today.

The news brought artists and copyists. In the nineteenth century, painters worked for years making copies of the walls to send to Europe. Sadly, many of these copies were later destroyed by fire in England, so the originals here became even more precious. Exposure, damp, bats, and well-meaning varnish have all harmed the paintings over time. Today conservators work carefully, and the light is kept low on purpose. That dimness is not neglect. It is protection.

Near the far end of the curve, Cave 26 holds a quieter kind of power. Along its left wall lies a huge carved figure of the Buddha, reclining on his side. This shows his final passing from the world, called the parinirvana. Below him, mourners grieve. Above him, celestial beings celebrate his release. The two moods sit together in the same stone. In 1983, the whole complex was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Before you leave, step back out onto the ledge. Look across the gorge to the opposite hillside, the side where that hunting party once stood. From there, the doorways look like a row of dark notches in the rock, almost invisible among the trees. Listen again for the water, the birds, the shuffle of other visitors. The monks who lived here heard much the same sounds. As you walk back along the path, notice how the cliff keeps its secrets... one doorway at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry a small torch with a soft beam for dim interiors.
Expect to remove shoes inside some caves, so wear slip-ons.
Never use flash photography near the fragile wall paintings.
Visit the viewpoint across the gorge for the full horseshoe view.
Come during the monsoon to see waterfalls at the gorge's head.
