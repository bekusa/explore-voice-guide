The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the river runs fast and cold through the valley. It is a steady rushing sound that never quite stops. Around you, forested slopes climb steeply on every side. The air carries the smell of damp stone, pine and wild grass. Underfoot, the ground is uneven, worn by centuries of footsteps. Ahead, a cluster of pale grey and rose-tinted stone buildings sits on a shelf of the mountainside. This is Dadivank.

The monastery stands in the Kalbajar District, in the mountains of the southern Caucasus. The setting matters as much as the buildings. Medieval monks often chose places like this, remote, high and hard to reach, as a deliberate choice. Distance from towns meant fewer distractions and more protection. As you look around, notice how the complex follows the slope. It is not one building. It is a small village of stone, with churches, chapels, a bell tower and the remains of living quarters.

The name carries a story. According to tradition, the monastery takes its name from Saint Dadi. He was said to be a disciple of the Apostle Thaddeus, one of the early Christian missionaries in the region. Legend has it that Dadi preached in these mountains in the first century and was buried here. For a long time this was simply a tale told by the faithful. Then, in the early 2000s, excavations beneath the altar of the main church uncovered a burial with human remains.

That discovery did not prove the legend. Bones in a grave cannot tell us a name. But it gave the old story new weight. For pilgrims, it confirmed what they already believed. For historians, it showed that this spot was treated as sacred very early. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The holiest point of the whole site may lie under your feet, beneath the floor of the church in front of you, rather than in anything you can see.

Now turn to the largest church, often called the Katoghike, meaning the main cathedral church. Its walls carry long inscriptions carved in the Armenian alphabet. One of them is the best known text at Dadivank. It records that the church was built in 1214 by a noblewoman named Arzu Khatun. She was the wife of a local prince, and she dedicated the church to the memory of her husband and her sons.

Pause on that for a moment. This is a monument built by a woman in grief. Her words are cut directly into the stone, so anyone standing here can still read her dedication eight centuries later. In the medieval world, noblewomen sometimes paid for churches. It was rarer for their names to be so clearly preserved. The careful rows of letters across the wall are not decoration. They are a mother speaking about her family across the centuries.

The architecture is simple and solid. The church follows a cross-shaped plan, with a central dome raised on a drum, a short cylinder of stone that lifts the roof toward the sky. The walls are made of carefully cut blocks, fitted tightly together. Look at the colours in the stone. In soft light, some blocks show warm pinks and creams. Others are grey or weathered almost to white. Lichen and rain have added their own patterns over time.

Here is the second surprise. The inside of the main church once held wall paintings, and some still survive. Frescoes are paintings made on wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall. Medieval frescoes rarely survive in this region, because damp, fire and neglect usually destroy them. At Dadivank, fragments of thirteenth century painting remain. Restoration work in the early 2000s cleaned and stabilised them. If you can see them, look for faded figures, halos and traces of deep red and blue.

Outside, near the main church, stand two carved stone crosses. In Armenian tradition they are called khachkars, which simply means cross-stones. They are dated to 1283, and they are among the finest examples of this art anywhere. Each slab shows a central cross surrounded by dense carving: interlaced knots, rosettes and plant-like patterns. The stone is cut so deeply that the shadows form part of the design. Step close and the detail looks almost like lace.

As you walk through the complex, you will pass smaller chapels and the remains of other buildings. Some were places of prayer. Others were where monks lived, ate, studied and stored food. Monasteries like this were also centres of learning. Manuscripts were copied here, and the community supported itself from the land around it. Imagine the sounds of daily life: bells, chanting, footsteps on stone, animals in the fields, and always the river below.

The twentieth and twenty-first centuries brought hard years to this valley. The region changed hands during conflicts between Armenia and Azerbaijan. Since late 2020, the Kalbajar District has been under Azerbaijani administration. The monastery's heritage is now a subject of strong and sometimes painful debate between the two countries. Scholars and officials disagree about how its history should be presented. As a visitor, you are standing in a place that many people, on more than one side, care about deeply.

Whatever the arguments, the stones themselves remain. The inscriptions are still on the walls. The cross-stones still stand in the open air. The dome still rises over the slope, as it has for eight hundred years. Wars, earthquakes and long periods of abandonment have all passed through this valley. Each left marks you can see, in patched walls, newer stone set into old, and places where carvings have worn smooth.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot facing the main church. Let your eyes move slowly from the ground to the dome. Notice the line of the inscription, the shadow cut into the cross-stones, the green of the hills behind. Listen again to the river. It was running here before the first monk arrived, and it will keep running after you walk back down the path. Take a last look... and carry that sound with you.
