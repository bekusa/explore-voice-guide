The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the hiss of the surf there is a lower sound, a deep roar that rolls out of the trees like distant thunder. That is a howler monkey, somewhere in the canopy above you. The air is thick and warm. It smells of salt, wet leaves and crushed fruit on the path. Underfoot, the trail turns from packed red earth to boardwalk to soft pale sand. Welcome to Tayrona, on the Caribbean coast of Colombia.

This park sits in the Magdalena Department, a short drive east of the old port city of Santa Marta. It became a national natural park in 1964. It protects a strip of coastline where the jungle runs straight down to the sea. There is almost no flat land here. The forest comes down the hills, stops at a wall of granite boulders, and then the water begins. That meeting of green, grey and blue is the reason people walk for hours to get here.

Look inland, if the clouds allow it. Behind these coastal hills rises the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. It is often described as the highest coastal mountain range on Earth. Its summits carry snow, yet they stand only a few dozen kilometres from these warm beaches. That steep climb from sea level to ice creates many climate zones in a small space. As a result, the region holds an unusual range of plants and animals, from dry scrub to cloud forest.

The park takes its name from the Tairona people. They were a pre-Hispanic culture who lived across these mountains and coasts for centuries before the Spanish arrived. They were skilled builders and goldsmiths. They laid stone terraces, stairways and paved paths through steep forest, and they connected their towns with stone roads. Their gold work, with its small figures and ornaments, is now held in museums across Colombia.

Here is something many visitors miss. The Tairona did not simply vanish. Their descendants still live in the Sierra Nevada today. The Kogi, Arhuaco, Wiwa and Kankuamo peoples consider these mountains and this coast sacred. For them, the land is a living body that needs care. Some places inside the park are sacred sites. That is why you may see signs asking visitors to stay out of certain areas. Those signs are not only about ecology. They are also about respect.

This connection has a practical result. The park closes to visitors for some weeks at certain times of the year. The closures allow the ecosystem to rest. They also give the Indigenous communities time for spiritual cleansing ceremonies on their ancestral land. The dates are announced in advance by the national parks authority, and they can change. So it is always worth checking before you travel here.

Now, the walk itself. Most people reach the beaches on foot, along a trail that starts near the main entrance. The path climbs over roots and rocks, crosses wooden walkways, and dips through shaded forest. It can be hot and muddy, especially after rain. Along the way, look down as well as up. Lines of leaf-cutter ants carry green fragments across the trail. Small crabs run into holes in the sand. Large iguanas rest on branches and sometimes on the rocks beside you.

The first big beaches you meet may look perfect for a swim. Be careful. At Arrecifes, the sea is open and powerful, with strong currents. Signs on the sand warn that people have drowned here. Many of these beaches are not safe for swimming at all. The calmer water lies further along the coast, in coves sheltered by rocks and reefs. A beach called La Piscina, which means the swimming pool, is named for exactly that calmer water.

Keep walking and you reach Cabo San Juan del Guía. This is the picture many people carry home from Tayrona. Two curved beaches sit side by side, divided by a rocky point. On top of the rocks stands a small thatched hut, with the sea on almost every side. Visitors camp here in tents and hammocks under the palms. In the evening the day hikers leave, and the beach becomes much quieter.

Notice the boulders. They are huge, rounded blocks of granite, smooth from centuries of wind and waves. Some sit alone in the water. Others pile up in groups that look almost placed by hand. Their grey surfaces turn warm gold in the late afternoon light. At high tide, the waves hit them with a heavy, hollow boom. These rocks shape the coves, break the currents and give each beach its own character.

For those with more energy, a steep trail climbs from the coast into the hills to an archaeological site known as Pueblito, also called Chairama. Here you can see Tairona stone terraces, foundations and paths among the trees. The climb is rough and slippery in places, with large rocks to scramble over. But it gives a real sense of how these people built a world on steep ground. Please note that the more famous Lost City, Ciudad Perdida, lies outside this park, deeper in the Sierra Nevada.

The forest keeps changing as you move through it. Near the entrance, the land can feel drier, with cactus and thorny scrub. Closer to the beaches, it becomes humid and green, with tall trees, vines and coconut palms leaning over the sand. Birds call constantly. In the mornings, you may hear monkeys crashing through the branches. In the heat of the day, the forest grows quieter, and the sound of the sea takes over.

Before you leave, stop for a moment. Look at the line where the trees end and the rocks begin. Feel the warm sand, or the cool spray if you are near the water. Remember that people have walked these coves for many centuries, and their descendants still care for these mountains today. As you head back along the trail, listen again for that deep roar in the canopy. It will follow you most of the way out.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check official announcements for seasonal park closures before travelling.
Start hiking early to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Swim only at sheltered beaches such as La Piscina.
Carry plenty of water, sunscreen and insect repellent.
Wear shoes with grip for muddy, rocky trail sections.
