The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the silence. Wind moves through the canyon. A raven calls somewhere above. Under your boots, loose volcanic gravel shifts and clicks. Then you step into the shade of a rock shelter, the air cools by a few degrees, and the wall above you fills with figures. Some are taller than a grown man. They are painted red and black, arms raised, staring out across the canyon as they have for thousands of years.

You are in the Sierra de San Francisco, a range of mountains in the heart of Baja California Sur. This is one of the driest corners of Mexico. It sits inside the El Vizcaíno Biosphere Reserve, a protected area of desert, lagoons and highlands. Deep canyons cut through the range, and their walls hold hundreds of painted rock shelters. Together they form one of the largest concentrations of prehistoric rock art in the Americas. UNESCO listed the site as World Heritage in 1993.

Archaeologists call this style the Great Mural tradition. The name fits. These are not small scratches or quick sketches. The painters worked on a grand scale, sometimes high on the ceilings of shelters, far above the floor. Researchers still debate how they reached those heights. Scaffolds of wood or palm are one theory. Ladders are another. Nobody knows for certain, and that open question stays with you as you look up.

Look closely at the human figures. Many stand straight, facing you, with both arms lifted above their heads. Some wear what may be headdresses. And here is a detail many visitors miss at first. Some figures are split down the middle, one half painted red and the other half black. The meaning of this division is unknown. It may have marked status, a ritual role, or an idea about two sides of a person. The painters did not leave an explanation.

The animals are just as striking. You will see deer, bighorn sheep, rabbits, birds and mountain lions. In some shelters there are marine creatures too — whales, fish, sea turtles. That is a surprise in a mountain range far from the shore. It tells you these people moved across the land, between the coasts and the high canyons, following seasons and food. Their world was wider than one valley.

Many animals and humans are pierced by arrows. Some scholars read these as hunting scenes. Others think they show ritual, magic, or conflict. Again, the honest answer is that we are interpreting from the outside. What is clear is the skill. The painters understood the shape of a running deer, the curve of a sheep's horn, the weight of a body in motion.

Who made these paintings? That question has no simple answer. When Spanish Jesuit missionaries reached this region in the eighteenth century, they met the Cochimí people. The missionaries asked about the paintings. According to their accounts, the Cochimí said the images were made long before, by a race of giants who had come from the north. That is a story, not history, but it shows that the paintings were already ancient to the people living here three centuries ago.

How old are they really? Dating rock art is difficult. The pigments are mostly minerals, such as iron oxides for the reds, and minerals cannot easily be dated. Studies using small traces of organic material suggest a long period of painting, stretching back several thousand years. Different shelters, and even different layers on one wall, may come from different eras. In places you can see newer figures painted over older ones.

The outside world took a long time to notice. For generations, the only people who knew these shelters well were local ranchers, who herded goats and cattle through the canyons. In the twentieth century, writers and researchers began to document the sites. Detailed recording work in the late twentieth century helped bring the Great Murals to scientific attention, and eventually to the UNESCO list.

That ranching history still shapes your visit today. You cannot simply wander in. Access to the painted sites is controlled, and visitors must go with a registered local guide. Many of these guides come from families who have lived in the sierra for generations. They know the trails, the water sources and the mules. A trip into the deeper canyons can take several days, riding and walking, camping under very clear desert skies.

If your time is short, some shelters are easier to reach. A few sites near the small village of San Francisco de la Sierra can be visited in a single outing. The longer journeys lead down into the canyons, where the larger shelters sit. Along the way the landscape changes. Cardón cacti, the giant columnar cactus of this desert, rise from the slopes. Small palm oases appear near water. The rock turns from grey to rust to deep brown.

The paintings survive because of this place. The dry air, the deep overhangs and the remote location have protected them. But they are fragile. Touching the wall transfers oil from your skin. Dust kicked up near the base settles on the pigment. That is why walkways and barriers exist in some shelters, and why guides ask you to keep your distance. The rules are not about bureaucracy. They are about keeping these images for the next thousand years.

So take a moment before you leave the shelter. Step back, out of the shade, and look at the wall as a whole. Notice how the figures sit inside the natural curve of the rock. Listen again to the wind in the canyon. The painters stood roughly where you are standing now, under the same sky, looking at the same stone. As you walk back along the trail, watch the cliffs around you. Every overhang you pass might hold a painted figure of its own.
