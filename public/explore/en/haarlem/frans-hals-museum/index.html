The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the entrance, the street noise of Haarlem drops away. You hear soft footsteps on old floors and the creak of a door. Through the windows, a small enclosed garden sits under a square of open sky. The light here is pale and even, the kind of northern light that painters in this city knew well. It is a quiet place. That fits the building's first purpose.

This is the Frans Hals Museum, and the building is older than the museum by about three centuries. It began in the early seventeenth century as an almshouse for elderly men. The Dutch call this kind of home a hofje: a group of rooms set around a private courtyard. Poor old men of Haarlem lived here, ate here, and spent their last years here. Today their rooms hold some of the most lively portraits of the Dutch Golden Age, the period of great trade and art in the seventeenth century.

The museum itself has a longer story. Haarlem first opened a city museum in the 1860s, inside the town hall. The collection grew, and the town hall became too small. In 1913 the paintings moved here, to the old almshouse on the street called Groot Heiligland. The choice was a good one. The rooms are small and human in scale. You stand close to the canvases, almost at conversation distance, which is exactly how many of these faces were meant to be seen.

So who was Frans Hals? He was born in Antwerp, in the south, around the early 1580s. As a small child he came north with his family. At that time many Protestant families fled the southern Netherlands during the war with Spain. Hals grew up in Haarlem and worked here for the rest of his long life. He died in 1666, well into his eighties. He is buried in the great church of Saint Bavo, a short walk from this door.

Now look at how he painted. Up close, many of his surfaces look almost unfinished. A cuff is a few fast strokes of white. A beard is a quick twist of the brush. Step back a few paces, and those strokes become lace, hair, and skin. This is the first surprise for many visitors. Hals worked fast and loose at a time when most painters smoothed every mark away. The effect is energy. His sitters seem to be in the middle of a movement, or about to speak.

The largest works in the collection are the group portraits of the city's civic guards. These were companies of citizens who trained with weapons and helped defend the town. Over time they also became social clubs, with large and famous banquets. The officers paid to be painted together. Hals had a difficult task here. Every man paid, so every man wanted to be clearly seen. Look at how he solves it: with turned heads, raised glasses, sashes in strong colours, and hands that point and gesture across the table.

In these banquet scenes, try to follow one figure's eyes, then another's. Hals builds a chain of glances across the canvas. One man looks out at you. His neighbour looks at someone else. The group feels like a real dinner, not a row of statues. Notice the colours of the sashes too. The bands of orange, white, and other colours marked rank and company. They also carry your eye from one side of the picture to the other.

Then comes a very different mood. Late in his life, Hals painted the governors of this almshouse, both the men and the women. These are the regents and the regentesses: the citizens who ran this institution. So these paintings hang today in the same building where those people once held their meetings. That is the second surprise. You may be standing a few steps from where the sitters themselves once walked.

These late portraits are dark and spare. The clothes are almost all black, with white collars. The brushwork is even freer than in the early work. Many later painters admired these blacks. Vincent van Gogh wrote with real enthusiasm about the many different blacks that Hals could paint. Look closely, and you will see black that is warm, black that is cool, and black that shines like silk. Edouard Manet and other painters of the nineteenth century also came to study Hals's free brush.

There is also a story about one of the men in the regents' portrait. Some viewers believe his hat sits at an odd angle and his face looks a little drunk. Others see only a tired old man. Nobody can prove either reading. It is a good example of how Hals leaves room for the viewer. He gives you a person, not a final judgement, and each visitor decides what they see.

The museum does not hold only Hals. You will also find the Haarlem painters who came before him and around him. Among them are Hendrick Goltzius and Cornelis van Haarlem. They belong to a style called Mannerism, which shows twisting, muscular figures and dramatic poses. Compare their smooth, polished bodies with Hals's quick, rough strokes. The contrast shows how much painting changed in this city within one or two generations. Look also for still lifes and landscapes by other local masters.

One more object surprises many people: a large eighteenth-century dollhouse. A wealthy woman from this region, Sara Rothé, had it made as a collector's piece, not as a child's toy. Its small rooms are filled with tiny furniture, porcelain, and household goods. Lean in close. It shows how a rich Dutch household looked and how it was organised, room by room. After the big civic guard canvases, the scale change is almost funny.

The museum also has a second location on the Grote Markt, the main square of Haarlem. There, in historic buildings beside Saint Bavo's church, it shows modern and contemporary art. Together, the two sites cover roughly four centuries of art in this city. If you have time, the walk between them is short and pleasant. It takes you past narrow brick streets, small shops, and the tall church tower that Hals himself saw every day.

Before you leave, return to the courtyard window for a moment. Look at the brick, the small windows, the green of the garden. Old men once sat in this light. Their governors once posed in black and white in these rooms. And the painter who recorded them was himself an old man, still working, still taking risks with his brush. As you step back out onto the street, notice the faces passing you. Hals would have found something in each one.
