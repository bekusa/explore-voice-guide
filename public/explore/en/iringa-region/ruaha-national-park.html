The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through the grass with a sound like paper being turned. Somewhere a francolin calls, harsh and insistent. The air smells of dust, warm bark and, faintly, of elephant. Under your feet the earth is red and fine, and it rises in small clouds behind every vehicle. This is Ruaha National Park, in the Iringa Region of southern Tanzania. It is a big, quiet, open place, and it rewards people who slow down and pay attention.

The park takes its name from the Great Ruaha River, which runs along its south-eastern edge. The name itself comes from the language of the Hehe people, who live in the highlands around Iringa. Their word for river is often given as luhava. So, in a sense, this is simply the park of the river. That fits. Almost everything here, from the movement of the herds to the routes the guides choose each morning, depends on where the water is and where it is not.

Ruaha became a national park in 1964, a few years after Tanzanian independence. Before that, the land had been part of a game reserve set up during the colonial era. Later, the park grew significantly when neighbouring wetland areas were added to it. Today it ranks among the largest national parks in Tanzania, and indeed in East Africa. Yet it receives only a fraction of the visitors who crowd the northern parks. Many people who come here say the same thing: the space feels almost empty of people.

Here is something many visitors miss. Ruaha sits where two great African landscapes meet. To the south lies miombo woodland — a dry forest of broad-leaved trees that covers much of southern Africa. To the north and east lies the acacia savanna typical of East Africa. Because the two zones overlap here, you can see animals that rarely share a single park. Both greater kudu and lesser kudu live here. So do sable and roan antelope, which are more usually associated with countries further south.

Now look at the trees. The baobabs are impossible to ignore. Their trunks are swollen and grey, and their branches look like roots pushed into the sky. In the dry season they stand leafless, and they seem almost sculpted. Look closely at the bark, low down. You may see deep gouges and ragged hollows. Those marks are made by elephants. In hard times they strip the bark and chew the soft, moist wood inside to get at the water it stores.

Elephants are central to the story of Ruaha. The park and the wider ecosystem around it have long been known for one of the larger elephant populations in Tanzania. Like many places in Africa, Ruaha suffered heavy losses to ivory poaching in past decades. Conservation work continues today. Still, a herd crossing a sand river at dusk is a common sight here. The calves walk close to their mothers, and the dust turns gold in the low sun.

And then there are the lions. The Ruaha landscape is widely regarded as one of the most important strongholds for lions left in Africa. Prides here can be large. Guides often find them resting in the shade of a riverbank or a thicket during the heat of the day. Leopards are present too, though they are much harder to spot. Cheetahs hunt on the more open plains. Ruaha is also one of the places where African wild dogs, among the most endangered large predators on the continent, can still be seen.

Let us return to the river, because it changes the whole character of the park through the year. In the rainy season the Great Ruaha flows wide and brown. In the long dry months, roughly from the middle of the year into the later months, parts of the river shrink to pools and bare sand. Researchers have linked some of this drying to heavy water use upstream, especially for irrigation. So the river carries a modern story as well as an ancient one.

Watch the dry riverbeds carefully. A surprising detail: elephants here dig for water. With their feet and trunks they scoop holes in the sand until water seeps up from below. Once they move on, other animals come to drink from the same holes. Baboons, impala and warthogs all use them. In this way, the elephants act almost like engineers for the whole community. A dry river in Ruaha is never truly empty. It is a road, a well and a meeting place.

Lift your eyes to the sky and the branches. Ruaha has hundreds of recorded bird species, and you do not need to be an expert to enjoy them. Look for the flash of a lilac-breasted roller, with its pale violet chest and bright blue wings. Listen for the loud, laughing calls of hornbills. Along the river, fish eagles perch high and throw back their heads to call. Their cry is one of the most recognisable sounds in Africa, and it carries a long way across the water.

The human story here is older than the park. The Hehe people of the Iringa highlands are remembered across Tanzania for their resistance to German colonial rule in the late nineteenth century, under their chief Mkwawa. That history belongs mostly to the hills around Iringa town rather than to the park itself. But it is worth knowing as you drive in. Long before the land was fenced on maps, people lived alongside these animals, hunted here and named this river.

Reaching Ruaha takes effort, and that is part of its character. The road from Iringa town is long and unpaved, and it can take several hours. Many visitors fly in on small aircraft instead, landing on a bush airstrip. Either way, the arrival feels like a crossing. The farms thin out. The hills soften into woodland. And then, quite suddenly, you are looking at a giraffe stripping leaves from an acacia, with no one else in sight.

The light here is worth noticing too. Early morning is cool and blue, and the animals are most active. By midday the colours flatten and the heat presses down, and many creatures retreat into the shade. Late afternoon brings the best hours. The grass turns the colour of straw and honey. Shadows stretch long behind every tree. Many guides plan their drives around these two windows, so it is worth being ready early and staying out late.

So, before you move on, take a moment. Look at the ground near you — the red dust, the tracks pressed into it, perhaps the round print of an elephant's foot. Look at the nearest tree and ask what has fed on it. Listen for the wind, the birds, the distant engine of another vehicle, or simply nothing at all. Ruaha is not a place of single dramatic moments. It is a place of patience. Whatever you notice next, as you drive on, was probably there all along.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan game drives for early morning and late afternoon.
Visit in the dry season for easier wildlife spotting.
Allow several hours for the unpaved road from Iringa.
Bring binoculars for birds along the river.
Carry dust protection for your camera and phone.
