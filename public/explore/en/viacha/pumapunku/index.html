The guide

The story

Listen first. On the high plain of the Bolivian Altiplano, the wind is often the loudest thing around you. It moves across short, dry grass and over the scattered stones at your feet. The air is thin and cold, even under a strong sun. You may feel your heartbeat a little faster than usual. That is the altitude. You are close to 3,800 metres above the sea, on open ground that is very old.

The place in front of you is called Pumapunku. In Aymara, the language still spoken across this plateau, the name is usually translated as the Gate of the Puma. It is one part of the larger ancient city of Tiwanaku. The site lies in the province around Viacha, west of La Paz, in the plain that runs toward Lake Titicaca. Together with the main Tiwanaku complex, it was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000.

At first glance, Pumapunku can look like chaos. Huge blocks lie tilted, broken and scattered, as if a giant had kicked them over. But look again. Under the rubble is a planned structure. It was a large stepped platform, a raised mound built in terraces and faced with stone. On its top stood courtyards, walls and doorways. Today most of that upper structure is gone, and the pieces lie around the base.

Who built it? The answer is the Tiwanaku people, one of the most important civilisations of the ancient Andes. They lived here long before the Inca empire. Tiwanaku grew into a major religious and political centre during the first millennium of the common era. Radiocarbon dates suggest that work on Pumapunku began around the sixth century. That makes this platform roughly 1,400 years old, perhaps more.

Now look at the stones themselves. You will see two main colours. The large, flat slabs are a reddish sandstone. The smaller, darker grey pieces are andesite, a hard volcanic rock. The sandstone was quarried a few kilometres away on the plain. Much of the andesite came from much farther, from volcanic sources near the shores of Lake Titicaca. Moving it here was a serious feat of labour and organisation.

The biggest sandstone slabs are enormous. The largest is estimated to weigh well over a hundred tonnes. There were no wheels in use here, no draft animals strong enough, and no iron tools. Archaeologists think teams of people dragged the blocks on ramps and sledges, perhaps with ropes made of plant fibre. Some researchers believe reed boats helped carry stone across the lake. The exact method is still debated.

Here is the detail that surprises most visitors. Find the grey andesite blocks shaped like the capital letter H. Walk around a few of them. Notice how similar they are. The inner corners, the flat faces, the right angles all repeat from block to block. Many researchers see this as a kind of standard design. The masons seem to have produced shapes to a shared pattern, so pieces could fit together in a modular way.

The second surprise is small and easy to miss. On some blocks, look for little channels shaped like a narrow letter I or a double T. These are sockets for metal clamps. The builders used clamps to hold neighbouring stones together, which helped the walls resist movement. Studies of the remaining metal suggest a copper alloy. Some clamps appear to have been poured as molten metal straight into the sockets. That means the builders brought a small foundry to the stones.

The precision here has inspired many wild theories. You may have heard claims about lost technologies or visitors from other worlds. Archaeologists do not accept these ideas. The evidence points to skilled Tiwanaku stonemasons. They worked with harder hammerstones, sand and water for grinding, and a great deal of time and patience. The real story is in some ways more impressive. Human hands, careful planning and generations of skill made this.

Why is the site so broken? Part of the answer is time. Earth, weather and centuries of neglect all left their mark. Part of the answer is people. After Tiwanaku declined, later communities reused the cut stone. Spanish chroniclers in the sixteenth century saw ruins here that were already damaged. In later centuries, stone from the wider Tiwanaku site was taken to build churches, houses and even a railway line. What you see is what remained.

Try to picture the platform when it was complete. Its terraces were faced with smooth stone. Bright surfaces and carved doorways likely caught the high-altitude light. Some scholars think metal sheets or coloured finishes once decorated parts of the walls. People may have come here for ceremonies, processions and gatherings. Pumapunku was not a fortress. It was most likely a sacred and public space, built to impress and to bring people together.

This connection to ritual has not fully ended. Every year around the June solstice, crowds gather at Tiwanaku for Willkakuti, the Aymara New Year. People arrive in the dark and wait in the cold for the first light of the sun. For many Aymara families, these ruins are not only an archaeological site. They are part of a living identity on the Altiplano. Please treat the stones with that respect, and do not climb on them.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the flat horizon and the distant mountains. Feel the wind again on your face. Then look down at one single H-shaped block near you. Somebody shaped those clean edges by hand, more than a thousand years ago, in this same thin, bright air. As you walk back toward the main Tiwanaku complex, notice how quiet the plain is... and how much of the story still lies under the grass.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk slowly and drink water — the altitude is near 3,800 metres.
Bring sunscreen, a hat and warm layers for strong sun and wind.
Visit the Tiwanaku site museums to see carved stones up close.
Hire a local guide to understand the layout of the platform.
Combine Pumapunku with the main Tiwanaku complex nearby.
