The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Below the walls, water moves slowly against the rocks. Above you, wind pushes across slate roofs and pale stone towers. The castle stands on a rocky spur over the Kwisa river, and from here the land drops away into green, folded country. This is Czocha Castle, in the quiet borderlands of Lower Silesia, in southwestern Poland. It looks like a castle from a storybook. And that is not entirely an accident.

Start with the setting, because the setting explains almost everything. This corner of Europe has changed hands many times. Over the centuries, the land beneath your feet belonged to Bohemian kings, to Saxon rulers, to Prussia, to Germany, and finally to Poland. The river below once marked a frontier. A fortress on a rock above a river crossing was a practical thing. It watched the road, it guarded the border, and it told travellers exactly who was in charge.

The first stronghold here goes back to the thirteenth century, when this region sat in the orbit of the Bohemian crown. Bohemia is the historic heart of today's Czech Republic. Very little of that earliest fortress is easy to see today. What you are looking at is the result of many hands, many owners, and many rebuildings. Medieval walls, later residential wings, and early twentieth century restoration all sit together in one silhouette.

Over the following centuries, the castle passed between noble families. Each one adapted it for their own needs and their own tastes. A fortress built for defence slowly became a residence built for comfort. Windows widened. Rooms grew warmer. Among the longest-lasting owners was the noble von Nostitz family, who held the estate for a large part of the early modern period. Fire, war, and neglect also left their marks between those generations of care.

Now for the first surprise. Much of the romantic, fairy-tale look you see today comes from the early twentieth century, not the Middle Ages. Around 1909, the castle was bought by Ernst Gütschow, a wealthy cigarette manufacturer from Dresden. He hired Bodo Ebhardt, a German architect who specialised in restoring castles. Ebhardt rebuilt the place with real knowledge of medieval building. But he also shaped it into an idea of what a castle should look like.

That makes Czocha a little like a stage set built on genuine foundations. The old stone is real. The rock is real. The strategic position is very real. But the towers, the roofline, and many interior spaces carry the confident vision of an early twentieth century owner and his architect. So when you admire the skyline, you are admiring two eras at once. One is the medieval fortress. The other is a modern dream of the Middle Ages.

Here is the second surprise. Gütschow's rebuilding included secret passages and hidden doors. Some are said to be concealed behind panelling and bookshelves, so that a section of wall can open into a narrow route between rooms. Visitors on guided tours are often shown how these hidden routes work. It is a playful detail, but it also tells you something about the owner. This was a man who wanted his castle to keep secrets.

And a castle with secrets attracts stories. Locals tell of ghosts in the corridors and tragic figures from the past. One well-known legend tells of a well in the courtyard where unfaithful wives were said to be punished. Another tale tells of a young girl who haunts the castle. These are legends, not documented history. But they are part of the place now, and guides here tell them with real enjoyment.

The Second World War brought a darker layer of rumour. Stories persist that the castle was used by German intelligence during the war, perhaps for training or for work on codes. Historians treat many of these claims with caution, and the details remain unclear. After 1945, the region became part of Poland. The castle suffered from looting in the chaotic years after the war, and much of its original furnishing was lost.

In the decades that followed, the Polish military used the castle, and it later served as a holiday and recreation centre. That long institutional chapter helped keep the buildings standing at a time when many other estates in the region fell into ruin. Today the castle operates as a hotel and a visitor attraction. People sleep in rooms where, a century ago, a Dresden industrialist planned his hidden doors.

The castle has had a busy second life on screen, too. It has appeared in Polish films and television productions, where its towers make an easy shortcut to mystery and adventure. Since the 2010s, it has also hosted an international live-action role-playing event called College of Wizardry. During these events, players from many countries arrive in robes and spend several days playing students at a school of magic. For a few days, the castle becomes the fantasy it was rebuilt to resemble.

As you walk, pay attention to the water. Below the castle, a dam on the Kwisa holds back a long reservoir, and the lake curves around the rocky hill. On a calm day, the surface reflects the trees and sometimes the castle itself. On a grey day, mist can lift off the water and soften the towers. Either way, the lake gives the castle a quieter, gentler frame than its military past might suggest.

Before you leave, stand back and look at the whole outline once more. Notice how the towers rise at different heights. Notice how the walls follow the shape of the rock rather than a neat plan. That uneven line is a record of centuries of building, repair, and reinvention. Listen again for the river, for the wind, for voices drifting from the courtyard. Then take one last look over your shoulder as you go. Fortress, home, rumour, film set — it is all still here, on the same rock above the same water.
