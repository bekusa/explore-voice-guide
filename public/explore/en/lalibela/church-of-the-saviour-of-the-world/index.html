The story

Step down into the wide rock courtyard and look up. Row after row of stone pillars surrounds a long, low building, like trees standing guard around a hall. This is Bete Medhane Alem, the Church of the Saviour of the World. It is the largest monolithic rock-hewn church on earth, and everything you see was carved from a single piece of mountain.

The numbers are hard to believe when you stand in front of it. The church is about 33 meters long, about 23 meters wide, and around 11 meters high. Yet it was never built. Workers cut a deep trench into the hillside, isolated one enormous block, and then carved that block, outside and inside, into a finished church.

Think about the method for a moment. There was no scaffolding to raise and no stone to lift. Every line had to be planned before the first cut, because a mistake could not be repaired. The carvers worked from the top down with hand tools, shaping the roof first, then the pillars, then the halls within. What they removed became the courtyard you are standing in.

The name Medhane Alem means Saviour of the World in Amharic. The church belongs to the northern group of the eleven rock churches of Lalibela. Tradition credits the whole complex to King Lalibela, a ruler of the Zagwe dynasty who reigned around the year 1200 and who, the stories say, wanted a new Jerusalem in the Lalibelan highlands.

Tradition adds one more layer here. It says this church was modeled on the original cathedral of Saint Mary of Zion in Aksum, the holiest church of early Lalibela. That cathedral was destroyed in the sixteenth century. If the tradition is true, the building in front of you is an echo of a vanished one, preserved in solid rock.

Walk slowly around the outside. A colonnade of square pillars circles the church on all four sides, carrying the wide overhang of the roof. Look closely and you will see that a few pillars differ slightly in color and texture. Some corners collapsed over the centuries, and those pillars were replaced during repairs in the twentieth century.

Now step inside, into cool air and low light. Rows of carved columns divide the space into five long aisles. The central aisle rises into a curved vault, while the side aisles stay low and shadowed. Your eyes need a minute to adjust. Then the room slowly appears around you, larger than any rock-cut space you expect.

This church guards one of Lalibela's most treasured objects, the gold processional cross known as the Lalibela Cross. It was stolen in 1997, smuggled abroad, and recovered in 1999. Today the priests keep it safely inside and bring it out to bless pilgrims. Believers say that its touch can heal. Whatever you believe, watching the blessing is a moving sight.

Come at dawn if you can. The liturgy here is sung in Ge'ez, an ancient language kept alive by the church. Drums beat slowly, and the chant rises and falls for hours. Worshippers wrapped in white lean on tall prayer sticks. Many walked long distances to be here. This building has held such mornings for some eight hundred years.

Above the church stretches a huge modern canopy on tall steel masts. The rock here is soft volcanic tuff, and rain slowly eats into it. The shelters were raised with international help to protect several of the churches while longer-term conservation is worked out. They change the view, but they buy the carvers' work more time.

From the courtyard, passages and trenches lead onward to Bet Maryam and the rest of the northern group. The churches connect through a maze of walkways cut in the rock, crossed by a channel named after the Jordan River. Pilgrims move through it barefoot, from church to church, as they have for centuries.

Before you move on, put your hand near the cool stone and think about what this place is. A mountain was hollowed into a room. Hands and chisels did all of it, guided by faith and patient planning. The church has no foundation stone, because it never needed one. It was always here, waiting inside the rock.
