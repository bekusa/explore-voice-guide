The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wind there is a low, steady hum from the motorway nearby, and under that, the dry crunch of gravel and broken stone beneath your shoes. The light here is wide and pale. The hills around you are low and brown, and the air in summer can smell of dust and wild thyme. You are standing in the ruins of a city that, for many centuries, was one of the most important places in this part of the Balkans.

This is Stobi. It sits a short drive from Kavadarci, at the meeting point of two rivers. One is the Vardar, which the ancient Greeks called the Axios. The other is the Crna Reka, the Black River, known in antiquity as the Erigon. That meeting point is the whole reason the city existed. Rivers meant water, fertile land and, above all, roads. Whoever held this spot could watch traffic moving between the Aegean coast and the interior of the Balkans.

The earliest settlement here is linked to the Paeonians, an ancient people of this region who lived north of the Macedonian kingdom. Over time, the area came under Macedonian control, and later under Rome. Under Roman rule, Stobi grew into a proper town with civic status, public buildings and its own coins. Think of it as a crossroads market town that slowly became a regional capital. The traders, soldiers and officials who passed through left their money, and their buildings, behind.

Here is the first surprise. Stobi was not a quiet provincial backwater at the end of the Roman age. It was the opposite. In late antiquity, around the fifth century, it became the capital of a Roman province called Macedonia Secunda, or Second Macedonia. Emperors and bishops paid attention to this place. The emperor Theodosius the First stayed here in the year 388, and laws issued during his visit carry the name of Stobi.

Let us walk toward the theatre, the structure most visitors remember. It was built into the slope in the Roman period, with curved rows of stone seats facing a stage. Look closely at some of the seats. On several of them, names were carved into the stone. These marked places reserved for particular people or families, a little like a season ticket. It is one of the most human details on the whole site, a reminder that real people argued over the best view.

The theatre changed with the times. In a later phase, it was adapted for a different kind of show. The lower area was altered so that it could host fights with wild animals and, possibly, gladiator contests. Theatre, in other words, gave way to spectacle. As you stand near the front rows, try to imagine the noise of a crowd here, and the very different sounds of plays and of beasts, separated by a few generations.

Now move toward the heart of the Christian city. Stobi had bishops from an early date. A bishop of Stobi is recorded at the Council of Nicaea in 325, the great meeting of church leaders that shaped early Christian belief. Over the following centuries, the city filled with churches. The largest is the Episcopal Basilica, the bishop's main church. Its floors were covered with mosaics, and some of the finest of these survive in and around its baptistery.

The baptistery is worth slowing down for. Baptism in this era usually meant stepping down into water, and the floor around the font was decorated with care. Look for peacocks and deer among the mosaic designs. In early Christian art, the peacock often stood for eternal life, and deer drinking water recalled a line from the Psalms about a soul longing for God. The tiny stones, called tesserae, were set one by one by hand.

The second surprise lies close by, and it is one of the reasons scholars from around the world know the name Stobi. Excavations uncovered the remains of a synagogue, a Jewish place of worship, beneath a later Christian church. A Greek inscription records that a man named Claudius Tiberius Polycharmos gave part of his own house for the synagogue's use. It is rare evidence of a Jewish community living, praying and building here inside a Roman city.

Walk on through the streets and you will pass the remains of large private houses. Several have been given names by archaeologists, such as the House of Peristeria and the House of Parthenius. These were the homes of wealthy families, built around open courtyards lined with columns. Look at the low walls and doorways. You can still trace where rooms began and ended, where water ran, and where guests would have been welcomed out of the summer heat.

The good years did not last forever. In 479, the Ostrogoths, a Germanic people led by Theodoric, attacked and plundered the city. Stobi recovered, but in 518 a powerful earthquake struck the region and caused heavy damage. Later centuries brought new migrations and a changing world. Slowly the city emptied. Soil and silt covered the streets, and the place passed out of memory for a long time.

Modern rediscovery began in the early twentieth century, and serious excavation has continued in many seasons since. Much of the city is still under the ground around you. That is worth remembering as you look out across the fields. The paths and walls you can see are only part of the story. Each new season of digging can add a room, a mosaic or an inscription to what we know.

Stobi also connects to the land around it. You are in the Tikveš region, known across North Macedonia for its vineyards and wine. The warm, dry climate that preserved these stones also ripens grapes on the surrounding slopes. A modern winery even takes its name from the ancient city. After the ruins, many visitors continue into the valley, where the same hills that fed Roman tables are still planted in long green rows.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the theatre or on a rise above the streets. Find the line where the two rivers meet. Feel the wind come across the open ground. The traders, bishops, families and soldiers of Stobi saw these same low hills. As you walk back toward the entrance, watch the ground under your feet. Every pale fragment of stone here was once part of someone's city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a hat and water; the site has little shade.
Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and uneven stone.
Combine your visit with a Tikveš winery tour nearby.
Allow at least two hours to walk the main areas.
