The guide

The story

Start with the steps. They climb the hillside in a long, steady line, and the dark basalt under your feet is often warm from the sun. Listen as you climb. You may hear birds in the scrub, a goat bell from the fields below, and the wind moving along the cliff. Then the rock face opens in front of you. A row of doorways and arches has been cut straight into the stone. This is Bhaja, and it was made by hand.

The caves sit on a low hill above the small village of Bhaje, near the hill town of Lonavala. Today the road between Mumbai and Pune runs close by. Long before that road, this was a trade route. Merchants climbed from ports on the Arabian Sea up onto the Deccan plateau, the high tableland of central India. Caravans needed rest and shelter on the way. Monasteries like this one grew along the route, and traders often supported them with gifts.

The people who carved these caves were Buddhist monks and their patrons. Scholars generally place the earliest work here around the second century BCE. That makes Bhaja one of the oldest groups of rock-cut caves in western India. The monks belonged to an early stage of Buddhism. In this period, the Buddha was usually not shown in human form. Instead, his presence was marked by symbols, and above all by the stupa — a rounded mound that holds or honours sacred relics.

It helps to picture how this place was made. Nothing here was built from blocks. Every room, column and arch was carved out of the living rock. The workers started high on the cliff and cut downward, removing stone as they went. There was no way to add material if a chisel slipped. Each mistake was permanent. So when you look at a straight wall or a smooth curved ceiling, you are looking at years of patient, careful subtraction.

The largest cave is the prayer hall, called a chaitya. Look at its entrance. A tall, horseshoe-shaped arch rises above the doorway. This shape copies the wooden and thatched buildings of the time, which have almost all disappeared. Stone lasted, so stone remembered them. Around the arch you can see small holes and grooves in the rock. These held a wooden front that once covered the opening. The timber screen has long gone, but its outline is still cut into the cliff.

Now step inside and look up. This is the detail many visitors miss. The curved ceiling still carries wooden ribs — real timber beams fixed along the vault. The stone ceiling did not need them for support. The builders added them because a proper hall, in their minds, had wooden rafters. It is unusual for wood of such age to survive in place at a site like this. Let your eyes follow the ribs from the entrance toward the back of the hall.

Next, look at the columns along the sides. They are plain and many-sided, and they do not stand perfectly straight. They lean slightly inward, toward the centre of the hall. In a wooden building, posts are often set at an angle to carry the weight of a roof. Here, the stone carvers copied that habit, even though solid rock has no roof to push outward. It is a small, quiet sign of a building tradition in transition, from timber to stone.

At the far end of the hall stands a stupa, carved from the same rock as the floor. It is round, with a simple dome. Monks and pilgrims walked around it in a slow circle, keeping it on their right side. The aisle behind the columns gives exactly enough room for that walk. If the hall is quiet, try speaking softly. The curved ceiling carries sound, and even a low voice seems to linger in the air.

On either side of the prayer hall, smaller caves run along the cliff. Many of these were viharas — living quarters for the monks. Inside, you will find small cells opening off a central space. Some cells have raised stone benches, cut to the length of a person. These were beds. Imagine monsoon rain falling outside, lamps flickering in the dark, and monks sleeping on bare rock. Life here was simple, damp and cool, and it followed the seasons.

Further along, there is a group of stupas clustered together, some inside a cave and some under an overhang. They come in different sizes. This group is a memorial. Inscriptions on several of them are understood to record the names of monks who lived and taught here. So this is not only a place of worship. It is also a kind of family record for a community — the teachers they wanted to remember, carved into stone.

Near the southern end of the caves, look for a veranda with carved panels in relief. One panel shows a figure riding in a chariot drawn by horses. Another shows a figure on a large elephant. Some scholars identify them as Surya, the sun god, and Indra, king of the gods. Others suggest different readings. The debate is still open. What is clear is the energy of the carving — the movement of the animals and the small figures around them.

If you visit during or just after the monsoon, the whole hillside changes. The rock turns dark and wet. Green covers the slopes, and a waterfall often pours down the cliff near the caves. From the terrace, look out across the valley. On the ridges nearby stand the hill forts of Lohagad and Visapur, from a much later age of Indian history. The same landscape that sheltered monks later protected soldiers and kings.

Before you leave, stand still on the terrace for a moment. Feel the cool air that comes out of the cave mouths. Look at the tool marks on the walls, the leaning columns and the old wooden ribs inside. Then turn toward the fields and the road below. Travellers have passed through this valley for more than two thousand years. As you walk back down the steps, you are following the same path they took.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for the long stone staircase.
Arrive early to climb in cooler morning air.
Carry drinking water; shade on the climb is limited.
Expect slippery rock if you visit during the monsoon.
Pair the visit with the nearby Karla caves.
