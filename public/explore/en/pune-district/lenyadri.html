The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The steps up to Lenyadri are cut into the hillside, worn smooth in places by countless bare soles. The rock feels cool in the morning and warm by afternoon. Around you, the slope is dry and rough, dotted with scrub and the occasional tree. Somewhere above, a bell rings. Monkeys move along the ledges, watching every bag that passes. Below, the farmland of the Junnar area spreads out in squares of green and brown.

Lenyadri is a group of rock-cut caves in the hills near the town of Junnar, in Maharashtra's Pune district. The name is often explained as a joining of two words. Lena means cave, and adri means mountain or hill. So the name simply describes the place: caves in the mountain. There are around thirty of them, carved directly into the basalt, the dark volcanic rock that forms much of the Deccan plateau, the high tableland of central India.

These caves were not built. They were cut. Workers began at the outer face of the cliff and removed stone, chip by chip, until rooms appeared inside the hill. Nothing was added. Every pillar, bench and doorway you see was left behind when the surrounding rock was taken away. Stand in any doorway and run your eye along the walls. In some places, the tool marks are still visible, faint grooves where a chisel struck the stone many centuries ago.

The caves were made by Buddhist communities, most likely in the early centuries of the Common Era. Junnar was an important place at that time. Trade routes connected the inland plateau with ports on the western coast, and merchants passed through this region. Wealthy donors paid for caves as acts of religious merit. Across the hills around Junnar, there are several other cave groups from the same broad period. Lenyadri is one part of a much larger story.

Most of the caves here are viharas. A vihara is a dwelling for monks. Look for small square cells opening off a central hall, or a simple veranda in front. Each cell held a monk, a stone bench for sleeping, and very little else. The rooms are plain and quiet. Imagine the sound of rain in the monsoon, falling past the cave mouth, while the monks inside studied, meditated, and waited for the dry season to travel again.

A few caves are different. They are chaityas, which are prayer halls built around a stupa. A stupa is a rounded, dome-shaped monument that represents the Buddha and his teachings. Here, the stupas are carved from the living rock, just like the walls. Look closely at one of these halls. Pillars line the space, and in some places their tops carry carved animal figures. Devotees once walked in a circle around the stupa, always keeping it on their right side.

Now for the first surprise. Most visitors do not climb these steps for the Buddhist caves at all. They come for Ganesha, the elephant-headed Hindu god of beginnings and the remover of obstacles. One of the large viharas, usually called Cave 7, was later turned into a Hindu temple. It is dedicated to Ganesha in a form called Girijatmaj. The name means son of Girija, and Girija is another name for the goddess Parvati, Ganesha's mother.

This temple is one of the Ashtavinayaka, a circuit of eight Ganesha shrines in Maharashtra. Pilgrims often try to visit all eight, travelling from shrine to shrine across the region. Lenyadri is the only one of the eight set inside a cave on a hillside. That is why the climb matters. For many pilgrims, every step upward is part of the devotion, not just a way to reach the top.

Locals tell a story about this place. According to the legend, Parvati came to these hills and performed long penance here, hoping to have Ganesha as her son. Her wish was granted, and the cave became linked with his birth and childhood. This is a story of faith, not of archaeology. But it explains why devotees treat this hall with such tenderness, and why families often bring young children here.

Inside the temple cave, the image of Ganesha is carved into the rock wall and covered in bright orange vermilion paste. The shape is not sharply defined, like a statue in a museum. Some devotees say that the image shows Ganesha from the back, and that his face looks toward the other side of the mountain. Whether you see a face or a form, notice how the hall is wide and open. This is the second surprise. A space carved for Buddhist monks now holds Hindu worship.

Spend a moment at the edge of the hall and look at the layers of use. The rock was cut for one religion and adopted by another. Painted colour, oil lamps, flowers and incense have been added over the generations. Smoke has darkened parts of the ceiling. The air smells of camphor and marigolds. Yet the original structure of the vihara is still there, with its cells around the edge. Both histories share one room.

As you move between the caves, look for short lines of ancient writing on some walls and pillars. These are donor inscriptions, carved in an early script called Brahmi, which is an ancestor of many modern Indian scripts. They usually record who paid for a cave or a cistern. You may also find small water cisterns cut into the rock. In a dry landscape, water was precious, and the monks planned for it carefully.

The site is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India, the national body that looks after ancient monuments. That is why some areas are fenced or restricted. Please respect the barriers, and do not touch carved surfaces. The basalt is hard, but centuries of hands and weather wear it down. On busy festival days, especially around celebrations for Ganesha, the stairs fill with pilgrims, and the mood becomes loud, joyful and crowded.

Before you start down, turn around and look out from the cave mouths. The monks who lived here saw a similar view... the river valley, the fields, the line of hills on the horizon. The cities and kingdoms have changed many times since then. The rock has barely moved. As you walk back down the steps, listen to the bells behind you, and feel the same smooth stone under your feet that so many others have climbed before you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb early to avoid midday heat on the steps.
Keep food and loose bags hidden from the monkeys.
Wear shoes easy to remove at the temple entrance.
Expect large crowds during Ganesha festival days.
Bring water, since the climb offers little shade.
