The guide

The story

Feel the stone steps under your feet. They are worn smooth and slightly uneven, cut from the dark volcanic rock of the Sahyadri hills. The air up here is cooler than in the valley below. On a clear morning you may hear only wind, the call of a kite overhead, and the soft scuff of other walkers' sandals behind you. This is Shivneri Fort, a hilltop stronghold above the old town of Junnar, in the northern part of Pune district.

To understand why people climb this hill with such feeling, start with one name. Shivaji Bhosale was born inside these walls. He grew up to found the Maratha kingdom in the seventeenth century, and he is remembered across Maharashtra simply as Shivaji Maharaj. The state government marks his birth date as 19 February 1630. Some historians have argued for a slightly earlier year. Either way, for millions of people in this region, this hill is where the story of Maratha self-rule begins.

Now look at the shape of the land around you. Shivneri stands alone, with steep cliffs on almost every side. The Sahyadri range, which English speakers often call the Western Ghats, is full of flat-topped hills like this one. They were made by ancient lava flows, layer on layer. Builders here did not need to raise tall walls everywhere. The rock itself did most of the defending. Walls and gates were placed only where a climber could find a way up.

As you climb, you pass through a series of gateways. Tradition counts seven of them. Notice how the path bends sharply before each gate. This was deliberate. An attacking force could not build up speed, and an elephant could not charge a door straight on. Some gates still carry heavy wooden doors studded with iron spikes. Those spikes were a defence against elephants, which armies of the time used as battering rams.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The fort is much older than Shivaji. Long before any Maratha king, Buddhist monks carved caves into the hills around Junnar. Some of those caves are cut into Shivneri hill itself, and they date back roughly two thousand years. You can see some rock-cut openings in the cliff faces. So the hill was a place of shelter and prayer long before it became a place of soldiers.

In the centuries that followed, the fort passed between several powers. It was held by the Bahmani Sultanate, a Muslim kingdom of the Deccan plateau, and later by the Nizam Shahi rulers of Ahmadnagar. Shivaji's father, Shahaji, was a nobleman in Nizam Shahi service. In a time of war and shifting loyalties, he sent his pregnant wife, Jijabai, to the safety of this hilltop. The cliffs made it one of the most secure places she could be.

Inside the walls, look for the small temple of Shivai Devi, a local form of the mother goddess. According to widely shared tradition, Jijabai prayed here for a healthy child. When her son was born, she named him after the goddess. So the name Shivaji does not come from the god Shiva, as many visitors assume. It comes from Shivai, the goddess of this very hill. The temple is modest, but people still come here to pray.

Further on, you reach the building pointed out as Shivaji's birthplace. It is a simple two-storey stone structure, not a grand palace. Inside, a cradle marks the spot of his birth, often decorated with flowers. Many families pause here in silence. Others sing or chant. Listen for the sound of a lullaby or a devotional song. On festival days the room can feel crowded, warm, and full of marigold scent.

Now for the second surprise. Shivaji spent only his earliest years here. Soon after his birth, the fort fell into Mughal hands. The Mughals were the great empire of northern India, and they held Shivneri for much of Shivaji's later life. He went on to capture many forts across these hills, but this one, his own birthplace, largely stayed outside his control. It is a strange twist. The fort that made him a legend was a fort he could not keep.

Water was always the key to survival on a hill like this. Walk past the rock-cut tanks and you will see how carefully the builders planned. Two famous cisterns are called Ganga and Jamuna, after India's two great sacred rivers. Locals say they hold water through the year. There is also a larger pond, known as Badami Talav. In a long siege, these tanks meant the garrison could hold out for months without coming down.

Near the birthplace, you will see a statue of Jijabai with the young Shivaji at her side. She holds his hand, and he looks ahead. In Maharashtra, Jijabai is honoured almost as much as her son. She is remembered as the mother who raised him with stories of justice and courage. Families often take photographs here, with children standing beside the bronze boy who became a king.

At the edge of the hill, there is a sheer drop called Kadelot. According to local tradition, this was the cliff from which condemned prisoners were thrown. Stand well back from the edge. From here the view opens wide across the Junnar plain. You can see fields in squares of green and brown, small villages, and on clear days the line of other Sahyadri hills. On February birthdays, this whole plateau fills with saffron flags and processions.

Take a moment before you head down. Look at the black stone under your hands, the dry grass between the rocks, the long shadow the walls throw across the path. People have climbed this hill as monks, soldiers, mothers, and pilgrims. Today you are one more of them. As you walk back through the bending gates, notice how the sounds of the town slowly return... and how quiet the top of the hill remains behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb early to avoid midday heat.
Wear shoes with good grip for worn stone steps.
Carry drinking water for the climb and the plateau.
Expect large crowds around Shiv Jayanti in February.
Visit during the monsoon for green slopes, but watch slippery steps.
