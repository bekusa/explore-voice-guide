The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind the gates, the courtyard is almost silent. You can hear the slap of your sandals on smooth paving, a little wind off the Gulf of Oman, and maybe a gull above the harbour. Ahead of you rises a facade of deep blue and bright gold, held up by tall, flared columns. Around it, bare brown mountains close in on every side. This is Al Alam Palace, and few places in Muscat feel this quiet.

The name means "the flag" in Arabic. It is the ceremonial palace of the Sultan of Oman. It is not a museum and not a home in the everyday sense. Instead, this is where the ruler receives visiting heads of state and holds official occasions. That is why you cannot walk inside. Visitors come to these gates, look through the railings, and take in the view from the forecourt. Most people never see more than this, and that is part of the experience.

Look first at the columns. They rise in pairs across the front, slim at the base and widening sharply at the top. Some visitors say they look like mushrooms. Others say they look like open lotus flowers, or the tops of palm trees. Whatever you see, they are clearly modern. They do not copy the old mud-brick forts of the Omani interior. The palace in its current form dates from the early 1970s, a period of fast change in Oman.

That change has a name: Sultan Qaboos bin Said. He came to power in 1970 and ruled for about half a century, until his death in 2020. In those years, Oman built roads, schools, hospitals and ministries at great speed. This palace was part of that new image of the country. It looked forward, yet it stood on ground used by earlier rulers. An older palace stood on this same spot before the modern building replaced it.

Now turn your head slowly to the left, and then to the right. On the rocky heights on either side of the cove, two fortresses guard the harbour. These are Al Jalali and Al Mirani. Their present forms are linked to the Portuguese, who controlled this coast in the sixteenth century. Omani forces drove the Portuguese out in the following century. Today the forts frame the palace like two stone bookends, one old and rough, one between them bright and new.

Here is the first surprise. The position of this palace is not about views. It is about defence. Old Muscat sits in a small bay, almost closed in by steep mountains. For centuries, the only easy way in was by sea, and the forts watched that sea. Land routes came through narrow passes and through gates in a city wall. So the ruler's seat sits in the safest pocket of the coast, behind rock and water.

As you walked here, you probably came along a wide, straight avenue. Notice the neat lamp posts, the clipped lawns, the white government buildings with their arches and wooden screens. Much of this ceremonial quarter was laid out at the same time as the palace. The colours repeat on purpose. White walls, blue details and gold trim appear again and again. The whole area is designed as one composition, with the palace as its centre.

About that name, "the flag". Muscat has an old and much-repeated story connected to flags in this part of the town. According to a popular tale, enslaved people who reached a flagpole at the nearby British consulate, and touched it, were granted their freedom. Historians debate how literally the story should be taken. Treat it as a local legend rather than a documented rule. Still, it shows how strongly this small harbour was tied to trade, empire and the Indian Ocean world.

That ocean world matters here. For centuries, ships from Muscat sailed to India, Persia and the coast of East Africa. Omani rulers once held power far down the Swahili coast, including the island of Zanzibar. Merchants, sailors and families moved back and forth across these waters. When you look at the palace, you are looking at the seat of a dynasty that once faced outward, toward the sea, as much as inward toward the desert.

Here is the second surprise. If you look closely at the gates and the railings, you will see that the detail is fine and careful. Gold paint catches the light and changes colour through the day. In the morning it looks pale. In the late afternoon it turns warm and almost orange. The blue also shifts, from bright and flat at noon to a deep, cool shade in the evening. Come back at a different hour and the building looks different.

Old Muscat itself is small, and you can walk much of it. Near the palace you will find the National Museum of Oman, which tells the country's story in far more depth. A little further along the road, the old city gate now holds a small museum. The streets are calm compared with the souq at Muttrah, a few kilometres up the coast. Here, life moves slowly, especially in the heat of the day.

A few practical thoughts. The forecourt has little shade, and the stone reflects the sun. Bring water, a hat and sunglasses, especially in the hotter months. Dress modestly, out of respect for an official royal site. Guards may be present. Photography of the facade from the public area is common, but follow any instruction you are given. Do not try to climb the railings or enter closed areas. A short, respectful visit is the right rhythm here.

Before you leave, take one more look. The blue and gold front. The pale columns rising in pairs. The dark rock of the mountains pressing in, and the two old forts watching from above. Listen again for the wind and the water in the cove. When you turn to walk away, notice how quickly the palace disappears behind the walls and the hills. Old Muscat keeps its ruler close, and keeps its centre quiet.
