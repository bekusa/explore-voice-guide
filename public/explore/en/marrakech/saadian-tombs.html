The guide

The story

Before you see anything, you feel the passage. It is narrow and shadowed, a slim corridor squeezed between high walls of pinkish earth. The noise of the Kasbah quarter fades behind you. Scooters, vendors and the call of a nearby muezzin all grow softer. Then the corridor opens, and you step into a quiet garden. There are palms, rose bushes and low green hedges, and small graves lie scattered among the plants. For centuries this was one of the most private places in Marrakech.

You are standing in the royal burial ground of the Saadian dynasty. The Saadians ruled Morocco from the sixteenth century into the seventeenth century. They traced their family line to the Prophet Muhammad, and this claim helped them win support across the country. They made Marrakech their capital. This garden sits right beside the Kasbah Mosque, the old royal mosque of the quarter. Burial close to a mosque was a mark of honour. It was meant to keep the dead within the sound of prayer.

The man most linked to these tombs is Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur. He came to the throne in 1578, after a famous battle in which three kings died in a single day. Al-Mansur had enormous ambition. In 1591 his army crossed the Sahara and defeated the Songhai Empire of West Africa. Caravans then brought gold north to Marrakech. People began to call him al-Dhahabi, which means the golden one. Much of that wealth was spent on beauty.

Some of it was spent here. Look toward the largest building, on your left as you face the garden. Inside is the room known as the Hall of the Twelve Columns. Al-Mansur himself lies there, with members of his family around him. The columns are made of Italian marble, which tradition says came from Carrara. The Saadians traded sugar for it, weight for weight. That last detail is a popular story rather than a recorded fact, but it shows how precious this stone was.

You usually cannot walk into the hall. Visitors look in through a doorway, and on busy days a short line forms. It is worth the wait. Let your eyes travel upward, slowly. At the bottom, the walls carry zellij, which is Moroccan mosaic made from small hand-cut pieces of glazed tile. Above that, you see carved stucco as fine as lace. Higher still, the ceiling is cedarwood, carved and painted, with traces of gold leaf.

Now look at the arches and the corners of the dome. Do you see the shapes that hang down like honeycomb, or like icicles? This technique is called muqarnas. Craftsmen build it from many small carved niches stacked in layers. It is common in Islamic architecture from Spain to Iran. Here it catches the light and breaks it into hundreds of small shadows. Even on a bright afternoon, the room glows softly instead of shining.

Here is something many visitors miss. The beauty of this place is mostly on the inside. From the garden, the buildings look fairly plain. Their roofs are green tile and their walls are simple. This was deliberate. In much Moroccan architecture, a modest outside wall protects a rich interior. The same idea shapes the traditional riad houses in the medina around you. The best things are kept inward, out of sight of the street.

The second, smaller mausoleum stands deeper in the garden. It is older, and it is linked to al-Mansur's mother, Lalla Messaouda, who was a respected figure in her own right. Look for the small prayer room with its mihrab. A mihrab is the decorated niche that shows the direction of Mecca. Here it is framed by slender columns and fine carved plaster. In many ways it is the most elegant corner of the whole site.

And then there are the graves in the open garden. Dozens of them lie among the plants, many marked with bright tiles. Tradition says these belong to people who served the court. Soldiers, servants and advisers were buried here, close to the rulers they knew in life. Some graves are very small. They are often said to be children's graves. Take a moment with them. They make this royal site feel human.

Now the second surprise. For more than two centuries, almost nobody came here. After the Saadians fell, a new dynasty took power, the Alaouites, who still rule Morocco today. The Alaouite sultan Moulay Ismail destroyed much of Saadian Marrakech, including al-Mansur's great El Badi Palace nearby. But he did not destroy these tombs. Perhaps he feared the shame of disturbing the dead. Instead, the entrance was walled up. The garden was left sealed behind the mosque.

The tombs became widely known again in the early twentieth century, during the French protectorate. The usual account says aerial photographs revealed the hidden enclosure around 1917. Officials then cut the narrow passage that you walked through today, and the buildings were restored. That passage is not part of the original design. It is a modern entrance to a place that was built to be reached only by a privileged few. You entered by a side door.

As you move through the garden, listen. The sounds here tell you about the city. Sparrows chatter in the bushes. A cat may stretch out on a warm grave, quite unbothered by visitors. If you look up at the walls, you may see storks, which nest across this part of Marrakech. Beyond the walls, the Kasbah quarter carries on as normal. People shop, children run home from school, and engines rattle in the lanes.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the garden path. Look once more at the green roof of the main hall and the faded tiles at your feet. A sultan who had gold carried across the desert chose to rest here, in a garden of roses and stone. His palace is now a ruin, but this quiet room survived. As you walk back through the narrow passage, notice how the city's noise slowly returns.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid long lines at the main hall.
Be patient at the doorway for a clear view inside.
Combine your visit with the nearby El Badi Palace ruins.
Dress modestly, as this is a burial site beside a mosque.
Keep your voice low out of respect for the graves.
