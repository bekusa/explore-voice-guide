The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, a voice may be rising from the tower. The call to prayer drifts over the rooftops. It carries across palm trees, horse carriages and the low roar of traffic. Under your feet, the paving is warm and dusty. In front of you, a single square tower of rose-brown stone rises over Marrakech. You are standing beside the Kutubiyya Mosque. For more than eight centuries, this minaret has been the tallest marker of the city.

The name comes from books. Kutubiyyin means booksellers in Arabic. In the medieval city, a market of booksellers and manuscript copyists gathered around this mosque. Picture stalls stacked with handwritten volumes, scribes bent over paper, scholars arguing over texts in the shade. Those stalls are long gone. The name has stayed. So a building that most visitors know for its stone tower is named for something much quieter, the trade in words.

The mosque belongs to the Almohads. They were a Berber dynasty from the High Atlas mountains, the peaks you can sometimes see to the south on a clear day. In the mid-twelfth century, they took Marrakech from the Almoravids, the dynasty that had founded the city. The Almohads followed a strict reform movement. Their caliph, Abd al-Mu'min, ordered a great new mosque for the conquered capital. It was a statement of faith, and also a statement of power.

Here is the first surprise. There was not one Kutubiyya. There were two. The first mosque was built, and then a second one was built right beside it, almost the same size. A common explanation says the first building did not face Mecca accurately enough. Historians still discuss the exact reasons. But the evidence is on the ground. Walk along the northwest side and look for the low excavated foundations. Those are the remains of the earlier prayer hall.

Now look up at the tower itself. The minaret was completed under a later Almohad ruler, Yaqub al-Mansur, toward the end of the twelfth century. It rises roughly seventy-seven metres, including the slender lantern on top. The design is simple and very disciplined. It is a square shaft, a smaller square lantern above, and a dome with a pointed finial. There are no curves in the main outline. The strength comes from proportion.

Walk slowly around the tower, and the faces change. Each side carries its own pattern of windows and carved panels. You will see pointed arches, scalloped arches and interlaced arches that cross over one another like woven ribbons. Near the top, there is a band of glazed ceramic tile. Much of the colour has faded, but traces of turquoise still show in good light. The stone is rough rubble and sandstone. In the late afternoon, it turns a warm red-gold.

Inside the tower, there is no ordinary staircase. A wide ramp winds upward around a central core, past a series of rooms stacked one above the other. The ramp allowed the muezzin, the man who gives the call to prayer, to climb to the top. Legend has it that the ramp was wide enough for him to ride a horse. Whether anyone ever did is another question. Visitors are not able to climb it.

The second surprise sits right at the summit. Look for the metal globes on the spire, stacked one above another. They catch the sun like brass. According to one popular tale, they were made from the melted gold jewellery of a sultan's wife. She gave it up as penance after breaking her fast during Ramadan. It is a story, not a documented fact. Other tales say the globes were enchanted to protect the city. Locals still tell both versions.

This tower had a long reach. The Almohads ruled lands on both sides of the sea, including much of southern Spain. Their minarets followed a shared language. The Giralda in Seville, now the bell tower of Seville Cathedral, began as an Almohad minaret. The Hassan Tower in Rabat, never finished, was planned on a similar model. So when you look at this tower, you are looking at a relative of buildings in Spain and northern Morocco.

A practical note. In Morocco, non-Muslims generally cannot enter active mosques, and the Kutubiyya is no exception. The prayer hall is for worship. From outside, you can still sense its size. It is a wide, low building with many aisles of columns inside, and it fills much of the block. One of its great treasures has left the building. The carved wooden minbar, the pulpit, was made in Córdoba. Today it is displayed in the Badi Palace, across the medina.

Around the mosque, gardens soften the scene. Rows of orange trees, palms and roses line the paths. Families sit on benches in the evening. Pigeons gather on the old stone. Calèches, the green horse-drawn carriages, wait nearby, and you may hear hooves on the road. Locals often say that no building in the old city should rise higher than this minaret. Whatever the rule on paper, the skyline mostly respects it. The tower still stands alone.

Just a few minutes' walk to the east is Jemaa el-Fna, the great open square of Marrakech. In the evening, it fills with food stalls, musicians, storytellers and smoke from grills. The minaret acts as a compass for anyone lost in the narrow lanes of the medina. Look up, find the tower, and you know roughly where you are. People have used it this way for centuries. It is a landmark in the oldest, most practical sense.

The tower has also survived real tests. Earthquakes have shaken this region more than once, including the powerful quake of 2023 in the High Atlas. Through it all, the Almohad stonework has kept its shape. Restorers have patched and cleaned it over the generations, and you can see repairs in lighter stone. But the outline is the one the twelfth-century builders drew. Few buildings anywhere keep their original silhouette for so long.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment. Watch how the light shifts on the carved panels as clouds pass. Listen for the next call to prayer, and notice how the street pauses around it. When you walk away, toward the square or into the gardens, turn back once. The tower will still be there above the palms, square and patient, marking the centre of Marrakech as it always has.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in late afternoon when the stone glows red-gold.
Respect prayer times; non-Muslims cannot enter the prayer hall.
Walk the full circuit to compare all four tower faces.
Rest in the Kutubiyya gardens under the orange trees.
Use the minaret as your compass inside the medina.
