The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. The wind moves across the flat plain, and the dust settles softly against your shoes. Ahead of you rises a single vast arch of pale brown brick. It is the colour of dry earth, and it holds a deep shadow inside it. Everything around it is low and open. That is why the arch looks even larger than it is. You are standing at Taq Kasra, the great vaulted hall of the old Persian kings.

The name means, roughly, the Arch of Khosrow. Khosrow was a royal name used by Sasanian rulers. The Sasanians were the last Persian dynasty before the arrival of Islam. Tradition links this hall to Khosrow the First, who ruled in the sixth century. Scholars still debate the exact date. Some believe parts of the palace are older. What is certain is that this was the heart of a royal capital.

That capital was Ctesiphon. It sat on the banks of the Tigris River, a short distance southeast of modern Baghdad. Across the water stood an older Greek-founded city called Seleucia. Together they formed one of the largest urban centres of the ancient world. Today almost all of it is gone. Low mounds and scattered brick are what remain. The arch is the one piece that still stands tall.

Now look up into the vault itself. This is an iwan, a Persian word for a large hall with one side fully open. The open side faced a courtyard, and the king would hold audience inside. Visitors walked from bright sunlight into deep shade. The ruler sat at the far end, framed by the curve of the brick. It was architecture designed to make people feel small. Even empty, it still has that effect.

Here is the first surprise. This vault is among the largest single spans of unreinforced brick ever built. It rises more than thirty-five metres, and it measures roughly twenty-five metres across. There is no steel inside it, and no concrete. It is fired brick and mortar, nothing more. For centuries, engineers have come here simply to study how it stays up.

The answer lies in an old Mesopotamian technique. Wood was scarce in this land, so builders could not easily make large timber frames. Instead, they laid the bricks in leaning rows. Each row rested on the one behind it as the vault grew outward. The curve itself is slightly pointed, not a perfect half circle. That shape sends the weight down into the walls more safely. It is clever, patient work, done by hand.

Turn now to the long wall beside the arch. Notice the rows of blind arches and half-columns stacked in tiers. They are decoration, not windows. Once, this face of the palace probably carried painted plaster and other ornament. Today the bare brick shows centuries of weather and repair. Originally, a matching wing stood on the other side of the arch. That wing collapsed in the late nineteenth century, after river floods weakened its foundations.

Chroniclers from the early Islamic period describe great wealth inside this hall. The most famous story concerns an enormous carpet, often called the Spring of Khosrow. Writers said it showed a garden in bloom, worked with gold and precious stones. After the city fell, the carpet was reportedly cut into pieces and shared among the victors. No fragment is known to survive. But the story tells you how this place was remembered.

That fall came in the seventh century. Arab Muslim armies took Ctesiphon in the 630s, and the Sasanian empire soon collapsed. The new rulers built their own cities elsewhere. In the eighth century, the Abbasid caliphs founded Baghdad not far upriver. According to a well-known tale, one caliph wanted to tear down this arch for its bricks. He was told the cost of demolition would be greater than the value of the material. So the arch stayed.

Here is the second surprise. Legend has it that the arch cracked on the night the Prophet Muhammad was born. In this telling, the crack was a sign that the Persian kingdom would end. It is a story of faith, not of records. Still, many visitors know it before they arrive. They often look for the crack when they first walk up to the vault.

The town around you carries another layer of memory. Salman Pak takes its name from Salman the Persian, an early companion of the Prophet Muhammad. His shrine stands nearby, and pilgrims visit it throughout the year. So this ground holds two stories at once. One is the end of a Persian empire. The other is the early life of Islam, told through a Persian convert.

The twentieth century also passed through here. During the First World War, British and Ottoman forces fought a major battle near this site in 1915. Later decades brought neglect, conflict, and harsh weather. In recent years, heavy rains brought down parts of the brickwork. Restoration teams have worked to stabilise the vault. You may see scaffolding, fencing, or fresh brick that looks lighter than the old.

Before you leave, step back to where you can see the whole arch against the sky. Notice how the light changes inside the vault as the sun moves. Notice the birds that nest in the gaps between the bricks. Then look at the flat land stretching away on every side. A great capital once filled it. Now there is wind, dust, and this one curve of brick... still holding its shape.
