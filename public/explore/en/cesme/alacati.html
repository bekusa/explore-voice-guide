The guide

The story

Feel that push against your cheek? That is the first resident of Alaçatı you will meet — the wind. It arrives from the north most summer days, steady and dry, rattling the shutters and carrying the smell of thyme, salt, and jasmine down the lanes. Underfoot, the cobbles are smooth and pale. Around you, walls of honey-coloured stone hold the morning heat. This town on the Çeşme peninsula is built from three materials: cut stone, moving air, and wild herbs.

The story told locally begins with a swamp. In the nineteenth century, the low ground near the bay was marshy and unhealthy. Landowners brought in Greek Orthodox workers from the islands and coasts nearby to drain the wetlands and plant the land. The workers stayed. They planted vineyards, built houses, and raised churches, and the town grew prosperous on wine and farm produce. Much of what you see in the old quarter took shape in their hands.

Look closely at those houses. They are built from local sandstone, cut into neat blocks that have weathered to shades of honey and grey. Most rise two storeys, with bay windows leaning over the street and shutters painted blue, turquoise, or deep red. Behind heavy wooden doors hide courtyards with fig trees and wells. The stone breathes — it keeps rooms cool in the summer heat and holds warmth on winter nights. It is the town's signature, and it is protected.

Then history turned the town inside out. In 1923, after the war between Greece and Turkey, the two countries exchanged their minority populations. The Greek families of Alaçatı left for Greece. Muslim families, uprooted from the Balkans and the Greek islands, arrived and settled into the empty stone houses. The vineyards gave way, in time, to tobacco fields and quiet decades. For most of the twentieth century, Alaçatı was simply a farming town that the world drove past.

On the ridge above the rooftops stand the windmills — squat stone towers that once caught this same wind to grind the town's grain. Several have been restored, and the walk up to them is short and rewarding. From the mills, you can read the whole geography at once: the tiled roofs below, the vineyards and olive groves beyond, and the flat blue bay to the south where the wind writes patterns on the water.

That bay changed everything. Its water is shallow for hundreds of metres, and the summer wind blows across it steadily, day after day. In the 1990s, windsurfers discovered this combination and schools opened along the shore. Flat water and reliable wind make one of the best learning spots anywhere, and international competitions followed. On a windy afternoon, the bay fills with sails — dozens of bright triangles cutting back and forth. Beginners fall, stand up, and fall again, happily.

In the market square stands a mosque with an unusual past. It began in the nineteenth century as a Greek Orthodox church, and after the population exchange it was converted for the town's new Muslim residents. Walk around it and study the masonry — the proportions and stonework still carry the logic of a church. Buildings here changed religion the way the houses changed families. The town kept using what it had, and that thrift preserved it.

Alaçatı also eats differently. The peninsula is a pantry of wild herbs, and spring is the season when hillsides are picked by hand. Menus fill with sautéed greens, herb fritters, samphire with lemon and olive oil, and stuffed zucchini flowers. Every spring the town holds a herb festival, when chefs, farmers, and visitors celebrate weeds that most of the world ignores. Order whatever green thing the waiter recommends. It was probably growing wild a few kilometres away.

The town's third act began in the 2000s. Families restored the old stone houses, and small hotels opened behind the old facades — then cafes, wine bars, and boutiques followed. Alaçatı became one of the most fashionable escapes in Turkey, filling with visitors from İzmir and Istanbul on summer weekends. Prices climbed with the popularity. Come midweek or outside high season, and the town relaxes back into itself — cats asleep on warm stone, coffee cups clinking in courtyards.

Wander without a plan for a while. The old quarter, including the lanes around Hacımemiş, rewards slow walking: bougainvillea pouring over walls in purple and red, doorways framed in carved stone, a barber chatting across the street to a grocer. Look up at the bay windows and their wooden brackets. Look down at doorsteps worn into curves. Nothing here is a museum. People live behind these shutters, water these flowers, and complain about the same wind you are enjoying.

Evenings are the reward. The wind usually softens, and the stone gives back its stored heat. Jasmine takes over from thyme as the town's perfume. Lanterns come on over restaurant tables, and the lanes fill with slow walkers deciding where to eat. If you are here in the right season, taste a local wine — vines are returning to the peninsula after a century's pause. The grapes are growing where the Greek farmers once planted theirs.

Before you leave, stand still in a lane and let the wind pass you one more time. It turned the millstones, dried the tobacco, filled the sails, and carried every generation's cooking smells down these same lanes. Two peoples built this town, decades of quiet kept it whole, and the wind connects all of it. Put your palm flat on a warm stone wall. Somewhere above, a shutter taps twice, softly, like the town acknowledging you.
