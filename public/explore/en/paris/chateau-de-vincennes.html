The guide

The story

Listen first. On the eastern edge of Paris, the traffic noise fades behind you. Gravel crunches under your shoes, and pigeons call from high stone ledges. The air carries the smell of the nearby woods. In front of you, a pale grey tower rises above a deep, dry moat. Its walls are so tall that you have to tilt your head right back to see the top. This is the Château de Vincennes, and for centuries it was one of the great seats of French royal power.

The story begins with hunting. The forest you passed on your way here, the Bois de Vincennes, was once a royal hunting ground. In the twelfth century, King Louis the Seventh built a modest lodge at its edge. Later kings enlarged it. For them, this place was close enough to Paris for business, but far enough away to breathe. They had fresh air, game in the woods, and room to build. That simple combination shaped everything you see today.

One of the most loved figures in French history spent long days here. Louis the Ninth, later known as Saint Louis, often stayed at Vincennes in the thirteenth century. His friend and chronicler, the Lord of Joinville, wrote that the king sometimes sat beneath an oak tree in these woods. There, ordinary people could come to him with their disputes, and he would listen. The original tree is long gone. But the image of a king giving justice under an oak became one of the lasting symbols of fair rule in France.

Now look at the great tower again. This is the donjon, the keep. Work on it began in the fourteenth century, during the long and bitter conflict with England that we now call the Hundred Years' War. Three kings worked on it in turn, and King Charles the Fifth completed it. The keep stands around fifty metres high. It is often described as the tallest medieval fortified keep in Europe. Here is the first surprise: it was not only a fortress. It was also a home.

Charles the Fifth was a scholar as well as a king. He loved books and kept a large library. Inside the keep, he had private rooms, a chapel, and a small study where he could read and work in peace. Stone walls several metres thick kept out noise and danger. Imagine the contrast. Outside, the country was at war. Inside, a king sat among his manuscripts, close to a warm fireplace, with the forest below his windows.

Charles had even bigger plans. He surrounded the whole site with a huge rectangular wall, strengthened by tall towers at regular intervals. Inside it, he imagined a fortified royal town, with homes for his trusted lords and officials. Walk along that wall today and notice something odd. Most of the towers look short and flat at the top. They were not built like that. In the nineteenth century, most of them were cut down to the height of the wall. Only one gate tower, on the side facing the town, still keeps its original height.

Across the courtyard from the keep, you will see a tall Gothic church. This is the Sainte-Chapelle of Vincennes. Charles the Fifth began it, inspired by the famous royal chapel in central Paris. Work went on slowly for almost two centuries. It was only completed in the sixteenth century. Step inside if you can. The windows hold Renaissance stained glass, with deep blues, reds, and golds. On a bright day, coloured light falls across the stone floor and moves slowly as the sun crosses the sky.

In the seventeenth century, the château changed character again. Two long, elegant buildings face each other at the southern end of the courtyard. These are the King's Pavilion and the Queen's Pavilion. The architect Louis Le Vau designed them for the young Louis the Fourteenth. Cardinal Mazarin, the powerful minister who guided the boy king, lived here, and he died at Vincennes in 1661. Notice how calm and symmetrical these buildings are. They look like the start of a palace, not a fortress.

Soon after, though, the court moved away. Louis the Fourteenth chose Versailles, and the royal life of Vincennes faded. The great keep found a darker purpose. It became a state prison. This is the second surprise for many visitors. Some of France's most famous writers were locked inside. The philosopher Denis Diderot was held here in 1749 for his ideas. The Marquis de Sade and the politician Mirabeau also spent long months behind these walls. Some prisoners scratched words and drawings into the stone of their cells.

The moat below you holds one of the château's grimmest memories. In 1804, the young Duke of Enghien, a member of the old royal family, was brought here at night. After a hasty military trial, he was shot in the moat. The order came from Napoleon Bonaparte, who was about to declare himself emperor. All across Europe, people were shocked. The event damaged Napoleon's reputation for years. A small monument marks the place. Many visitors walk past it without knowing the story.

Under Napoleon, Vincennes became a military arsenal, full of weapons and gunpowder. Its governor was General Daumesnil, a veteran who had lost a leg in battle. When enemy armies surrounded Paris in 1814, he refused to hand over the fortress. According to a popular story, he told them he would give up Vincennes when they gave him back his leg. Whether he said those exact words or not, Parisians loved the tale. The army still keeps a strong link to this place today. Its historical archives are stored inside the walls.

The twentieth century also left marks here. During the Second World War, German forces occupied the château. In August 1944, just before they left Paris, they shot a group of prisoners here and set off explosions that damaged parts of the buildings. Long restoration work followed. As you walk, look closely at the stone. Some blocks are bright and clean. Others are dark and worn. Together, they show centuries of building, damage, and repair, side by side in the same wall.

Now take a moment to stand still in the courtyard. To one side, you have the medieval keep, heavy and defensive. Across from it, the Gothic chapel reaches upward. Behind you, the classical pavilions stretch out, orderly and calm. Few places in France gather so many periods of history in one view. As you leave, listen again to the gravel under your feet, and glance back at the keep. Kings read here, prisoners wrote here, and soldiers guarded the gate. Now it is your turn to walk through it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take Metro line 1 to its eastern terminus, Château de Vincennes.
Book a keep visit ahead, as entry inside can be limited.
Combine your visit with a walk in the Bois de Vincennes.
Visit the chapel on a sunny day for the stained-glass light.
Wear comfortable shoes for gravel paths and steep spiral stairs.
