The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The street outside fades behind a heavy door, and the air inside is quieter, slightly cooler. Floorboards creak under your feet. Somewhere above, light falls through tall windows onto walls covered from floor to ceiling with paintings. You are standing in a house in Paris that one painter turned into a single, enormous self-portrait. Welcome to the home and studio of Gustave Moreau.

Moreau was born in Paris in 1826 and died in 1898. He was a painter of myths, saints, and dreams. His world was full of goddesses, heroes, chimeras, and jewelled princesses. Critics later placed him among the Symbolists, a group of artists who cared less about painting the visible world and more about painting ideas, emotions, and the inner life. Moreau did not love labels. But he understood very well what he wanted to leave behind.

This was his family home. Moreau lived here with his parents, and the house sits in a part of the ninth arrondissement that Parisians call the Nouvelle Athènes, or New Athens. In the nineteenth century, this slope below Montmartre attracted writers, actors, and painters. Many of the houses nearby still have the calm, classical façades of that era. As you walked here, you probably noticed the quiet streets and the pale stone. That neighbourhood mood continues inside these walls.

Here is the first thing most visitors do not realise. Moreau planned this museum himself. Late in his life, he decided that his work should stay together, in one place, rather than scatter across private collections. He had the house transformed and enlarged, with vast studios built on the upper floors. Then he left the whole building, and everything inside it, to the French state. The museum opened to the public in the early twentieth century, a few years after his death.

So this is not a museum that someone else assembled. It is a museum the artist designed as his own legacy. That makes it one of the earliest museums of its kind, a space created by a painter to hold his life's work. When you look around, you are seeing the arrangement he imagined. The density is deliberate. Moreau wanted visitors to feel surrounded by his world, not to see a few polite pictures on a clean wall.

Before you climb to the studios, take time on the lower floor. This is the private apartment, where Moreau gathered furniture, family portraits, and personal objects. The rooms are small and crowded. They feel very different from the studios above. Here you sense the man rather than the master. There are photographs, souvenirs, and small works collected over a lifetime. The light is softer here, and the mood is intimate, almost hushed.

One small room deserves your attention. It is often called the boudoir, and it remembers Alexandrine Dureux, a woman who shared a long and discreet relationship with Moreau. They never married. After her death, he kept objects and drawings connected with her, and he arranged this space in her memory. Many visitors walk through quickly. Pause instead. It is one of the few places in the house where the painter's private feelings become visible.

Now climb up to the great studios. The change is sudden. The ceilings rise, the windows open wide, and canvases fill the walls in rows, frame against frame. Some are huge. Some are barely larger than a book. Many are unfinished. Moreau often worked on the same painting for years, returning to it again and again. On some canvases, you can see where he added extra strips to make the picture larger as his ideas grew.

That unfinished quality is the second surprise. Look closely and you will find areas of rich colour next to bare outlines, as if the painter has just stepped out of the room. Fine lines of drawing run over thick patches of paint. Gold details sit beside empty sky. For a modern eye, these half-finished works can feel strangely contemporary. They show the process of painting, not only the result.

At the centre of the house rises the spiral staircase. It is probably the image most people carry home from here. Its iron curves turn upward between the two studio floors, light and elegant, like a ribbon of metal. Stand at the bottom and look up. Then climb slowly, and watch how the paintings shift around you at each step. From the top, look down. The staircase becomes a drawing in space.

Do not miss the cabinets. Moreau left many thousands of drawings and watercolours, far too many to hang on the walls. So the house holds special furniture with hinged panels. You can turn these panels like the pages of a large book, gently, one by one. Each reveals sketches, studies of hands and drapery, and small coloured scenes. It is a quiet, hands-on way to enter the painter's mind.

Moreau was also a teacher. In his later years he taught at the École des Beaux-Arts, the official art school of Paris. He was known as an open-minded master who encouraged students to find their own path. Among those students were Henri Matisse and Georges Rouault, two painters who would change modern art. And here is a final twist: Rouault became the first curator of this very museum. The student came home to guard his teacher's house.

The house later found admirers far beyond painting. The Surrealist writer André Breton, one of the founders of that movement, was deeply moved by this place and wrote about the power of Moreau's women and myths. For the Surrealists, who explored dreams and the unconscious, Moreau looked like an early ancestor. Standing among these crowded walls, you can understand why. The paintings feel less like stories and more like visions.

Before you leave, stand for a moment in the upper studio. Let your eyes move without a plan, from a golden figure to a dark rock, from a finished face to a bare sketch. Notice the creak of the floor and the light on the varnish. When you step back into the street, the Nouvelle Athènes will look calm and ordinary again. But behind one door, a painter's dream is still at work.
