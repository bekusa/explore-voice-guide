The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look closely, let the sound reach you. Water slaps against a shallow basin. Somewhere a metal arm creaks and turns. A jet hisses, stops, then starts again. Around you, skateboards clatter on the paving, and a street musician may be tuning a guitar. This is the Stravinsky Fountain, a pool of moving machines and painted creatures on a square in the heart of Paris. It feels less like a monument and more like a small, noisy party.

The square takes its name from Igor Stravinsky, the Russian-born composer. In the early twentieth century his ballet music shocked Paris and then changed it. The fountain is a tribute to him. Each sculpture in the pool refers to one of his works or to an idea from his music. You will not hear Stravinsky playing here. Instead, the fountain tries to turn his rhythms, his sharp edges and his sudden bursts of colour into objects you can see.

Two artists made it together, and they were also a married couple. Jean Tinguely was a Swiss sculptor known for machines that move, clank and seem a little absurd. Niki de Saint Phalle was a French-American artist known for round, joyful figures painted in bold colours. The fountain was inaugurated in 1983. Look at the pool and you can almost split it in two. The black metal mechanisms are his. The bright, curving figures are hers.

Tinguely built his pieces from scrap-like parts. You can see wheels, rods, belts and small motors, all painted black. They do not try to look elegant. They spin, nod and spray water in slightly clumsy patterns. He liked machines that seem to have a personality, almost a sense of humour. Watch one of them for a full minute. You will notice that its movement repeats, but never quite in the rhythm you expect.

Saint Phalle's figures answer his machines with colour. Her pieces are painted in red, yellow, blue and green, with thick dark outlines, almost like a children's book. Look for the Firebird, named after one of Stravinsky's most famous ballets. It is a large bird with wide wings, and it is often the first figure people photograph. Nearby there is a pair of big red lips, a heart, a mermaid and a coiled snake.

There are sixteen sculptures in total, and the fun is in matching them to the music. A black treble clef turns slowly, a plain symbol of written music. A grinning skull suggests death, a theme that runs through some of Stravinsky's darker works. An elephant, a fox and a frog appear too. Some of these refer to specific pieces of his music. Others feel more like playful ideas. You do not need to know every score to enjoy them.

Here is something most visitors never notice. The fountain is not built on ordinary ground. Below this square lies IRCAM, a research institute for music and sound. Its studios and laboratories sit underground. Because of this, the pool had to be light and shallow. The basin works partly as a roof over the rooms below. So, while you watch the water, composers and sound engineers may be working just beneath your feet.

IRCAM was founded with the support of the composer and conductor Pierre Boulez. He was one of the most important figures in modern music in France. The institute is linked to the Centre Pompidou, the large museum of modern art just beside you. That connection explains the choice of Stravinsky. A square above a music laboratory, next to a modern art museum, honours a composer who broke the rules of music in his own time.

Now turn around and look at the buildings that frame the pool. On one side rises the Centre Pompidou, with its pipes and structures on the outside. On another side stands the church of Saint-Merri. It is a Gothic church, with pointed arches and carved stone that are centuries older than anything in the fountain. The contrast is deliberate, or at least it feels that way. Old stone faith meets bright modern play.

Look up at the walls around the square, too. Large painted murals cover some of the blank sides of the buildings. One of them shows a giant face with a finger raised to its lips, as if asking the whole square for silence. Given the noise of the fountain, the joke is hard to miss. Small mosaic pieces of street art also appear on corners nearby, so keep your eyes open as you walk.

The fountain has not always looked as fresh as it does on a good day. Water, sun and the Paris weather are hard on painted surfaces and moving parts. Over the years, the sculptures have needed cleaning, repainting and mechanical repairs. On some visits, a few machines may be still or switched off. That is normal for a work that is meant to move outdoors. It is a living piece, and living pieces need care.

The square itself is part of the experience. People sit on the edge of the pool with coffee or a sandwich. Children point at the snake and wait for it to spray. Students read, tourists take photos, and local workers pass through on their way somewhere else. There is no ticket and no entrance. The fountain belongs to whoever stops here. That openness was part of the idea: modern art placed in the street, not behind walls.

If you want to understand the fountain, try one small exercise. Pick one Saint Phalle figure and one Tinguely machine that stand close together. Watch how they behave side by side. One is bright and still, the other dark and restless. Together they feel like two dancers with very different styles. That pairing, of colour and motion, of warmth and wit, is perhaps the truest portrait of the two artists who made it.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the edge of the pool. Let the clicks and the splashes become a kind of rhythm. Stravinsky built music from rhythms that seemed broken, then fitted them together in surprising ways. The fountain does something similar with water and metal. As you walk away toward the old streets of the Marais or the open plaza of the museum, listen behind you. The machines will keep playing, long after you have gone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Sit on the pool's edge and watch one machine's full cycle.
Visit on a sunny day when the colours look brightest.
Combine with a short walk into the Marais streets nearby.
Step inside Saint-Merri church for a quiet contrast.
Keep a little distance from the jets on windy days.
