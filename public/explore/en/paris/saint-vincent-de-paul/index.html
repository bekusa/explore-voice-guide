The guide

The story

Start at the foot of the steps. Below you, traffic moves along the streets of the tenth arrondissement. Above you, a broad stone staircase climbs toward a row of tall columns. The sound changes as you rise. The engines fall behind, and pigeons settle on the pale stone. This is the Church of Saint-Vincent-de-Paul, and it was built to be approached slowly, like a temple on a hill.

The square at your back is Place Franz-Liszt, named after the Hungarian composer and pianist. Around it, the neighbourhood is a working part of Paris. Two of the city's great railway stations, the Gare du Nord and the Gare de l'Est, stand a short walk away. Travellers rush past with suitcases. Few of them look up. That is a pity, because this church holds one of the most unusual stories in nineteenth-century Parisian architecture.

First, the man behind the name. Vincent de Paul was a French priest of the seventeenth century. He gave his life to the poor, the sick, abandoned children, and prisoners. With Louise de Marillac, he founded the Daughters of Charity, a community of women who worked directly among the poor. He also founded a society of priests often called the Lazarists. Their headquarters stood in this same district, at the old priory of Saint-Lazare. So this church honours a saint who once worked on these very streets.

The building you see dates from the first half of the nineteenth century. Work began in the 1820s and finished in the 1840s. It was started by the architect Jean-Baptiste Lepère. It was completed by his son-in-law, Jacques Ignace Hittorff. Hittorff was born in Cologne and settled in Paris. He became one of the leading architects of his age. If you arrived by train at the Gare du Nord, you have already walked through his work. He designed that station too.

Look at the shape of the front. Two square towers rise on either side. Between them stands a porch of columns with a triangular gable above. This is a neoclassical design, meaning it borrows the forms of ancient Greece and Rome. Yet the two towers give it a Christian outline. Inside, the plan follows the early Christian basilica — a long central hall with aisles on each side, the oldest model for a church.

Now raise your eyes to the triangular gable above the columns. It is filled with carved figures. The sculptor Charles-François Lebœuf, known as Nanteuil, created this scene. It shows Saint Vincent de Paul in glory, surrounded by figures who represent his charity. On a sunny afternoon, shadows sharpen the faces. On a grey day, the figures seem to melt back into the stone. It is worth looking twice.

Here is the first surprise. Hittorff believed the temples of ancient Greece were not white at all. He argued they were brightly painted. This idea was controversial in his time, and it started fierce debates among scholars. Hittorff wanted to prove his point here. Under the porch, he installed large painted panels made of enamelled volcanic stone, with religious scenes by the painter Jules Jollivet. Bright colour on a Paris church front was a bold idea.

The idea did not last. Many people found the bright panels shocking. Some critics thought the figures were not proper for a church. Within a few years, the panels were taken down. So the porch you see today is calmer than the one Hittorff imagined. Stand under the columns and look at the back wall. Try to picture it covered in glowing colour. That lost vision is part of this building's story.

Now step inside, if the doors are open. The light softens. Your footsteps echo on the stone. Two rows of columns lead your eye down the nave toward the altar. Above them runs a long painted band, called a frieze. It is one of the great treasures of nineteenth-century French religious painting. It was painted by Hippolyte Flandrin, a student of the famous painter Ingres.

The frieze shows a long procession of saints. Men on one side, women on the other, they walk slowly toward the altar. They are drawn in calm profile against a golden background. Flandrin took inspiration from early Christian mosaics in Italy, especially those of Ravenna, where saints walk in similar lines across the walls. This is the second surprise. The effect is closer to a Byzantine church than to a Paris parish. Follow the figures with your eyes, and you walk with them.

At the far end, the curved wall behind the altar carries another large painting. It is the work of François-Édouard Picot. Christ sits in majesty, with figures gathered around him. Together with Flandrin's frieze, it gives the interior a warm, painted richness. The cool grey stone of the exterior did not prepare you for this. Remember Hittorff's love of colour. Here, inside, he partly got his wish.

Listen, too. The church holds a large organ from the workshop of Aristide Cavaillé-Coll. He was the most celebrated French organ builder of the nineteenth century. If you are lucky enough to arrive during a service or a concert, the sound fills the whole basilica. Even in silence, the space carries a deep acoustic weight. A cough at the back can travel all the way to the altar.

This is still a working parish church. People come here to pray, to light candles, and to sit quietly away from the stations. Please move gently, keep your voice low, and avoid photography during services. That respect is fitting. The saint honoured here spent his life serving others, often people the city ignored. A quiet visit suits his memory better than any grand gesture.

When you leave, pause at the top of the steps. Turn around. The square opens below you, with its trees, its cafés, and its streets running toward the stations. From up here, you can see why the architects raised the church so high. It watches over a neighbourhood of travellers and workers. Walk down slowly. Notice how the noise of the city rises to meet you, one step at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with a walk from the Gare du Nord.
Visit on a sunny afternoon to see the carved gable sharply.
Check the parish schedule for organ concerts before visiting.
Keep quiet and skip photos during services inside.
Rest afterwards at a café on Place Franz-Liszt.
