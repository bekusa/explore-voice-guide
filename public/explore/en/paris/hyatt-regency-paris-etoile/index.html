The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of this tower and tilt your head back. The glass and pale panels climb in one clean line, and the noise of Porte Maillot rolls around you. Cars circle the square. Buses brake and sigh. Somewhere below your feet, metro trains pass through the dark. Few places in Paris feel this open to the sky, and that is part of the story we are about to walk through together.

The building in front of you is the Hyatt Regency Paris Étoile. For most of its life, Parisians knew it by another name — the Concorde Lafayette. It opened in the 1970s as one of the largest hotels in the city, with hundreds of rooms stacked high above the western edge of Paris. The Hyatt name arrived much later, in the 2010s. Many older Parisians still use the old name without thinking.

Why is the hotel called Étoile? The word means star in French. It refers to the Place de l'Étoile, the great roundabout where the Arc de Triomphe stands. Twelve avenues spread out from that arch like the points of a star. One of them, the Avenue de la Grande Armée, runs straight from the arch to where you are standing now. On a clear day, the arch sits at the end of that long, straight line.

That line is no accident. It is part of what Parisians call the historic axis — a single straight route that runs from the Louvre, through the Tuileries Garden, up the Champs-Élysées, under the Arc de Triomphe, and on to the west. Kings and planners extended it over several centuries. This tower stands close to that axis. Look west, and the line continues through the town of Neuilly toward the business towers of La Défense on the horizon.

Now think about what this spot used to be. In the nineteenth century, Paris was ringed by a wall of fortifications with gates where roads crossed into the city. Porte Maillot was one of those gates — porte simply means gate. The wall was later torn down. In its place came the Boulevard Périphérique, the ring road that still divides Paris from its suburbs. This tower rose right on that border, at the very edge of the city.

Here is something many visitors miss. Central Paris has very few tall buildings, and that is a deliberate choice. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the city briefly allowed several towers, mostly at its edges. This hotel belongs to that short era. The Montparnasse Tower, finished in the same period, provoked so much anger that Paris soon tightened its height limits. So this skyline is a kind of time capsule — a moment when Paris tried to grow upward, and then changed its mind.

The tower was not built alone. Right beside it stands the Palais des Congrès, the large convention centre that also opened in the 1970s. The two were planned as a pair. Conference visitors could sleep upstairs and walk to their meetings without stepping into the traffic. That is still how the place works today. On a busy morning, you will see name badges on lanyards, rolling suitcases, and people talking quickly in many languages.

Take a moment to notice the shape of the building. Unlike the stone apartment blocks of central Paris, with their iron balconies and grey zinc roofs, this tower is smooth and vertical. Its surface is a grid of windows. From the ground it looks severe. But from the upper floors, the view does the work. The whole western half of the city opens up, with the Eiffel Tower to the south and the Arc de Triomphe straight down the avenue.

If you go inside, the mood changes at once. The lobby is calm and air-conditioned, a world away from the roundabout outside. Hotels like this one are small cities in their own right. Staff move between shifts at all hours. Breakfast, conferences, late check-ins and early taxis to the airport overlap all day. Behind the reception desk, a building with hundreds of rooms runs on careful timing that guests rarely see.

The tower has long had a bar on its upper floors, and it is the main reason non-guests come here. From up there, Paris looks surprisingly flat and low, a sea of grey roofs broken by domes and spires. The second surprise is the green to the west. The Bois de Boulogne, one of the city's two great woodland parks, spreads out just beyond the ring road. From street level you barely notice it. From above, it looks like a forest pressed against the city.

Back down at ground level, Porte Maillot itself has changed a great deal in recent years. Major works have reshaped the square, with new transport links, new crossings and more space for pedestrians and cyclists. Metro line 1, the oldest line in the Paris network, stops right here. It runs straight under the Champs-Élysées toward the centre. If you want to feel the historic axis in your body, ride it east and watch the station names pass by.

It is fair to say this is not the Paris of postcards. There are no cobbled lanes here and no café terraces under chestnut trees. But it is a very real Paris — the Paris where people arrive for work, where the city meets its suburbs, and where modern buildings stand beside the old royal line of the axis. Many travellers sleep here for a few nights and never think about where they are. Now you know.

Before you leave, turn slowly in a full circle. To one side, the tower rises above you. Across the square, the low, wide shape of the convention centre. Down the avenue, if the air is clear, the Arc de Triomphe. And behind you, the trees of the Bois de Boulogne. Listen to the traffic for a moment, and then choose your direction — into the heart of Paris, or out toward the green.

Practical tips

Visit the upper-floor bar for wide views over western Paris.
Come on a clear evening to see the Arc down the avenue.
Use metro line 1 at Porte Maillot to reach the centre.
Pair your visit with a walk in the Bois de Boulogne.
Expect conference crowds on weekday mornings in the lobby.
