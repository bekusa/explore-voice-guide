The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the chestnut trees, gravel crunches under small shoes. A little bell rings somewhere ahead. There is the creak of a carousel, the splash of a boat ride, and the steady chatter of ducks on the water. The air smells of cut grass and sweet waffles. You are at the northern edge of the Bois de Boulogne, the great wooded park on the western side of Paris. This is the Jardin d'Acclimatation, and families here have been walking these paths for more than a century and a half.

The garden opened in 1860, during the reign of Emperor Napoleon the Third. He and Empress Eugénie came to inaugurate it. The idea behind it was scientific, not playful. It was created by a learned society devoted to what the French called acclimatation. Put simply, that meant bringing plants and animals from distant lands and testing whether they could live and thrive in the French climate. The founding figure was the naturalist Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire. So this place began as a kind of open-air laboratory.

Think about the world of the mid nineteenth century. France was building an empire overseas. Ships were carrying seeds, cattle, birds and exotic creatures across the oceans. Scientists hoped that useful animals, such as new breeds of sheep or llamas for wool, might one day become part of French farming. Visitors came to see these animals up close. For many Parisians, it was the first time they ever saw a kangaroo, a zebra, or an ostrich. Curiosity and science walked side by side here.

Then came a very hard winter. During the Franco-Prussian War, in 1870 and 1871, Paris was surrounded and cut off. Food ran out. People ate horses, cats, even rats. And the animals of this garden were not spared. Two elephants named Castor and Pollux, who had given rides to children here, were killed and their meat was sold by a city butcher. It is a strange and sad story, and a reminder of how close hunger came to the heart of the capital.

There is a darker chapter too, and an honest guide must mention it. From the late 1870s and into the early twentieth century, the garden staged so-called ethnological exhibitions. Groups of people from Africa, the Arctic, Asia and elsewhere were brought here and displayed to crowds, often in reconstructed villages. Historians today often describe these shows as human zoos. Huge numbers of Parisians came to watch. Remembering this does not spoil the garden. It simply helps us understand the attitudes of the colonial age.

Over time, the purpose of the place changed. The scientific mission faded. What remained was a garden for leisure, and above all for children. Rides, games and small attractions slowly appeared among the lawns and ponds. Generations of Parisian families made it part of their childhood. Grandparents who come here today with their grandchildren may remember the same paths, the same trees, and perhaps the same feeling of a special Sunday outing. That sense of continuity is part of what makes this place feel so gentle.

If you arrived on foot from the Porte Maillot side, you may have seen the little train. It runs on a narrow track between the edge of the city and the garden gates. Riding it has long been a ritual for Parisian children. Here is a detail many visitors miss — the train is not only a ride, it is also a practical way into the park. It carries you from the noise of the ring road into the quiet of the trees in just a few minutes.

Look around you at the mix of old and new. In the 1980s, the garden came under the management of a group linked to the luxury company LVMH. A large renovation followed much later, and the garden reopened in 2018 with many new rides and redesigned spaces. Yet care was taken to keep the older character. You will notice wooden pavilions, curving paths, and ponds that still follow the gentle layout of a nineteenth-century landscape garden.

The animals have not disappeared completely. Today, though, they are mostly familiar ones. You can find farmyard animals, birds, and creatures that children can watch calmly from close by. The big exotic species of the past are gone. That change says a lot about how our ideas have moved on. Once, people came here to marvel at the far corners of the empire. Now, a child can stand quietly beside a goat or a hen and learn where milk and eggs come from.

Now turn your eyes toward the trees on the western side. Above them, you may catch sight of something that looks like a ship with glass sails. That is the Fondation Louis Vuitton, an art museum designed by the architect Frank Gehry. It opened in 2014, right beside the garden. The contrast is striking. On one side, a nineteenth-century children's park. On the other, curved glass and steel from the twenty-first century. Few places in Paris put these two worlds so close together.

Pause by the water for a moment. The ponds and little streams are not only pretty, they are part of the garden's old design. Water was always central here, for the animals and for the landscape. Today, small boats drift along a winding channel, and children lean over the sides to touch the surface. Ducks and other birds move between the reeds. On a warm afternoon, the light comes down through the leaves and spreads in soft patches across the water.

As you walk, notice who is around you. This is not mainly a tourist site. It is a local place. You will hear mostly French spoken — parents calling to children, grandparents telling stories, school groups counting heads. Birthday parties gather at picnic spots. Toddlers sleep in pushchairs under the trees. If you want to see an ordinary Parisian weekend, away from the monuments and museums, this garden is one of the best places to watch it unfold.

Before you leave, stand still for a few seconds. Listen again to the gravel, the bells, the laughter, the birds. Think of everything this ground has seen — emperors and scientists, a besieged city, colonial crowds, and now families with ice cream. The garden has changed its purpose many times, but it has never stopped drawing people in. As you walk back toward the gates, look up at the chestnut trees once more. They have watched it all, quietly, season after season.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the little train from the Porte Maillot side.
Visit on a weekday morning for quieter paths.
Combine your visit with the neighbouring Fondation Louis Vuitton.
Bring a picnic and use the shaded lawns.
Wear comfortable shoes for the gravel paths.
