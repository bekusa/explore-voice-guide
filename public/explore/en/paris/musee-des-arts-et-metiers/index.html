The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the high stone vaults of an old church, there is a faint hum of air and the soft scuff of shoes on the floor. Above you, where saints once looked down, early aircraft hang on wires. A heavy brass sphere swings slowly through the nave. This is one of the strangest rooms in Paris. Here a medieval chapel became a cathedral of machines.

You are in the Musée des Arts et Métiers, the museum of arts and crafts, in the third arrondissement. It belongs to the Conservatoire national des arts et métiers, a public institution for technical learning. The museum keeps the tools, prototypes and instruments that changed how people work, travel and communicate. It holds no paintings of kings. Its stars are calculators, looms, engines and clocks, and each one carries a story of human patience.

The idea was born during the French Revolution. In 1794, a priest and revolutionary named Abbé Henri Grégoire proposed a conservatory. It would be a place to collect machines and teach people how to use them. His reasoning was practical. A new republic needed skilled workers, not only new laws. The machines were meant to be studied, copied and improved. In this way, a museum became a classroom for a whole nation.

The buildings around you are much older than that idea. This was the priory of Saint-Martin-des-Champs, a monastery whose name means Saint Martin of the Fields. When it was founded, this area was open country outside the city walls. Paris grew around it, and the fields disappeared under streets. During the Revolution the monastery was closed. The Conservatoire then moved into its halls, its cloister and its church.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. Across the courtyard stands the old monks' refectory, the dining hall. It is a tall, light Gothic room with slender columns and pointed arches. Gothic is the medieval style of ribbed vaults and tall windows that also shaped Notre-Dame. Today the hall serves as the Conservatoire's library. So students still read and work in a room where monks once ate in silence.

Now return to the chapel, where the past and the modern world sit side by side. Look up at the aircraft. One is the Blériot Eleven, a small wooden monoplane with fabric wings. A plane of this type carried Louis Blériot across the English Channel in 1909. Nearby hangs a dark, bat-like machine with ribbed wings. It is the Avion Three of Clément Ader, a French pioneer who dreamed of flight in the late nineteenth century.

Then there is the pendulum. In 1851, the physicist Léon Foucault hung a long pendulum in the Panthéon to show the public something remarkable. Over the hours, the line of its swing slowly turns. The pendulum does not change. The floor turns under it, because the Earth is rotating. It was simple and clear proof that our planet spins. Foucault's original pendulum belongs to this museum, and a pendulum still swings here for visitors to watch.

The second surprise is a heavy wooden carriage on three wheels with a large copper boiler at the front. This is the steam carriage of Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot, built in the late eighteenth century for the French army. It was designed to pull artillery. Historians often call it the first self-propelled vehicle in the world. It looks clumsy, almost like a cart from a fairy tale. Yet every car on the street outside descends from this idea.

The main galleries follow a clear order. The collections are divided into themes such as scientific instruments, materials, construction, communication, energy, mechanics and transport. You can walk from early clocks to early cameras, from telescopes to televisions. One of the most famous objects is the Pascaline. It is a mechanical calculator invented by Blaise Pascal in the seventeenth century, when he was still a young man. Small brass wheels turn to add and subtract numbers.

Look also for the laboratory of Antoine Lavoisier, the chemist who helped found modern chemistry in the eighteenth century. His glass vessels, balances and gas tanks are displayed together. They look like the tools of a careful craftsman, and in a way they were. Not far away you will find looms, including the punched-card system of Joseph Marie Jacquard. Those cards controlled patterns in silk. Later, the same principle helped inspire early computing.

The museum also has a life in fiction. The Italian writer Umberto Eco set the final scenes of his novel Foucault's Pendulum here, in this chapel. In the book, the machines and the old stones become the stage for a dark conspiracy. Many readers come because of the novel. They stand under the vaults and try to picture the night that Eco described. The real room is calmer than the book, but just as unusual.

When you leave, take the metro from the station named Arts et Métiers on line eleven. Step down to the platform and you will find walls covered in riveted copper. Large portholes and round shapes make it feel like the inside of a submarine. The design pays tribute to Jules Verne and his Nautilus. The Belgian artist François Schuiten created it for the Conservatoire's two hundredth anniversary. It is a small extension of the museum, under the street.

Before you go, stand still in the chapel once more. Notice the light through the old windows, falling across wings, wheels and brass. The monks built this space to look toward heaven. The engineers filled it with machines that let people rise into the sky. Watch the pendulum for one more swing. While you stand here, the Earth is turning under your feet.
