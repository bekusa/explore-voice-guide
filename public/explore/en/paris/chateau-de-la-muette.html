The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of western Paris softens here into a low hum. Gravel crunches under someone's shoes. Wind moves through tall chestnut trees at the edge of the Bois de Boulogne. In front of you, behind gates and railings, stands a pale stone mansion. It looks calm and very orderly, like a country house that somehow ended up inside a capital city. This is the Château de la Muette, and the quiet around it has a long story.

Today this building is the headquarters of the OECD, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. Delegates from dozens of countries meet here to compare economies, publish reports, and argue about tax rules and education. So the first surprise is this. One of the most important rooms for global economic policy sits in a residential corner of the sixteenth arrondissement, a short walk from children's playgrounds and quiet apartment blocks.

But the story of this place begins long before economists arrived. The name itself is a small puzzle. Historians do not agree on where it comes from. One theory links it to the old French word for a pack of hunting hounds. Another connects it to the moulting of deer, when stags shed their antlers. Both ideas point to the same past. This land was once royal hunting ground, at the edge of the great forest that later became the Bois de Boulogne.

For centuries, a hunting lodge stood here. French kings and their families came out from the city to ride, to hunt, and to escape the pressure of court. In the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, the estate passed through royal hands. Marguerite de Valois, the queen often remembered as Queen Margot, owned it for a time. She passed it to the young prince who would become King Louis the Thirteenth. From then on, the lodge belonged firmly to the world of the monarchy.

In the eighteenth century, the château became a favourite retreat. Louis the Fifteenth enlarged and rebuilt the old lodge, and spent time here away from the formality of Versailles. Later, the young Louis the Sixteenth and Marie Antoinette stayed here in the early years of their reign. Picture carriages on these roads, hunting horns in the woods, and gardens running out toward the forest. Very little of that royal building survives. The house you see now is a much later creation.

Now comes the moment that made this ground famous across the world. On 21 November 1783, a large hot-air balloon rose from the gardens of the château. It was built by the Montgolfier brothers, paper makers from the south of France. On board were two men, Jean-François Pilâtre de Rozier and the Marquis d'Arlandes. Theirs is recognised as the first free flight with human passengers. In a sense, human flight began very close to where you are standing.

Imagine the scene that afternoon. A crowd gathered on the lawns. The balloon was a huge cloth envelope, decorated in bright colours, filled with hot air from a fire of straw and wool below. The two passengers fed the flames as they drifted across Paris. They crossed the river Seine and came down safely on the other side of the city, after a flight of around half an hour. People had looked at birds for thousands of years. That day, two of them followed.

After the Revolution, the royal estate was broken up and sold. Over the nineteenth century, the land was divided, and the old château was reduced and changed. Part of the property belonged to the Érard family, known across Europe for making pianos and harps. Meanwhile, the city of Paris grew outward. Passy, once a separate village of vineyards and country houses, became part of the capital. Streets, boulevards, and elegant apartment buildings slowly filled the old hunting grounds.

In the early twentieth century, the site found a new owner. Baron Henri de Rothschild, a member of the famous banking family, built the present mansion here in the 1920s. He chose a classical French style that looks back to the eighteenth century. That is the second surprise for many visitors. The building looks old and royal, but it is roughly a hundred years old. It is a modern house wearing the clothes of the age of Louis the Fifteenth.

After the Second World War, Europe needed to rebuild. American aid under the Marshall Plan required a new body to coordinate the recovery. That body was the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation, and in 1949 it moved into the château. In 1961, it became the OECD, with a wider membership that reached beyond Europe. Since then, newer office buildings and a large conference centre have grown up around the old mansion. The château remains the symbolic heart of the complex.

Because this is a working international headquarters, it is not a museum. Access is controlled, and most visitors see it from outside the gates. That is no loss. The real pleasure here is the setting. The château sits beside the Jardin du Ranelagh, a long green space with lawns, old trees, and benches. Families walk dogs here. Children ride small carousels. Nearby, the Musée Marmottan Monet holds a famous collection of paintings by Claude Monet.

Take a moment to notice the neighbourhood around you. The Métro station nearby is called La Muette, after the château. The street names, the shops, and even local cafés carry the same name. The whole district still remembers a hunting lodge that mostly disappeared long ago. The streets of Passy are quiet and polished, with stone apartment buildings, iron balconies, and small bakeries. This is a calmer, more domestic Paris than the crowded centre.

Before you walk on, look once more at the pale facade and the trees behind it. Think about the layers on this one piece of ground. A royal hunting forest. A king's retreat. A crowd watching two men rise into the sky. A banker's private mansion. And now, a meeting place for governments from around the world. As you head toward the gardens or the Métro, listen again for the wind in the chestnut trees. It is the oldest sound here.

Practical tips
