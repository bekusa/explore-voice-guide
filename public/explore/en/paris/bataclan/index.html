The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of Boulevard Voltaire and listen for a moment. Scooters hum past. A café terrace clinks with glasses. Then your eye lands on something that does not quite belong to a Paris boulevard. It is a facade painted in bright colours, with curved eaves and ornament that looks borrowed from a dream of faraway China. This is the Bataclan, one of the oldest concert halls in the city.

The building opened in the mid-1860s, during the reign of Napoleon the Third. Paris was being torn up and rebuilt in those years. Wide new boulevards cut through old neighbourhoods, and the city filled with places of entertainment. The Bataclan was built as a café-concert, a very Parisian kind of venue. People sat at tables, ate and drank, and watched songs, comic sketches and short musical shows on a small stage.

The name sounds strange, even playful, and that is the point. It comes from a comic operetta by Jacques Offenbach, the composer who gave Paris the cancan. His short piece, called Ba-ta-clan, appeared in 1855. It was a light farce set in an imaginary China. The title itself was a joke. In French slang, the whole bataclan means the whole lot, all that stuff, the entire mess. The founders of the hall chose a name that already made audiences smile.

That explains the architecture in front of you. Nineteenth-century Europe had a fashion called chinoiserie. It was a European fantasy of Chinese style, not a copy of real Chinese buildings. Designers used pagoda shapes, painted wood, lanterns and curling decoration. The Bataclan once had a tall pagoda-like roof on top. That roof is long gone, but the facade still carries the spirit of the original idea... a theatre dressed up as an exotic pavilion.

Here is something many visitors miss. For much of its life, the Bataclan was not a rock venue at all. Over the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, it hosted operetta, music hall, and variety acts. A young Maurice Chevalier, later one of France's best-known singers and film stars, performed here early in his career. In the 1920s, the hall changed again and became a cinema for a period. Like many Paris theatres, it followed the money and the fashions of each decade.

The building's fortunes rose and fell. Some parts of the original complex were sold off or rebuilt over the years. The hall you see today is smaller than the grand entertainment palace of the 1860s. Still, its core function survived. For more than a century and a half, people have queued on this stretch of pavement, waiting for the doors to open and the lights to go down.

In the 1970s, the Bataclan found a new identity. It became a home for rock, pop and alternative music. One evening in early 1972 is still remembered by music fans around the world. Lou Reed, John Cale and Nico, three key figures from the American band the Velvet Underground, played together here. A recording of that night was later released, and it is often simply called Le Bataclan '72. For many listeners, this hall became a name on an album cover long before they ever saw Paris.

In the decades that followed, the room built a reputation that musicians loved. It holds around fifteen hundred people. That is large enough for real energy, but small enough that the crowd feels close to the stage. Rock bands, rappers, comedians and singers from many countries passed through. For the people of eastern Paris, it was simply the neighbourhood concert hall — a place for a Friday night out.

Then came the night that changed how the world hears this name. On 13 November 2015, a series of coordinated terrorist attacks struck Paris and its northern suburb of Saint-Denis. Café terraces and the national stadium were targeted. Here, at the Bataclan, gunmen entered during a concert by the American band Eagles of Death Metal. Ninety people were killed inside this hall. Across the city that night, 130 people lost their lives. It was the deadliest attack in France since the Second World War.

If you look near the entrance, you will find a memorial plaque. It lists the names of the people who died here. Many visitors stop in silence. Some leave flowers. The plaque is modest in size, and that is part of its weight. It sits at the height of a passer-by, on an ordinary street, where people walk dogs and carry shopping home. The memory is not sealed away in a museum. It lives in the everyday city.

The question after 2015 was whether the hall would ever open again. It did. Almost exactly one year after the attack, in November 2016, the Bataclan reopened with a concert by the British singer Sting. The interior had been fully renovated, but the owners kept the building's character. The decision sent a clear message. Music would return to this room. Parisians often describe that choice as a refusal to let fear decide the shape of their city.

Today, the Bataclan works as a concert venue again. On show nights, the pavement fills with fans checking tickets on their phones. Musicians from France and abroad still choose it, partly for the sound, and partly because of its history. The neighbourhood around it, close to Rue Oberkampf, is known for small bars, late dinners and a young crowd. The area carries its loss, but it is not quiet. Life here has continued, and that continuity is part of the story.

Before you move on, take one more look at the facade. Notice the bright paint, the curved lines, the playful Offenbach joke still written into the building. Then notice the plaque, the people pausing beside it, and the traffic moving past on Boulevard Voltaire. Both stories belong to this place — the laughter it was built for, and the grief it now holds. As you walk away, listen for the music of the street again... the cafés, the voices, the scooters. That ordinary sound is what this corner of Paris chose to keep.
