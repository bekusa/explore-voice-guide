The guide

The story

Close your eyes for a moment on this stretch of avenue George V. You hear taxis, the soft hiss of tyres, heels on stone, and a doorman's low greeting. This is one of the smartest addresses in Paris. Luxury hotels and fashion houses line the street. The Champs-Élysées is a short walk one way, and the Seine is a short walk the other. And here, behind a modest entrance, is one of the most famous small theatres in the world.

This is the Crazy Horse. It opened in 1951, and it was the idea of one man, Alain Bernardin. He was not a showman by training. He came from the world of art and antiques. That background matters, because he did not want to build a loud music hall full of feathers. He wanted something closer to a gallery, where the female form was presented with the care of a painter or a sculptor.

The name surprises many first-time visitors. Why would a Paris cabaret carry the name of a famous Lakota leader from the American plains? Bernardin was fascinated by the American West, and the venue first opened as the Crazy Horse Saloon. The early decor leaned into cowboy imagery. Over the decades the Western theme faded. The name stayed, and today few people inside the theatre think of saloons or prairies at all.

As you go in, notice how quickly the street disappears. The light drops. The colour shifts to deep red. The room is intimate, not grand. Velvet seating and small tables face a compact stage, and almost every seat is close enough to see a performer's expression. That closeness is part of the design. The Crazy Horse was never meant to feel like a stadium show. It was meant to feel like a private viewing.

Here is the first idea that changes how people see this place. At the Crazy Horse, light is the costume. Bernardin and the creative teams who followed him treated lighting as the main material of the show. Stripes, patterns, coloured beams and projected images fall across the dancers' bodies. A performer can appear dressed one moment and abstract the next, simply because the light has changed. The effect is often more geometric than sensual, closer to modern art than to burlesque.

The dancers themselves are part of that design. The company is known for choosing performers with similar proportions, so that a line of dancers looks almost like one repeated figure. Many of them train in classical ballet before they arrive. Rehearsals are demanding, and the choreography depends on exact timing with the lights. One step out of place, and the illusion breaks. What looks effortless from the seats is a very precise piece of stagecraft.

Another detail most visitors miss is the names. The dancers perform under playful stage names, often built from puns, wordplay or invented personalities. It is a long tradition of the house. The names add humour and a little mystery. They also separate the performer on stage from the woman who walks out the stage door afterwards, into the ordinary Paris night.

After Bernardin's death in the 1990s, the Crazy Horse faced a real question. Could a show built around one man's taste survive without him? The answer was to invite outside artists in. Over the following years, the theatre worked with choreographers, designers and performers from other fields. This kept the house style, but it also let new ideas enter the room.

Some of those collaborations reached far beyond the cabaret world. The French choreographer Philippe Decouflé, known for his playful and highly visual stage work, created a show here. The shoe designer Christian Louboutin, famous for his red-soled heels, also directed a production at the Crazy Horse. And the American burlesque performer Dita Von Teese appeared here as a guest star. Each name brought a different audience through these doors, from dance lovers to fashion followers.

That mix of audiences is what you might notice tonight. Look around the room before the curtain rises. You may see couples on an anniversary, groups of friends, business visitors, and people who simply came because of the theatre's reputation in design and fashion circles. Paris has larger and more lavish revues. The Crazy Horse built its name on a smaller scale, sharper ideas, and a strong visual identity.

It is worth being clear about what kind of show this is. It is an adult performance, with artistic nudity, and it is meant for grown-up audiences. But the tone is closer to a fashion shoot or a light installation than to anything crude. Humour plays a part too. Some numbers are slow and hypnotic, while others are quick, cheeky and almost cartoon-like in their timing.

When you step back outside, pause on the pavement and let your eyes adjust. The contrast is sharp. Inside, everything is controlled — every beam of light, every pose, every second of music. Out here, the city returns with its traffic and its untidy noise. Walk a few minutes south and you reach the Place de l'Alma and the river, where the Eiffel Tower often appears between the buildings, lit up against the dark.

So before you leave avenue George V, look once more at that discreet entrance. It is easy to walk past it in daylight without a second glance. Yet for more than seventy years, this small room has shaped how people think about light, the body and the stage. Listen to the street again — the tyres, the footsteps, the doorman's voice — and carry a little of that red glow with you into the Paris night.
