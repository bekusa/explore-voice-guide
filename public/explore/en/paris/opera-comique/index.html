The guide

The story

Stand for a moment in the small square in front of you. Notice how quiet it is. Only a short walk away, the Grands Boulevards carry buses, scooters and crowds. Here the noise drops, and pale stone fills your view. Tall arched windows look down on you, and a row of statues lines the roofline. This is the Opéra-Comique, also known as the Salle Favart. It is one of the oldest musical institutions in France, and one of the most surprising.

Here is the first surprise. The building does not face the boulevard. Its main façade looks onto this small square, Place Boïeldieu, with its back turned to the busy street behind. A popular story says the original company chose this on purpose. They did not want anyone to mistake their elegant theatre for the rough, cheap stages of the boulevard. Whether that story is fully true or not, the effect is real. You arrive here almost by accident, as if you found a private room in a crowded house.

The name can confuse first-time visitors. Opéra comique does not mean comic opera, or at least not only that. In French tradition, the term describes a style of musical theatre. The singers perform songs and arias, but they also speak lines of ordinary dialogue between the music. Many of these works are tragic. Some end in death and heartbreak. So the word comique here points to the form, not the mood. It is closer to a play with songs than to a farce.

The company began far from this elegant square. In the early eighteenth century, its roots lay in the seasonal fairs of Paris. These were the Saint-Germain and Saint-Laurent fairs, where traders sold goods and performers entertained the crowds. The great official theatres held legal monopolies over spoken drama and opera. So the fair performers found clever ways around the rules. They used songs, signs and popular tunes. From that mix of street wit and music, a new kind of theatre grew.

In the 1760s, the fair troupe joined forces with the Comédie-Italienne, the Italian actors who had long performed in Paris. Together they needed a permanent home. The first theatre on this site opened in the 1780s. The street on the right is Rue Favart, and the theatre carries the same name. Both honour Charles-Simon Favart, an eighteenth-century writer of these sung plays. He helped shape the genre at a time when it still fought for respect.

Now look at the square itself. It takes its name from François-Adrien Boieldieu, a composer of the early nineteenth century. His opera La Dame blanche, which means The White Lady, became one of the most performed works in this house. So the composer, the playwright and the theatre all share one small corner of the city. Few places in Paris tie a neighbourhood so closely to a single art form. Take a moment to read the street signs around you.

The building in front of you is not the first theatre here. It is the third. The second hall saw the most famous premiere in the company's history. In 1875, Georges Bizet presented a new work called Carmen. The audience was not ready for it. They saw a working woman who smoked, flirted and chose her own fate. Many critics found it shocking. Bizet died only a few months later, before he could know that Carmen would become one of the most performed operas in the world.

That same stage hosted other premieres that are now part of every opera lover's memory. Offenbach's The Tales of Hoffmann opened here in 1881, after the composer's death. Delibes' Lakmé followed, with its famous Flower Duet, which many listeners know from films and advertisements. Massenet's Manon also had its first performance in this house. When you hear any of these melodies, you are hearing music that first sounded a few steps from where you stand.

Then came disaster. In 1887, during a performance, fire broke out in the second Salle Favart. The theatre was lit by gas, and the flames spread quickly through the upper levels. Many people died, most of them in the cheaper seats high up. The tragedy shocked Paris. It led to stricter safety rules for theatres across France, including better exits and the move toward electric light. The building you see today rose from those ashes, and it opened at the end of the nineteenth century.

The architect of this third hall was Louis Bernier. He gave Paris a theatre in the rich style of the Belle Époque, the confident years before the First World War. If you go inside for a performance or a guided visit, you will find red velvet, gilded balconies and painted ceilings. The foyers hold large paintings and sculptures linked to music and theatre. Compared with the grand Palais Garnier, this house feels intimate. The audience sits close to the stage, and every spoken word carries.

Here is the second surprise. This smaller house gave the world one of the most radical works of early modern opera. In 1902, Claude Debussy premiered Pelléas and Mélisande on this stage. Its music floats and drifts, with no grand showpiece arias. Some listeners were confused, and some were deeply moved. Many musicians now see that night as a turning point for twentieth-century music. The new century began, in a sense, in this quiet square.

The theatre is still very much alive. It became a national theatre of France in the early twenty-first century, and it closed for a long renovation in the 2010s. Today it presents both rediscovered works from its own past and new productions. The company often brings back forgotten operas that once filled these seats. So when you check its programme, you may find a title that has not been staged for generations.

Before you leave, look up at the façade once more. Notice the statues along the top and the carved details around the windows. Then turn and listen to the square. You may hear footsteps on the paving, a café chair scraping, or the distant hum of the boulevard. Somewhere behind those walls, perhaps, a singer is warming up. As you walk away, remember that Carmen first stood on a stage just here, and that Paris turned its back on her at first.
