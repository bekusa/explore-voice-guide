The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Gravel crunches under passing feet. Somewhere behind you, a child chases pigeons across the Tuileries Garden. Ahead, a long, pale stone building sits on a raised terrace, its tall arched windows catching the grey-gold light of Paris. It looks calm and orderly, almost polite. Yet this quiet hall has held a sports craze, a royal emperor's whim, the darkest years of the twentieth century, and some of the most loved paintings in the world.

This is the Jeu de Paume. The name sounds elegant, but it simply means "the game of the palm." It refers to an old court game, the ancestor of modern tennis. In its earliest form, players struck the ball with the palm of the hand. Later came gloves, and then rackets. The English call it real tennis. For centuries, French nobles played it with real passion, in enclosed courts where the ball bounced off walls and sloping roofs.

The building you see dates from the reign of Napoleon the Third, in the early 1860s. It was built as a court for this game, right inside the gardens of the old royal palace. Think about that for a second. In the middle of the most formal garden in Paris, the emperor's circle wanted a place to sweat, shout, and chase a ball. The high windows were not decoration first. They let in the daylight that players needed to follow a fast, hard ball.

Now turn and look across the garden toward the river. Near the Seine, you may notice a second long building that looks almost like a twin. That is the Orangerie, built a little earlier to shelter the garden's orange trees through the winter. Together, the two buildings frame the western end of the Tuileries, close to the Place de la Concorde. One was made for plants, the other for play. Both ended up as homes for art.

That change came in the early twentieth century. The game of palm had faded from fashion, and the empty hall found a new life as an exhibition space. Between the two world wars, it became known for showing art from outside France. Paris was then the centre of the art world, and this gallery became a window onto painters from other countries. It was a modest start, but it set the pattern for everything that followed.

And then the story turns dark. During the German occupation of Paris in the Second World War, the Nazis took over this building. A special looting unit used it as a sorting depot. Paintings, drawings, and sculptures arrived here by the truckload. Most were stolen from Jewish families and collectors across France. Inside these walls, officials sorted the works, valued them, and selected pieces for Nazi leaders and for shipment to Germany.

Here is a detail that many visitors miss. A French museum employee named Rose Valland stayed on in the building throughout the occupation. She was quiet, careful, and easy to overlook — and that was her strength. She secretly noted what came in, what went out, and where the trains were heading. She risked her life to do it. After the liberation, her records helped Allied teams track down and recover a large number of stolen works.

Some works never left at all. Accounts from the time describe paintings by modern artists, condemned by the Nazis as "degenerate," being destroyed by fire nearby. It is hard to imagine, standing here among the clipped trees and green metal chairs. When you walk around the building, look for a plaque that honours Rose Valland. It is small. Most people pass it without slowing down. It is worth a pause.

After the war, the Jeu de Paume found a much happier role. It became the home of France's great Impressionist collection. For decades, people climbed these steps to see Monet, Renoir, Degas, and their circle. Many Parisians of an older generation still remember the gallery this way — bright rooms, crowds in front of water lilies and dancers, and the garden glowing outside the windows. It became one of the most beloved small museums in the city.

In the 1980s, that collection moved across the river to the Musée d'Orsay, the former railway station on the Left Bank. Its vast spaces could hold far more paintings. The Jeu de Paume closed for renovation, and the interior was rebuilt as a modern gallery. The old stone shell stayed. Inside, the rooms became clean, white, and full of controlled light. It reopened in the early 1990s as a gallery for contemporary art.

Today, the Jeu de Paume is devoted above all to photography, film, and the moving image. It does not keep a fixed permanent display in the way a classic museum does. Instead, it presents changing exhibitions. One season you might find a great twentieth-century photographer. The next, a young video artist, or a show about how images shape politics and memory. So every visit can be different, and the building feels alive rather than finished.

There is something fitting in this. A building made to follow a ball by daylight now asks you to think about light itself — light on film, light on a screen, light caught in a single frame. A building once used to steal images now shows how images are made and how they can be misused. Few places in Paris carry so many different lives inside one set of walls.

Before you go, step back from the entrance and look again at the facade. Notice the rhythm of the arched windows, the pale stone, the terrace that lifts the building above the busy square. Listen to the traffic swirling around the Place de la Concorde, and then to the softer sound of the garden behind you. Take a slow breath here. As you walk away, think of the players, the painters, the thieves, and the one quiet woman with her notebook — all of them once stood exactly where you are standing now.
