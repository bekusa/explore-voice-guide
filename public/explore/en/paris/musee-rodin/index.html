The guide

The story

Listen for the gravel first. It crunches softly under your shoes as you walk the garden paths, and between those footsteps you may hear birds in the lime trees and a faint hum of traffic beyond the walls. Bronze figures stand among the hedges, dark against the green. Some lean forward. Some twist away. Their surfaces carry the marks of fingers that pressed into wet clay more than a century ago. Welcome to the Musée Rodin. It is a museum, yes, but it was also one of the last homes of the sculptor himself.

The elegant pale stone building in front of you is called the Hôtel Biron. In Paris, the word hôtel here means a grand private townhouse, not a place to stay overnight. It was built in the early eighteenth century for a wealthy financier. Look at its calm, balanced facade. You can see tall windows, gentle stone ornament, and a sense of restraint. This is the refined taste of the French aristocracy in the decades before the Revolution.

The house had many lives before art filled it. During the nineteenth century, it became a convent school run by Catholic nuns. They educated the daughters of well-off families here. When French law separated the Church from the State in the early twentieth century, the nuns had to leave. The building then sat in an odd state of waiting. Its rooms were rented out cheaply, and the garden slowly grew wild.

Here is the first surprise. Rodin did not discover this house by himself. The poet Rainer Maria Rilke, who worked for a time as Rodin's secretary, lived here and told the sculptor about it. Rodin came, saw the light in the rooms and the overgrown garden, and rented space around 1908. His neighbours were remarkable too. At different times, the painter Henri Matisse, the writer Jean Cocteau, and the dancer Isadora Duncan also rented rooms in this building.

By then, Auguste Rodin was the most famous living sculptor in the world. It had not always been that way. As a young man, he failed to win entry to the most prestigious art school in Paris, and he spent years working for other artists. His work often shocked critics. People said his figures looked too real, too rough, too unfinished. Today, that roughness is exactly what visitors come to see.

Rodin made the state an unusual offer. He would give his sculptures, drawings, and personal collection to the French nation. In return, the house would become a museum dedicated to his work. The agreement was made during the First World War. Rodin died in 1917, and the museum opened two years later. So when you walk these rooms, you walk where he worked, received visitors, and planned how he would be remembered.

Now turn toward the most famous figure in the garden — The Thinker. A muscular man sits on a rock, his chin resting on his hand, his whole body tense with effort. Many people assume he is a philosopher. In fact, Rodin first imagined him as the poet Dante, looking down at the souls described in his long medieval poem about the afterlife. Only later did the figure take on a life of its own as a symbol of human thought.

That poem leads us to The Gates of Hell. This huge bronze doorway was commissioned in 1880 for a museum of decorative arts that was never built. Rodin worked on it for decades. Look closely and you will find small bodies falling, clinging, and twisting across its surface. Here is the second surprise. Many of Rodin's best-known works began life as tiny figures on these gates. The Thinker sits near the top. Even the lovers of The Kiss were first planned for this doorway.

In another part of the garden stands The Burghers of Calais. The city of Calais asked Rodin to honour an episode from the Hundred Years' War, the long medieval conflict between England and France. According to the chronicles, six leading citizens offered their lives to the English king to save their besieged town. Rodin did not show proud heroes. He showed tired, frightened men with heavy hands and bare feet. Walk around the group and look at each face in turn.

Then find Balzac, the great nineteenth-century novelist. A writers' society commissioned this monument, and when Rodin presented his plaster version, they rejected it. The figure is wrapped in a heavy robe, leaning back, with a huge, rough head. Critics mocked it. Rodin kept it and believed in it. Many art historians now see it as one of the moments when sculpture turned toward modern art, more interested in presence and energy than in a careful likeness.

Inside the house, the mood changes. Daylight falls across wooden floors and white marble. Here you will find The Kiss, carved in smooth stone, with the two lovers seeming to rise out of the rough block beneath them. Look for that contrast between polished skin and unfinished stone. Rodin used it again and again. He often left assistants to carve the marble from his models, but the ideas, and the clay, came from his own hands.

Do not miss the room given to Camille Claudel. She was a gifted sculptor who worked in Rodin's studio. She became his student, his collaborator, and for some years his lover. Their relationship ended painfully, and her later life was deeply sad. For a long time, her own work stood in his shadow. Today her sculptures have their own space here, and many visitors find them as moving as anything Rodin made.

Step back outside for a moment and look up past the trees. Above the garden wall, you can see a golden dome shining in the sky. That is Les Invalides, the old military hospital where Napoleon is buried. It is only a short walk away. From the garden, the dome and Rodin's bronzes share the same view, a quiet meeting of two very different kinds of French memory.

Before you leave, take one slow walk along the paths. Notice how the bronzes change as clouds pass and the light shifts. A shoulder catches the sun. A face falls into shadow. Rodin wanted his figures to feel alive, and out here, in the open air, they almost do. As the gravel crunches under your feet again, look back once at the pale stone house — the home he chose, and the place he left to all of us.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the garden while the morning light is soft.
Walk all the way around The Burghers of Calais.
Save time for the Camille Claudel room inside.
Bring a light jacket for the shaded garden paths.
Pause on a garden bench to view the sculptures slowly.
