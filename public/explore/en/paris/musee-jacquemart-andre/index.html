The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the traffic of Boulevard Haussmann rolls past in a steady grey hum. Ahead, a gently sloping ramp leads up into a quiet courtyard of pale stone. Your footsteps change as you climb. The noise of modern Paris drops away, and a private house appears, set back from the street like a secret kept on purpose.

This is the Musée Jacquemart-André. Before it was a museum, it was a home. A very particular home, built for a very particular couple. Almost everything you will see inside was chosen by the two people who lived here. That is the key to this place. You are not walking through a gallery designed by curators. You are walking through the taste, the ambition, and the habits of a marriage.

The man was Édouard André. He came from a wealthy Protestant banking family and inherited a large fortune. He lived in the age of the Second Empire, when Paris was being torn up and rebuilt along wide new boulevards. Boulevard Haussmann itself was part of that great rebuilding. Édouard wanted a house that matched the new city — grand, modern, and made for entertaining. He hired the architect Henri Parent, and the mansion rose here in the late 1860s and early 1870s.

The woman was Nelly Jacquemart. She was a painter, and a respected one, known for her portraits of the Parisian elite. Here is the first surprise. She first entered Édouard's life not as a fiancée, but as an artist hired to paint his portrait. Years later, in 1881, they married. She was not a wealthy heiress. She brought something else to the partnership — a trained eye, and a hunger for art that would shape every room of this house.

As you enter, notice how the house was built for two kinds of life. Some rooms are formal and public. They were made to impress hundreds of guests at evening receptions. Other rooms are smaller and more intimate. They were made for the couple themselves. The architect planned the reception rooms in a sequence, so that a visitor would move from one space into the next, each one opening onto another. Walk slowly, and you will feel that rhythm.

In the grand salons, look at the gilding, the carved panels, and the silk on the walls. This is the style of the eighteenth century, the age of Louis the Fifteenth and Louis the Sixteenth, admired again by wealthy Parisians in the late nineteenth century. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Some of the partitions between these rooms were designed to move. When the couple held a large party, the walls could be shifted, and several salons became one great hall.

The French paintings hang in these rooms as if they were family furniture. You will find works by artists such as Boucher, Fragonard, and Chardin — painters of soft skin, pale skies, and quiet domestic scenes. There is also a portrait by Élisabeth Vigée Le Brun, one of the most successful women painters of her century. It is worth pausing on her. In a house shaped by a woman artist, her presence on these walls feels like a quiet conversation across time.

Now find the staircase. This is the moment that stops many people. A double staircase curves upward in two graceful spirals, set inside what was once a winter garden. In the late nineteenth century, a winter garden was a glass-roofed room full of plants, where wealthy families could enjoy greenery through the grey Paris months. Stand at the bottom and look up. The marble, the railings, and the light from above make the whole space feel like a stage.

Raise your eyes higher, to the wall above the stairs. Here is a fresco by Giovanni Battista Tiepolo, the great Venetian painter of the eighteenth century. It was not painted here. Nelly and Édouard bought it from a villa in the Veneto, the region around Venice, and had it carefully removed and brought to Paris. Think about that for a moment. A painting made on the plaster of an Italian wall now floats above a staircase on Boulevard Haussmann.

Upstairs is the part of the house the couple called their Italian Museum. This was their real passion. Year after year, they travelled to Italy, and Nelly in particular hunted for paintings and sculpture of the Renaissance — the period, roughly the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, when Italian artists rediscovered the art of ancient Rome and Greece. The rooms are arranged by city. One room gathers Florentine sculpture. Another holds Venetian painting, with its warm colour and soft evening light.

Among the treasures here, look for Paolo Uccello's Saint George and the Dragon, a small panel with a knight, a princess, and a very strange beast. Look too for works by Mantegna and Botticelli. Here is the second surprise. Few private collectors in France owned Italian Renaissance art at this level. Many Paris museums would envy these rooms. And elsewhere in the house hangs a painting attributed to Rembrandt, showing the pilgrims at Emmaus — so the Dutch Golden Age has a voice here too.

Édouard died in 1894. Nelly lived on for almost two more decades, and she kept collecting. The couple had no children. Before her death in 1912, she made a decision that explains why you can stand here today. She left the house and its collections to the Institut de France, the great French academic institution, on the condition that it open to the public. The museum opened its doors in 1913, and the rooms were kept close to the way the couple knew them.

Before you leave, consider stopping in the café. It sits in the couple's former dining room, under a ceiling painted by Tiepolo. Few cafés in the world can offer that. Sit for a while. Watch the light move across the painted sky, and listen to the clink of cups where Édouard and Nelly once hosted their guests. It is one of the simplest ways to feel what daily life in this house was like.

As you walk back down the ramp toward the boulevard, turn around once. Look at the pale facade, the windows, the quiet courtyard. Behind that stone is the private world of two people who spent their lives choosing beautiful things — and then gave them all away. Now step back into the traffic and the noise. The city of Haussmann is still moving around you, much as it did when this house was new.
