The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the edge of the little garden. Behind you, buses and scooters rush along the rue de Rivoli. In front of you, a pale stone tower rises out of the trees, covered in carved pinnacles, gargoyles and lace-like tracery. It looks as if it belongs to a cathedral. But look around its base. There is no nave, no choir, no church at all. The tower stands alone, a fragment that outlived the building it was made for.

This is the Tour Saint-Jacques. It is the bell tower of a church called Saint-Jacques-de-la-Boucherie, which once stood on this spot. The name tells you two things. Saint-Jacques is Saint James, the apostle whose shrine lies at Santiago de Compostela in Spain. And la Boucherie means the butchers. For centuries this was the butchers' quarter of Paris, close to the river and the old central markets. Wealthy meat traders helped to pay for the church, and their money shaped what you see now.

The tower was built in the early sixteenth century, roughly between 1509 and 1523. That date surprises many visitors. By then, the Renaissance was already arriving in France from Italy. Yet the masons here chose the late Gothic style known as Flamboyant — named for the flame-like curves in its stone tracery. So this tower is one of the last great statements of medieval Gothic in Paris, built just as that style was going out of fashion.

Let your eyes climb slowly. The tower rises more than fifty metres above the street. Notice how the surface is never flat. Every buttress ends in a small spire. Every ledge carries carved figures or leaves. Near the top, at the four corners, you can pick out the symbols of the four Gospel writers. There is a lion for Mark, an eagle for John, a bull for Luke, and a winged man, often read as an angel, for Matthew.

And at the very summit, a statue of Saint James looks out over the city. He was the patron of the whole church, and of the pilgrims who came here. For many travellers in the Middle Ages and after, this neighbourhood was a gathering point on the long road south to Santiago de Compostela. Pilgrims would set out from Paris along the rue Saint-Jacques on the Left Bank. The street still carries his name today.

That pilgrim connection is why this lonely tower has international status. In 1998, UNESCO listed the Routes of Santiago de Compostela in France as a World Heritage Site. The listing covers a chain of churches, bridges and hospices across the country. The Tour Saint-Jacques is one of the links in that chain. It is a strange thought — a modern Paris traffic junction, officially part of a medieval walking route.

So what happened to the church? The answer is the French Revolution. In the 1790s, many religious buildings were seized by the state and sold. Saint-Jacques-de-la-Boucherie was sold off and demolished for its stone. Only the tower was spared. Historians generally say the buyers were required to keep it standing, though the exact reasons are still discussed. Either way, the rest of the church vanished, and the tower was left alone.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. In the early nineteenth century, the empty tower found a new and very practical job. It was used to make lead shot for guns. Molten lead was poured from a height, and the drops cooled into small round pellets as they fell. A tall, hollow tower was ideal for this. For a time, this Gothic monument was a piece of industrial equipment, not a place of prayer.

The City of Paris later bought the tower and protected it. In the 1850s, during the great rebuilding of Paris under Napoleon the Third and his prefect Baron Haussmann, the streets around it were cut wide and straight. The ground level changed, and the tower was set on a new raised base. The garden around you was laid out at this time. It is often described as one of the first public squares of its kind in Paris, inspired by the green squares of London.

Now walk to the base of the tower and look for a seated figure in white stone. This is Blaise Pascal, the seventeenth-century mathematician and philosopher. He is here because of his experiments on air pressure. Pascal wanted to prove that the air presses less heavily the higher you go. The most famous test took place on a mountain in central France. But tradition links this tower to his work too, and the statue celebrates that link to the science of the atmosphere.

That scientific story did not end with Pascal. In later years, the top of the tower was used for weather observation, and instruments have been placed up there. So this old bell tower has served faith, then industry, then science. Few buildings in Paris have changed their purpose so many times while keeping the same shape.

There is one more name that floats around this place — Nicolas Flamel. He was a real fourteenth-century Parisian, a scribe and book dealer who lived nearby and gave money to this parish. After his death, a legend grew up that he was an alchemist who had discovered how to make gold, and even how to live forever. There is no good evidence for any of that. But locals still connect his name to this quarter, and you will find a street named after him a short walk away.

The tower was carefully restored in the early twenty-first century, and the stone is now cleaner and paler than it was for generations. In some seasons, small guided groups can climb the narrow spiral staircase to the top. There are roughly three hundred steps. From the summit, you can see Notre-Dame, the Seine, and the rooftops of the Marais spread out around you.

Before you leave, step back to the edge of the garden once more. Watch how the light moves on the carved stone, and how pigeons settle in the hollows of the pinnacles. Listen to the traffic, and then to the quieter sound of people on the benches. Five hundred years ago, this spot was full of butchers, bells and pilgrims. Today it is a small green pause in the middle of Paris. As you walk on toward Châtelet or the river, look back once. The tower will still be there, alone above the trees.

Before you go

Practical tips

Rest on a garden bench to take in the tower slowly.
Book ahead if you want to join a summit climb.
Bring binoculars to study the carvings near the top.
Combine your visit with a walk to Notre-Dame nearby.
Visit in late afternoon when sunlight warms the pale stone.
