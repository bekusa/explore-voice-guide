The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the plane trees, the noise of Paris drops away. You hear water falling softly into a long, dark basin. The light here is green and filtered, even at noon. The gravel crunches under your shoes, and the air feels a few degrees cooler than the open lawns behind you. This corner of the Luxembourg Garden feels set apart, a shaded room without walls. At the far end stands the fountain itself, a dark stone grotto with white figures inside.

This is the Medici Fountain, and its story begins with a queen. Marie de' Medici was born in Florence, into the powerful Medici family of bankers and rulers. She married King Henri the Fourth of France. After his assassination in 1610, she ruled as regent for her young son, Louis the Thirteenth. Later she wanted a palace of her own, far from the Louvre and its politics. She chose land on the Left Bank, and the Luxembourg Palace began to rise in the 1610s.

Marie missed Florence. Her new palace was designed to remind her of home, especially the Pitti Palace, where she grew up. The heavy, rusticated stone you see on the palace walls follows that Florentine taste. Rusticated means the blocks are cut with deep grooves and rough faces, so each stone stands out. The fountain grew from the same longing. It was built around 1630 as a grotto, an artificial cave, a fashion Italian gardens had loved for generations.

The design is traditionally credited to Tommaso Francini, a Florentine engineer who worked on fountains for the French court. Some historians are less certain about who drew it, so the attribution stays open. What matters is the idea. In Italian Renaissance gardens, a grotto was a place for surprise and shade. Water dripped over rough stone. Visitors stepped out of the bright sun into something cool and a little mysterious. Look at the fountain now, and you can still feel that intention.

Here is the first surprise. This fountain is not in its original spot. In the 1860s, Baron Haussmann was cutting wide new boulevards through Paris. One of them, the Rue de Médicis, ran right along this edge of the garden. The fountain stood in the way. Instead of demolishing it, the city took it apart and moved it, stone by stone, about thirty metres closer to the palace. It was rebuilt facing a new direction.

That move also gave the fountain its most famous feature. Before the 1860s, there was no long basin. The architect in charge of the work, Alphonse de Gisors, added the narrow pool stretching out in front of you, lined with stone urns and framed by plane trees. It created this long, quiet perspective. So the scene that feels so old and settled is partly a nineteenth-century creation. It is a Renaissance grotto placed inside a Second Empire stage set.

Now look into the central niche. The sculptures there date from the same restoration, carved by Auguste Ottin. They tell a tale from ancient Greek myth, retold by the Roman poet Ovid. Below, in white marble, two young lovers rest together. They are Acis, a shepherd, and Galatea, a sea nymph. Their bodies are pale and smooth against the dark rock. They look calm, unaware of any danger. But they are not alone in this cave.

Raise your eyes. Above them, crouching on the rocks, is a huge bronze figure. This is Polyphemus, the one-eyed giant, or Cyclops. He also loved Galatea, and he has found the two lovers together. In the myth, he crushes Acis with a boulder in his jealousy. Galatea then turns her lover into a river, so he lives on as flowing water. That is a fitting story for a fountain. The water you hear is, in a sense, Acis himself.

Notice the contrast in materials. The lovers are white marble. The giant is dark bronze, almost blending into the stone behind him. Ottin used colour to tell the story. Your eye finds the bright couple first, and only later notices the dark threat above. It is a small piece of theatre. Many visitors photograph the lovers and walk away without seeing the Cyclops at all. Take a moment to step to one side and see his face.

On either side of the central niche stand two more figures. One is a faun, a woodland spirit from Roman myth, half man and half goat. The other is a huntress, linked to Diana, goddess of the hunt and of wild places. Between the columns, you can also see carved details on the stone, including the coats of arms that link the fountain to royal France. Look up, too, at the top of the structure, where carved figures recline above the arch.

Here is the second surprise, and it is easy to miss. Walk around to the back of the fountain. There you will find a second, separate fountain, back to back with the first. It shows Leda and the Swan, another tale from Greek myth, in which the god Zeus takes the form of a swan. This fountain once stood on a nearby street, the Rue du Regard. When that area was redeveloped, it was moved here and joined to the grotto.

Come back to the front and look along the basin. In warm months, garlands of ivy hang between the urns, linking them like a green chain. Fish move slowly in the dark water. On sunny afternoons, the green metal chairs of the Luxembourg Garden gather along the edges. People read, sketch, or simply close their eyes. Students from the nearby Latin Quarter come here between classes. Today the palace behind you houses the French Senate, so politics still lives next door.

Before you leave, stand at the far end of the basin for a moment. Let your eye travel the whole length, from the urns to the grotto, from the white lovers to the dark giant above them. A Florentine queen wanted a corner of home. A nineteenth-century city wanted a wider street. Somehow both wishes ended up here, in this cool, green room. As you walk back into the open garden, notice how the sound of water fades behind you... and how loud Paris suddenly feels again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit on a summer afternoon when the plane trees shade the basin.
Grab one of the green garden chairs along the basin edge.
Walk behind the grotto to find the second fountain.
Stand at the far end of the basin for the full perspective.
Combine with a slow loop of the Luxembourg Garden lawns.
