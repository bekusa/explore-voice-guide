The guide

The story

Stand on Avenue Montaigne and listen for a moment. Taxis hum past. Shopping bags rustle outside the fashion houses. Then look up at a flat, pale facade, almost severe, with clean lines and very little ornament. After the curling balconies of older Paris, it feels quiet, even modern. That calm surface hides one of the loudest nights in the history of music.

Here is the first surprise. The Théâtre des Champs-Élysées is not on the Champs-Élysées at all. It sits on Avenue Montaigne, a short walk away, closer to the Seine than to the famous avenue. The name was chosen in the early stages of the project. The theatre then moved to this site, and the original name stayed. Many visitors walk the length of the Champs-Élysées looking for it and never find it.

The building opened in 1913, just before the First World War. Its driving force was the impresario Gabriel Astruc. Astruc wanted a modern home for music in Paris. He wanted a hall for symphony concerts, opera, and dance, in a style that looked forward, not back. This was an ambitious dream. As you will hear, it was also a costly one.

Now look closely at the structure itself. The theatre was built by Auguste Perret, working with his brothers. Perret was a pioneer of reinforced concrete — concrete strengthened with steel bars inside it. In 1913 this material was still new for a major public building. It let Perret build a strong frame with wide, open spaces inside. The design went through several hands, and the Belgian architect Henry van de Velde also shaped the early plans.

The outside is simple, but it is not bare. Look above the entrance for the carved marble panels. They are the work of the sculptor Antoine Bourdelle. His figures show Apollo, the Greek god of music and light, along with the Muses, the goddesses of the arts. Notice how the bodies are firm and blocky, almost architectural. Bourdelle carved them to match the straight lines of the building, not to fight them.

The early twentieth century was a time of deep change in the arts. Paris was full of painters, composers, and dancers breaking old rules. This theatre was built at the centre of that moment. Its clean style sits between the flowing Art Nouveau of the 1900s and the sharp geometry of Art Deco that came later. Some people see it as an early sign of that later style.

And then came the night of 29 May 1913. The Ballets Russes, the Russian ballet company led by Sergei Diaghilev, presented a new work here. The music was by Igor Stravinsky. The dance was by Vaslav Nijinsky. The piece was called The Rite of Spring. It showed an ancient ritual in which a young woman dances herself to death. Nothing about it sounded or looked like ballet as Paris knew it.

The audience reacted almost at once. The music was harsh, with pounding rhythms and clashing chords. The dancers stamped and turned inward instead of floating. People shouted, laughed, and argued in the seats. Accounts from that night describe so much noise that the dancers struggled to hear the orchestra. Over the years the story has grown in the retelling. But the uproar was real, and the work went on to change modern music.

That was not the only premiere of that first season. Only a couple of weeks earlier, the same company had presented Jeux, a ballet with music by Claude Debussy. So in a few short weeks, this new stage heard two major works of twentieth-century music. Yet the excitement did not pay the bills. Astruc's company ran into serious money trouble within months of the opening, and he lost control of his theatre.

The building survived and kept surprising Paris. In 1925, the American dancer and singer Josephine Baker appeared here in a show called La Revue Nègre. Her energy and her jazz-age style made her a star in France almost overnight. It is a strong contrast. The same stage that shocked the city with Russian ballet later thrilled it with American jazz and dance.

If you can step inside, look up in the main hall. The ceiling and the dome carry paintings by Maurice Denis, a French painter who loved soft colours and graceful figures. The hall is a large, round space with a wide view of the stage. The complex also holds smaller stages, including the Comédie des Champs-Élysées and a little studio theatre. Today the main hall still hosts opera, ballet, and concerts through much of the year.

Take a moment to notice the neighbourhood around you. Avenue Montaigne is lined with great fashion names and elegant hotels. Just a short walk downhill, you reach Place de l'Alma and the river. From the bridges there, you can see the Eiffel Tower across the water. The theatre sits a little apart from all this luxury. Its plain front feels like a frame for art, not a shop window.

When you look at this facade again, think about how new it once was. Concrete instead of stone carving. Flat planes instead of curves. Gods and Muses cut in straight, strong lines. This building was a promise that music in Paris could move forward. That promise was tested on its very first season — and it held.

Before you walk on, stand back across the avenue. Let your eye travel from the doors, up past Bourdelle's figures, to the top of the facade. Listen to the street again, the traffic and the footsteps. More than a century ago, people poured out of these same doors, still arguing about what they had just heard. As you walk away, you are following the same path they took into the Paris night.
