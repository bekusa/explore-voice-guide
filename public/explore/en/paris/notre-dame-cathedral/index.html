The story

On an island in the middle of the Seine rises one of the great churches of the world, its twin towers, flying buttresses, and rose windows the very image of the Gothic cathedral. This is Notre-Dame de Paris, a building more than eight centuries old that sits at the historic and symbolic heart of the city.

Work on the cathedral began in 1163 and went on for the best part of two centuries, an enormous effort that helped define the Gothic style. Its pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and great stained-glass rose windows were a leap in how high and how light a stone building could be made to feel.

The flying buttresses that ring the outside are not mere decoration but clever engineering, propping the high walls from outside so that they could be opened up with windows within. Walking around the back of the cathedral shows this skeleton of stone at its most dramatic.

For centuries Notre-Dame has stood at the centre of French life, the setting for coronations, state funerals, and moments of national mourning and joy. Distances across the whole country are even measured from a point on the square in front of it.

In 2019 a terrible fire tore through the cathedral, destroying its roof and the tall spire and shocking the world. The great stone structure and many treasures survived, and a vast restoration has since repaired the damage and returned the building to use, a remarkable feat of craft.

The restoration drew on both old skills and new study, with carpenters, stonemasons, and other craftspeople working as their medieval forebears did. The reopened cathedral lets visitors see the building cleaned and renewed, in some ways closer to its original brightness than it had been for generations.

Inside, the scale and the light are the wonder. The great rose windows glow in deep blues and reds, the stone columns soar, and the long nave draws the eye toward the altar. It is a working cathedral, so services and music are part of its daily life.

The towers, when open, reward the climb up a tight spiral stair with a close view of the famous carved gargoyles and grotesques, and a wide outlook over the rooftops of the city. The carved creatures, both drainspouts and decoration, are among the cathedral's best-loved details.

The setting on the island is part of the experience. The square in front gives the classic view of the west front with its towers and central rose window, and the riverbanks and the garden behind offer quieter angles on the building.

The cathedral sits at the very centre of old Paris, within easy walking distance of other great sights, so it falls naturally into a day exploring the island and the riverbanks on foot.

A few practical notes. Entry to the cathedral itself is free, though queues can be long, and tower climbs or special areas may be ticketed and best booked ahead. Check current arrangements, as access has changed with the restoration, and dress modestly as in any church.

Stand on the square and take in the west front, then step inside into the coloured light, and consider that this building has stood, burned, and risen again across more than eight centuries. Few monuments carry a city's identity so completely. When you walk back along the river, the towers stay in view above the rooftops.
