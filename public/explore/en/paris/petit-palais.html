The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the foot of the steps. The stone under your feet is pale and smooth, and above you a pair of tall gates shine with gold. Traffic hums along the avenue behind you, but the building in front of you seems to hold its breath. Look up at the dome over the entrance. Sunlight catches the carved figures and the long curve of the facade. This is the Petit Palais. In a moment, it will change what you expect from a city museum.

The name means the Small Palace, and that can sound modest. It is anything but. The building was raised for the Universal Exhibition of 1900. That was a great world's fair, where Paris wanted to show the twentieth century what it could do. The architect was Charles Girault. He designed it as a permanent showcase for art, in stone, iron and glass. Many exhibition buildings were temporary and were torn down afterwards. This one was meant to stay.

Turn around and look across the avenue. That enormous glass-roofed hall facing you is the Grand Palais, built for the same exhibition. The two palaces were planned as a pair, face to face. Together they frame a view that runs down towards the Seine and the gilded Pont Alexandre III, another bridge from that same fair. So you are standing inside one single idea of a city. Paris in 1900 built a ceremonial route of bridge, avenue and palaces, all at once.

Here is something many visitors miss. The Grand Palais across the street is the bigger building, and it made the louder statement. But the Petit Palais was the one designed from the start to become a museum. Soon after the exhibition closed, the City of Paris turned it into its Museum of Fine Arts. It has filled that role ever since. The small palace, in other words, won the longer life.

As you walk through the gates, notice the plan of the building. It is shaped roughly like a trapezoid, wrapped around a central garden. The galleries run along the outside edges. In the middle, a semicircular courtyard opens to the sky. This means that wherever you are inside, you are never far from daylight. Girault wanted visitors to see the art in natural light. Large windows and glass ceilings bring that light into the rooms.

Inside the entrance, slow down and look at the floor. Much of it is covered in mosaic, with small tiles in soft colours set into flowing patterns. Then look up. The ceilings and domes carry large painted decorations, framed with plaster and gold. Painters of the period were commissioned to decorate the building itself. So the palace is not only a box for art. The walls and ceilings are part of the collection too.

Now, the collection. It covers a very long span of time. There are objects from the ancient Mediterranean world, Greek and Roman pieces among them. There are medieval and Renaissance works, including religious objects and decorative arts. There are Dutch and Flemish paintings from the seventeenth century, the age of Rembrandt and his contemporaries. The museum belongs to the city, and over the years many collectors gave their treasures to Paris. That is why the collection feels so varied.

The nineteenth century is where many visitors linger longest. This was the century of Paris as the capital of painting. You will find French artists from that time, including Gustave Courbet, the painter who insisted on showing real life without polish. You will also find painters of the Impressionist generation. They worked outdoors and tried to catch changing light on water and fields. Notice how often their light echoes the light coming through the museum windows.

Around 1900, a new style was everywhere in Paris. People called it Art Nouveau, which simply means new art. It loved curving lines, plant shapes and natural forms. The Petit Palais was born in that same moment, and the museum holds fine examples of the style. Look for glass, jewellery and furniture where stems, leaves and flowers seem to grow out of the material. Then look back at the building's ironwork. You will see the same love of curves.

Here is a second surprise. Find your way to the inner garden. After the gold and marble of the galleries, it feels almost private. A curved colonnade of columns runs around it. Its walls are decorated with mosaics, and there are planted beds and a small pool. There is usually a café here too. On a warm day, people sit with a coffee under the columns. It is one of the calmest places on this side of the city.

The garden also helps you understand the architecture. From here you can see how Girault mixed old and new. The columns, the arches and the carved stone follow classical models from ancient Rome and the French royal tradition. But the large windows and the light metal frames belong to the industrial age. It was an age of railway stations and iron bridges. The building wears classical clothing over a modern skeleton.

As you move through the rooms, take a moment to notice the other visitors. You will see students sketching in notebooks, families pausing on benches, and older Parisians who have clearly come many times. The museum often hosts temporary exhibitions as well. So the mood inside can change from season to season. Some days it is quiet and echoing. Other days a special show fills the halls with people and conversation.

When you are ready to leave, walk back out through the golden gates. Stop again on the steps where you began. The avenue is still busy, and the Grand Palais still faces you across it. Down to your right, the bridge and the river wait. Notice the light on the pale stone, and the curve of the dome above your head. Paris built all of this for one summer's celebration... and then decided to keep the small palace for everyone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Rest in the inner garden café between gallery visits.
Check for temporary exhibitions, which may need a separate ticket.
Visit on a bright day to enjoy the natural gallery light.
Combine your visit with a walk across Pont Alexandre III.
Pick two or three periods rather than seeing everything.
