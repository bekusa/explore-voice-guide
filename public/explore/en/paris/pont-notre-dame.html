The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the stone under your feet. Below you, the Seine moves fast and green-grey, pushing against the piers. Behind you, traffic hums along the quays. Above, gulls turn over the water. This is Pont Notre-Dame, a short and quiet bridge. Most visitors cross it without a second look. Yet this narrow span holds one of the oldest stories of any crossing in Paris.

First, get your bearings. The bridge links the Right Bank to the Île de la Cité, the island where Paris began. To one side, upstream, you can see the Pont d'Arcole. To the other side, downstream, is the Pont au Change. The name can mislead you. Notre-Dame Cathedral is not right at the end of this bridge. It stands further along the island. The bridge simply takes its name from the cathedral, as so much in this part of the city does.

People have crossed the river at roughly this point for a very long time. In Roman times, a main road ran north to south through the settlement then called Lutetia. Historians believe that road crossed the Seine close to here, using the island as a stepping stone. For centuries afterward, a bridge near this spot was one of the few ways onto the island from the north. So, in a sense, you are walking along one of the city's oldest routes.

Now picture this bridge very differently. In the Middle Ages, Paris bridges were not open roads with views of the water. They were streets. Houses and shops lined both sides, packed tightly together. You could walk across without ever seeing the river. In the late fifteenth century, a wooden bridge here carried rows of these buildings. It was crowded, busy, and full of trade.

Then, in 1499, disaster struck. The wooden bridge collapsed into the Seine, taking its houses with it. The city had known the structure was weak, and the collapse caused a scandal. City officials were blamed for neglecting the repairs, and several of them were jailed. It is a rare early example of people in power being held to account for a public failure. Paris needed a new bridge, and it needed a better one.

The replacement was built in stone and finished in the early sixteenth century. An Italian friar and engineer, Giovanni Giocondo, is often linked to the project. The new bridge again carried houses, but this time they were planned as a matched row. They looked alike, a single orderly street over the water. Here is a detail many visitors miss. These houses are often described as among the first in Paris to carry numbers. Long before the whole city used house numbers, this bridge already had them.

The bridge also became a stage for ceremony. When kings and queens made their formal entry into Paris, processions often passed this way toward the island. The street over the river was hung with decorations for these occasions. Imagine the crowds leaning from the upper windows, watching the riders pass below. For a while, this was one of the most fashionable addresses in the city.

And here is the second surprise. In the early eighteenth century, an art dealer named Edme-François Gersaint kept his shop on this bridge. His friend, the painter Antoine Watteau, made a large painted sign for the shop. Today it is known as the Shop Sign of Gersaint. It shows elegant customers browsing paintings, and staff packing a portrait into a crate. That painting is now considered a masterpiece of French art. It began as an advertisement, hanging right here over the Seine.

The bridge also served a very practical purpose. In the seventeenth century, a pump was built here to lift water from the Seine. The river's current drove the machinery, and the water was sent to fountains in the surrounding neighbourhoods. In a city without modern pipes, this was important work. So the bridge was not only a street and a market. It was also part of how Parisians got their water.

By the late eighteenth century, attitudes had changed. The houses on the bridges were now seen as unhealthy and unsafe. They blocked air and light, and they placed heavy loads on old stone. In the 1780s, the houses on this bridge were cleared away. For the first time in centuries, people crossing here could see the river on both sides. The view you are enjoying now is, in historical terms, fairly new.

The bridge itself was rebuilt again in the nineteenth century. But the river kept causing trouble. The arches were narrow, and the current between the piers was strong. Boats struck the stonework, and accidents were common. Boatmen reportedly gave the crossing a grim nickname, the Devil's Bridge. In the early twentieth century, engineers found a solution. They replaced the central stone arches with a single wide metal arch, giving boats a clear passage.

Look over the side and you can see the result. The bridge is a blend of two materials and two eras. The outer arches are pale masonry. The middle arch is metal, lighter and flatter, spanning the main channel. Watch a barge or a tour boat pass beneath it. It glides through the opening that was made for exactly this purpose. The bridge you see today is the answer to centuries of problems on this stretch of water.

Before you leave, take in the neighbourhood around you. On the Right Bank side, the grand Hôtel de Ville, the city hall, stands a short walk upstream. On the island side, you are close to the old flower market and the great hospital, the Hôtel-Dieu. Many crossings in Paris are wider and more decorated. This one is modest, and that is part of its charm. It does its work quietly, as it has for centuries.

So pause here once more. Listen to the water against the piers. Notice the steady flow of people crossing, most of them unaware of the houses, the painters, and the pump that once stood here. As you walk on, look back at the low arches. Somewhere under the modern road lie traces of every bridge that came before. Paris has crossed the river at this point for a very long time... and it still does, one step at a time.
