The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under shoes in the Luxembourg Gardens, and somewhere nearby a child is chasing pigeons. Plane trees move in the breeze. Then you turn towards the rue de Vaugirard and find a long, pale stone building with tall arched windows. It looks almost modest beside the palace next door. This is the Musée du Luxembourg. It is small, calm, and easy to miss. Yet it holds a place in the story of art that many larger museums cannot claim.

Here is the first surprise. Many people assume the Louvre was the first public art museum in France. It was not. In 1750, decades before the Revolution, the royal collection opened in the east gallery of the Luxembourg Palace. Ordinary Parisians could walk in on set days of the week and look at paintings that had belonged only to kings. Old Masters hung on the walls, including works by Raphael, Titian, and Rembrandt. For the first time in France, great art was something the public could simply go and see.

To understand the setting, look at the palace beside you. It was built in the early seventeenth century for Marie de' Medici, the widowed queen of Henry the Fourth. She came from Florence, and she missed it. Her architect, Salomon de Brosse, gave the palace hints of the Pitti Palace, her childhood home in Florence. You can see this in the heavy, banded stonework, with rough horizontal grooves. It was a Tuscan memory, built in Parisian stone.

Marie de' Medici also wanted paintings that told her own story. She commissioned the Flemish master Peter Paul Rubens to create a huge cycle of canvases about her life. In these pictures, gods and goddesses attend her birth, her marriage, and her years in power. The series once filled a gallery in this palace. Today those enormous paintings hang in the Louvre. But they were made for this place, for these walls, and for one ambitious queen.

The early public museum did not last long. In the 1770s the royal paintings were taken away, as plans grew for a grand museum at the Louvre. Then came the Revolution, and the palace changed roles many times. It served as a prison for a period. Later it became a seat of government. The building carries traces of all these lives, even if the stone looks calm and orderly today.

The second great chapter began in 1818. The Luxembourg reopened as a museum for living artists. This was a bold idea. Painters and sculptors who were still working could see their art hung in a national museum. Some years after an artist's death, the most important works might move on to the Louvre. So the Luxembourg became a kind of waiting room for the classics of the future. Generations of French artists dreamed of a place on these walls.

And here is perhaps the best surprise of all. At the end of the nineteenth century, the painter Gustave Caillebotte left his collection to the French state. It included works by his friends, the Impressionists — Monet, Renoir, Degas, Cézanne, and others. Many officials disliked this new style, with its loose brushwork and everyday scenes. After long arguments, part of the collection went on display here in 1897. For many Parisians, the Luxembourg was where Impressionism first entered a national museum.

The building in front of you today is not the original gallery inside the palace. In the late nineteenth century the museum moved into this neighbouring structure, a former orangery. An orangery is a garden building with large windows, used to shelter orange trees and other delicate plants through the winter. Look at those tall glazed arches. They were made to let in as much light as possible. That same soft light now falls on paintings instead of citrus leaves.

In the twentieth century, the collection of modern art grew too large for these rooms. In the 1930s the works moved on, and many of them later became part of France's national modern art collections. Some of the Impressionist paintings you might have seen here in 1900 now hang across the river, at the Musée d'Orsay. For decades afterwards, the Luxembourg had a quieter life, with occasional exhibitions and long pauses between them.

Today the museum belongs to the French Senate, which sits in the palace next door. That is another detail most visitors miss. While you look at art, senators may be debating laws only a short walk away. The museum no longer has a permanent collection. Instead it holds temporary exhibitions, usually one or two a year. They often focus on a single artist, a period, or a theme, and the rooms are redesigned for each show.

Because of this, every visit feels different. The galleries are intimate, not vast. You will not lose an afternoon walking through endless corridors. Instead you can spend an hour with a focused group of works and really look at them. Many Parisians like this scale. It is a museum you can visit before lunch, then step straight back into the gardens with your thoughts still fresh.

The gardens themselves are part of the experience. Just a short walk away is the Medici Fountain, a shaded stone grotto with a long pool, also linked to Marie de' Medici. Further in, you will find the green metal chairs that Parisians drag into the sun, the octagonal pond where children sail small boats, and paths lined with statues of French queens. The museum and the garden belong together. One feeds the eyes; the other gives them rest.

Before you leave, stand back for a moment and look again at the long stone front. Think of the people who walked in here in 1750, seeing royal paintings for the first time. Think of young painters in the nineteenth century, hoping their work would hang inside. Then listen once more to the gravel, the voices, the leaves above you... The building is quiet, but it has seen a great deal. Take that quiet with you as you walk on.
