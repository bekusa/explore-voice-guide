The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the traffic of the rue de Rivoli hums and brakes beneath the long stone arcades. In front of you is a pale limestone wall, carved and ordered, that belongs to the same great palace as the Louvre. Many people walk past this doorway without a second look. They are heading for the Mona Lisa. But here, in this wing, the subject is not kings or saints. It is the chair you sit on, the cup you drink from, the dress you wear.

This is the Musée des Arts Décoratifs, the museum of decorative arts. It occupies the western end of the Louvre palace, in the Marsan pavilion and the Rohan wing. It shares walls with the most visited museum on Earth. Yet it is a separate institution, with its own entrance, its own staff and its own way of seeing things. Here, a well-made spoon receives the same careful attention that a painting receives next door.

The story begins in the nineteenth century, when France was worried. Industry was producing goods faster than ever before. Many designers, collectors and manufacturers felt that taste and craftsmanship were falling behind. In the 1860s, a group of them formed an association to bring art and industry closer together. In 1882, it merged with another group and took on the name that still runs this museum today. The idea was simple. Show people beautiful, useful objects, and design across the country would improve.

The building itself carries a dramatic past. This end of the Louvre once connected to the Tuileries Palace, which stood across the gardens to the west. In 1871, during the uprising of the Paris Commune, fires destroyed the Tuileries and badly damaged this wing. The pavilion you see was rebuilt in the years that followed. The Tuileries Palace was never rebuilt. So this stone wall marks the edge of a royal residence that no longer exists. The open garden beyond was once a closed courtyard.

The museum opened here in the Marsan pavilion in 1905. From the start, it was organised in a particular way. Instead of keeping furniture in one hall and ceramics in another, many galleries arrange objects by period. You move through time, from the Middle Ages and the Renaissance to the present day. A tapestry, a cabinet, a clock and a set of plates from the same decades sit close together. You start to see how a whole room once looked and felt.

Here is something most visitors do not expect. The collection is not only about luxury. Yes, there are gilded commodes and fine porcelain. But there are also toys, wallpaper, advertising posters, glass bottles and everyday tools. The museum treats a child's tin figure and a royal desk as two sides of the same question. How do people shape the objects around them, and how do those objects shape people? That question makes this place feel surprisingly personal.

As you enter, you may pass through the great central hall, often called the nave. It is a tall, light space with galleries running around its upper levels. The museum uses it for large exhibitions, and its appearance changes completely from one show to the next. One season, it might be filled with furniture. Another season, it might hold a forest of mannequins in couture gowns. Look up at the balconies. From there, you can see the whole hall at once.

Fashion is one of the great strengths of this museum. Its collections of clothing and textiles cover several centuries, from embroidered silks to modern ready-to-wear. Because fabric is fragile and fades in light, these pieces are not kept on permanent display. Instead, they appear in rotating exhibitions. Some of these fashion shows have drawn very long queues, with visitors waiting along the arcades of the rue de Rivoli. If a fashion exhibition is running during your visit, it is worth planning around it.

Among the period rooms, one is especially loved. It is the private apartment of the fashion designer Jeanne Lanvin, created in the 1920s by the designer Armand-Albert Rateau. It was later taken apart and rebuilt inside this museum. The rooms are known for a deep, rich blue, combined with bronze furniture and carefully placed decoration. Here is the surprise. You are standing inside a real home, not a copy of one. These are the walls and fittings that surrounded a working Paris couturière.

Nearby, the museum shows the flowing lines of Art Nouveau, the style that spread across Europe around 1900. This style took its shapes from plants, insects and water. Paris itself still carries this style in the curling green entrances of some old metro stations. Inside the galleries, you can see the same curves in furniture, glass, jewellery and lamps. Notice how a table leg can look like a growing stem. Notice how a vase can look like a flower opening.

The jewellery gallery is quieter and darker, and it rewards slow looking. Small cases hold rings, brooches and necklaces from many periods. Lean in close. The skill involved is often too small to see from a normal distance. In the same way, the toy collection can surprise adults even more than children. Dolls, games and miniature theatres show how childhood itself has changed. A wooden horse from long ago and a plastic figure from your own youth can share the same room.

Take a moment at one of the upper windows if you can. On one side, the Tuileries Garden stretches west in straight gravel paths and clipped trees. On the other side, the courtyards of the Louvre open toward the glass pyramid. From here, you see the palace from an unusual angle. Then, before you leave, look for the museum's shop and its library. The library holds a large collection of books and images on design, and researchers come here from around the world.

Now, step back out onto the rue de Rivoli, or into the garden behind the museum. Notice the objects around you with fresh eyes. Look at the iron railing, the shape of a café chair, the lettering on a shop sign, the curve of a lamppost. Somebody designed each of these. Somebody chose the line, the material and the colour. That is the quiet lesson of this museum. Art is not only on the walls. It is in your hand, every day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Use the separate entrance on the rue de Rivoli.
Check which temporary exhibition fills the nave.
Book ahead for major fashion exhibitions.
Follow the period galleries in order, oldest to newest.
Pair your visit with a walk in the Tuileries Garden.
