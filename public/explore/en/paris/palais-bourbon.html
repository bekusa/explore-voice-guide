The guide

The story

Stand on the Pont de la Concorde for a moment and listen. Traffic hums behind you, and the Seine slides grey-green beneath the stone arches. Ahead, a row of tall pale columns rises above a broad flight of steps. Pigeons settle on the cornice. On a clear afternoon the stone looks almost white, and it warms to honey as the light falls. This is the Palais Bourbon, home of the French National Assembly. It is the room where France writes its laws.

Before we cross, turn around. Look back across the river, past the obelisk in the Place de la Concorde, and up the Rue Royale. At the far end you can see another temple front with columns. That is the Church of the Madeleine. The two buildings face each other like a pair of bookends. This is no accident. It is the first surprise of this place, and it is a lesson in how Paris was planned on purpose.

The palace did not begin as a parliament. In the 1720s it was built as a private residence for Louise-Françoise de Bourbon, the Duchess of Bourbon. She was a daughter of King Louis XIV and his mistress, Madame de Montespan, later recognised by the king. She wanted a riverside home within easy reach of the court. Several architects worked on it in turn. Their model was the Grand Trianon at Versailles, a low and elegant royal pavilion.

So the original palace was quite different from what you see now. It was low, horizontal, and graceful, with gardens running down toward the river. Next door, a smaller mansion rose at the same time for a close friend of the duchess. That neighbouring house is the Hôtel de Lassay. Today it is the official residence of the President of the National Assembly. The two buildings are still linked, a pairing that is over three centuries old.

Then came the Revolution, and everything changed. Like many noble properties, the palace was taken by the state. In the late 1790s it became the meeting place of the Council of Five Hundred. This was one of the two chambers of the government of that period. From that moment on, the building has been tied to the idea of elected representatives. Kings, emperors, and republics came and went, but the lawmakers mostly stayed here.

Now look again at the columns in front of you. They were not part of the duchess's home. Napoleon ordered this grand facade in the early nineteenth century, and the architect Bernard Poyet designed it. It was built to match the Madeleine across the river. Here is the second surprise. The portico was set to face the bridge and the long view, not the older palace behind it. In a sense, this front is a stage set built for the city.

Lift your eyes to the triangular pediment above the columns. The sculpture there was added in the nineteenth century. It shows France as a central figure, with allegories of liberty, public order, and the arts gathered around her. Below, on the steps, seated statues watch the river. They represent great statesmen and jurists from the French monarchy, including Sully and Colbert. These ministers served kings, yet they now guard a republic. That small irony says a lot about French history.

Walk along the side of the building toward the south. The mood changes quickly. The river noise fades, and the streets grow quieter and narrower. You reach the Place du Palais-Bourbon, a calm square with cafés and old townhouses. Here you can see the other face of the palace. It is the older courtyard entrance, closer in spirit to the original eighteenth-century residence. Deputies and staff often come and go through this side.

Inside, the heart of the building is the hémicycle. The word means half-circle, and it describes the shape of the debating chamber. Rows of red seats rise in a curve around the speaker's platform. Deputies sit by political group, and the terms left and right in politics come from French assemblies. Today there are 577 deputies, each elected for a district of France or for French citizens living abroad.

The palace also holds one of its great artistic treasures, though few passers-by know it exists. In the nineteenth century, the painter Eugène Delacroix decorated the library ceiling. His paintings tell the story of human civilisation, from Orpheus bringing the arts to humanity to Attila the Hun bringing destruction. He also painted another room nearby. Delacroix worked on these scenes for years. They are a quiet masterpiece, hidden inside a working parliament.

This is still a working building, not a museum. Security is serious here, and you will often see police near the gates. Visitors can sometimes join guided tours, usually arranged in advance through a deputy or offered during France's annual heritage days. On those days, people queue to see the chamber and the library for themselves. Plan ahead, because you cannot simply walk in from the street.

If you cannot go inside, the outside still rewards your attention. From the bridge you can line up the columns, the obelisk, and the Madeleine in one view. Evening is a good time to stand here, when the floodlights come on and the facade glows over the dark water. Behind you, boats pass under the bridge with their lights reflected below. The city feels open and wide from this spot.

Take one more look before you walk on. Notice the steps, the tall columns, and the stone figures looking out over the river. Behind that formal front, a duchess once walked through her gardens. Today, deputies argue about budgets and laws in the same building. As you leave, listen for the traffic on the bridge and the water below. Then glance back once more and look for the Madeleine at the far end of the view.
