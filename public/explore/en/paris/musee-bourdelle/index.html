The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Just beyond the street door, the traffic of Montparnasse goes quiet. In its place you may hear gravel under your shoes and a little wind in the garden trees. Then you notice the bronzes. There are dark, heavy figures everywhere, standing among the plants as if they had walked out of the studios and stopped for air.

This is the Musée Bourdelle. It is the home and workplace of the French sculptor Antoine Bourdelle, kept almost as he left it. Most museums bring art to a building. Here the building came first. These rooms are where the work was made, so the plaster dust, the north light and the clutter of a working sculptor are part of the collection.

Bourdelle was born in 1861 in Montauban, in the south-west of France. His father was a cabinetmaker, and the boy grew up around wood, tools and the patience of craft. He trained in Toulouse and then in Paris. By the mid-1880s he had taken a studio here, in what was then a modest lane of artists' workshops on the edge of the city. He kept working on this site until his death in 1929.

Here is the first surprise. For roughly fifteen years, Bourdelle worked as an assistant to Auguste Rodin, the most famous sculptor of the age. He carved marble versions of Rodin's models and absorbed his methods. But he grew restless in that shadow. He wanted something more solid and more ordered than Rodin's flickering, rippling surfaces. Much of what you see here is Bourdelle working his way out of that influence.

The breakthrough came with a single figure: Heracles the Archer. The hero leans back with one foot braced against a rock, and he pulls an enormous bow. His body is a tight knot of tension. The forms are blocky and almost architectural, closer to ancient Greek temple sculpture than to Rodin. Shown in 1910, this work made Bourdelle's name. You can find versions of it here, and it is worth walking all the way around it.

Now step into the great hall. The scale changes suddenly. This is a tall gallery with high ceilings, built after the artist's death to hold his largest plaster models. Plaster was the working stage of sculpture. The artist modelled a figure, cast it in plaster, and then foundries used that plaster to make the final bronze. Here the plasters stand pale and chalky, and you can often still see seams, marks and small repairs.

The largest pieces in this hall come from a monument to General Carlos María de Alvear, a hero of Argentine independence. The finished bronze stands in Buenos Aires, on the far side of the Atlantic. Here you see its full-size studies: a horseman and allegorical figures representing virtues such as Strength and Victory. Standing at their feet, you understand how public monuments were really made, one enormous piece at a time.

A second surprise hangs on the walls of a famous Paris building. Bourdelle carved the stone reliefs on the front of the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, which opened in 1913. That theatre is the same place where Stravinsky's Rite of Spring caused its well-known uproar that year. Bourdelle's flat, rhythmic figures of music and dance suited that modern hall perfectly. Studies connected with that project can be found in the museum.

Then there is Beethoven. Bourdelle returned to the composer again and again for most of his life, producing dozens of heads and busts. Some are calm. Others are wild, with deep-cut eyes and hair like a storm. He felt a strong personal bond with Beethoven's struggle and solitude. Seeing several versions together is like watching one artist argue with the same face for decades.

Bourdelle was also a teacher, and that is part of this story too. He taught sculpture at a well-known Montparnasse art school, the Académie de la Grande Chaumière. Among his students were Alberto Giacometti, later famous for his thin, stretched figures, and Germaine Richier. When you later see Giacometti's work in other museums, remember that part of his training passed through Bourdelle's hands.

The museum exists because of his family. After his death, his widow, Cléopâtre Sevastos, and his daughter, Rhodia, worked to keep the studios and the collection together. They offered them to the City of Paris, and the museum opened in 1949. Later extensions added new galleries, including a modern wing by the architect Christian de Portzamparc. Notice how the newer spaces sit beside the old workshops without hiding them.

Take time in the historic studios themselves. Look at the worn floors, the tall windows and the simple shelves. Sculptors needed steady, even daylight, so these rooms face the light carefully. Tools, small models and sketches give a sense of daily work. It is easy to picture the sound of chisels, the dust in the air, and visitors from the neighbourhood's artistic circles stopping by to talk.

Before you leave, return to the garden. The bronzes look different outside. Rain and weather have given them a green and brown skin, and sunlight moves across their surfaces through the day. Around you, the apartment buildings of Montparnasse rise close on every side. This little enclosure feels like a pocket of an older Paris, when this district was a place of cheap studios and hard work.

So pause here for a moment. Look again at the nearest figure, at the weight in its shoulders and the firm set of its feet. Bourdelle wanted sculpture that felt built, like architecture. As you walk back out into the street, notice the stone facades and doorways around you. You may find yourself looking at the city's buildings a little more like a sculptor does.
