The guide

The story

Listen first to your footsteps under the arcade. The stone vaults overhead carry each sound back to you, a soft echo that mixes with voices from the café tables and the trickle of fountains in the garden beyond. Around you, red brick and pale stone frame tall windows under steep slate roofs. This is the Place des Vosges, the oldest planned square in Paris. Somewhere in this corner, behind one of those windows, a writer once paced the floor at night, searching for the next line.

You are standing at the Maison de Victor Hugo, the house where France's great nineteenth-century writer lived for sixteen years. He rented an apartment here from 1832 to 1848, on an upper floor of a townhouse called the Hôtel de Rohan-Guéménée. The building itself is much older than his stay. It was part of the original square, which went up in the early seventeenth century. Hugo arrived in his early thirties, already famous, with a wife, four children, and a restless pen.

Before going inside, take a moment with the square. It began life under King Henri the Fourth and was first called the Place Royale. It was inaugurated in 1612 with grand celebrations for a royal engagement, the planned marriage of the young Louis the Thirteenth. The houses around it follow one shared design, so the whole square reads as a single composition. Look for the two taller pavilions in the middle of opposite sides. These were known as the King's Pavilion and the Queen's Pavilion.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The square gained its present name after the French Revolution. In 1800, it was renamed in honour of the Vosges, a region in eastern France. According to the usual explanation, the Vosges was the first department to pay its taxes to the new government. So one of the most elegant addresses in Paris carries the name of a punctual group of taxpayers. In the centre of the garden, a statue of Louis the Thirteenth on horseback still watches over the lawns.

When Hugo lived here, his apartment became a meeting place for writers and artists of the Romantic movement. Romanticism, in simple terms, was a reaction against strict classical rules, favouring emotion, history, and the drama of the individual. Hugo was one of its loudest voices. Friends came up these stairs to talk late into the evening. Here he wrote plays, poems, and essays. One of his best-known plays from these years is Ruy Blas, a drama of love and power at the Spanish court.

And here is the second surprise. In the 1840s, Hugo began work on a long novel about poverty and justice. He called it at first by a different title, something close to Les Misères. Years later, far from this square, it became Les Misérables. So the first pages of the story of Jean Valjean took shape in rooms you are about to visit. The book was not finished here, though. Politics would interrupt everything.

In December 1851, Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte seized power in a coup and soon made himself emperor as Napoléon the Third. Hugo opposed him openly. He fled France and lived in exile for nearly twenty years, first in Brussels, then on the Channel Islands of Jersey and Guernsey, close to the English coast. On Guernsey he bought a house, Hauteville House, and filled it with carved wood and strange decoration. He returned to Paris only after the Second Empire collapsed in 1870.

The museum opened here in 1903, after the house was given to the city of Paris. Much of the collection came from Paul Meurice, a close friend and literary executor of Hugo. Inside, the visit follows the writer's life in three chapters: before exile, during exile, and after his return. The rooms are not a frozen copy of his apartment. Instead, they gather furniture, portraits, objects, and manuscripts that tell his story room by room.

As you walk through, look out for the Chinese drawing room. Hugo designed this decoration himself during his exile on Guernsey, for the house of Juliette Drouet, the woman who was his companion for some fifty years. The painted panels, lacquer, and porcelain were later brought here and reassembled. It shows a side of Hugo many readers never meet — the decorator, the designer, a man who loved to arrange objects as carefully as words.

Another side appears on the walls. Hugo drew constantly, often in brown ink and wash, sometimes using coffee, soot, or his own fingertips. His drawings show ruined castles, stormy seas, and dark, dreamlike landscapes. He usually kept them private, as gifts for friends rather than works for sale. Today many artists admire them as bold and experimental. Lean close to one, and you can see blots and smudges that he turned into clouds and waves on purpose.

The visit ends in a quieter room. It recreates the bedroom from Hugo's last home in Paris, where he died in 1885. By then he was a national figure, and his funeral drew enormous crowds through the city before he was buried in the Panthéon. Standing in this room, it is worth remembering the distance he travelled. A young poet once climbed the stairs of this house. An old man, honoured by a whole nation, left the world in a room much like this one.

In recent years the museum was renovated, and it opened a small garden courtyard with a café behind the house. It is a good place to sit after the visit. The noise of the square fades, and you hear birds, cups, and quiet conversation. Many visitors miss it completely and walk straight back out under the arcade. If you have time, stay a little and let the rooms settle in your memory.

When you step outside again, look up at the windows above the arcade. Imagine the lamplight up there on a winter night in the 1840s. A man writes quickly, crosses out lines, and starts again. Below, the square looks much as it does now — brick, stone, slate, and trees. Walk slowly along the arcade as you leave, and listen to your footsteps once more.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early on weekdays to avoid crowded upstairs rooms.
Rest afterwards in the garden café behind the house.
Walk the full arcade loop around Place des Vosges.
Check ahead whether temporary exhibitions need a separate ticket.
Bring a light layer for shaded stone arcades in cooler months.
