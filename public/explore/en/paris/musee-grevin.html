The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out on the Boulevard Montmartre, the traffic hums and scooters weave between buses. Step inside, and the sound changes. Footsteps soften on carpet. Voices drop to a murmur. Somewhere ahead, a crowd of people stands perfectly still, and not one of them is breathing. Welcome to the Musée Grévin, the wax museum of Paris. For well over a century, it has asked visitors the same quiet question: how close can a copy come to the real thing?

The story begins in 1882, in the newspaper world of nineteenth-century Paris. A journalist named Arthur Meyer ran a daily paper called Le Gaulois. Newspapers then printed few photographs, so readers often had no idea what famous people actually looked like. Meyer had a bold idea. Why not show the faces from the front page in three dimensions, life-sized, in one room? The public could finally stand face to face with the people they read about every morning.

Meyer needed an artist, and he found one in Alfred Grévin. Grévin was a caricaturist and a designer of theatre costumes. He knew faces, and he knew how to dress a body for an audience. His name went on the door, and it has stayed there ever since. The idea also had a famous model across the Channel. Madame Tussaud's in London had already shown that wax figures could draw huge crowds. Paris, naturally, wanted its own version, with its own personality.

Notice the neighbourhood you walked through to get here. This is the area of the Grands Boulevards, the wide avenues that became the centre of Parisian entertainment in the nineteenth century. Theatres, cafés and newspaper offices stood side by side along these streets. People came here at night to see and to be seen. A museum full of famous faces fit this setting perfectly. In a way, the museum is a piece of that old boulevard culture, still open for business.

Now, here is the first surprise. Many visitors rush past the most valuable thing in the building, because it is not made of wax. The museum has its own small theatre, built in the Italian style, with red velvet and gilded decoration. The sculptor Antoine Bourdelle worked on its decoration. The painter and poster artist Jules Chéret, famous for his colourful advertising posters of the Belle Époque, created its curtain. The Belle Époque was the optimistic period around 1900, before the First World War. Take a moment here, and look up.

The second surprise waits in a room called the Palais des Mirages, the Palace of Mirages. It was first created for the great Universal Exhibition held in Paris in 1900, a world's fair that showed off new technology and spectacle. Later, the attraction found a permanent home here. You stand inside a room of mirrors. The lights change, music plays, and the walls seem to multiply into endless halls. It feels like a kind of magic show from the age before cinema took over.

Between these older treasures, the wax figures themselves fill the rooms. You will meet actors, singers, athletes, political leaders and artists. Some are French household names. Others are international stars that visitors from anywhere will recognise. The line-up changes over time. New figures arrive when someone becomes famous, and older ones sometimes leave quietly. So the museum is never quite finished. It works like a mirror of celebrity, showing who matters to the public at a particular moment.

Take a close look at any face, and think about the work behind it. Making a wax figure is slow, careful craft. Artists begin with measurements and many photographs. They sculpt the head first in clay, then cast it in wax. Hair is usually inserted strand by strand, a task that can take weeks on its own. The skin is painted in thin layers, so that tiny veins and freckles show through. Even the eyes and teeth are made separately, to match the real person as closely as possible.

That level of detail creates a strange feeling. From across the room, a figure looks alive. As you come closer, your brain keeps searching for movement — a blink, a breath, a shift of weight. It never comes. Many visitors find this both fun and slightly uncomfortable. That tension is part of the experience. It is also why people love to pose beside the figures. In a photograph, the line between the real visitor and the wax guest becomes very hard to see.

The museum also tells stories from French history. Scenes show moments and characters from the past, often arranged like a stage set, with costumes, furniture and dramatic lighting. These rooms connect to the museum's early purpose. Before cinema and television, a well-built scene was one of the few ways ordinary people could picture history. Walk through slowly. Look at the hands and the clothing, not only the faces. The small details often carry the strongest sense of a moment.

Think, too, about how the museum handles fame. A wax figure is expensive and takes a long time to make. So choosing who appears here is a real decision. Over the decades, the faces have followed changing tastes, from stage stars and statesmen to film actors, footballers and pop musicians. If you look around and ask yourself who is missing, or who seems surprising, you are doing exactly what Parisians have done here since the 1880s.

When you leave, pay attention to where you come out. The museum sits beside the Passage Jouffroy, one of the covered shopping arcades of nineteenth-century Paris. These glass-roofed passages were the city's first shopping galleries, built so people could stroll and browse away from mud and rain. The arcade has small shops, old bookstores and a quiet, warm light from above. It is a gentle way to return to the city after so many still faces.

So take one last look around you. The figures will stay exactly where they are, frozen in their best moment, long after you have gone. Outside, the boulevard is still moving, full of real people who blink, laugh and hurry past. As you step back into that noise, notice the faces of the strangers around you. After an hour among wax, every small movement — a smile, a turn of the head — looks a little more remarkable.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a timed entry online to avoid long boulevard queues.
Visit early on weekdays for quieter rooms and easier photos.
Pause in the Palace of Mirages for the full light show.
Exit through Passage Jouffroy for an easy arcade stroll afterwards.
Leave camera flash off so reflections do not spoil photos.
