The guide

The story

Listen first. Water drops in thin sheets down a stack of stone steps, and the sound mixes with footsteps, scooter wheels, and the low talk of people sitting on the basin's edge. The stone is pale and creamy, warm in the sun and cool in the shade. Around you, the square fills with the smell of coffee and crêpes. Right in the middle stands a small square pavilion of arches, carved with slender women who seem to move. This is the Fontaine des Innocents, and it is older than almost everything you can see from here.

This is the oldest monumental fountain in Paris. It went up in the late 1540s, during the French Renaissance. That was the period when French kings looked south to Italy and brought back new ideas about columns, proportion, and classical gods. Before this time, Parisian fountains were mostly simple and practical. This one was different. It was built to impress, and it still does, almost five centuries later, in the middle of one of the city's busiest shopping districts.

The design is linked to the architect Pierre Lescot. He also worked on the Louvre, which was then being turned from a medieval fortress into a Renaissance palace. The sculpture is the work of Jean Goujon, one of the finest carvers of his age. Together they created something unusual for Paris at that time. It was a piece of street architecture that looked like a small classical temple, with fluted pilasters, arches, and carved figures set between them.

Now look closely at those figures. They are nymphs, or naiads, water spirits from Greek and Roman mythology. Each one holds a tilted jar, as if pouring water. Their robes cling to their bodies in fine, rippling folds, like wet cloth. This is the effect that made Goujon famous. In flat, shallow stone, he gave the illusion of movement and depth. Step close, then step back, and notice how the folds seem to shift as your angle changes.

Here is the first surprise. The fountain you see today is not the fountain that was first built. The original was not freestanding at all. It stood against a wall on a street corner, as a kind of open loggia, or covered balcony. It had only three arches, two on one side and one on the other. It is closely tied to the ceremonial entry of King Henry the Second into Paris in 1549. Nobles could watch the royal procession from its platform.

The name comes from what once stood behind it. This was the Cemetery of the Holy Innocents. The name refers to the children killed by King Herod in the Bible story of Christmas. For centuries, this was the largest burial ground in Paris. Generations of Parisians were buried here, often in mass graves. Bones were later stacked in galleries along the walls, called charnel houses, where passers-by could see them.

Life and death sat side by side here in a way that is hard to imagine now. Merchants traded under the galleries. People met friends and did business among the bones. In the early fifteenth century, a famous painting of the Dance of Death was made on one of the walls. It showed skeletons leading popes, kings, and peasants alike. Its message was simple. Death takes everyone, whatever their rank.

By the late eighteenth century, the cemetery had become a serious health problem. Neighbours complained of the smell, and some cellars nearby were damaged by the weight of the graves. In the 1780s, the authorities closed it. Over many nights, the remains were moved by cart to old quarries under the city. Those quarries became the Catacombs of Paris, which you can still visit today. So in a sense, the people once buried here now lie under southern Paris.

The empty ground became a market square, and the fountain was saved. It was taken apart and rebuilt in the centre of the new market, this time standing on its own. That created a problem. A freestanding pavilion needs four sides, but the original only had three arches. The sculptor Augustin Pajou carved new nymphs for the missing side. He worked hard to match Goujon's style, more than two centuries later. Try to spot the difference. Many visitors cannot.

The fountain moved again in the nineteenth century, when Paris was reshaped with wide streets and new squares. It was placed on a raised base, and the stepped cascades were added so that water could flow down all sides. That is the version you see now. Some of Goujon's original low reliefs, with sea creatures and small figures, were removed over time. Several of them are now kept in the Louvre, a short walk from here.

The square around you is named after Joachim du Bellay, a poet of the French Renaissance. He wrote at the same time that this fountain was carved, so the name fits well. Today the square sits at the edge of Les Halles. For centuries, that district was the great central food market of Paris. The old market halls are gone, replaced by a modern shopping centre and a large transport hub underground.

It is worth thinking about how much has changed around this single object. A royal procession, a cemetery, a market, and a shopping district have all passed through this spot. The fountain has been dismantled, rebuilt, extended, and moved. Yet the carved women still pour their stone jars in the same calm way. Teenagers sit on its steps with phones. Pigeons drink from the basins. Delivery bikes pass without slowing down.

Before you go, walk once slowly around all four sides. Watch how the light falls across the folds of the robes, and how the water catches the sky in the basin. Listen again to that soft, steady sound of falling water under the noise of the street. Then, as you walk away toward Les Halles or the Louvre, remember the old cemetery ground beneath your feet... and the long life of this small stone temple.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk all four sides to compare Goujon's and Pajou's carvings.
Come in morning or late afternoon for soft side light.
Pair the visit with Goujon reliefs kept in the Louvre.
Sit on nearby benches to watch daily square life.
Use the Les Halles transport hub to arrive easily.
