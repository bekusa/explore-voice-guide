The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Rue de Varenne is narrow and quiet, and its pavements are made of pale stone. A car door closes somewhere. A gendarme shifts his weight beside a tall wooden gate. Behind that gate stands one of the most closely watched houses in France, and you can hear almost nothing from it. That silence is part of the place. Welcome to the Hôtel Matignon, the official home and office of the French Prime Minister.

First, a small point about the name. In French, an hôtel particulier is not a hotel for travellers. It is a grand private townhouse, built for one aristocratic family. Paris has hundreds of them, and this district, the Faubourg Saint-Germain, holds some of the finest. In the early eighteenth century, noble families left the crowded old centre and came here. They built wide houses with courtyards in front and gardens behind.

This house began in the early 1720s. The man who commissioned it was a nobleman from the powerful Montmorency family, the Prince de Tingry. His architect was Jean Courtonne, who was also a respected writer on architecture. But the project grew expensive — very expensive. Before the work was finished, the prince sold the unfinished house. The buyer was Jacques de Goyon de Matignon, and his family name stayed on the building. It has carried that name for three centuries.

Now look at the layout, because it follows a classic Parisian pattern. The French call it entre cour et jardin, which means between courtyard and garden. From the street, you see only a gateway and a wall. Behind the gate, a paved courtyard leads to the main house. Behind the house, a long garden opens out. Courtonne had a clever problem to solve here. The street entrance and the garden do not sit on the same axis, so he arranged the rooms to hide that shift from visitors.

Here is the first surprise. Behind that modest street front lies the largest private garden in Paris. It covers about three hectares, a long green park of lawns and old trees. Few Parisians have ever walked in it. From the pavement, you cannot even guess it exists. In a city where every square metre is precious, this quiet green space stays hidden behind a single doorway.

Over the years, the house passed through many famous hands. In the early nineteenth century it belonged to Talleyrand, the diplomat who served the Revolution, Napoleon, and the restored kings. He was known for his elegant dinners, and he used food and conversation as tools of politics. Guests at his table here included ministers and foreign envoys. Even then, this house was a place where power came to talk.

Later in the nineteenth century, the house had other owners, including the wealthy Duchess of Galliera, a great patron of the arts. Then it took on an international role. For decades it served as the embassy of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. When the First World War began in 1914, that empire became France's enemy. The French state took control of the building, and it never returned to private hands.

In the 1930s, the house received the role it holds today. It became the seat of the head of the French government. Today that person is the Prime Minister, appointed by the President of the Republic. The President lives and works at the Élysée Palace, on the other side of the Seine. The Prime Minister lives and works here. Many journalists simply say Matignon when they mean the Prime Minister's office, in the same way people say Downing Street in London.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a gentle one. There is a tradition that each Prime Minister plants a tree in the garden. The custom is usually traced to Raymond Barre in the late 1970s. Each new head of government chooses a species. Some pick a classic French tree, others something more unusual. So the park is slowly becoming a living record of French politics. Each tree stands for one government, and each grows at its own pace.

The house has a famous front step as well. When one Prime Minister leaves office and another arrives, they meet in the courtyard for a handover ceremony. The French call it the passation de pouvoirs, the passing of powers. Cameras gather at the gate. The two leaders shake hands on the steps, give short speeches, and then the old one drives away. For many French viewers, this courtyard is a familiar image from the evening news.

Can you go inside? Usually, no. This is a working seat of government, and security is strict. The guards at the gate are there for a reason, so please keep a respectful distance. But once a year, during the European Heritage Days in September, the doors often open to the public. On those days the queue can stretch far down the street. People wait for hours to see the salons and to walk the long garden.

If you are here on an ordinary day, the street itself rewards attention. Rue de Varenne is lined with other old townhouses, many now used by ministries and embassies. Look at the heavy carriage doors, the stone carvings above them, the iron balconies. A short walk away, the same street leads to the Musée Rodin, set in another fine eighteenth century house with a garden full of sculpture. The whole neighbourhood keeps the calm, formal manners of an aristocratic past.

Before you leave, look once more at that gate. Behind it, a garden of old trees grows slowly, one Prime Minister at a time. Behind it, decisions are made that will reach the whole country tomorrow morning. And yet, out here, the street stays quiet. A cyclist passes. A guard nods. As you walk on along rue de Varenne, notice how much of Paris lives behind closed doors... and how much you can still read from the stone outside.
