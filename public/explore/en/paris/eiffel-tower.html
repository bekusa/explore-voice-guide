The guide

The story

The iron lattice hums faintly in the wind — a low metallic note that rises from the rivets and beams overhead. Stand at the base and look up. The structure disappears into the sky, a geometry so intricate it feels almost weightless, even though eighteen thousand pieces of wrought iron hold it together.

Gustave Eiffel built this tower for the 1889 Exposition Universelle, a world's fair celebrating the centenary of the French Revolution. Paris was meant to dazzle the world with modernity, and Eiffel delivered. The tower rose in just over two years — a feat of engineering that stunned contemporaries. Critics called it an eyesore, a metal monstrosity that would ruin the skyline. A group of artists and writers published a petition against it. They lost. The tower stayed.

Eiffel designed the structure to withstand wind. The curve of the legs, the open lattice, the taper toward the top — all of it calculated to let air pass through. On gusty days, the summit sways a few inches. You will not feel it from the ground, but the tower moves, breathing with the weather. In summer heat, the iron expands and the tower grows taller by several centimeters. The metal contracts again in winter.

The paint is not decorative. Iron rusts, and rust weakens the structure. Every seven years, a team of painters applies fifty tons of paint by hand, working from the bottom up. The color has changed over the decades — reddish brown at first, then yellow, then the warm bronze shade you see today, called Eiffel Tower Brown. The paint is applied in three graduated tones, darker at the base and lighter at the top, a trick to make the tower look more uniform against the sky.

Climb to the first level and the city spreads out in all directions. The Seine curves below, bridges stitching the left and right banks together. To the northwest, you can trace the line of the Champs-Élysées stretching toward La Défense. To the southeast, the golden dome of Les Invalides catches the light. The second level brings you higher still, above the rooftops, where the city flattens into a grid of avenues and parks. The summit — three hundred meters up — offers a view that extends forty kilometers on a clear day.

Eiffel kept a small apartment at the top of the tower, a private retreat where he entertained scientists and thinkers. Thomas Edison visited in 1889 and signed the guestbook. The apartment is still there, preserved with wax figures of Eiffel and Edison in conversation. Few visitors make it that far, but the space remains a quiet reminder that this tower was never just a monument. It was a laboratory, a radio antenna, a weather station, a symbol that shifted meaning with every decade.

At night, the tower lights up. Every hour after sunset, twenty thousand bulbs flash in a five-minute sparkle show, a choreography added in 1985. The light does not obscure the ironwork — it traces it, highlighting the lattice against the dark. Parisians complain about the crowds, the security lines, the vendors selling miniature towers on every corner. But when the lights come on, even the locals pause.

The grass of the Champ de Mars stretches south from the base, a long green corridor that was once a military parade ground. On warm evenings, people gather here with wine and bread, sitting in loose clusters, watching the tower as the sky fades from blue to violet. The iron structure becomes a silhouette, then a constellation of lights. The wind picks up. The lattice hums again.

Walk around the base and notice the names. Eiffel inscribed seventy-two names in gold letters on the first level — engineers, mathematicians, scientists who contributed to French industrial progress in the nineteenth century. No artists, no writers, no politicians. Just the builders. It is a monument to making things, to the belief that iron and calculation could reshape the world.

The elevators are original, updated over the years but still running on the hydraulic principles Eiffel designed. The cables pull the cabins at an angle, following the curve of the legs. Riding up feels less like ascending a building and more like climbing inside a machine. The gears click. The structure creaks. The city drops away.

As you leave, look back once more. The tower does not dominate Paris the way skyscrapers dominate other cities. It rises alone, open to the air, a frame rather than a wall. The light passes through it. The wind passes through it. It was built to be temporary, dismantled after twenty years. It stayed because it worked — as a radio mast, as a symbol, as a shape that lodged itself in the world's imagination. The iron hums. The city continues around it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit at sunrise to avoid crowds and see soft light on the iron
Book tickets online weeks in advance to skip long queues
Walk around the base to see all four legs and the inscribed names
Stay on the Champ de Mars after sunset for the hourly light show
Take the stairs to the second level for a closer view of the ironwork
