The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Gravel crunches underfoot as people cross the open ground between the Louvre and the Tuileries Garden. Somewhere behind you, a tour group gathers near the glass pyramid. In front of you rises an arch of pale stone and deep pink marble. It is smaller than you might expect, and much more detailed. This is the Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel. It is the older and quieter sibling of the larger arch at the top of the Champs-Élysées.

Many visitors walk past this arch on their way somewhere else. They assume it is a minor copy of the famous one up the hill. In fact, the order is the other way around. This arch was built first, in the years around 1806 to 1808. Napoleon commissioned it soon after his victories of 1805, and above all after the Battle of Austerlitz. The larger arch was started around the same time, but it took decades to complete. So this was the arch that Napoleon actually saw finished.

The design came from Charles Percier and Pierre-François-Léonard Fontaine. They were Napoleon's favourite architects, and they shaped much of the official style of his reign. That style is often called Empire style. It borrowed openly from ancient Rome to give a new regime the look of something old and permanent. Here the model was the triumphal arches of the Roman emperors, especially the Arch of Septimius Severus in the Roman Forum. Look at the three openings, with a tall central passage and two smaller ones on either side. That layout comes straight from Rome.

Now look at the columns. They are made of red and pink marble, polished smooth, with Corinthian capitals. Corinthian is the most decorative of the classical orders, crowned with carved acanthus leaves. On top of each column stands a soldier in full uniform. These figures represent different corps of Napoleon's Grande Armée, his main army. Take a moment to compare them. The hats, the coats and the equipment change from one soldier to the next. Each figure shows a different branch of the army.

Between and above the arches, marble panels carry scenes in relief. They show episodes from the campaign of 1805. You can find scenes of surrender, of treaties and of armies entering conquered cities. There is a scene from the surrender at Ulm and a scene from Austerlitz itself. There are also triumphal entries into foreign capitals. The figures wear the clothes of their own time, but the poses follow ancient sculpture. It is propaganda, but it is very good propaganda. These panels were meant to be read slowly, almost like a strip of illustrated news.

Here is the first surprise. The bronze horses on top of the arch today are not the originals. When the arch was finished, Napoleon placed four ancient horses up there. They were the famous Horses of Saint Mark, taken from the Basilica of Saint Mark in Venice after French troops occupied the city in the late eighteenth century. For a few years, one of the great treasures of Venice stood here in Paris, above a monument to French victory.

That display did not last. After Napoleon's final defeat in 1815, the allied powers forced France to return many looted works of art. The horses went back to Venice. For a time, the top of the arch had nothing to hold. Later, under the restored Bourbon monarchy, the sculptor François Joseph Bosio made a new group in bronze. It shows a chariot pulled by four horses, with a figure representing Peace. The meaning changed quietly, from conquest to peace, while the shape of the monument stayed almost the same.

Now for the second surprise, and it concerns what is missing. This arch was not built to stand alone in an open space. It was designed as a ceremonial gateway. Behind it, toward the Tuileries Garden, stood the Tuileries Palace. That was the main residence of French rulers, including Napoleon himself. The arch marked the formal entrance to the palace courtyard. Guards paraded here, and visitors passed under it on their way to the emperor.

In 1871, during the uprising known as the Paris Commune, the Tuileries Palace was set on fire. It burned for days. The ruins stood for years before they were cleared away. The palace was never rebuilt. So the arch lost the building it was made to serve. Today it frames an empty view of trees, lawns and sky. If you stand with your back to the Louvre, try to imagine a long palace façade closing the space in front of you. That gap is part of the story.

The square around you has an older history too. It is called the Place du Carrousel. The name comes from a grand horse festival held here in the seventeenth century under Louis the Fourteenth. A carrousel was a show of riding skill, with costumed riders, music and ceremony. The word survived long after the riders had gone. Today it also gives its name to the underground shopping hall beneath your feet, with its small inverted glass pyramid.

Next, look through the central arch toward the west. On a clear day, you can follow a long straight line. It runs through the Tuileries Garden, past the Egyptian obelisk on the Place de la Concorde, up the Champs-Élysées to the larger Arc de Triomphe. Beyond that, far in the distance, it reaches the modern business district of La Défense. Parisians often call this line the historical axis. This small arch is one of its starting points.

There is a subtle detail here that many people miss. The axis through the gardens and the axis of the Louvre do not line up perfectly. The old palace buildings follow a slightly different angle. If you look carefully from the arch toward the pyramid, the line feels very slightly off. The difference is small, but it shows how the city grew piece by piece over centuries. It was never drawn on a single plan.

Before you move on, walk once through the arch and look up at the underside of the vaults. Then step back to see the whole monument against the sky. Notice how the pink marble warms in the afternoon light, and how the white stone around it shows the dark marks of age. Children run across the open ground. Pigeons settle on the soldiers' caps. The arch was built to celebrate an empire. Today, mostly, it watches people walk between a museum and a garden. And that is not a bad fate for a gateway.

Key facts

Built around 1806 to 1808 to honour Napoleon's victories.
Designed by architects Charles Percier and Pierre Fontaine.
Modelled on the Arch of Septimius Severus in Rome.
Once topped by the Horses of Saint Mark from Venice.
Original horses returned to Venice after Napoleon's 1815 defeat.
Look closer

What to look for

Pink and red marble columns with carved Corinthian capitals.
Soldier statues in different army uniforms above each column.
Relief panels showing Ulm, Austerlitz and treaty scenes.
Bosio's bronze chariot with a figure representing Peace.
Distant Arc de Triomphe framed through the central arch.
Before you go

Practical tips

Stand in the central passage to see the axis westward.
Come in late afternoon when sunlight warms the marble.
Walk around all sides to read every relief panel.
Pair your visit with a stroll into the Tuileries Garden.
Bring a zoom lens for the soldier statues up high.
