The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The noise of the Place du Tertre, with its easels and its crowds, has faded behind you. Here on the quiet rue Cortot you hear gravel under your shoes, birdsong, and perhaps the creak of a gate. The air smells of damp earth and cut grass. You have reached the Musée de Montmartre, set in one of the oldest houses on the hill. It feels less like a museum and more like the home of an old friend.

The main building dates back to the seventeenth century. That makes it far older than most of what you see around Montmartre today. When it was built, this hill was not part of Paris at all. It was a village of farms, quarries, and windmills, outside the city walls. Wheat and grapes grew on these slopes. Millers ground grain in the wind. The house you are standing beside comes from that rural world, long before artists arrived.

One of the first known owners was an actor. His stage name was Rosimond, and he performed with the theatre company that Molière had founded. Molière was the great French comic playwright of the seventeenth century. Rosimond owned this house in the late sixteen hundreds. So, before it became a home for painters, this place already had a link to the stage. It is a small surprise that most visitors walk past without noticing.

The great change came in the nineteenth century. Paris grew outward, and Montmartre joined the city in 1860. Rents on the hill stayed low, and the light was good. Painters, poets, and musicians began to move in. The old house was divided into studios and small apartments. For several decades, it became a kind of shared home for artists. Many of them were poor. Some of them later became very famous.

The best known of these residents is Pierre-Auguste Renoir. He rented space here in the 1870s. Renoir was one of the Impressionists, a group of painters who tried to capture light and movement as the eye sees them. While he lived here, he worked on one of his most loved paintings. It shows a crowded Sunday dance at the Moulin de la Galette, a dance hall just up the hill. That painting now hangs in the Musée d'Orsay.

Renoir also painted in this very garden. One canvas, often called The Swing, shows a young woman on a garden swing, with patches of sunlight on her dress. Today the gardens around the museum carry his name, the Renoir Gardens. You will even find a swing hanging among the trees. Take a seat if it is free. It is one of the few places in Paris where you can sit inside the setting of a famous painting.

Another remarkable story belongs to Suzanne Valadon. She began her career as a model, posing for painters such as Renoir and Toulouse-Lautrec. But she watched closely while she posed. She taught herself to draw and paint, and she became a serious artist in her own right. Her work is bold, with strong outlines and frank, unidealised bodies. In her time, it was unusual for a woman to paint the nude with such directness.

Valadon lived and worked here in the early twentieth century. She shared the space with her son, Maurice Utrillo, and with the painter André Utter. Utrillo became famous for his paintings of Montmartre streets, with their white walls and quiet corners. The household was known for its arguments, its debts, and its talent. The museum has recreated Valadon's studio and apartment. You can stand in the room where she worked and look out of her tall window.

Notice the light in that studio. Large north-facing windows were valued by painters, because they give a steady, even light through the whole day. Look at the stove, the easels, and the simple furniture. These rooms were not luxurious. They were working spaces, cold in winter and crowded with canvases. Standing there, you get a sense of daily life behind the paintings that now hang in grand museums.

The museum itself opened in 1960. Its collection tells the story of the hill through paintings, drawings, photographs, and posters. You will see material from the cabarets of Montmartre, including the famous Chat Noir, or Black Cat. This was a lively cabaret where poets read their work and shadow plays were performed. Look out for the posters by Toulouse-Lautrec and Steinlen. Their bold colours and flat shapes helped invent the modern advertising poster.

The collection also follows the people who made the neighbourhood. There are images of dancers, singers, and the crowds of the dance halls. There are old views of the windmills and the vineyards. Together, they show how a farming village became a centre of art and nightlife within a few decades. And they show how the people who lived here saw their own world, often with humour and affection.

Now step back outside into the gardens. From the terraces, look down the slope. Below, you can see the Clos Montmartre, a small vineyard that still produces wine. It is a reminder of the old village, when vines covered much of the hill. Nearby stands the Lapin Agile, a small cabaret with a pink-walled house that artists have visited for generations. The view here feels private, almost secret, compared with the crowded steps below Sacré-Cœur.

Before you leave, pause in the garden once more. Feel the gravel underfoot and look at the old stone walls and green shutters of the house. Painters once carried their canvases along these paths. A swing still moves in the breeze. As you walk back toward the busy square, listen for the moment when the quiet ends... and carry a little of this garden with you into the streets of Montmartre.

Before you go

Practical tips

Sit on the garden swing for a quiet photo.
Visit on a weekday morning to enjoy calmer gardens.
Allow time for the garden terraces, not just galleries.
Combine your visit with a walk past the Lapin Agile.
Wear comfortable shoes for Montmartre's steep cobbled streets.
