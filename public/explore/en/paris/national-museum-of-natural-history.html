The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your feet, and the long avenues of plane trees throw stripes of shade across the path. Somewhere ahead, a child is laughing. Beyond the formal flower beds, pale stone galleries rise at the edge of the garden. This is the Jardin des Plantes, on the Left Bank of the Seine. It is the historic home of France's National Museum of Natural History, one of the oldest scientific institutions of its kind in Europe.

The story begins not with a museum but with medicine. In the seventeenth century, King Louis XIII created a royal garden of medicinal plants here. Physicians and apothecaries needed living specimens to study and teach. By 1640, the garden had opened its gates to the public. That was an unusual gesture at a time when most royal grounds stayed firmly closed. So the idea of shared knowledge has deep roots in this soil.

In the eighteenth century, the garden grew into a real centre of research. Its most famous director was the naturalist Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon. He ran the royal garden for decades. Alongside that work, he wrote a huge multi-volume natural history that tried to describe the whole living world. Buffon expanded the grounds, enlarged the collections, and gave the place a European reputation. Many of the paths you walk today follow the layout of his time.

Then came the French Revolution. Here is the first surprise. Revolutionaries attacked many royal institutions, but they did not abolish this one. They renamed it instead. In 1793, the royal garden became the National Museum of Natural History. It was reorganised around professorships, each devoted to a different branch of science. The new republic wanted science to belong to the nation, not to the crown. The garden survived because it was useful.

The decades that followed made the museum a laboratory for some of the biggest ideas in biology. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck worked here, developing one of the first full theories of how species might change over time. Georges Cuvier also worked here. He used comparative anatomy, the study of how bodies are built, to show that some animals had vanished forever. Extinction is a basic idea today. Two centuries ago, these halls helped prove it was real.

Now turn toward the largest building, the Grand Gallery of Evolution. From outside, it looks like a classical stone palace. Inside, it is a different world. An iron and glass structure rises above a vast central hall, and soft light falls from the roof. The gallery first opened in the late nineteenth century as a hall of zoology. Later it fell into decline and closed. After major renovation, it reopened in 1994 with a new design and a new purpose.

The centrepiece is a long procession of African animals moving across the floor of the main hall. Elephants, giraffes, zebras and antelopes walk together, as if crossing a savannah. Above them, the skeleton of a whale hangs in the air. The lighting changes slowly, so the space can feel like day, then dusk. Stand at the edge of the parade for a minute. Notice how the animals seem to be heading somewhere together.

Here is the second surprise. Among the specimens is a rhinoceros with a royal past. This Indian rhinoceros lived in the menagerie of Louis XV at Versailles. It died around the time of the Revolution, and its body was preserved. Today it stands in the gallery as a witness to two worlds — the court that collected exotic animals as status symbols, and the science that studied them afterwards.

Some of the most moving rooms deal with loss. The gallery has a space devoted to extinct and endangered species. Here you can see animals that no longer exist anywhere in the wild. The room is quieter and darker than the main hall. It is not designed to shock. It asks you to slow down and look closely at what has already gone, and at what might still be saved.

At the far end of the garden, near the Austerlitz side, stands a very different building. The Gallery of Palaeontology and Comparative Anatomy opened at the very end of the nineteenth century. Walk through its doors and hundreds of skeletons face you in tight ranks. They seem to be moving toward the entrance like a herd. The arrangement follows the old scientific style, dense and orderly, and many visitors find it even more memorable than the modern galleries.

The garden itself is part of the museum. There are botanical beds sorted by plant family, large greenhouses full of tropical plants, and a small zoo that is one of the oldest in the world. It was created in the 1790s, partly with animals from the royal menagerie at Versailles. In 1827, Paris crowds came here to see Zarafa, a giraffe sent as a diplomatic gift from Egypt. For many Parisians, she was the first giraffe they had ever seen.

One tree carries a story of its own. On a small hill in the garden grows an old cedar of Lebanon, planted in the eighteenth century by the botanist Bernard de Jussieu. According to a popular tale, he carried the young seedling all the way to Paris in his hat. Historians are not sure that part is true. But the tree is real, and it has watched the garden change for close to three hundred years.

As you leave, look back at the long view across the flower beds toward the stone front of the main gallery. The paths are wide and straight, the trees are clipped into neat lines, and the buildings sit at the edges like guardians. Around you, students sketch, joggers pass, and families sit on the benches. This was a royal garden, then a revolutionary laboratory. Now it is simply a place where Paris comes to look closely at the living world.
