The guide

The story

Stand at the corner where the boulevards meet, and listen. Buses hiss and scooters whine past. But behind the iron railings there is a garden, and beyond it a wall of rough, banded stone that looks nothing like the creamy facades of the rest of Paris. Those stripes of brick and rubble are Roman. You are looking at one of the oldest standing structures in the city. And the museum built around it holds a thousand years of the Middle Ages under one roof.

This is the Musée de Cluny. Its official name is the National Museum of the Middle Ages. It sits in the Latin Quarter, close to the Sorbonne, where students have argued and studied since medieval times. The museum is really two buildings fused together. One is a Roman bathhouse. The other is a late Gothic mansion. Between them, they cover roughly fifteen centuries of history. That is an unusual thing to find on a single city block.

Start with the Romans. Long before Paris was Paris, there was a Gallo-Roman town here called Lutetia. Its people built public baths on this slope, around the end of the first century or the start of the third — historians still discuss the exact date. The baths had hot rooms, warm rooms, and a cold room. Romans moved through them in order, a little like a modern spa. Most of the complex is gone. But one great room survived almost whole.

That room is the frigidarium — the cold bath. When you walk inside, look up. The vaulted ceiling rises high above you, and it is still the original Roman vault. Few rooms of this age anywhere in northern Europe keep their roof. The air feels cool, even in summer. The stone carries soot marks, patches, and repairs from many centuries. Look closely at the brackets near the top of the walls. They are carved as ship prows.

Those ships are not decoration by chance. In this same room stands the Pillar of the Boatmen. It is a set of carved stone blocks, made by the guild of river boatmen of Lutetia and dedicated to the Roman emperor Tiberius. It is often called the oldest monument in Paris. The blocks were found in 1711, buried under the choir of Notre-Dame. On them you can see Roman gods next to Celtic gods. Two worlds share one column.

Now jump forward more than a thousand years. In the late fifteenth century, the abbots of Cluny built a town house on top of the old ruins. Cluny was a powerful abbey in Burgundy, in eastern France. Its leaders needed a base in Paris, near the university. Their mansion is in the Flamboyant Gothic style. That means late Gothic, with stone tracery shaped like flickering flames. Look at the turret, the dormer windows, and the carved balustrade along the roofline.

Here is a story that locals like to tell. In 1515, the young widow of King Louis the Twelfth is said to have stayed in this house. She was Mary Tudor, sister of Henry the Eighth of England. According to the popular tale, she was kept here while the court made sure she was not carrying a royal heir. One room is still linked with her name in local memory. Treat it as a good story, gently told, rather than a certain fact.

Inside the mansion, find the chapel on the upper floor. It is small, but the ceiling surprises almost everyone. A single slender pillar stands in the centre. From it, ribs of stone spread outwards like the branches of a palm tree, holding up the whole vault. Light comes in through the tall windows. Carved niches line the walls. This chapel is one of the finest pieces of late Gothic interior design in Paris.

How did a private mansion become a museum? In the early nineteenth century, a collector named Alexandre Du Sommerard moved into the house. He filled it with medieval objects at a time when many people saw the Middle Ages as dark and crude. After his death, the French state bought the house and his collection. The museum opened in the 1840s. It joined the Roman baths and the Gothic house into one place of memory.

Now for the room most visitors come to see. In a softly lit, circular space hang the six tapestries of the Lady and the Unicorn. They were woven around 1500. The background is deep red, covered with tiny flowers — a style called millefleurs, meaning a thousand flowers. Five panels show the five senses: touch, taste, smell, hearing, and sight. The sixth carries the words À mon seul désir. In English that means roughly, to my only desire. Nobody agrees on exactly what it means.

The tapestries nearly vanished. In the nineteenth century they hung in a castle in central France, damaged by damp and rodents. The writer George Sand admired them and wrote about them. The writer Prosper Mérimée, who worked to protect French monuments, pushed for them to be saved. They came to this museum in the 1880s. Today the light is kept low to protect the wool and silk. Give your eyes a minute to adjust.

The second big surprise is a row of stone heads. They once belonged to statues of the kings of Judah on the front of Notre-Dame. During the French Revolution, crowds thought they showed kings of France and pulled them down. The heads were lost for almost two centuries. Then, in 1977, workers digging in the courtyard of a Paris building found them buried. Someone had hidden them with care. Their faces are worn, but many still show traces of paint.

The museum reopened in 2022 after a long renovation. It has a new, modern entrance building and a smoother route through the rooms. You will also see stained glass from the Sainte-Chapelle, ivory carvings, gold altar pieces, and armour. Outside, a small garden takes its ideas from medieval plants. Some beds hold herbs and flowers that medieval doctors and cooks would have known. It is a quiet place to sit between rooms.

When you step back onto the pavement, look at the wall once more. Roman brick sits at the bottom. Gothic stone rises above it. Modern Paris moves around both, loud and fast. The boatmen who carved their pillar, the abbots who built their house, the weavers who made the unicorn — they all worked on this same small patch of ground. Take a moment before you walk on. The city under your feet is much older than it looks.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the Roman cold room before the Gothic floors.
Let your eyes adjust in the dim tapestry room.
Rest in the medieval-style garden between galleries.
View the Roman walls from the boulevard corner too.
Book ahead during school holidays and busy weekends.
