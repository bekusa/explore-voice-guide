The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the traffic of Rue du Temple hums and rattles. In front of you, a great carriage gate opens onto a paved courtyard. The cobbles are uneven underfoot, worn smooth by centuries of wheels and boots. The noise of the street falls away almost at once. Pale stone walls rise on every side, and the light here is soft and even. You are standing inside one of the grand private mansions of the Marais. Today it holds the Museum of Jewish Art and History.

The Parisian word for a house like this is hôtel particulier. That is not a hotel. It is a private city palace, built around a courtyard and set back from the street. This one is the Hôtel de Saint-Aignan. It went up in the seventeenth century, when the Marais was the most fashionable district in Paris. Aristocrats and royal officials built their homes here, close to the royal square now called the Place des Vosges. The architect was Pierre Le Muet, a respected designer of his day.

Look at the courtyard façades. Tall pilasters run up the walls. A pilaster is a flat column, set into the wall instead of standing free. They give the courtyard a calm, ordered rhythm. This style is French classicism — balanced, restrained, and proud. The first owner was a diplomat from the de Mesmes family. Later the house took its name from the Duke of Saint-Aignan, who owned it for a time. Each owner left marks, but the bones of the courtyard remain.

Here is the first surprise. After the Revolution, this aristocratic palace stopped being a home for one family. It became a building of workshops and small apartments. Tailors, furriers, craftsmen, and their families lived and worked behind these noble walls. By the early twentieth century, many of them were Jewish immigrants from Central and Eastern Europe. The streets around you became their neighbourhood. The grand house turned into a working beehive.

That history leads to one of the most quiet and powerful details in the museum. Look at the walls around the courtyard and inner spaces for printed names. This is a work by the French artist Christian Boltanski. It lists the people who lived in the building in 1939. Some of these residents were deported during the Nazi occupation of France and never returned. The work does not shout. It simply records names on a wall. Many visitors walk past it without noticing.

After the war, the building fell into poor condition. The City of Paris acquired it, and a long restoration followed. The museum opened here in 1998. Its mission is to tell the story of Jewish communities in France and Europe, and around the Mediterranean, across many centuries. It is a museum of art, of daily life, and of memory. It is not a museum about one single tragedy.

Inside, the galleries move roughly through time. One of the oldest groups of objects comes from Paris itself. In the medieval period, Paris had a Jewish community and a Jewish cemetery on the Left Bank. Gravestones from that cemetery, carved in Hebrew, were found during building works much later. Some of them are displayed here. They are among the few physical traces left of medieval Jewish Paris. The French kings expelled the Jews from the kingdom more than once in the Middle Ages.

Much of the collection is about ritual and the home. You will see Torah scrolls and their decorations — silver crowns, finials, and breastplates. A Torah is the scroll of the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, read aloud in the synagogue. You will also see Hanukkah lamps, spice boxes, and wedding objects. Notice how each region gave them its own style. A lamp from North Africa looks very different from one made in Germany or Italy.

Here is the second surprise. A large part of the early collection was gathered by one man in the nineteenth century, a musician named Isaac Strauss. He was a conductor of court balls in Paris and a passionate collector of Jewish ceremonial objects. His collection first went to the Cluny Museum, the national museum of the Middle Ages. Much of it later came here. So many of these pieces have been in Parisian public collections for well over a century.

One gallery is devoted to Alfred Dreyfus. He was a Jewish officer in the French army, wrongly convicted of treason in the 1890s. The Dreyfus Affair split France for years. Writers, politicians, and ordinary families took sides. The novelist Émile Zola published a famous open letter in his defence, known by its first words, J'accuse. Dreyfus was finally cleared. The museum holds a large archive of documents connected to the affair, given by his family.

The art collection reaches into the twentieth century. In the early 1900s, many Jewish artists came to Paris from across Europe. They worked in studios in Montparnasse and became part of what critics called the School of Paris. You may find works by artists such as Marc Chagall, Amedeo Modigliani, and Chaïm Soutine. Their paintings sit beside objects of everyday life. The two together show how art and identity moved together in the city.

The museum also hosts temporary exhibitions, talks, and film screenings. These often look at a single artist, a city, or a theme in Jewish culture. Check what is on when you arrive, because it can shape your visit. Security checks at the entrance are part of the routine here, so allow a few extra minutes. Inside, the rooms are calm. Give yourself time with the smaller objects. Their details reward a slow look.

When you leave, the Marais is all around you. A short walk away is the Rue des Rosiers, long the heart of the old Jewish quarter. Older Parisians still call this area the Pletzl, a Yiddish word meaning little square. You will find bakeries, falafel counters, and small shops. The Centre Pompidou, with its bright pipes and tubes, is also only a few minutes away. The contrast between the two buildings is striking.

Before you go back through the gate, stand in the courtyard once more. Look up at the stone pilasters, then down at the names on the wall. This one house has been a noble palace, a crowded home for workers, and a place of memory. All three lives are still visible here. As the noise of Rue du Temple rises again around you, carry that thought with you into the streets of the Marais.
