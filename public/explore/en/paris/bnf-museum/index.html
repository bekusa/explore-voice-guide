The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Above the hum of the second arrondissement, the courtyard here is oddly quiet. Pale limestone, tall windows, and the soft scrape of shoes on stone. You are standing at the Richelieu site of the Bibliothèque nationale de France, the French national library. For centuries this was the heart of the library's collections. Today part of it is a museum, and it is one of the quietest places in central Paris to look at very old, very rare things.

First, a little orientation. The national library is now spread across several sites. Many visitors know the large modern towers by the Seine, in the east of the city. But this older complex, between rue de Richelieu and rue Vivienne, is where the story began. The buildings grew in stages over several centuries. Each generation added wings, reading rooms and staircases. The result is less a single palace and more a block of joined-up history.

The oldest layers go back to the seventeenth century. This was the world of Cardinal Mazarin, the powerful Italian-born minister who governed France while Louis the Fourteenth was still a child. Mazarin collected on a huge scale — paintings, sculpture, books and manuscripts. He gathered his treasures in a residence on this site. Part of that residence still stands, and you can walk through it today.

The finest survivor is the Galerie Mazarine, the Mazarin Gallery. Look up when you enter. The long vaulted ceiling is covered with painted scenes from classical mythology, the work of the Italian painter Giovanni Francesco Romanelli. Mazarin brought Italian artists to Paris on purpose. He wanted the taste of Rome in the French capital. Standing beneath it, you get a sense of how a seventeenth-century statesman wanted to be seen — learned, wealthy, and very well connected.

The gallery now holds rotating displays of the library's treasures. That is the first surprise for many visitors. The museum is not a fixed hall of permanent exhibits. Fragile objects like manuscripts, prints and photographs cannot stay in the light for long. So the library rotates them. A medieval book of hours might share the room with an early photograph or a sketch by a famous writer. If you come back in a year, you may see completely different things.

The second great collection here is older than the museum itself. It is the Cabinet of Medals, a royal collection of coins, medals, engraved gems and antiquities. French kings gathered these objects over many generations. They were seen as tools of learning, and also as symbols of power. Today they fill tall display cases with gold, silver, bronze and carved stone.

Among them is a bronze folding seat known as the Throne of Dagobert. Dagobert was a Frankish king of the seventh century. Tradition links the throne to him, but historians debate its real age, and parts of it may be much older or later. It is a good example of how royal objects collected stories over time. Nearby, look for a set of carved ivory chess pieces. Popular legend calls them Charlemagne's chessmen, but specialists date them to well after his lifetime, probably from southern Italy.

Then there is the Great Cameo of France. It is a large carved gem from ancient Rome, made of layered sardonyx — a stone with bands of different colours. The carver cut through the layers to make pale figures stand out against a darker background. The scene shows members of the imperial family of early Rome. It is one of the largest cameos to survive from the ancient world. Bring your face close to the glass. The detail in the faces and folds of cloth is extraordinary for something cut by hand.

Now, a moment for the reading rooms. The Salle Ovale, the Oval Room, is a vast reading hall under a glass-and-iron ceiling. It was built in the early twentieth century, and it took decades to finish. After the recent renovation, it opened to the general public without the old reader's card. You can sit at the long wooden tables, under the soft daylight from above. Students, retirees and tourists now share the same space, and the quiet is real. Please keep it that way.

The whole Richelieu site went through a long renovation that ended in 2022. The architects worked to open the old buildings to the city. New routes link the streets on either side. A modern staircase now connects the floors of the museum, made of light materials that contrast with the old stone. Here is the second surprise: until recently, much of this complex was closed to anyone without a research purpose. For a long time, Parisians walked past these walls without ever coming inside.

If you step into the garden on the rue Vivienne side, you will find another change. A planted garden now runs along the building, with paths and benches. It is a small, green pause in a dense part of the city. Look back at the façades from here. You can see the different periods of construction in the window shapes, the colours of the stone, and the changing roof lines.

The neighbourhood around you is worth a slow walk too. A few steps away are the covered passages of the early nineteenth century, with glass roofs and mosaic floors. The Palais-Royal gardens are close by, with their lines of trees and quiet arcades. This was a district of bankers, printers and booksellers. Even now, the streets around the library carry that bookish, slightly formal character.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Think about the layers under one roof here — a cardinal's painted gallery, a king's coins, a Roman gem, a modern public reading room. Listen again to the quiet of the courtyard. As you walk back out onto rue de Richelieu, notice how quickly the city noise returns. The library stays behind those pale walls, as patient as the pages it keeps.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current displays first, since fragile objects rotate regularly.
Sit quietly in the Oval Room reading hall.
Use the rue Vivienne garden for a calm break.
Combine your visit with the nearby covered passages.
Bring a small torch-free magnifier for the cameos and coins.
