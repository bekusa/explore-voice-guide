The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic circles you on every side — scooters, buses, the long sigh of brakes. Under your feet the paving is ordinary city stone. Yet somewhere beneath this roundabout, a line of darker cobbles traces the outline of a building that no longer exists. You are standing on the site of the Bastille, the fortress whose fall became the founding story of modern France. Almost nothing of it remains. That absence is the point.

The Bastille began in the fourteenth century as a defensive gate. It guarded the eastern approach to Paris, on the road toward the royal estates at Vincennes. Over time it grew into a heavy fortress with eight round towers and thick walls, surrounded by a moat. By the seventeenth century its military role had faded. The crown found another use for it. It became a state prison, a place where the king could hold people without a public trial.

That is why the name carried so much fear. Prisoners could be sent here by a sealed royal order, known as a lettre de cachet. In practice, that meant a letter signed by the king, with no court and no explanation. Many inmates were writers, pamphleteers, or people who had embarrassed powerful families. The young Voltaire, later one of the great thinkers of the French Enlightenment, spent time inside these walls for verses that offended the regent.

Here is the first surprise. On the morning of 14 July 1789, the fortress held only seven prisoners. Most were forgers, and one or two were considered mentally ill. The Marquis de Sade, the notorious writer, had been held here too — but he was moved out only days before the attack. So the crowd that gathered that day was not mainly coming to free people. They were coming for gunpowder.

Paris was tense that summer. Bread was expensive, royal troops were gathering around the city, and a popular minister had just been dismissed. Earlier that day, crowds had seized muskets from the Invalides, the military hospital across the river. But they needed powder, and the powder was stored here. The governor refused to hand it over. Negotiations broke down, shots were fired, and by late afternoon the fortress had surrendered.

The violence was real. Around a hundred attackers died, and the governor was killed by the crowd soon after his surrender. Yet the symbolic weight of that afternoon grew far larger than the event itself. A royal prison had fallen to ordinary Parisians. Within a year, 14 July became a national celebration. Today it is still France's national day, marked with fireworks, parades, and dancing in fire stations across the country.

Now the second surprise. Demolition started almost immediately, and one man turned it into a business. A building contractor named Pierre-François Palloy organised the work. He had stones carved into small models of the fortress and sent them to towns across France as souvenirs of liberty. Other stones from the walls were reused in construction, and many accounts say some went into the Pont de la Concorde, the bridge you can still cross today.

Look up now at the column in the centre of the square. Many visitors assume it marks 1789. It does not. This is the July Column, and it honours a later uprising — the July Revolution of 1830, three days of street fighting that removed another king. The tall bronze shaft carries the names of people who died in that fighting. Beneath it lies a vault where their remains were placed.

At the very top stands a gilded winged figure, balanced on one foot. It is called the Spirit of Freedom. In one hand it holds a broken chain, and in the other a torch. On a bright day the gold catches the sun and seems to float above the rooftops. Victims of the 1848 revolution were later laid to rest in the same vault below, so this column carries more than one chapter of Parisian revolt.

Before the column, there was a very different plan. Napoleon wanted a huge fountain here, shaped like an elephant. A full-size plaster model was built and stood on the square for years, slowly crumbling. Victor Hugo, the author of Les Misérables, gave it a second life in fiction. In his novel, the street boy Gavroche sleeps inside the hollow elephant. The real model was eventually taken down.

Turn toward the large curved building of glass and grey stone on one side of the square. This is the Opéra Bastille, opened in 1989 to mark two hundred years since the storming. Its modern style divided Parisians at first. It was designed as a people's opera house, a contrast to the gilded older opera in the west of the city. Its presence here keeps the square tied to the idea of public, shared culture.

The neighbourhood around you has its own character. Streets running east, especially Rue de la Roquette and Rue de Lappe, fill with bars and small music venues at night. Historically this was a district of furniture makers and craftsmen. To the south, the square opens onto the Port de l'Arsenal, a marina where boats rest on the water of a canal. That canal continues north, flowing hidden beneath the boulevard.

Before you leave, look down once more. Search for the line of different paving stones marking the old walls. If you take the Metro, watch the platform walls on line 5 — part of the fortress foundations is visible there. A building that was meant to last forever survives only as an outline and a few stones. Around you, the city keeps moving, and that motion is perhaps the most fitting monument of all.
