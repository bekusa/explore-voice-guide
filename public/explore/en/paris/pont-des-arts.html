The guide

The story

Listen to the sound under your feet. It is not stone, and it is not the dull thud of asphalt. It is wood: long planks that give a soft, hollow note with every step. Around you, the Seine moves slowly between its grey stone embankments. The light here comes from everywhere at once, from the sky, from the water, from the pale limestone of the buildings on either bank. You are standing on the Pont des Arts, one of the very few bridges in Paris where no car will ever pass.

This is a footbridge, and it always has been. It links two of the great institutions of French culture. On one side stands the Louvre, once a royal palace and now one of the largest art museums in the world. On the other side rises the Institut de France, crowned by its elegant dome. Under that dome sits the Académie française, the body that has guarded the French language for centuries. So this short crossing joins art to words, painting to grammar.

The name comes from the Louvre itself. In the early nineteenth century, the palace was known as the Palais des Arts, the Palace of the Arts. The bridge that led to it simply took the same name. It was built at the very start of that century, while Napoleon Bonaparte held power as First Consul. And here is the first surprise. This was the first iron bridge ever built in Paris. At the time, iron was a modern, almost daring material for a bridge in the heart of a capital.

The original bridge was meant to feel less like a road and more like a garden in the air. Parisians came here to stroll, to look at the river, and to be seen. At first, crossing was not free. Walkers paid a small toll to step onto the deck. That detail tells you something about the bridge's purpose. It was never about getting somewhere fast. It was about the pleasure of the walk itself, high above the water.

The iron did not age gently. Over the decades, the structure suffered. Bombing during the two world wars weakened it, and river barges struck its piers more than once. By the late 1970s, engineers judged it unsafe, and the bridge closed to the public. Then, in 1979, a barge hit it again, and a long section of the deck collapsed into the Seine. For a few years, this famous crossing simply did not exist.

The bridge you are standing on now was rebuilt in the early 1980s and reopened in 1984. The architect Louis Arretche designed it to look very close to the original. But look carefully at the arches beneath you, and you will find the second surprise. The old bridge had nine arches. The new one has seven. The builders widened the spans so that the piers would line up with those of the nearby Pont Neuf, which makes life easier for boats on the river.

Now turn and face upstream, toward the east. The river divides ahead of you around the Île de la Cité, the island where Paris began. At its western tip, you can see a small green point of trees at water level. That is the square du Vert-Galant, a quiet garden that sits almost on the river itself. Just above it, the Pont Neuf crosses the island. Despite its name, which means New Bridge, it is the oldest standing bridge in the city.

In the twenty-first century, this footbridge became known for something very different. Couples began to fasten padlocks to the metal railings, write their names on them, and throw the keys into the Seine. The habit grew fast. Soon the railings were covered with thousands of locks, layer upon layer, glinting in the sun. For many visitors, the locks became the whole reason to come here. For the city, they became a serious problem.

Locks are heavy, and the railings were never designed to carry them. In 2014, part of a railing gave way under the weight and fell onto the deck. Nobody was hurt, but the warning was clear. In 2015, the city removed the locks, and the total weight came to many tonnes of metal. The old mesh panels were replaced with clear panels that locks cannot grip. Look at the sides of the bridge now, and you see water and sky instead of steel.

With the locks gone, the older life of the bridge has returned. On fine days, you will often see painters with their easels, trying to catch the light on the water. Photographers set up tripods at the centre of the deck. Musicians sometimes play near the ends. And in the evening, people sit on the planks with bread, cheese, and a bottle of wine, watching the sky change colour. The bridge has always been a place to stay, not only to cross.

Take a moment to look at the two banks together. The Louvre side shows long, regular façades, repeated windows, and a strong sense of royal order. The Institut de France, on the Left Bank, curves around its dome, softer and more theatrical. Between them, the bridge is light and thin, almost delicate. That contrast is part of the design. The iron frame does not try to compete with the palaces. It frames them.

This stretch of the river is part of a larger protected area. In 1991, the banks of the Seine in central Paris were listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. From here, the reason is easy to see. Within a few minutes' walk lie the Louvre, the Île de la Cité, the old booksellers' stalls along the quays, and the dome of the Institut. Few places in the city put so much history inside one view.

If you have time, walk slowly from one end of the bridge to the other. Notice how the view changes with every few steps. Toward the west, the river opens wide, and on a clear evening the sun sets in that direction, turning the water gold. Toward the east, the islands and their stone bridges stack one behind the other. Stop in the middle, where the wind is strongest and the noise of the city feels furthest away.

As you leave, listen once more to the wooden planks under your feet. Feel the slight movement of the deck as people pass. The water below has carried barges, boats, and keys thrown by lovers. The bridge above it has fallen, been rebuilt, and changed its railings. But its purpose is the same as it was two centuries ago. It is a place to pause between the arts and the words, and to look at Paris for a while.

Practical tips

Come at sunset and face west for golden river light.
Bring a picnic and sit on the deck in the evening.
Stand mid-bridge for the clearest view of Île de la Cité.
Visit early morning for empty planks and calm photographs.
Combine with a stroll along the booksellers' stalls on the quays.
