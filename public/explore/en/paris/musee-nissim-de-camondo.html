The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The traffic of Paris fades on this quiet street beside Parc Monceau. Gravel crunches underfoot in the courtyard. Ahead of you stands a pale stone house with tall windows and a calm, symmetrical face. It looks like it has stood here since the age of powdered wigs. It has not. This mansion is a carefully built illusion of the eighteenth century, finished only a few years before the First World War, and every room inside carries a family story.

Welcome to the Musée Nissim de Camondo. The house was built for Moïse de Camondo, a banker from a Jewish family with deep roots in Constantinople, the old name for Istanbul. The Camondos had been among the most powerful bankers of the Ottoman Empire. In the nineteenth century, members of the family moved to Paris and settled in this elegant district around Parc Monceau. Here they joined a world of financiers, collectors and artists who were reshaping the city's taste.

Moïse had a passion. He loved French decorative art of the eighteenth century — the furniture, porcelain, silver and tapestries made for the court before the Revolution. He bought slowly and with great care. In the years before the First World War, he had the family's older house on this site demolished. In its place, he commissioned a new home designed to hold his collection. His architect was René Sergent, who specialised in this classical French style.

Sergent took his inspiration from the Petit Trianon at Versailles, the small, graceful palace associated with Marie Antoinette. Look at the restraint of the facade. There is little ornament, just clean lines, balanced windows and fine pale stone. Yet behind this old-fashioned face, the house was thoroughly modern. It had electricity, central heating, lifts and up-to-date plumbing. Moïse wanted the beauty of the past with the comforts of the twentieth century.

Here is the first surprise. Many visitors assume the rooms were simply moved here from an old palace. In fact, the house was designed around the objects. Wood panelling, fireplaces and furniture were chosen and fitted so that each room would feel complete. The result is not a museum gallery with labels on white walls. It is a home, arranged as if the owner has just stepped out and will return for dinner.

As you move through the ground floor and the main reception rooms, notice how light behaves. The garden side faces the green trees of Parc Monceau. On bright days, sunlight falls across polished parquet floors and soft carpets woven at the Savonnerie, the royal carpet workshop of France. The colours are gentle — faded blues, warm golds, creamy whites. Nothing shouts. This was the taste of a man who valued harmony over display.

Look closely at the furniture. Much of it was made by the master cabinetmakers who worked for the French court under Louis the Fifteenth and Louis the Sixteenth. You will see delicate marquetry, which is a picture made from small pieces of different coloured wood. You will see gilded bronze mounts on the corners and handles. These pieces were luxury objects in their own time, and Moïse searched for the finest examples he could find.

In the dining room, watch for the porcelain. One of the treasures of the house is a Sèvres service decorated with birds, often called the Buffon service. It takes its name from the Count of Buffon, a great eighteenth-century naturalist who published a vast illustrated study of nature. Each plate shows a different bird, painted with care, and often carries the bird's name. Lean in, and you can enjoy a small aviary of the Enlightenment on a dinner table.

Now the story turns. Moïse had a son, Nissim, and a daughter, Béatrice. Nissim joined the French army in the First World War and became a pilot. In 1917, he was killed in aerial combat. He was still a young man. His father was heartbroken. From that moment, the house and the collection took on a new meaning. Moïse decided they would become a museum in his son's memory.

Moïse left the house and its contents to Les Arts Décoratifs, the Paris institution dedicated to the decorative arts. He asked that everything remain as he had arranged it. He died in the mid-1930s, and the museum opened its doors soon afterwards, carrying his son's name. That is why the rooms feel so personal. You are walking through a father's tribute, kept almost exactly as he left it.

The family story has a darker final chapter, and the museum does not hide it. During the Second World War, Moïse's daughter Béatrice, her husband Léon Reinach, and their two children, Fanny and Bertrand, were deported by the Nazi occupiers. None of them returned. They died at Auschwitz. With them, this branch of the Camondo family came to an end. A memorial plaque in the house remembers them.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors walk past it. Go down to the service areas and the kitchens. They have been preserved, with large stoves, copper pans and tiled surfaces. Here you see how a grand Parisian household actually worked in the early twentieth century. The staff rooms and kitchens show the labour behind the elegance upstairs. It is one of the most complete views of domestic life of this period anywhere in Paris.

Before you leave, pause once more in a quiet room or at a window facing the park. Notice the hush. Notice the soft colours, the smell of old wood and wax, the faint creak of the floor. When you step back outside, look again at that calm stone facade. It is a house built to celebrate beauty, and it became a place of memory. As you walk toward the trees of Parc Monceau, you carry a little of both with you.
