The guide

The story

Rue de la Gaîté is a narrow street, and in the evening it fills with the sound of footsteps heading toward a show. Theatre posters cover the walls. Café terraces sit close to the pavement, and a small crowd gathers under a lit sign. You are standing in front of the Comédie-Italienne, a compact theatre in Montparnasse. Its name is far older than the building that carries it.

This street has been a place of popular entertainment for a long time. In the nineteenth century, this part of Paris sat just beyond the old city tax walls. Drink was cheaper here, so dance halls, cabarets and small playhouses opened along the road. Today several theatres still share this short stretch of pavement. The Comédie-Italienne is one of the smallest. Its stage belongs to one tradition above all — the masked comedy of Italy.

The modern theatre was founded by Attilio Maggiulli, an Italian director who settled in Paris. His idea was simple. He wanted to bring back to the French capital an art form that once filled its stages. That art form is commedia dell'arte. The phrase roughly means comedy of the profession, because it was played by trained professional actors. These actors were not amateurs reading lines from a book.

Commedia dell'arte began in Italy in the sixteenth century. Troupes travelled from town to town with a cart, a few costumes and a set of leather masks. They did not perform fixed scripts. Instead, they followed a short outline of the plot and improvised the dialogue. Each actor played the same character for years, sometimes for a whole career. Audiences knew these figures the way we know characters from a long-running television series.

You may already know some of them. Harlequin is the quick, hungry servant in a costume of coloured patches. Pantalone is the old Venetian merchant who is greedy and easily fooled. Columbina is the clever maid who usually understands the plot better than her masters. Then there is the Doctor, the boastful Captain and the young lovers. The lovers are the only characters who play without masks. Their faces had to show sincere emotion.

Here is the first surprise. The name Comédie-Italienne did not start with this theatre. For more than two centuries, it was the name of a royal troupe of Italian actors in Paris. Italian players were already performing at the French court in the late sixteenth century. By the seventeenth century, an Italian company had settled in the city. They shared stages with Molière's own troupe and played on alternate days.

Molière learned a great deal from them. The Italian actor Tiberio Fiorilli was famous for the character Scaramouche. Many historians believe Molière studied his timing and physical comedy closely. The fast servants, foolish fathers and secret lovers in French classical comedy owe much to these Italian masks. So when you watch a show here, you are seeing the roots of plays that French schoolchildren still study today.

Then came a scandal. In 1697, Louis the Fourteenth expelled the Italian actors from Paris. The usual story says they planned a play called The False Prude. Many people at court believed it mocked Madame de Maintenon, the king's devout and powerful wife. Historians still debate how true that story is. But the result is certain. The Italians lost their theatre, and their stage stood silent.

They did not stay away. Louis the Fourteenth died in 1715. The following year, the Regent, Philippe of Orléans, invited a new Italian company back to Paris. It was led by the actor Luigi Riccoboni. This second Comédie-Italienne slowly began to play more in French. One writer in particular shaped its new style. That writer was Marivaux, who wrote many of his finest comedies for these Italian actors.

Here is the second surprise. One of the most loved plays in French theatre, The Game of Love and Chance, was first performed by this Italian troupe in 1730. It is not a French national institution's original work at all. The painter Antoine Watteau also loved these actors. His paintings of Pierrot, standing pale and still in his white costume, helped turn the Italian comedians into a lasting image of French art.

Over time, the old troupe changed form. In the eighteenth century, it merged with the Opéra-Comique, a company that mixed spoken dialogue with music. Later the company moved to a new hall near the Grands Boulevards. That is why one of the grand avenues on the Right Bank is still called the Boulevard des Italiens. It is named after the actors, even though the original troupe no longer exists.

So the theatre in front of you carries a name with a long memory. It is not the same institution, and it does not pretend to be. It is a small, independent house that keeps the masked tradition alive. The productions often draw on Italian classics, especially the comedies of Carlo Goldoni, a Venetian playwright who reshaped commedia in the eighteenth century. The hall is intimate, and the actors are close to the audience. Some laughter here feels almost personal.

If you go inside, notice how much the actors speak with their bodies. A mask hides the face, so every emotion must travel through posture, gesture and voice. A shrug, a sudden jump, a slow turn of the head... these small movements carry the joke. This physical style is the real heart of the tradition. You can follow it even if you do not understand every word of French or Italian.

Before you walk on, look once more at the entrance and the posters beside it. Listen to the street around you — the café conversations, the queues for other shows, the evening traffic of Montparnasse. Four centuries ago, Parisians gathered in much the same way to laugh at Harlequin's tricks. The masks are older than the city's boulevards. And on this short, noisy street, they are still being worn.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book seats ahead because the auditorium is very small.
Arrive early to eat at a café on rue de la Gaîté.
Read a short plot summary before the show begins.
Sit near the front to see the actors' gestures closely.
Combine your visit with a walk through Montparnasse Cemetery nearby.
