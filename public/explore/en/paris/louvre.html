Louvre Museum

48.8606° N · 2.3352° E · Art museum

Louvre Museum is the world's largest and most-visited art museum, set in a former royal palace beside the Seine in Paris, France. Plan 2–4 hours for the highlights — the Mona Lisa, the Venus de Milo, the Winged Victory. The free Lokali audio guide tells its story in 45 languages, offline, at no cost.

Masterpieces and where to find them

01

Mona Lisa

Leonardo da Vinci

Leonardo's portrait of Lisa Gherardini, whose shifting expression made it the most famous painting in the world.

1503-1519 · Denon Wing, Level 1, Salle 711

02

The Wedding Feast at Cana

Paolo Veronese

Veronese's vast Venetian banquet hangs directly opposite the Mona Lisa — the largest painting in the Louvre.

1562-1563 · Denon Wing, Level 1, Salle 711

03

Venus de Milo

The Hellenistic marble Aphrodite found on Milos in 1820, famous precisely for her missing arms.

150-125 BCE · Sully Wing, Level 0, Salle 345

04

Winged Victory of Samothrace

A winged Nike landing on a ship's prow at the top of the Daru staircase, her drapery pressed flat by sea wind.

200-175 BCE · Denon Wing, Level 1, Salle 703

05

The Raft of the Medusa

Théodore Géricault

Géricault's monumental scene of a real 1816 shipwreck scandal, painted from corpses he studied in morgues.

1818-1819 · Denon Wing, Level 1, Salle 700

06

The Coronation of Napoleon

Jacques-Louis David

David's wall-sized ceremony in which Napoleon crowns Josephine himself, before a court he partly invented.

1805-1807 · Denon Wing, Level 1, Salle 702

07

Liberty Leading the People

Eugène Delacroix

Delacroix's Liberty carries the tricolour over the barricades of the July Revolution of 1830.

1830 · Denon Wing, Level 1, Salle 700

08

Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss

Antonio Canova

Canova's marble holds the instant just before a kiss, polished until stone looks like skin.

1787-1793 · Denon Wing, Level 0, Salle 403

09

The Lacemaker

Johannes Vermeer

Vermeer's smallest and most concentrated painting: a young woman bent over her lace pillow.

1669-1670 · Richelieu Wing, Level 2, Salle 837

10

Seated Scribe

A painted limestone official from ancient Egypt whose inlaid crystal eyes still look alive after 4,500 years.

2675-2545 BCE · Sully Wing, Level 1, Salle 635

11

Great Sphinx of Tanis

Nearly five metres of granite lion with a pharaoh's head, guarding the entrance to the Egyptian galleries.

2675-2545 BCE or 1900-1865 BCE · Sully Wing, Level -1, Salle 338

12

Code of Hammurabi

The basalt stele carrying Babylon's laws in cuneiform — one of the oldest legal texts on earth.

1792-1750 BCE · Richelieu Wing, Level 0, Salle 227

02

The story

Stand still for a moment in the courtyard. You can hear several languages at once. Suitcase wheels rattle over the stones. Somewhere a guide raises her voice above the crowd. In the centre sits a pyramid of glass and metal. The old palace walls stand around it on three sides. The scale takes a moment to absorb. This courtyard is wide, and the buildings are long. Look up at the carved stone facades. Then look down at the glass. These walls were built over several centuries. The glass is from the 1980s.

The story starts underground. Around 1190, a king named Philip Augustus built a fortress on this spot. Paris was smaller then, and the fortress guarded the river approach to the city. That building disappeared for centuries. Then, during construction work in the 1980s, archaeologists uncovered its base. You can walk down and see it today. The medieval foundations sit in the basement of the museum. Stand close to the old stone. It is rough, and much darker than the walls above your head.

The fortress did not stay a fortress. French kings kept rebuilding on the site. They widened it, added wings, and turned defence into display. By 1364 it had become a royal residence, and it kept expanding for centuries. Each ruler wanted to leave a mark, so the styles do not match. Walk along the outside and you will see the changes. Some facades are plain and heavy. Others carry columns, carved figures and deep window frames. This palace is really many buildings, joined together over a very long time.

Then came the French Revolution. In 1793 the palace opened as a public museum. That date matters more than it first sounds. The royal collection had mostly been kept for the court, with only occasional public showings. Now ordinary people could walk in and look at paintings that had belonged to kings. The idea spread across Europe. Many national museums later followed the same model. So when you queue here, you are joining something that began as a political act. The art was declared to belong to the public.

The glass pyramid is much newer. The architect I. M. Pei designed it, and it opened in 1989. Many Parisians disliked it at first. They argued that glass and steel did not belong beside a royal palace. The complaints faded. The pyramid solved a real problem, because the museum needed one clear entrance and a large space underneath to hold the crowds. Stand close to it and look through the panes. The glass was made extra clear, so you can see the old stone facades through it.

Most visitors come for one painting. The Mona Lisa, known in French as La Joconde, was painted by Leonardo da Vinci. It is smaller than people expect. It hangs behind glass, and a crowd stands in front of it at almost every hour of the day. Follow the signs rather than a map. In the room a line usually leads to a barrier in front of the painting. The line moves quickly, so your time at the front is short. You can also look from further back, where fewer people stand.

Here is the part that surprises people. In 1911 the painting was stolen. An Italian workman who had fitted glass in the museum took it off the wall and walked out. The theft was not noticed straight away. The painting was missing for more than two years, and crowds came to look at the empty place on the wall. It was found in Italy and returned. Many historians argue that the theft is what made it famous. Before then it was admired, but it was not the painting everyone wanted to see.

Two sculptures compete for second place. The Venus de Milo is a Greek marble figure of a woman, missing both arms. They were already missing when the statue was dug up in 1820. The Winged Victory of Samothrace stands on a carved stone shaped like the front of a ship. She has no head and no arms either. She still looks like she is moving forward. Her clothing is carved as if wind is pressing the fabric against her body. Look at those folds from the side, not only from the front.

The collection reaches far beyond Europe. There are rooms of ancient Egypt, with coffins, statues and everyday objects. There are galleries from the ancient Near East. It also holds Greek and Roman sculpture, Islamic art, French furniture and decorative objects. Some sections close for renovation, so check which rooms are open on the day you come. The paintings run up to the middle of the nineteenth century. Later art went to other museums in the city. So this place is not really one museum. It is several museums that share a roof, a ticket and a very long system of corridors.

One object in the Near Eastern rooms is worth a detour. It is the Code of Hammurabi, a stele, which means an upright slab of carved stone. It carries a long list of laws from ancient Mesopotamia, cut into the stone in wedge-shaped writing. The text covers trade, family matters, property and punishment. Stand close and look at the top of the stone. A seated figure hands a symbol of authority to a standing king. The carving is shallow, but the detail is still sharp.

The building has not always been full. During the Second World War, staff packed the most valuable works and moved them out of Paris. Paintings travelled in trucks and were hidden in country houses far from the city. Sculptures too large to move were protected where they stood. For a long period the galleries held empty frames and sandbags. The works that left the building came back afterwards. That is worth remembering when a room feels crowded. The crowd is actually a good sign.

Now a warning about size. You cannot see all of this in one visit. The galleries stretch for kilometres, and a determined walk through every room would take days. Trying to do it makes people tired and unhappy. Choose three or four things instead. Walk to them slowly, and let yourself stop at whatever catches your eye on the way. The best hours here usually come from wandering into a quiet room and staying much longer than you planned.

So take a breath where you are standing. Around you the palace still holds the shape of its old courtyards and long wings. Kings ate here. Soldiers guarded these walls. Painters had studios inside. Now the doors are open to anyone with a ticket. The objects inside have travelled from Egypt, Greece, Mesopotamia and half of Europe to end up in one place. Look once more at the stone and the glass together. Then go inside and find one thing you love.

04

Key facts

A fortress stood on this site from around 1190.
The palace opened as a public museum in 1793.
Architect I. M. Pei's glass pyramid opened in 1989.
The Mona Lisa was stolen in 1911.
The Code of Hammurabi stele came from ancient Mesopotamia.

05

What to look for

Wind-pressed carved folds on the Winged Victory's clothing.
The broken joints where the Venus de Milo's arms once fitted.
A seated figure carved at the top of the stele.
Old stone facades seen through the pyramid's glass panes.

06

Practical tips

Book a timed entry slot online before you come, because in summer everyone needs a reservation.
Check the museum website for room closures before you plan your route.
See everything you want in one go, because you cannot come back in after you leave.
Go down to the medieval foundations in the basement.

Common questions

How long do you need at the Louvre?

Plan 2–4 hours for the highlights. First-time visitors usually want at least 3 hours; seeing everything would take days, so pick a few wings and walk slowly. The free 8-minute Lokali audio guide gives you the story as you go.

What should you not miss at the Louvre?

Start with the Mona Lisa (Denon Wing, Salle 711), the Winged Victory at the top of the Daru staircase and the Venus de Milo. Then pick from the table on this page — every work lists its wing and room, so you can build your own route in minutes.
