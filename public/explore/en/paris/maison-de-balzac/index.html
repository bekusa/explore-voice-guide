The guide

The story

Listen for the gravel under your shoes as you come down the garden path. The city noise drops away here. You can hear birds in the trees, a coffee cup set down on a saucer, and somewhere below the hill, the faint hum of traffic along the Seine. It does not feel like the house of a famous man. It feels like a modest country cottage that someone forgot to tell about the twenty-first century.

This is the Maison de Balzac, on the slopes of Passy, in the sixteenth arrondissement of Paris. Honoré de Balzac lived here in the 1840s, for about seven years. It is the only one of his many Paris homes that still stands. The others were rented, abandoned, or later demolished. So this small house carries a rare weight: it is the one place in the city where you can stand in the rooms where he actually worked.

When Balzac arrived, Passy was not yet part of Paris. It was a village on a hillside west of the city, a place of vineyards, gardens and quiet lanes. The city absorbed it later in the nineteenth century. For a writer who needed silence and long working nights, it offered calm. But Balzac had another reason to choose this hill. He was hiding.

Balzac spent much of his adult life in debt. He borrowed, invested in failed business ventures, and spent freely on furniture, clothes and fine objects. Creditors followed him across Paris. So he rented this house under an assumed name, and visitors reportedly had to give a password before they were let in. It sounds like a scene from one of his own novels, and in a way, it was.

Now here is the detail most visitors miss. The house is built into the slope, so it has two levels of access. The main entrance faces the street at the top. But a lower door opens onto a narrow lane below, called rue Berton. If an unwelcome visitor knocked at the front, Balzac could slip out the back and disappear down the hill. Before you leave today, walk around to see that lane. It is one of the oldest-looking streets in this part of Paris.

Inside, the rooms are small and simple. Balzac rented only part of the building, a handful of rooms on one floor. The most important of them is his study. Here you will find his writing table, a small, plain piece of furniture with no grandeur at all. Yet on this table, Balzac corrected and reshaped a huge body of work, the vast cycle of novels he called La Comédie humaine, meaning The Human Comedy.

The title is worth a moment. Balzac wanted to describe all of French society in his time: bankers and beggars, priests and lawyers, young men arriving from the provinces with nothing but ambition. He wrote dozens of novels and stories, and many characters return from one book to another. A doctor who treats a patient in one novel may appear at a dinner party in the next. It was a new idea in literature, a whole fictional world built book by book.

In these rooms, Balzac also wrote some of his late works, including the novels Cousin Bette and Cousin Pons. Both are dark, sharp stories about family, money and envy in Paris. If you have read them, you may notice how often money drives the plot. Balzac knew the subject from the inside. He understood debt and desire because he lived with both every day, just a few steps from where you stand.

Look for one small object that tells you a great deal about his working habits: his coffee pot. Balzac was famous for drinking enormous amounts of strong black coffee. He often wrote through the night, sometimes for many hours at a stretch, and coffee kept him going. Many biographers believe this habit damaged his health. Here, the pot sits behind glass, ordinary and a little worn, a quiet witness to all those long nights.

As you move through the house, you will see portraits, manuscripts, printed proofs and editions of his books. Look closely at the proof pages if any are on display. Balzac was known for covering his printers' proofs with corrections, arrows and additions, sometimes rewriting whole passages again and again. Printers reportedly dreaded his pages. Seeing that dense handwriting gives you a real sense of how restless and demanding his mind was.

The museum is run today by the City of Paris. It was renovated and reopened a few years ago, and the garden gained a small café. That garden is perhaps the best surprise of the visit. From its edge, you look out over the rooftops toward the river, and the top of the Eiffel Tower rises in the distance. Balzac never saw that tower. It was built decades after his death. But he would have known this same view of the river valley.

Balzac left this house in 1847 and moved to a grander home elsewhere in the city. He had spent years courting a Polish noblewoman, Ewelina Hańska, through long letters. They finally married in 1850. Only a few months later, Balzac died in Paris, at the age of fifty-one. His years of overwork had worn him out. Seen in that light, this small house marks his last long period of steady creative work.

Before you go, take a seat in the garden for a few minutes. Notice the plants, the stone walls, the steps that lead down the slope. Think of the novels that passed across that little table inside, and the man who kept one ear open for a knock at the door. Paris today is very different from his Paris. Yet here, on this hillside, the scale of his life still feels close and human.

When you walk away, take the lower route if you can. Go down toward rue Berton, with its old stone walls and uneven paving. Imagine a heavy man in a monk-like dressing gown, slipping quietly out of his back door and down this lane, leaving a creditor waiting at the front. Then look up at the house one last time — a modest building, and the last home in Paris of one of its greatest observers.
