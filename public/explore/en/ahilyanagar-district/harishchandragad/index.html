The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here, the wind does most of the talking. It moves across open grass, over black basalt, and around the edges of cliffs that drop away into cloud. Underfoot, the rock is rough and dark, warmed by the sun or slick with monsoon rain. This is Harishchandragad, a hill fort high in the Western Ghats, the long mountain range that runs down India's western side. You have climbed to get here. Your legs probably feel it.

The Western Ghats are made of basalt, a volcanic rock laid down in enormous lava flows a very long time ago. Over ages, rain and wind cut that rock into flat-topped plateaus, sharp ridges and sheer walls. Harishchandragad sits on one of these plateaus. It is a remote place. There is no road to the top. Everyone who stands here, pilgrim or trekker, has arrived on foot. That shared effort gives the summit a quiet, friendly feeling among strangers.

The name comes from Harishchandra, a king from Hindu legend. In the old stories, he was famous for never telling a lie, even when the truth cost him his kingdom and his family. Locals connect the fort and its temple to his name, and to ancient tales of sages and gods. These are traditions, not documented history. But they explain why people have walked up this mountain for centuries, long before trekking became a weekend hobby.

The fort itself is older than most visitors expect. Historians generally place its first settlement many centuries ago, and the caves and carvings here show long use by religious communities. Later, during the age of the Maratha kingdoms, forts like this one guarded routes between the coastal lowlands, called the Konkan, and the inland plateau. Do not expect grand palace walls, though. What survives here is mostly stone, water and carving, rather than towers and battlements.

The centre of the plateau is the Harishchandreshwar temple, dedicated to the god Shiva. Walk slowly around it. The whole structure is built from the same dark grey stone as the mountain. Some parts are cut directly from the rock. Its tall spire rises in layers, and the outer walls carry carved figures and decorative bands, softened by wind and rain. Moss and lichen grow in the cracks. In the monsoon, water runs down the stone and drips from every ledge.

Near the temple there is a stepped water tank, often called Saptatirtha. Stone steps lead down to the water on its sides, and small niches line the walls. Those niches once held carved images of gods. Many of the images have since been moved into nearby caves for safekeeping. So when you see the empty niches, you are looking at a gap in the story. Tanks like this kept a mountaintop community supplied with water through the long dry season.

Now comes one of the places most visitors remember. Close to the temple is the Kedareshwar cave. Inside, a large Shiva lingam, a rounded stone symbol of the god, stands in a pool of cold water. Four pillars once surrounded it. Today only one pillar stands complete. Local legend says that each pillar marks an age of the world, and when the last one falls, the present age will end. It is a story, not a prophecy, but standing in that chilly water, it is easy to understand why people tell it.

The cave water is very cold, often waist deep, especially after the monsoon. Some visitors wade in to walk around the lingam. Others simply watch from the edge. Either choice is respectful. Notice how the sound changes inside the cave. Outside, there is wind and voices. Inside, every drip echoes off the rock, and the light comes in sideways from the entrance. It feels far away from everything, even though the temple is only a short walk behind you.

The rock around the plateau is full of caves. Some were cut as shelters for monks and holy men. A few carry old inscriptions. Tradition links one of them to Changdev, a medieval saint of Maharashtra, who is said to have meditated here. Today, trekkers often use the larger caves to sleep overnight. On a busy weekend, you will see backpacks lined up against walls that were carved by hand, many centuries ago, for prayer and silence.

Then there is the edge. Walk west across the plateau, and the ground simply stops. This is the Konkan Kada, a vast cliff that curves inward like a giant wave of stone frozen in place. The face drops for hundreds of metres toward the Konkan lowlands. Please stay back from the lip, because the rock overhangs and there are no railings. Lie flat if you want to look down. Many people do exactly that, holding on to the grass.

Here is the second surprise. On some misty mornings and evenings, the Konkan Kada can show a rare effect called a Brocken spectre. When the sun is behind you and cloud fills the valley in front, your shadow falls onto the mist. Around that shadow, a circular rainbow can appear. You see yourself standing inside a ring of colour, floating in the air. It does not happen often. It depends on the exact angle of light and the right clouds. But people come back again and again hoping to see it.

Above the plateau rises Taramati, the highest point of the fort. The climb is steady rather than difficult, and the view from the top is wide. On a clear day you can see ridge after ridge of the Western Ghats, some flat-topped, some sharp. The area around the fort belongs to a wildlife sanctuary that protects this landscape. In the monsoon, the slopes turn deep green, waterfalls appear on every cliff, and small wildflowers cover the grass after the rains.

The people of the surrounding villages are part of the experience too. Many of them run small stalls and shelters near the temple. They serve hot tea and simple Maharashtrian food, such as pithla, a thick chickpea flour curry, with bhakri, a flatbread made from millet or sorghum. After a long climb, it tastes wonderful. Buying a meal here supports the families who keep this mountain welcoming, and who know its paths better than any map.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Look at the dark stone of the temple, the empty niches by the water, the long line of the cliff. Feel the wind again. Pilgrims, monks, soldiers and trekkers have all stood on this plateau and felt the same cold air. As you start the long walk down, listen for the change in sound — the wind fading, the birds returning, the forest closing in around the path.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb early to avoid midday heat.
Hire a local guide for the steeper or less-marked routes.
Carry a headlamp if you plan to stay overnight.
Keep well back from the Konkan Kada cliff edge.
Visit Konkan Kada on misty mornings for Brocken spectre chances.
