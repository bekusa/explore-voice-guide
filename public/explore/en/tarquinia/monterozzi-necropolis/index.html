The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the dry grass on this long, low ridge, and somewhere below you a car passes on the road to the sea. The ground underfoot is pale and chalky, and here and there a small, plain hut sits alone in the field. These huts do not look like much. But each one covers a staircase, and each staircase leads down into the dark, toward a room the Etruscans painted for their dead.

You are standing on the Monterozzi necropolis, the great city of the dead that belonged to ancient Tarquinia. The Etruscans were the people who ruled much of central Italy before Rome rose to power. They left us very little writing that we can fully read. What they left instead is right under your feet... thousands of tombs, cut into the soft local rock along this hill.

The numbers are hard to picture. Archaeologists have recorded around six thousand tombs across the ridge. Of these, roughly two hundred carry painted walls. That makes this hill one of the largest collections of ancient painting anywhere in the Mediterranean world. In 2004, UNESCO added the necropolis to its World Heritage list, together with the Etruscan cemetery at Cerveteri, further down the coast toward Rome.

The oldest burials here go back to the early centuries of the first millennium before Christ. The painted tombs belong mostly to a later period, from around the sixth century BCE onward, when Tarquinia was a rich and powerful city. Its wealth came from trade, from farming, and from the sea. You can feel that sea connection even now. On a clear day, the ridge looks out across fields to a flat, silver line of water on the horizon.

Once, this hill looked very different. Many tombs were marked above ground by round earth mounds, called tumuli. Over the centuries, farming, weather and time flattened most of them. That is why the landscape seems so empty today. The real architecture is below the surface. Each tomb is reached by a steep passage cut into the rock, which archaeologists call a dromos, meaning a corridor or entrance way.

Now, walk down one of those staircases. The air turns cool almost at once. At the bottom, a glass panel stands between you and the chamber. This barrier is deliberate. For centuries, the paintings survived in sealed darkness. When the tombs were opened, breath, light and humidity began to damage the colours. Today the chambers are closed and climate controlled, so visitors look in through glass, and only a selection of tombs is open at any one time.

Here is the first surprise. Many people expect tombs to feel gloomy. These rooms feel almost cheerful. The walls show banquets, music, dancing, athletes and animals, painted in red, black, ochre and blue. In the Tomb of the Leopards, couples recline at a feast while servants bring wine. Above them, in the triangle of the back wall, two spotted leopards face each other like guardians.

In the Tomb of Hunting and Fishing, the scene opens out to the sea. Small boats float on the water. Birds fly in every direction. And on one wall, a young man dives from a high rock into the waves below. It is one of the most lively images to survive from the ancient world. It shows a moment of pure physical joy, painted for a person who was already dead.

Other tombs carry a darker tone. In the Tomb of the Augurs, a masked figure appears near scenes of contests and struggle. Painted inscriptions name him Phersu. Some scholars connect this masked character with ritual games held at funerals, and some link the word to the later Latin word for a mask, persona. That link is debated, but it is a striking thought... a word we still use today may have started on a wall like this one.

As the centuries passed, the mood of the paintings shifted. Later tombs often show demons and figures of the underworld, alongside the family of the dead. The Tomb of Orcus is known for a delicate profile of a young woman, often called Velia, with a wreath of leaves in her hair. Her face is calm and serious. Many visitors find that this single portrait stays with them longer than any banquet scene.

The second surprise is about the rock itself. The stone here is soft enough to carve with simple tools, which is why the Etruscans could dig so many chambers. But that softness also makes the tombs fragile. Some paintings were removed from the walls in the past, to save them from damage and theft. Several of these now hang in the National Archaeological Museum in the centre of Tarquinia, a short drive away.

That museum is the natural partner to this hill. It sits in a fifteenth century palace, and it holds sarcophagi, pottery and the famous terracotta winged horses that once decorated a temple of the ancient city. Seeing the tombs first and the museum second works well. Out here, you feel the setting and the darkness. In the museum, you meet the objects that once filled these rooms.

Writers have long been drawn to this place. In the 1920s, the English novelist D. H. Lawrence visited Tarquinia and walked down into these tombs. He later wrote about the Etruscans with real warmth, as a people who seemed to love life. Standing on this ridge, you can understand his reaction. The dead here were sent off with music, wine and colour, not with silence.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment between the huts. Look at the pale soil, the grass, the line of the sea in the distance. Under this quiet field, thousands of rooms are waiting in the dark, many still unopened. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again to the wind. It is the same sound that people heard here over two thousand years ago, when they carried their families down the steps and painted them a feast that would never end.

Before you go

Practical tips

Expect steep steps down into each tomb chamber.
Check on site which tombs are open today.
Visit the National Archaeological Museum in town afterwards.
Bring a hat; the ridge has little shade.
Wear shoes with good grip for the rock staircases.
