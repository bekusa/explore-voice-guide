The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves through the grass with a steady, dry hiss. Somewhere close, a sharp chirp cuts across it, then another. Underfoot, the ground is hard and pale, scattered with tufts of grey-green sage. Crush a leaf between your fingers and the smell is sharp and clean, almost like medicine. The sky here starts at your boots and runs to every horizon. You are standing on one of the last large pieces of untouched prairie in Canada.

Grasslands National Park sits in the far south of Saskatchewan, close to the border with Montana. It is not one continuous block of land. It comes in two main parts, the West Block and the East Block, with ranch country and small communities between them. The West Block centres on the Frenchman River valley near the village of Val Marie. The East Block holds the rougher, eroded country known as the Killdeer Badlands. Each part feels like a different world.

Most of the prairie that once covered the middle of North America is gone. Plough and fence turned it into wheat fields and pasture. What you see here is mixed-grass prairie that has kept its original plants. Look closely at the grass at your feet. Needle-and-thread grass has long, twisting seed tails that catch the light. Blue grama grows in short, curled tufts. Low mats of prickly pear cactus hide between them, so watch where you sit down.

Now, that chirping. It comes from black-tailed prairie dogs, and this is one of the surprises most visitors do not expect. These are not dogs at all. They are ground squirrels that live in large colonies, called towns. In Canada, this park and the land around it are the only place where they live in their natural habitat. Stand still near a colony and you will see them stand upright at their burrow mounds, watching you, calling warnings to each other.

The prairie dog towns matter far beyond the prairie dogs themselves. Their burrows give shelter to burrowing owls, small owls that nest underground and bob their heads at intruders. The colonies also draw hawks, eagles, badgers, and coyotes looking for a meal. In the late 2000s, the park helped return the black-footed ferret to Canadian soil. This slim, masked predator had disappeared from the country for decades. It depends almost entirely on prairie dogs to survive.

Another animal came back too. Plains bison once crossed these grasslands in enormous herds before hunting nearly wiped them out in the nineteenth century. In the mid-2000s, the park brought a herd back to the West Block, moving animals from Elk Island National Park in Alberta. Today you may see them as dark shapes on a distant slope. Give them a very wide distance. A bison looks slow and heavy, but it can run far faster than any person.

Keep scanning the open ground and you may spot pronghorn. They are tan and white, with bold markings on the throat and rump. People often call them antelope, but they belong to their own family, found only in North America. They are the fastest land animal on the continent. On a quiet day you may also hear the drumming flight of a sharp-tailed grouse, or see a prairie rattlesnake warming itself on a dirt track.

This land also holds a long human story. For thousands of years, Indigenous peoples hunted bison across these plains. Across the park, archaeologists have recorded rings of stones on the ground. These are tipi rings. The stones once held down the edges of the hide lodges, and they stayed behind when the camps moved on. Finding one in the grass is a quiet moment. The circle shows exactly where a family once slept.

History came close in another way in the late nineteenth century. After the Battle of the Little Bighorn in 1876, the Lakota leader Sitting Bull led his people north into Canada. They stayed for several years in the Wood Mountain area, not far east of here. Later came ranchers, then homesteaders who tried to farm this dry land. Some of their weathered buildings and old corrals still stand in and around the park today.

If you travel to the East Block, the landscape changes sharply. The Killdeer Badlands are a maze of grey and buff clay hills, cut into ridges and gullies by wind and rain. The soft layers here are very old. In the 1870s, the geologist George Mercer Dawson, working with the survey that marked the international border, recorded dinosaur bones in this area. They are often described as among the first dinosaur remains found in western Canada.

When the sun goes down, the park offers something many people have never seen. Grasslands is a designated Dark Sky Preserve, and its skies are among the darkest in Canada. There are no city lights for a very long way in any direction. On a clear, moonless night, the Milky Way stretches overhead as a pale band of light. You can see the faint glow of distant galaxies with your own eyes, and the silence feels almost physical.

The park asks a little more of you than most places. Services are few and the distances are long. Water can be scarce, and summer heat on the open prairie can be intense. Weather moves fast, and a dry dirt road can turn to slick mud after rain. Many visitors drive the scenic loop road through the West Block, stopping at viewpoints and short trails. Others choose to walk out into the grass with no trail at all.

Before you move on, turn slowly in a full circle. Notice how the land is not flat at all. It rolls in long, gentle waves, cut by coulees, the narrow valleys where shrubs and shade gather. Notice the colours, which shift from green to gold to silver as the wind presses the grass. Listen again for the prairie dogs, and for the wind. As you walk away, the sound of the grass will follow you for a long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry plenty of water; services are sparse across the park.
Avoid driving dirt roads right after rain turns them muddy.
Bring binoculars for watching bison and pronghorn from distance.
Stay overnight on a clear, moonless night for stargazing.
Check where you sit; prickly pear cactus hides in grass.
