The guide

The story

Before you see the statue, you feel the hill. The granite under your bare feet is smooth, warm in the sun and cool in the shade. Steps cut straight into the rock climb the slope ahead of you. Somewhere above, a bell rings once. The air smells faintly of dust, flowers and incense. Then, near the top, a face appears over the temple wall. It is calm, enormous, and carved from the hill itself.

You are on Vindhyagiri, one of two granite hills that frame the small town of Shravanabelagola in southern Karnataka. The figure waiting for you is Gommateshwara, a giant image of the Jain saint Bahubali. He has stood here for more than a thousand years. He is carved from a single block of granite, and he rises close to eighteen metres. That makes him one of the largest free-standing monolithic statues in the world.

The climb itself is part of the visit. There are more than six hundred steps, and footwear stays at the bottom. This is a living place of worship, not only a monument. Take the climb slowly. Pause to look back. With every landing, the town below grows smaller, and the white water of the temple tank becomes clearer. That tank is in the town's name. Belagola means something close to "white pond", and a shravana is a Jain monk.

So who is the man in stone? In Jain tradition, Bahubali was a son of Rishabhanatha, the first of the twenty-four Tirthankaras. A Tirthankara is a spiritual teacher who has crossed beyond the cycle of rebirth and shows others the way. Bahubali and his elder brother Bharata quarrelled over a kingdom. The story says they fought, and Bahubali won.

But at the moment of victory, he stopped. He saw how empty the prize was. He gave the kingdom to his brother, left everything behind, and stood in meditation. Jains believe he stood so still, for so long, that he reached liberation in that standing posture. It is a story about a winner who chose to let go. That idea is the heart of the whole statue.

Now look closely at his legs and arms. Here is a detail many visitors miss at first. Thin vines curl up from the ground and wrap around his thighs and his upper arms. At his feet, you can make out the shapes of anthills, with snakes coming out of them. The sculptors carved what the legend describes — a man so deep in meditation that plants grew over him and creatures made homes at his feet.

The statue is nude. This reflects the Digambara tradition of Jainism, in which the most advanced monks give up even clothing as a final possession. The name Digambara means "sky-clad". Notice the pose too. The arms hang straight down, not touching the body, and the feet are planted firmly. This standing meditation is called kayotsarga, which means "giving up the body". The face carries a faint smile. It is serene, not stern.

The statue was made in the late tenth century, around the year 981. It was commissioned by Chavundaraya, a minister and general who served the Western Ganga kings, a dynasty that ruled much of this region. Popular tradition says he built it for his mother, who longed to see an image of Bahubali. Whatever the motive, the craftsmen faced an extraordinary task. They had no room for error. One wrong cut in a single block of stone could not be undone.

Here is the second surprise. Look near the base of the statue for old inscriptions cut into the rock. Several are in Kannada, the language of Karnataka. One short line, which records that Chavundaraya had the statue made, is often cited by scholars as one of the earliest known inscriptions in the Marathi language. A few carved words, set at the feet of a saint, became a landmark in the history of another language.

The most famous event here happens only about once every twelve years. It is called the Mahamastakabhisheka, which means "the great head anointing". Scaffolding rises behind the statue. From the top, devotees pour water, milk, sugarcane juice, saffron paste, turmeric and flower petals over Bahubali's head. The colours run down the granite in streams of white, yellow and red. The most recent one took place in 2018, and it drew pilgrims from across India.

There is a much-loved legend about one of these ceremonies. Locals tell of a time when the great offerings would not flow past the statue's chest. Then a poor old woman named Gullakayajji arrived with milk in a tiny pot. She poured it, and the story says it covered the statue from head to foot. The lesson is simple: humility mattered more than wealth. A small statue of her stands near the entrance — look for it.

Before you leave the top, turn around and face the other hill, Chandragiri. It is smaller, and it is covered with old temples and memorials. Jain tradition holds that Chandragupta Maurya, the emperor who founded one of India's first great empires, came there late in life as a monk with his teacher Bhadrabahu. Historians still discuss the details. But the tradition shows how long this valley has been a place of renunciation.

Take a moment now to simply stand with the statue. Watch the light move across the granite. In the morning it looks pale grey. Later in the day it turns warm and golden. Listen to the quiet chanting from the pilgrims around you, and the soft slap of bare feet on stone. Many people sit on the cool floor of the courtyard and look up without saying anything.

When you begin the walk down, notice how the face stays with you. It looks out over the town, the tank, and the green fields beyond. It was made by people who believed that real victory comes from letting go. As the steps carry you back toward the town, look up once more... and see the calm figure on the hill, still standing, just as he has for a thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Leave your shoes at the base before starting the climb.
Climb early morning while the granite steps stay cool.
Carry water and take rests on the stair landings.
Dress modestly; this is an active Jain pilgrimage site.
Visit Chandragiri hill opposite for older temples and quieter views.
