The guide

The story

Listen first. On this quiet residential street, you can hear water lapping against the shore of the Outer Alster, the large lake at the heart of Hamburg. Rowing boats glide past. A cyclist rings a bell. Then you turn, and a flash of blue catches your eye. Turquoise tiles, a pale dome, and two slender towers rise behind a garden. This is the Imam Ali Mosque, home for decades to the Islamic Centre Hamburg. Locals often simply call it the Blue Mosque.

The street you are standing on is called Schöne Aussicht, which means beautiful view. The name is honest. From here, the lake opens wide toward the city centre, and on a clear day you can pick out church spires on the far shore. This district, Uhlenhorst, is known for villas, white facades, and quiet lawns. That is exactly why the mosque feels so surprising. It sits among the old merchant houses like a visitor from another climate.

The story of this building begins with trade. Hamburg has been a port city for centuries, and in the twentieth century it drew merchants from Iran. Many of them worked in the carpet business, which grew strong in the city's warehouse districts. Over time, Hamburg became home to one of the largest Iranian communities in Europe. These families wanted a place of worship of their own, and they gathered support to build one in the city they now called home.

Construction began around 1960, and the mosque opened in the middle of that decade. That makes it one of the older purpose-built mosques in Germany. In those years, most Muslims in the country prayed in converted rooms, back offices, or rented halls. A full mosque with a dome and minarets was still rare. Here, on one of Hamburg's most elegant streets, the community built something that announced itself clearly.

Take a closer look at the facade. The blue and turquoise tilework draws on the traditions of Persian architecture, where glazed tiles have covered mosques and shrines for centuries. In Iran, cities like Isfahan are famous for this kind of colour. The tiles here are a reminder of that heritage, carried far north to the edge of a German lake. Notice how the colour changes with the weather. Under grey Hamburg skies, the blue looks deep and calm. In sunshine, it almost glows.

Now look up at the two minarets. A minaret is a tower traditionally used for the call to prayer. Here, the towers are slim and simple, and they frame the central dome. The dome itself is shallow and smooth, not tall and bulbous. This design reflects its time. It is a mid-century building, modern in its clean lines, but dressed in an older visual language. That mix of styles is part of what makes it unusual.

The mosque belongs to the Shia tradition of Islam. Islam has two main branches, Sunni and Shia, and most Muslims in Germany follow the Sunni branch. The name Imam Ali honours Ali, the cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet Muhammad. For Shia Muslims, Ali holds a central place as the first imam, or spiritual leader. So this building served a specific community within a larger faith, and for many years it was a gathering point for Shia Muslims across northern Germany.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Before the Iranian revolution of 1979, the Islamic Centre was led for a time by clerics who later became powerful figures in Iran itself. Mohammad Beheshti directed the centre in the 1960s and early 1970s. He went on to play a major role in the revolution. Later, Mohammad Khatami also headed the centre. Decades afterwards, he became president of Iran. A quiet lakeside building in Hamburg was, for a while, part of Iran's political story.

That connection did not end with history. Over the years, German security authorities came to view the Islamic Centre Hamburg with concern. They argued that the organisation was closely tied to the government of Iran and spread its ideology. In 2024, the German Federal Interior Ministry banned the association, and the authorities closed the mosque. The centre's leadership disputed the accusations. The decision drew wide public debate, both about security and about the place of religious communities in the city.

So the building you are looking at now stands in an unusual position. The organisation that ran it has been banned, but the mosque itself remains, and its future use has been discussed in public. Many ordinary worshippers had no part in the political dispute. For them, this was simply where they prayed, married, and mourned. It is worth holding both stories in mind as you stand here: the politics of a state, and the everyday faith of families.

Because the situation has changed in recent years, access may be limited. The gates may be locked, and you may only see the building from the street. That is still worthwhile. The best views are often from the pavement by the water, or from a little further along the lakeside path. From there, you can see the mosque framed by trees, with the villas of Uhlenhorst on either side.

If you have time, walk along the shore of the Outer Alster. The path here is popular with joggers, families, and dog walkers. Sailing boats move slowly across the lake, and swans often rest near the bank. In summer, people sit on the grass with coffee. In winter, the light turns silver and low. Seen from a distance, the blue dome becomes one more part of Hamburg's lakeside, alongside the white houses and the green lawns.

Before you move on, take one last look at the tiles. They were made to carry a memory of home for people who had crossed half a continent to trade and settle. Notice the quiet around you, the water against the stones, the wind in the trees. A city is made of many stories at once. This blue building, on a street named for its beautiful view, holds a few of them... and leaves the rest for you to think about as you walk away.
