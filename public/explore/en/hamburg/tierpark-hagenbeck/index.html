The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment just inside the park. Listen. Somewhere ahead, a peacock calls. Gravel crunches under strollers. Tall old trees throw a cool green shade over the paths, and through the leaves you catch something unexpected... a rocky ridge, with animals standing on it, and no bars anywhere in sight. That missing line of iron is the whole story of this place. Welcome to Tierpark Hagenbeck, in the Hamburg district of Stellingen.

To understand why this zoo matters, picture how most zoos looked around the year 1900. Animals lived in cages. Visitors peered through bars at a lion pacing on a tiled floor. It was close, cramped, and rather grim. Then a Hamburg animal dealer named Carl Hagenbeck had a different idea. He wanted people to see animals in something closer to a landscape, grouped together, with open space around them. The park you are walking through opened in 1907 as his answer.

The Hagenbeck story starts long before Stellingen. Carl's father was a fishmonger in Hamburg. In the middle of the nineteenth century, he put a few seals on display near the harbour, and curious people paid to look. That small show grew into a business. Carl took it over and built one of the largest wild animal trading companies of his time. He supplied circuses, menageries, and zoos across Europe and beyond. By the time he planned this park, he knew animals and audiences extremely well.

Here is the clever part. Instead of bars, Hagenbeck used moats and ditches. He studied how far each species could jump, and then he set the barriers just beyond that distance. The moats are hidden from the visitor's line of sight. So from the right spot, a predator and its prey appear to share one open scene, even though they are safely separated. Hagenbeck protected this panorama idea with a patent in the 1890s, before the park even opened.

The rocks you see were not quarried from a mountain. They are artificial, shaped by hand. A Swiss sculptor, Urs Eggenschwyler, worked on these early rock landscapes. Up close, look at the surfaces. You can see the texture of formed material, the weathering of more than a century, and patches of later repair. These rocks were theatre sets as much as habitats. They were designed to make a flat stretch of northern German land look like distant mountains and plains.

That theatrical eye did not stop at the animals. Around the grounds you may notice sculptures and decorative buildings that gave the park an exotic, staged atmosphere. Early on, Hagenbeck even placed life-sized models of dinosaurs here, made by the sculptor Josef Pallenberg. Imagine Hamburg families around 1910, walking past a giant prehistoric reptile on their way to see the elephants. For many of them, it was the closest they would ever get to the ends of the earth.

Now comes a harder chapter, and it deserves honesty. Long before this park opened, the Hagenbeck business also organised so-called ethnographic shows. People from places such as northern Scandinavia, Africa, and other regions were brought to Europe and displayed to paying audiences, sometimes in mock villages. Today these shows are widely understood as degrading and rooted in colonial thinking. Some participants fell ill and died far from home. It is part of this ground's history, and it sits uneasily beside the zoo's innovations.

The park also carries scars from the Second World War. Allied bombing raids on Hamburg in 1943 hit the zoo hard. Buildings were destroyed, and many animals and people died. After the war, the family rebuilt. What you walk through today is a mix of rebuilt, restored, and newer spaces, layered over the original layout. A surprising fact that many visitors miss: through all these upheavals, the park has stayed in the hands of the Hagenbeck family.

Of all the animals here, the elephants hold a special place. Carl Hagenbeck dealt in elephants for decades, and the park has long kept a herd of Asian elephants. Watch how they move as a group, how a younger animal stays close to an older one, how a trunk explores the ground. Feeding has been part of the visitor tradition here for generations, but always follow the signs and the keepers. The rules can change, and they exist for the animals' health.

For its hundredth anniversary in 2007, the park added a large tropical aquarium. Step inside and the air changes at once. It becomes warm and damp. The light dims. You move from the open Hamburg sky into a world of reef fish, reptiles, and creatures that live in caves and shadow. A few years later, a polar section called Eismeer, meaning Arctic Ocean, followed. There, cold-water animals such as walruses and polar bears live in rocky, water-filled habitats.

Notice how the newer areas still follow Hagenbeck's old logic. Wide views. Hidden barriers. Water and rock instead of wire. Zoo design has changed a great deal since 1907, with far more focus on animal welfare, space, and conservation breeding. Yet the basic idea of a landscape seen across an invisible boundary began here, and zoos around the world adopted it. Many visitors walk through the park without realising they are standing inside the prototype.

Take a moment to look beyond the animals too. This is also a place of old trees, ponds, and winding paths. Ducks and wild birds come and go freely. Hamburg families have spent Sunday afternoons here for over a century, and you will hear grandparents pointing out the spots they remember. The sound of the park is soft and layered... children, water, wind in the leaves, and every so often a trumpet from the elephant house.

As you walk on, keep looking for those lines of sight. Find a spot where the moat disappears and the rocks seem to roll on forever. That small illusion is the gift Carl Hagenbeck left behind, along with questions about how humans display the living world. When you leave, pause at the gates and look back. Behind you is a landscape built by hand, still telling a story about distance, wonder, and the animals we share the world with.

What to look for

Viewpoints where the moats vanish and enclosures seem to merge.
Hand-shaped artificial rock faces with visible century-old weathering.
Decorative historic gate with sculpted animal figures at the entrance.
Underwater viewing windows into the Arctic Ocean habitat.
Elephant herd members touching each other with their trunks.
Before you go

Practical tips

Take the U2 line to the station named after the zoo.
Arrive early to see animals during their more active hours.
Plan extra time for the separately ticketed tropical aquarium.
Bring layers; the aquarium feels warm and humid inside.
Follow keeper signs before offering any food to the elephants.
