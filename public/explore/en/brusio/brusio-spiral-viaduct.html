The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere up the valley, a faint electric hum grows louder. Then comes the steady clatter of steel wheels on curved rail. A red train appears, moving slowly, and begins to turn. It keeps turning. In under a minute it has drawn a full circle in the air above the meadow and passed beneath its own track. You are standing beside the Brusio spiral viaduct, one of the most unusual pieces of railway engineering in the Alps.

The viaduct carries the Bernina Railway, a mountain line run by the Rhaetian Railway, the main rail operator of the Swiss canton of Graubünden. The line links Saint Moritz in the Engadine valley with Tirano, just across the border in Italy. Here in the lower Val Poschiavo, the railway is in its final stretch. Tirano lies only a few kilometres to the south.

Look at the structure itself. It is built of grey stone, laid in a tight curve on a series of arches. Most counts give nine arches, all set along a single sweeping circle. The track runs across the top, then continues around and descends, finally slipping through one of the arches below. In other words, the train crosses over the same line it is about to use. Engineers call this kind of loop a spiral, and it is rarely built as a free-standing bridge in open ground like this.

Why build a circle at all? The answer is gradient. The Bernina line is an adhesion railway, which means the trains climb using only the grip of steel wheels on steel rail, with no toothed cog track. That limits how steep the line can be. Even so, parts of the route are among the steepest adhesion railways in the world, reaching about seven percent. Here the valley floor drops sharply, and the train must lose height in a very small space.

A straight line would have been too steep. A long zigzag would have needed more land than this narrow valley could offer. So the builders made the railway turn around on itself. Each metre of the circle lets the train descend gently. By the time it has completed the loop, it sits lower in the valley and can continue south at a manageable slope. It is a simple idea, carried out in solid stone.

The Bernina Railway was built in the early twentieth century. Sections opened in stages, and the full line across the Bernina Pass was completed around 1910. From the beginning it was designed for electric trains, which was forward-looking for a mountain railway of that era. The line is also metre gauge, narrower than standard main-line track. Narrow gauge allows tighter curves, and this viaduct shows exactly why that mattered.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Although the trains look modern, the stonework you see is part of the original design. The engineers chose masonry rather than steel, using local stone that matches the mountainsides around you. From a distance the viaduct looks less like a machine and more like part of the landscape. That harmony is one reason the route is so admired.

In 2008, the Albula and Bernina railway lines, together with the landscapes they cross, were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. UNESCO, the cultural agency of the United Nations, recognised them as an outstanding example of how a railway can be built through high mountains while respecting the scenery. This small circle of stone near Brusio is one of the most photographed parts of that listing.

Now turn your attention to the valley. Val Poschiavo is an Italian-speaking corner of Switzerland. Road signs, conversations, and church bells here belong to the southern side of the Alps. The air is milder than in the Engadine above. You may notice chestnut trees, terraced slopes, and gardens that feel closer to Lombardy than to Zurich. The railway has tied this valley to both north and south for more than a century.

The village of Brusio sits nearby, with stone houses and a church tower rising above the rooftops. For centuries, life here was shaped by the border. Trade moved back and forth between Swiss and Italian valleys, sometimes officially, sometimes less so. Locals still tell stories of goods carried quietly over mountain paths. Treat those tales as folklore, but they show how closely this valley has always been linked to its neighbours.

Trains pass the viaduct regularly through the day. Among them is the Bernina Express, a panoramic service with large windows that carries travellers between the glaciers of the Bernina Pass and the palm trees of the Italian lakes region. Ordinary regional trains use the same track, carrying commuters, schoolchildren, and shoppers. For many people here, this is not a tourist attraction. It is simply the way to get to Tirano.

If you stay for a few trains, watch how they behave on the curve. They slow as they enter the loop. The wheels squeal softly on the tight radius. Passengers press against the windows, often waving at people below. Then the train slides under the arch and disappears southward. A moment later, the valley is quiet again, apart from the river and the wind in the grass.

Before you leave, take one last look at the circle of arches. Notice how the line rises from the meadow and returns to it, like a stone ribbon tied in a single loop. Over a century of trains have followed that same path. As you walk away, listen for the next hum coming down the valley... and perhaps wait to watch it turn once more.
