The guide

The story

Listen first. Inside the great hall, voices drop to a murmur. Below you, behind a railing, lies an open pit of dark brown earth, stepped and squared by the patient trowels of archaeologists. Small markers still sit in the soil. The air is cool, a little damp, and it smells faintly of clay. This is the Jinsha Site, in the western part of Chengdu. You are standing above a place where people made offerings to their gods roughly three thousand years ago.

The story of how this place was found is almost accidental. In early 2001, construction crews were digging foundations for a housing development in this part of the city. Their machines began turning up pieces of jade, ivory, and gold. Work stopped. Archaeologists arrived. Within weeks it was clear that the builders had struck one of the most important finds in modern Chinese archaeology. The name Jinsha means golden sand, and it turned out to be fitting.

So who were the people who left all this behind? They belonged to the ancient Shu culture, a civilisation of the Sichuan Basin that grew up largely apart from the better-known kingdoms of the Yellow River in the north. Scholars generally date the main activity at Jinsha to somewhere between about 1200 and 650 BC. That places it after the famous Sanxingdui site, which lies north of Chengdu, and many researchers see Jinsha as the next chapter of the same story.

That link matters. For years, Sanxingdui puzzled historians. Its strange bronze masks and towering figures seemed to appear from nowhere and then vanish. Jinsha helped fill the gap. The objects here share a visual language with Sanxingdui — similar masks, similar gold work, similar rituals — but on a different scale. It suggests that the centre of Shu power may have moved south, onto the land where modern Chengdu now stands.

The pit below you is part of what archaeologists describe as a ritual area, a place for sacrifice and offering. Look at the layers in the soil. Over a long period, people buried precious things here: jade blades, stone figures, bronze pieces, and enormous quantities of elephant tusks. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Thousands of kilograms of ivory came out of this ground. Elephants once lived in this region, and their tusks were clearly sacred.

Take a moment with that thought. Today Chengdu is famous for pandas, teahouses, and spicy food. Three thousand years ago, this same plain was warm and wet enough for elephants. The tusks were laid in neat rows, sometimes in heavy piles. The ivory is fragile once it meets the air, which is one reason the excavation is protected under this roof. What you see in the pit is a working record, not a stage set.

Now walk across to the exhibition hall, where the finest objects are shown. The most celebrated piece is a small disc of gold foil, known as the Sun and Immortal Birds ornament. It is very thin, thinner than paper in places, and about the width of an open hand. At its centre is a ring of twelve curving rays. Around them, four birds fly in a circle, one after another, their long necks stretched forward.

Here is the second surprise. In 2005, this tiny disc was chosen as the official emblem of Chinese cultural heritage. So you may have already seen its outline without knowing it — on signs and documents across the country. Scholars still debate its meaning. Many read it as an image of the sun and the passing seasons, perhaps tied to beliefs about birds carrying the sun across the sky. The makers left no writing to tell us for certain.

That silence runs through the whole site. The Shu people of this period left no deciphered texts. Everything we know comes from objects, soil, and careful comparison. So look closely at the gold mask on display, with its wide eyes and its straight, firm mouth. Look at the small bronze standing figure, wearing a tall headdress shaped like a burst of rays. These faces are the closest we can come to hearing these people speak.

Some objects are harder to look at. In the galleries you will find a group of kneeling stone figures. Their hands appear to be tied behind their backs. Their hair is cut and parted in a distinct style. Researchers have suggested they may represent captives or ritual stand-ins used in ceremonies, but the exact purpose is not settled. They are quiet, modest pieces, yet many visitors find them the most moving things in the museum.

The jade deserves its own pause. Among the finds is a tall, green jade cong, a square tube with a round hole through the middle, divided into many stacked sections. Scholars believe it may have been made far to the east, in the region of the much older Liangzhu culture, near the lower Yangtze River. If so, it travelled a very long way, or was kept for many generations, before it was buried here. Objects, it seems, moved further than we once imagined.

Outside the halls, the museum grounds are planted with trees and lawns, with paths that wind between the buildings. On a mild day, local families walk here slowly, and the city noise fades behind the greenery. It is worth taking a few minutes in the garden after the galleries. The open space gives your eyes a rest, and it helps you picture the river plain as it may once have looked, before the towers rose.

Before you leave, go back for one last look at the pit, or at the golden disc behind its glass. Notice how small most of these treasures are. Notice the patience in them — the thin gold, the careful polish of the jade. Then step outside into modern Chengdu, with its traffic and its scooters. The same ground runs under both worlds. Somewhere beneath the streets around you, there may still be more golden sand waiting.
