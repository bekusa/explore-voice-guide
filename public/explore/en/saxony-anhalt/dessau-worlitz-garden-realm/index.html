The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against a wooden ferry. Oars creak somewhere across the lake. A breeze moves through old oaks, and the light breaks into pieces on the surface. Beneath your feet the gravel path crunches softly. Nothing here feels rushed. Yet almost everything you can see, every curve of shoreline and every opening between the trees, was planned on purpose more than two centuries ago.

Welcome to the Dessau-Wörlitz Garden Realm, in the German state of Saxony-Anhalt. This is not a single garden. It is a whole cultivated landscape, spread across the floodplains of the Elbe and Mulde rivers. Parks, palaces, villages, dykes, fields and avenues were all shaped into one connected design. In 2000, UNESCO added the Garden Realm to its World Heritage List. It was recognised as an outstanding example of how the ideas of the Enlightenment could be written onto the land itself.

The man behind it was Prince Leopold the Third Friedrich Franz of Anhalt-Dessau. His subjects called him Father Franz. He came to power young, in the second half of the eighteenth century, and he ruled a small principality. He could not compete with Prussia in armies or wealth. So he chose a different ambition. He wanted to improve his land and the lives of the people on it, and he wanted that improvement to be beautiful.

Franz travelled widely, and England made a deep impression on him. There he saw the new landscape gardens. They were very different from the strict French style, with its straight lines, clipped hedges and geometric beds. The English idea was to make a garden look like idealised nature. Lawns flowed into woods. Paths curved. Views opened slowly, like scenes in a painting. Franz brought this vision home, together with his architect and friend, Friedrich Wilhelm von Erdmannsdorff, and a team of skilled gardeners.

Here is the first surprise. Wörlitz Palace, the house at the heart of the park, is often described as one of the earliest Neoclassical buildings in Germany. Neoclassicism means architecture that looks back to the clean forms of ancient Greece and Rome. Erdmannsdorff designed it in the late 1760s and early 1770s. Look at the plain columns of the portico and the calm, balanced front. Compared with the heavy, decorated Baroque palaces of the time, it must have looked almost radical.

Now think about the word useful. For Franz, beauty alone was not enough. The Garden Realm mixes ornament with agriculture. Fruit trees line some of the avenues. Meadows were grazed. Model farms and new methods of cultivation were tried out here. The dykes that protect the land from the Elbe floods were also designed as walkways with long views. The flood defence and the pleasure path are the same structure. That combination of the practical and the beautiful is the core idea of the whole place.

The second idea is openness. Unusually for a prince of his era, Franz opened his gardens to ordinary people. Visitors of different social classes could walk these paths. The park was meant to educate as well as delight. Each building and view carried a lesson about history, nature, art or moral life. A walk here was supposed to make you a better and more thoughtful person.

As you move through the park, notice how small buildings appear at the end of views. There is a Gothic House, built in a medieval style that was fashionable among English romantics. There are temples, grottoes, statues and bridges. Several bridges are different from each other on purpose. One of them is a small iron bridge, inspired by the famous early iron bridge at Coalbrookdale in England. At the time, cast iron was a symbol of modern industry. Franz placed it here as a celebration of progress.

And now the second surprise. On a small rocky island called the Stein, there is an artificial volcano. It was modelled on Mount Vesuvius, near Naples, which fascinated travellers of the eighteenth century. The rock was built so that fire and smoke could be staged inside it, to imitate an eruption. Next to it stands the Villa Hamilton, named after the British diplomat in Naples who studied Vesuvius. After restoration, the volcano has occasionally been brought back to life for special evening events.

The town of Wörlitz itself is part of the design. Its church tower and its houses appear in views from the park. Among the buildings, look for the former synagogue, a small round structure shaped like a classical temple. Franz supported religious tolerance, and the synagogue stands as a quiet sign of that belief. Church, synagogue, farm and palace were all placed within the same landscape, as if to show they belonged together.

Wörlitz is only one part of the Garden Realm. Nearby you can find other parks and houses, each with its own character. Luisium is smaller and more intimate. Georgium sits close to the city of Dessau. Oranienbaum has a Baroque palace and an English-Chinese garden with a pagoda. Mosigkau has a palace from the earlier, more decorative age. Together they show how garden taste changed across the eighteenth century, from formal order toward open, romantic landscape.

The Garden Realm also attracted important visitors in its own time. The poet Johann Wolfgang von Goethe came here and admired it deeply. Many educated travellers followed. For a while, this small principality became a place where new ideas about nature, education and taste were seen in real form. Later generations protected it, repaired it after floods and neglect, and restored many lost views and buildings.

So take a moment where you are standing. Look at the line where the water meets the trees. That edge was shaped by hand, then softened by two centuries of growth. Look for the next building framed between the branches, and the next path curving out of sight. As you walk on, notice how each turn offers a new scene. That small pleasure of discovery is exactly what Father Franz hoped you would feel.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take a hand-operated ferry across the Wörlitz lake.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel paths and dyke walks.
Rent a bicycle to link the separate parks of the realm.
Check event listings for occasional volcano demonstration evenings.
Palace interiors are usually visited on guided tours only.
