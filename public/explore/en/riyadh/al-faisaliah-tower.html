The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and tilt your head back. The air on King Fahd Road is dry and warm, and the traffic hums in long, steady waves. Above you, a tall, tapering shape rises out of the city — and near its very tip, a ball of golden glass catches the sun. On a clear afternoon it glows like a lantern someone has hung high above Riyadh.

This is Al Faisaliah Tower. When it was completed around the year 2000, it became the first skyscraper in Saudi Arabia. That is worth pausing on. Today the skyline around you is full of towers. But a little over two decades ago, nothing in the Kingdom climbed this high. This building was the first to say that Riyadh would grow upward as well as outward.

The tower carries the name of King Faisal, who ruled Saudi Arabia in the 1960s and 1970s. The project was developed through the charitable foundation that bears his name. So the building is not only a commercial address. It is also a memorial of sorts — a modern structure that keeps a royal name in the daily life of the capital.

The design came from the London office of Foster and Partners, the firm led by the British architect Norman Foster. His studio is known for buildings where the structure itself becomes the decoration. You can see that idea here. Look at the four great legs at the corners. They lean gently inward as they rise, and they meet near the top, like the sides of a sharpened pencil.

Those legs are not just a visual flourish. They carry the weight of the tower down to the ground. Between them, the floors are hung and stacked so that the inner spaces stay open. The engineers had to solve a hard problem — how to make a very tall, very narrow building stand firm in a hot, windy desert city. The tapering form helps. It reduces the load near the top and gives the whole frame a steady, balanced base.

Now look closer at the faces of the tower. Much of the glass sits behind rows of metal shading fins. In a climate this hot, sunlight is the enemy of comfort. The fins cut the direct glare and help keep the offices cooler inside. From a distance they read as fine horizontal lines. Up close, they give the surface a texture, almost like woven cloth stretched over the frame.

And then there is the globe. It sits high up the tower, where the four legs draw together. Inside it, there is a restaurant, and visitors can look out across the whole city through the curved glass. Here is a detail many people miss — from inside, the panes are tinted, so the desert light softens. The view is brighter outside than you expect, and calmer inside than you imagine.

Above the globe, a slim spire continues upward and finishes the shape. Without it, the tower would end with the ball. With it, the eye keeps travelling into the sky. At night the upper section is lit, and the golden sphere becomes one of the clearest landmarks in Riyadh. Taxi drivers and residents often use it as a reference point when they give directions.

The tower is not a single isolated block. At its feet there is a complex of buildings, including a hotel, offices, and a shopping mall. On a hot day this matters a lot. Much of city life here moves indoors, where the air is cool. If you walk through the mall, you will hear families, the soft sound of fountains or music, and the echo of footsteps on polished stone.

Here is a second surprise. Al Faisaliah did not stay the tallest tower in Riyadh for very long. Only a couple of years later, the Kingdom Centre rose a short distance away along the same road. You may be able to see it from here — the tall tower with a large opening near its top, often described as looking like a bottle opener. The two buildings became a pair, and together they defined the skyline for years.

Think about what this street looked like before. Riyadh grew very quickly in the second half of the twentieth century. Older parts of the city were built of mud brick, low and thick-walled, designed to hold back the heat. Al Faisaliah belongs to a completely different idea of the city — glass, steel, concrete, and height. Standing here, you are looking at a turning point in how the capital chose to present itself.

Today the district around you, known as Olaya, is one of the busiest business areas in the city. Newer and taller towers have appeared in other parts of Riyadh. Yet this one still holds a special place. Its shape is simple enough to draw with four lines and a circle. That simplicity is part of its strength. Children can sketch it, and it still feels modern.

Before you move on, take one more long look upward. Notice how the light shifts on the glass as the clouds pass, or as the sun lowers toward the west. The golden ball may turn amber, then bronze. Listen to the traffic below, and feel the warm air on your face. Then, as you walk away along King Fahd Road, glance back once more — the tower will still be there, quietly marking the place where Riyadh first began to climb.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit near sunset to see the golden globe change colour.
Use the mall at the base to escape midday heat.
Book the globe restaurant ahead if you want the view.
Return after dark to see the upper section lit up.
Walk along King Fahd Road for wider views of the tower.
