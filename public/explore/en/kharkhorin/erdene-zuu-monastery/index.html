The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across the open steppe, and it carries a faint sound of wooden prayer wheels turning. Underfoot the ground is dry and firm, with short grass and loose gravel. Ahead of you runs a long white wall. Small white stupas stand along it in a steady line, close together. These are dome-shaped Buddhist shrines. Each one ends in a slender gilded spire, and against the wide Mongolian sky the row looks endless. This is Erdene Zuu, and you are standing at one of the oldest Buddhist sites in Mongolia.

The name means something close to "Hundred Treasures". The monastery was founded in 1585 by Abtai Khan, a powerful noble of the Khalkha Mongols. Not long before, he had met the Third Dalai Lama, the Tibetan spiritual leader. That meeting helped turn Mongolia back toward Tibetan Buddhism after a long gap. Erdene Zuu was one of the first great monuments of that revival. In many ways the story of Buddhism in modern Mongolia begins on this ground.

Now for the first surprise. The flat land around you was once the capital of the largest land empire in history. This was Karakorum, the city of the Mongol Empire in the thirteenth century. Genghis Khan chose the area, and his son Ögedei built the city up. Merchants, envoys and craftsmen came here from China, Persia and Europe. Today almost nothing of it rises above the grass. When the monastery was built centuries later, its builders took stone and brick from the ruins of the old capital.

So when you look at the walls and foundations, think about where those materials came from. Some of what you see once belonged to the imperial city. The monastery did not simply replace Karakorum. It quietly took parts of it in. Archaeologists still dig in the fields nearby, and their work has helped show the shape of the old streets and workshops. The steppe here holds two histories at once — the empire and the faith that came after it.

Walk along the outer wall for a moment. It forms a large square, and each side runs for roughly four hundred metres. The stupas along the top are traditionally counted as 108. That number is sacred in Buddhism. Prayer beads often carry 108 beads, and the number appears again and again in ritual. Pilgrims walk the circuit of the wall clockwise, always keeping the holy centre on their right. If you join them, move at an easy pace. The walk is part of the visit.

Inside the walls, the space feels surprisingly empty. Wide stretches of grass lie between the buildings. This emptiness has a hard history. At its height, the monastery held many temples, and a large community of monks lived here. Then, in the late 1930s, Mongolia's communist government launched violent purges against religion, closely following the policies of the Soviet Union. Monasteries across the country were destroyed. Thousands of monks were killed or forced out of religious life.

Erdene Zuu suffered badly. Most of its temples were pulled down. Yet a small group of buildings survived. Accounts differ on exactly why. One common explanation is that officials wanted to keep a few temples as a museum, or as a display for foreign visitors. Whatever the reason, these few buildings remained standing while so much around them disappeared. That is the second surprise many visitors miss: the open grass is not a park. It marks what was lost.

Look now at the three temples standing side by side. They are often called the West, Central and East Temples. Their roofs curve upward at the corners and carry green glazed tiles, in a style that shows strong Chinese influence. Painted wooden beams sit under the eaves, in reds, blues and greens. Inside, the light is low. Gilded Buddha figures sit on altars, surrounded by painted scroll images called thangkas, and by offerings of butter lamps, coins and small bowls of water.

Not far away stands a very different building. This is the Lavrin Temple, built in a Tibetan style. Its walls are white and solid, with fewer of the curved Chinese roof lines. This temple is in active use. Monks gather here, and in the mornings you may hear deep, rolling chanting, with drums and horns. Visitors can usually step quietly inside or stand at the doorway. Keep your voice low, and follow the lead of the people around you.

After the purges, the surviving buildings became a museum in the 1940s. For decades, religious practice here was limited. Everything changed after Mongolia's democratic revolution in 1990. Monks returned, and Erdene Zuu became a working monastery again. Today, prayer and tourism share the same courtyards. You may see a monk in maroon robes pass a group of visitors with cameras. You may see a local family spin the prayer wheels, one after another, as they walk.

Another building catches the eye inside the walls: a large white stupa topped with gold, often called the Golden Stupa. Near the temples you may also notice older stupas and memorial structures linked to the founding family. Many visitors pause here to make a small offering or to walk around the stupa in a slow circle. The movement matters more than speed. Each turn is a quiet act of respect, repeated by countless pilgrims over the centuries.

Since 2004, this whole area has been part of the Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The listing covers far more than the monastery. It includes the river valley, ancient stone monuments, and the remains of Karakorum itself. Outside the monastery, look out for a carved stone turtle sitting in the grass. Turtles like this once marked the edges of the old capital. Stories differ about their exact purpose, but they are among the few objects from the imperial city still in place.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the gate. Look back along the white wall and its long row of stupas. Listen for the wind, the turning prayer wheels, perhaps a distant horse on the steppe. Behind you is the small modern town of Kharkhorin. Ahead of you is a place that has been a capital, a ruin, a monastery, a museum, and a monastery again. As you walk away, notice how quickly the grass takes over — and how carefully people still keep this square of ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the outer wall clockwise, like local pilgrims.
Visit in the morning to hear monks chanting.
Ask before taking photos inside any temple.
Dress modestly and keep voices low indoors.
Bring layers; steppe wind can turn cold quickly.
