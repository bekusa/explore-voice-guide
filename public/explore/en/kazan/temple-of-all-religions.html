The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, a train rattles along the tracks. In front of you, a crowd of domes rises from a quiet suburban street. Some are gold, some are green, some are blue and white tile. The edges glitter with small pieces of glass and ceramic. It looks less like one building and more like a skyline that has been gathered into a single courtyard.

This is the Temple of All Religions, sometimes called the Universal Temple. It stands in Staroye Arakchino, a village-like district on the edge of Kazan, close to the banks of the Volga. Visitors often expect a church, or perhaps a mosque. They find both, and a great deal more, joined together into one restless, colourful structure.

Here is the first surprise. Despite its name, this is not a working place of worship. No regular services are held here, and no single faith claims the building. It was conceived as an architectural and cultural centre — a place to show, in stone and colour, the idea that the world's spiritual traditions share common ground. You can think of it as a building-sized statement of belief in harmony.

The man behind it was Ildar Khanov, a Tatar artist, sculptor and architect. He was a figure of many interests. People in Kazan remember him as a painter, a builder, and a man who saw his work as a kind of spiritual practice. He began this project in the early 1990s, a time of enormous change across the former Soviet Union, when people were rediscovering religion after decades of official atheism.

That timing matters. During the Soviet period, religious life was heavily restricted. Then, almost suddenly, churches and mosques reopened, and new ones appeared. Khanov chose not to build for one community. Instead, he set out to place many symbols side by side. In a city where Islam and Orthodox Christianity have lived next to each other for centuries, his idea had deep local roots.

Look up at the rooftops now. Try to pick out the shapes. You may notice onion domes that recall Russian Orthodox churches. Nearby, slender towers suggest the minarets of a mosque. Elsewhere there are forms that echo Buddhist pagodas and other Asian temples. Look for symbols too — crosses, crescents, and a six-pointed star associated with Judaism. They are placed close together, almost shoulder to shoulder.

Here is a second surprise. This building was not paid for by a state body or a large religious organisation. According to the commonly told story, Khanov funded much of the work himself, including money from his own art. Donations and volunteer labour filled the gaps. It is also often said that he worked with people trying to rebuild their lives, and that some of them helped with the construction. The building grew slowly, piece by piece.

That slow growth explains much of what you see. There is no single, tidy master plan visible from the street. Instead, you see layers. One section may look finished and polished. Another may still feel raw. The temple has always been a work in progress, and that sense of unfinished energy is part of its character. It feels made by hands rather than drawn on a computer.

Now move closer and look at the surfaces. The walls carry mosaics, painted panels, and inlaid pieces of coloured glass. Much of the decoration catches the light in small flashes. On a sunny day the facade changes as clouds pass. On a grey day the colours become softer, and the white walls stand out against the sky. Either way, the details reward a slow walk around the outside.

Ildar Khanov died in 2013. The work did not stop with him. Family members, supporters and volunteers have continued to care for the building and to add to it. So the temple is also a kind of memorial. Every new tile and every repair continues a conversation that one artist began more than thirty years ago. It is a reminder that some buildings are lived with, not simply completed.

It helps to place this temple in its wider setting. Kazan is the capital of Tatarstan, a republic within Russia where Tatar Muslim and Russian Orthodox communities have shared the same streets for generations. In the city centre, inside the Kazan Kremlin, a large mosque and an Orthodox cathedral stand within the same fortress walls. Khanov's temple takes that same idea of coexistence and pushes it further, gathering many more traditions into one place.

The surroundings also shape the experience. This is not a grand square or a tourist boulevard. Around you are ordinary houses, gardens and fences. The railway runs close by, and the Volga is not far away. Because of this, the temple feels unexpected. You turn a corner in a quiet suburb, and suddenly there is a cluster of domes and towers that seems to belong to several countries at once.

It is worth asking yourself what you see first. Some visitors focus on the symbols of their own faith. Others notice the craftsmanship, or the sheer number of shapes. Some people find the building playful. Others find it moving. Khanov seems to have wanted that range of reactions. He did not build a lecture. He built something to stand in front of, and to think about.

Before you leave, take one last look along the roofline. Let your eye travel from dome to tower to spire, without trying to name each one. Notice how the forms lean together against the sky. Listen again for the trains and the wind. As you walk away, the domes will slip behind the houses one by one... and the street will return to being an ordinary street on the edge of Kazan.
