The guide

The story

Look up from the cobbles and something feels slightly wrong. The red brick tower in front of you does not stand quite straight. It tilts, gently but clearly, as if it is leaning toward the old courtyard to listen. The brick is warm in colour, a deep rust red, and the white stone trim catches the light on each ledge. Wind moves across the Kremlin hill here. On a quiet day you can hear pigeons settling on the upper tiers.

This is the Söyembikä Tower, inside the Kazan Kremlin. It rises in a series of stacked tiers, each one narrower than the one below. The base is broad and heavy. The middle levels open into arches. At the very top, a slim green spire points into the sky. Many visitors count the tiers aloud. Locals often say there are seven, and the number matters, as you will hear in a moment.

First, the honest part. Nobody knows exactly when this tower was built. There is no clear founding document, and historians still debate its age. Most place it somewhere in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, after Kazan had become part of the Russian state. The style supports that view. The tiered red brick, the white details, and the octagonal upper stages look much like Russian towers of the same period, including several in Moscow.

So why does it carry a Tatar queen's name? Söyembikä was a real woman, and a powerful one. She was the wife of a khan of Kazan, the Muslim Tatar state that ruled this region before the Russian conquest. After her husband died, she acted as regent for her young son. In 1551, she was taken away to Moscow. The next year, in 1552, the armies of Ivan the Terrible captured Kazan, and the khanate came to an end.

Now comes the legend, and it is important to say clearly that it is a legend. Locals say that Ivan the Terrible wanted to marry the beautiful queen. She agreed, but only on one condition. He had to build a tower taller than any in Kazan, and he had to build it in seven days. One tier for each day. The tsar's builders finished in time. On the day of the wedding, the story says, Söyembikä climbed to the top.

There she said farewell to her city, and she threw herself from the tower. It is a dramatic tale, and it has been told for generations. But the dates do not fit. The real Söyembikä left Kazan before the conquest, and the tower was almost certainly built more than a century after her lifetime. Still, the story gave the tower its name. In a sense, the building became a memorial to a woman who never saw it.

Now look again at that lean. It is the tower's most famous physical feature. Over the centuries, the ground beneath it settled unevenly, and the structure slowly tipped toward the northeast. At the top, the spire sits noticeably off the line of the base. You can see it best if you step back and line up the edge of the tower with a nearby building. People sometimes compare it to the leaning tower of Pisa, though this one is made of brick, not marble.

A leaning tower is a worried tower, at least for engineers. During the twentieth century, specialists studied the foundations and carried out work to strengthen them and slow the movement. The aim was never to straighten the tower. That would be risky and would also remove its character. The aim was simply to keep it standing. When you look at it today, you are looking at a building held in careful balance.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Walk around the base and look at the lowest tier. It is built with an arched passage, so the tower once worked partly as a kind of gateway. Above it, the arches on the upper tiers are open to the air. From below, you can see daylight through them. That openness makes the heavy brick look lighter, almost like a lantern raised above the courtyard.

The tower does not stand alone. Turn slowly and notice its neighbours. Close by is the Governor's Palace, now the residence of the President of Tatarstan. Not far away rise the blue domes of the Annunciation Cathedral, and the tall turquoise minarets of the Qol Sharif Mosque. The mosque was rebuilt and opened in the 2000s, named after an imam who, according to tradition, died defending Kazan in 1552. Church, mosque, and tower share one hill.

That mix is the real story of this place. Kazan has long been a meeting point of Tatar and Russian, of Islam and Orthodox Christianity. The Kremlin as a whole was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2000, partly for exactly this reason. A Russian-style tower named after a Tatar queen, with legend wrapped around fact, fits that history well. It belongs to both communities, and both tell its story.

The tower also has a quieter, more personal life. Locals say that if you touch its walls and make a wish, the wish may come true. Wedding couples in Kazan often come to the Kremlin for photographs, and you may see a bride in white standing near the old brick. It is a tender irony. A tower whose legend ends in a tragic wedding has become a place where new marriages begin.

Before you leave, take one more slow look. Notice how the evening or morning light changes the brick, from orange to deep red to almost brown. Notice the green spire against the sky, and the small tilt you now cannot stop seeing. As you walk away across the Kremlin courtyard, glance back once. The tower will still be leaning, patiently, the way it has for centuries.
