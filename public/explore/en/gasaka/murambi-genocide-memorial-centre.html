The guide

The story

The air here is quiet in a way that is hard to describe. On the hilltop, the wind moves through the grass and carries the faint sound of distant voices from the valley below. The ground under your feet is red earth, packed hard, and the green hills of southern Rwanda roll away in every direction. Before anything else, take a slow breath. This is a place of memory, and it asks for your attention and your patience.

You are standing at Murambi, near the town once known as Gikongoro, in the Nyamagabe area of the Southern Province. The low buildings around you were never meant to be a memorial. They were planned as a technical school, a place where young people would learn practical trades. In early 1994, the school was still under construction. Its classrooms stood mostly empty, with concrete walls and simple roofs, waiting for students who would never arrive.

To understand why this hill matters, it helps to know a little about Rwanda in 1994. Over roughly one hundred days, beginning in April of that year, extremists carried out a genocide against the Tutsi. Neighbours were turned against neighbours. Radio broadcasts spread hatred and gave instructions. Government forces and militias, together with ordinary civilians, killed people in their homes, at roadblocks, in churches, and in schools. The scale and speed of the killing still shock historians today.

In the region around Murambi, Tutsi families were told to gather at this unfinished school. According to survivors and the memorial's own account, local officials promised that they would be protected here. Thousands came from the surrounding hills, carrying what little they could. They waited on this open ground. But the promise was a trap. Once the people were gathered, they were cut off from food and water, and the hill became easy to surround.

In the third week of April 1994, the attack came. Soldiers and militia members encircled the school and killed the people sheltering inside. The exact number of victims is still discussed, and figures vary between sources. What is clear is that tens of thousands of people died on and around this hill in a very short time. Only a very small number survived. Some of them later returned to tell what happened, and their testimony shaped this memorial.

After the genocide, the dead were buried in mass graves around the site. Later, many of the bodies were exhumed. Some were preserved with powdered lime and laid out in the old classrooms. This is the part of Murambi that most visitors carry with them for years. The decision to display the remains was deliberate. It was meant to show the truth of what happened, so that no one could deny it. Be aware of this before you go inside.

If you choose to enter the classrooms, move slowly. The rooms are simple and dim. The preserved bodies lie on low wooden racks, white from the lime. Some still show the marks of the violence they suffered. Many visitors find that they cannot stay long, and that is completely fine. There is no right way to respond here. You may step outside at any moment, stand in the open air, and return only if you wish.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. After the genocide, French troops arrived in this area as part of a military operation called Opération Turquoise, which was presented as a humanitarian mission. They set up a base on this hill. Survivors and the memorial say that a volleyball court was laid out on top of a mass grave. That claim remains part of a wider and painful debate about France's role in 1994.

The main memorial building is newer than the classrooms. It was opened in 2011, with support from the Aegis Trust, a British organisation that works on genocide prevention and also helped create the Kigali memorial. Inside, panels and photographs explain the history of the genocide. They trace how division between Rwandans was built up over decades, starting in the colonial era, when identity cards recorded each person's ethnic group. That history makes the events on this hill easier to understand.

A second surprise for many visitors is how much of the exhibition looks forward, not only back. The panels describe the long years of rebuilding that followed. They explain the community courts known as gacaca, where villagers gathered on the grass to hear cases against those accused of taking part in the killing. Victims and perpetrators often live in the same villages today. The memorial does not hide how difficult that coexistence is.

Outside, walk slowly around the grounds. You will see the long rows of former classroom blocks, their walls plain and weathered. You will see the mass graves, now covered and marked, where many victims were finally laid to rest with dignity. Look out across the valleys. The terraced fields, the banana groves, and the small houses on the hillsides belong to the same landscape that families crossed in April 1994 on their way here.

Every year, beginning on the seventh of April, Rwanda holds a national period of remembrance called Kwibuka, a word that means to remember. During these weeks, survivors, families, and officials come to memorials like this one. They light candles, read names, and share testimony. If you visit at that time, you may see people gathered here in mourning. Give them space, speak softly, and remember that for many Rwandans this is personal grief, not history.

Before you leave, stand for a moment on the open ground in front of the buildings. Listen to the wind in the grass and the sounds of daily life rising from the hills. Children may be walking home from school on the road below. Farmers may be working the slopes. Life goes on around this place, and that is part of its meaning. As you walk back down the path, carry what you have seen with care... and let it stay with you.
