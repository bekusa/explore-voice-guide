The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, somewhere down the slope, the Radika River runs over stone. Up here, the air is cooler and smells of wood smoke, pine and old beeswax. The walls around you are pale stone and dark timber. The upper floors lean out over the courtyard, the way mountain houses do in this part of the Balkans. You have arrived at Saint Jovan Bigorski, a working Orthodox monastery in the hills above Debar.

The name has two parts. Saint Jovan is Saint John the Baptist, the prophet who baptised Christ in the river Jordan. Bigorski comes from bigor, a local word for travertine. Travertine is a soft, porous limestone that forms around mineral springs. Look closely at some of the older stonework and you may see its spongy, honeycomb texture. The monastery is named for the rock it stands on.

The founding story is a legend, and the monks tell it with affection. According to tradition, early in the eleventh century, an icon of Saint John the Baptist appeared near the Radika River. A monk named Ivan Debranin found it and built a small church to hold it. Locals say the icon vanished several times and was found again in the same spot. Each time, it seemed to choose this hillside. The church took root here.

The history after that is harder to trace. During the long Ottoman period, the monastery was abandoned and fell into ruin. Its revival came in the eighteenth century, around 1743, when monks returned and began to rebuild. Over the following decades the complex grew. The church, the bell tower, and the long wooden dormitories took shape around the courtyard where you are standing. Much of what you see today grew outward from that revival.

Now walk toward the church. Inside, the light drops away. Candles flicker against gold and dark wood, and the air is thick with incense. In front of you stands the reason many people make this journey — the iconostasis. In an Orthodox church, the iconostasis is the screen that separates the main hall from the altar. It holds the icons in fixed rows. Here, it is something far more ambitious.

This iconostasis is carved entirely from walnut wood. It was made between 1829 and 1835 by a team of master carvers. They were led by Petre Filipovski, known as Garkata, and Makarije Frčkovski. Both men came from the Mijak community. The Mijaks are an Orthodox people from the villages of the Reka region along the Radika valley. They were famous across the Balkans as builders, painters and woodcarvers. This screen is often called their finest work.

Step closer and let your eyes adjust. The surface is not flat. It is cut in deep layers, so that leaves, vines and figures stand out from the background like living things. You will find grapevines and bunches of grapes. You will find birds, deer and lions. And among them are hundreds of small human figures acting out scenes from the Bible. Many visitors look at it for a long time before they notice just how many people are carved into it.

Here is one detail most people miss. Search the carving for the story of Salome. In the Bible, she dances for King Herod, and as her reward she asks for the head of John the Baptist. The carvers placed this scene in the church dedicated to John himself. It is a dark and powerful moment, carved with great care. The head on its platter is small, but once you find it, you will not forget it.

The second surprise is personal. Tradition holds that Petre Filipovski carved a portrait of himself into the iconostasis, along with his fellow craftsmen. Look for a small group of men holding carving tools. According to one popular tale, the master carver finished his life's work and then died soon after. Some versions of the story are more dramatic than others. Treat it as legend. But the self-portrait is a real signature, left in walnut by a man who knew what he had made.

The monastery also holds objects of deep devotion. Pilgrims come to venerate the icon of Saint John the Baptist and relics associated with the saint. You will see people cross themselves, kiss the icon, and light thin candles. If a service is underway, you will hear chanting in Church Slavonic and Macedonian. This is not a museum. It is a home for a living community of monks, and the rhythm of prayer still sets the pace of the day.

In 2009, a serious fire swept through the monastery's residential buildings. The wooden dormitories, with their long balconies, were badly damaged. The church and its iconostasis survived. Afterward, the monks and many supporters rebuilt the lost wings. They used traditional timber and stone, following the old forms. So when you look at the courtyard, you are seeing both the old and the new. Some beams are freshly cut, and others are darkened by long years of smoke and weather.

Take a moment outside on the terrace. The view opens over the Radika valley and the forested slopes beyond. The road you came on follows the river between Debar and the mountains to the north. Debar itself sits further south, near the border with Albania. This landscape shaped the Mijak craftsmen. Their villages lie in these same valleys, and the walnut trees they carved grew on hillsides like these.

Before you leave, stand still in the courtyard once more. Listen for the bells, the footsteps on the wooden stairs, the river below. Look up at the overhanging balconies and the dark timber against the pale stone. As you walk back to the road, notice the travertine under your feet and in the walls. It is the same porous rock that gave this place its name — and it has held this monastery on its hillside through ruin, revival and fire.
