The guide

The story

Start with the grass. It is soft underfoot, and then, without warning, it gives way to stone. Pale blocks, worn smooth, laid out in straight lines across a green valley. Around you, the hills of Hațeg rise in long, gentle folds. On a clear day, the higher peaks of the Retezat Mountains stand to the south. It is very quiet here. You might hear a cowbell from the village, or wind moving through the meadow. But the lines under your feet were drawn by people who planned a capital.

This is Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa, the first Roman city in the province of Dacia. Dacia covered much of what is now Romania. Rome conquered it in the early second century, after two hard wars under the emperor Trajan. When the fighting ended, Rome needed a centre for its new territory. It needed a place to govern from, to settle veterans in, and to show the local people who now held power. This valley became that place. Its builders started almost from nothing.

The name is worth hearing slowly. Ulpia comes from Trajan's own family name, Ulpius. Traiana honours the emperor directly. And Sarmizegetusa... that word was taken from the Dacians themselves. Their royal capital, Sarmizegetusa Regia, stood high in the mountains to the north-east, at a fair distance from here. The Romans took the name of the city they had defeated and gave it to a new city of their own. It was a political message. The old centre of power was finished, and a new one was beginning down here in the open valley.

Now look at how the ground is organised. Roman colonies followed a familiar plan, and you can still read it here. Streets met at right angles. Public buildings sat at the heart of the town, and walls marked its edge. The first settlers were largely veterans of the legions. They were men who had fought in the Dacian wars and received land as their reward. For them, this valley was a new home far from the Mediterranean. They tried to build it the way a Roman town should look.

Walk towards the forum, the city's main public square. This was where the town's business happened. Officials gave judgements, merchants made deals, and citizens read public notices cut into stone. Around the open space, you can trace the bases of columns and the outlines of rooms. Archaeologists have uncovered and partly rebuilt sections, so some walls stand higher than others. When a wall looks unusually crisp and regular, it is often modern restoration. The rougher, uneven stones are usually closer to the original Roman work.

Here is something many visitors miss. Look carefully at the surfaces of the larger blocks. In several places you may notice inscriptions, mouldings, or carved details. Roman towns spoke through stone. They honoured emperors, recorded donations, and named the people who paid for buildings. Much of what historians know about this city comes from these inscriptions. Some are still on the site. Many others are now kept in the museum nearby. Each one is a small, direct message from a real person who once lived in this valley.

As the city grew, it gained importance. In the third century, it received the honorary title of metropolis. That was a sign of high rank among the towns of the province. Temples rose to many gods. Some were Roman gods, and others came from across the empire, carried here by soldiers, traders, and officials. Dacia was a mixed province, with settlers from many regions. The religious buildings here show that mix. People in this valley prayed to a wide range of gods, in more than one language.

Now turn towards the amphitheatre. It stands a little apart from the forum, just outside the line of the old city wall. This was the arena for public shows, which could include gladiator fights and animal hunts. It is much smaller than the great arenas of Rome, but it is still impressive to stand inside. The oval shape is clear. The stone walls of the arena floor are easy to follow. Stand in the centre for a moment and imagine the noise of a full crowd around you.

A second surprise is waiting in this landscape. The city did not simply disappear when Rome left. In the later third century, the empire pulled its army and administration out of Dacia. The town slowly emptied. Over the centuries that followed, local people reused its dressed stone. Roman blocks ended up in houses, walls, and churches across the area. Some old churches in the Hațeg region still have Roman stones built into their walls. A few even contain carved fragments. So the city did not vanish. Its stones were carried into the surrounding villages.

That is part of why the ruins look the way they do today. You are not seeing a city frozen at one moment. You are seeing what remained after many centuries of farming, building, and reuse. Then came modern archaeology, which uncovered and studied what was left. Large parts of the ancient town are still under the fields around you. The excavated area is only one section of the whole. When you look across the meadow, remember that streets and foundations may lie just below the surface.

The village around the site also carries the ancient name. It took the name Sarmizegetusa in honour of the Roman city. The local museum holds objects found here, including inscriptions, statues, everyday tools, and fragments of decoration. It is a good companion to the open-air ruins. Out on the site you see the scale. In the museum you see the details, such as a carved face, a small lamp, or a name on a gravestone. Together they give a fuller picture of daily life.

It helps to see this valley as a crossroads. The Hațeg country is enclosed by mountains, with passes leading in several directions. Romans valued places like this for movement and control. Long before Rome, though, the land had its own deep past. The wider region is famous among scientists for fossils of unusually small dinosaurs from millions of years ago. So this valley holds three layers at once. There is deep time in the rocks, a Roman capital in the meadow, and a living village beside it.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot near the forum or on the edge of the amphitheatre. Look at the mountains first. Then look down at the stone at your feet. Notice the colour of the blocks, the moss in the joints, and the grass pushing back between them. People stood here nearly two thousand years ago and looked at these same hills. As you walk back towards the village, watch the walls and fences you pass. Now and then, a squared, ancient stone may still be there, built into everyday life.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the village museum to see inscriptions found on site.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and damp grass.
Come on a clear day for views of the Retezat.
Combine your visit with the old churches around Hațeg.
Bring water and sun protection; the meadow has little shade.
