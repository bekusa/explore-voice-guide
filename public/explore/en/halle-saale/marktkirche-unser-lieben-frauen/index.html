The guide

The story

Stand on the market square in Halle and look up. Four towers rise above you, not two. On one side, a pair of slender spires with pointed caps. On the other, two heavier towers joined high in the air by a narrow bridge. Pigeons circle between them, and the trams squeal as they curve across the cobbles below. Few churches in Germany show you this outline. It looks less like one building and more like a negotiation that was settled in stone.

In a sense, that is exactly what it is. This is the Marktkirche Unser Lieben Frauen, the Market Church of Our Dear Lady. It stands where two older churches once stood side by side. One was dedicated to Saint Gertrude, the other to the Virgin Mary. In the early sixteenth century, the naves of both churches were taken down. The four towers were kept, and a single new church was built in the gap between them. So the oldest parts of what you see are the towers, and the newer part is the body of the church.

The man behind this project was Cardinal Albrecht of Brandenburg. He was one of the most powerful churchmen in the Holy Roman Empire, and Halle was one of his favourite residences. He wanted a grand Catholic city, full of relics and display. Construction began around 1529. There is an irony here. The building was ordered by a cardinal, but by the time it was finished, around the middle of the century, it had become a Protestant church.

Look first at the eastern pair, the slender towers with the pointed tops. Locals call them the Blue Towers, after the colour of their roofs. Now turn to the western pair, the Hausmann Towers. The name comes from the Hausmann, the tower keeper. For centuries, a watchman lived up there. He looked out for fires, and he sounded signals over the town. The little bridge between the two towers was his walkway in the sky.

Now step inside. The first surprise is the space itself. This is a hall church — a design where the side aisles rise to almost the same height as the central nave. There is no dark, narrow central corridor. Instead, the light spreads evenly across one wide room. Slim pillars rise like tree trunks and branch out into a net of ribs across the vaulted ceiling. Follow those ribs with your eyes. They cross and curve in patterns that feel almost woven.

This is late Gothic architecture, the last chapter of a style that had dominated Europe for centuries. Gothic, put simply, is the style of pointed arches, tall windows and ribbed vaults. Here it is at its most confident and relaxed. The builders were no longer trying to reach heaven through sheer height. They were shaping a calm, open room. Take a moment and listen. Even a small cough travels a long way in here.

The Reformation came to Halle in the 1540s, and this church sits at the centre of that story. Martin Luther preached here more than once. His close ally, Justus Jonas, became the first Protestant preacher of the city. Cardinal Albrecht left Halle, and his grand Catholic plan passed into Protestant hands. The pulpit, carved in stone and raised above the congregation, reminds you that this became a church of the spoken word.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Luther died in 1546, in his birthplace of Eisleben, not far from here. His body was carried back to Wittenberg for burial, and on the journey the procession stopped in Halle. During that stop, a death mask was made, together with casts of his hands. The church still keeps these casts. Seeing them is a strange experience. You are looking at the actual shape of a face that changed the history of Europe.

The second great name of this church belongs to music. George Frideric Handel was born in Halle in 1685, and he was baptised here. As a boy, he studied with Friedrich Wilhelm Zachow, the organist of this church. Zachow taught him the organ, harmony and composition. Long before London, long before Messiah, Handel learned his craft under these vaults. Just outside on the square, a statue of the composer stands, facing the church where his music began.

Look for the small organ, a modest instrument from the seventeenth century. This is the organ that young Handel is believed to have played during his lessons. Compare it with the large main organ above, a modern instrument from the twentieth century. Between the two, you can hear the range of a church that has never stopped making music. If you are lucky, an organist may be practising while you visit. The sound fills the whole hall at once.

The church also has a scholarly side. Close by stands the Marienbibliothek, the library of this congregation. It was founded in the sixteenth century, soon after the Reformation, and it is one of the oldest church libraries in Germany that was open to the public. The idea was simple and radical for its time. If faith rested on the written word, then people needed access to books. Faith and reading grew up together in this corner of Halle.

Step back out onto the market square and look around. Across from the church stands the Red Tower, a tall free-standing bell tower, once part of the town's civic pride. Together, the four church towers and the Red Tower give Halle its famous skyline of five towers. The square around them has been a meeting place for centuries. Merchants traded here, sermons drew crowds here, and today students cross it with coffee in hand.

Before you leave, find a spot on the square where you can see all four towers at once. Notice how the two pairs do not quite match. One pair is older and taller, the other different in shape and spirit. A cardinal began this church, a reformer preached in it, and a boy who became one of the great composers learned music inside it. Listen for the trams, the bells, the voices of the market. The church is still part of the daily noise of Halle.
