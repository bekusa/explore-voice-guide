The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, there is the dry hiss of tall grass moving in the wind. A bird calls from somewhere you cannot place. The air smells of river mud and warm vegetation. Under the wheels of the jeep, the track is soft, pale earth, laid down by water over a very long time. This is Orang National Park, on the north bank of the Brahmaputra River in Assam. Out there, somewhere in that grass, a one-horned rhinoceros is almost certainly standing still and watching you.

Many people in Assam call this place the mini Kaziranga. Kaziranga is the larger and better known park on the opposite bank of the same river. The nickname makes sense. The landscape here is similar: open grassland, shallow wetlands and patches of woodland, all shaped by the river. The same great animals live in it. But Orang is smaller and much quieter. On many days you share it with only a handful of other vehicles. For a visitor, that quiet is the real difference.

The park covers roughly eighty square kilometres. It spreads across two districts of Assam, Darrang and Sonitpur. Rivers mark much of its edge. The Brahmaputra runs along the south side, and smaller tributaries such as the Pachnoi and the Belsiri flow down from the north. So this is really an island of wild land, held between moving water. Every year the rivers rise, spill over and retreat. Each flood leaves fresh silt behind, and the silt feeds the grass that feeds everything else.

Look at that grass closely. Some of it grows far taller than a person. Local people call it elephant grass, and the name is accurate. An adult elephant can disappear inside it completely. This is why a rhino can be thirty metres away and still be invisible. Drivers and guides here read the landscape constantly. They watch for a bent stalk, a fresh trail of flattened stems, or birds lifting suddenly from one spot. Watch them work. It is a skill learned over many seasons.

The animal most visitors hope to see is the greater one-horned rhinoceros. It is a heavy, armoured-looking creature with folds of grey skin like riveted plates. Here is something many visitors do not know. The single horn is not bone. It is made of keratin, the same material as your fingernails. Sadly, that horn has made the animal a target for poachers across its range. Protecting the rhinos is one of the main tasks of the forest staff here, and their camps are spread across the park.

The rhino is not the only large animal. Orang is also a tiger reserve. Tigers are rarely seen, because they move through dense cover and mostly at quiet hours. Still, their presence matters. A tiger at the top of the food chain shows that the whole system below it is working. Asian elephants pass through the park too, often in family groups. You may also see wild boar, and small hog deer feeding at the edge of the grass, ready to run.

Now for a surprise most visitors miss. One of the most important animals here is also one of the smallest. It is the pygmy hog, the smallest wild pig in the world. An adult is not much bigger than a large rabbit. It was once thought to be extinct. Conservation groups have bred pygmy hogs in captivity and released some of them into the grasslands of Assam, including here at Orang. You will almost certainly not see one. But knowing it is out there changes how you look at the grass.

The wetlands bring another kind of life. Shallow lakes and marshes, which people in Assam call beels, lie scattered across the park. Water birds gather here: storks, egrets, cormorants and many kinds of ducks in the cooler months. Kingfishers sit very still on branches above the water, then drop like small blue stones. The grassland also shelters the Bengal florican, a rare bird that depends on exactly this kind of open, seasonally flooded country. Bring binoculars if you can. Half of Orang happens at a distance.

The human story of this land is quieter. According to local accounts, people once lived and farmed in this area. The story goes that the settlements were abandoned long ago, and the land slowly returned to grass and forest. Treat that as tradition rather than firm record. What is documented is more recent. The area was protected as a wildlife sanctuary in the 1980s and became a national park in 1999. For a period it carried a different official name, before returning to Orang.

Life around the park is very close to the edge of it. Villages, rice fields and tea-growing country lie just beyond the boundary. In the early morning you may see farmers on bicycles, cattle on the embankments, smoke rising from cooking fires. This closeness is part of the challenge here. Elephants sometimes leave the park and raid crops. Floods affect people and animals alike. Conservation in Assam is not about a fence. It is a daily negotiation between wild animals and the families who live beside them.

The light here changes the whole experience. In the first hour after sunrise, mist often lies low over the grass. The rhinos appear as dark shapes, then slowly become solid. By midday the light turns flat and hot, and many animals move into shade. In the late afternoon the colour returns, and the grass glows gold and pale green. If you can choose your time, choose the edges of the day. The animals are more active, and the air is cooler.

The Brahmaputra itself deserves a moment of your attention. It is one of the great rivers of Asia. It begins far away in Tibet and crosses Assam on its way to Bangladesh and the sea. Along this stretch it is wide, braided and full of sandbanks that move from year to year. Its waters are home to the Gangetic river dolphin, a shy, almost blind animal that finds its way by sound. Without this river, there would be no grassland and no Orang.

So, before you leave, stop for a minute. Switch off the engine if your driver allows it. Listen again to the grass, the birds, the faint sound of insects. Look at the flat line of the horizon and the dark trees along the water. Somewhere close, a rhino is grazing, and a hog deer is lifting its head. As you drive back toward the villages, notice the moment the tall grass gives way to rice fields. That line is where the wild land ends, for now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book an early morning safari for mist and active animals.
Bring binoculars for distant rhinos and wetland birds.
Wear muted colours rather than bright clothing.
Plan around the monsoon when access is restricted.
Ask your driver to cut the engine briefly.
