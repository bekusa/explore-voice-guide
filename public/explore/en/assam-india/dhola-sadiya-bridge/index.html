The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind moves across open water, and the tyres hum on the concrete deck. Below you, the river spreads wide and pale, braided with sandbars and channels. On a clear day the hills of Arunachal Pradesh rise blue on the horizon. You are on one of the longest river bridges in India. The water underneath has shaped every journey in this corner of Assam for generations.

This is the Dhola–Sadiya Bridge, also known as the Bhupen Hazarika Setu. Setu is simply the Hindi and Assamese word for bridge. It crosses the Lohit River, one of the major rivers that feeds the Brahmaputra. The bridge stands in the far northeast of Assam, in Tinsukia district, close to the border with Arunachal Pradesh. Its length is a little over nine kilometres. That is long enough to make the far bank feel like a different country when you first set out.

To understand why this bridge matters, picture the crossing before it existed. For a long time, people moved between these banks mostly by boat and ferry. In the monsoon the river swells, the channels shift, and sandbars appear and vanish. A crossing that looked simple on a map could take many hours in practice. Sometimes it was not possible at all. Farmers, traders, students and patients all depended on the water and the weather.

The bridge changed that. Construction began in the early 2010s, and the bridge opened to traffic in May 2017. Prime Minister Narendra Modi inaugurated it. Suddenly a journey that once needed a ferry and a lot of patience became a drive of only a few minutes across the deck. The road distance between parts of upper Assam and eastern Arunachal Pradesh also became much shorter. Whole routes through the region were redrawn almost overnight.

Now, a detail many visitors miss. The bridge carries the name of Bhupen Hazarika, one of the most loved artists in the history of Assam. He was a singer, composer, poet and filmmaker. And he was born in Sadiya, the very town on this side of the river. So when the government named the bridge after him, it was a homecoming of sorts. Hazarika was later honoured with the Bharat Ratna, India's highest civilian award, after his death.

Hazarika's songs often spoke about rivers, especially the Brahmaputra. He sang about the people who live beside these waters, their labour, and their hopes. Many Assamese listeners know his melodies from childhood. It feels fitting that a structure built to tame a difficult river crossing should carry the name of a man who sang about the river all his life. Locals sometimes call the crossing simply by his name.

Look along the length of the bridge and notice its shape. It is not a dramatic suspension bridge with towering cables. Instead, it is a long, low beam bridge resting on a steady line of concrete piers. The piers march across the water in a rhythm that seems to go on and on. From the deck, the road ahead narrows to a point. The repetition is the beauty here. It is a piece of engineering built for endurance rather than display.

There is a second surprise in this plain design. The bridge was built to carry very heavy loads, including military vehicles such as tanks. This region sits not far from India's international border to the north. Moving troops and equipment quickly into Arunachal Pradesh has long been a strategic concern. So this quiet concrete span serves two purposes at once. It links villages and markets, and it also forms part of the country's defence planning.

Building across this river was not easy. The Lohit carries water down from the mountains with real force, especially during the monsoon months. Its bed is made of shifting sand and silt. Engineers had to set deep foundations that could stand against floods, strong currents and the risk of earthquakes, which are common in northeast India. When you see the piers rising cleanly from the water, remember how much of each one lies hidden below the surface.

Take a moment to look at the river itself. Depending on the season, it can look completely different. In the dry months, wide grey and golden sandbars appear, and the water splits into many thin channels. In the monsoon, those sandbars disappear under a broad brown flood. Birds often work along the edges of the water. Fishing boats may drift in the shallows. The river is never quite the same twice.

The towns at each end are worth noticing too. Dhola lies on the southern side, and Sadiya on the northern side. Both are small, working places rather than tourist centres. You may see tea gardens, rice fields, roadside stalls selling tea and snacks, and people waiting for shared vehicles. Life here moves to the pace of farming and trade. The bridge did not change the character of these towns, but it opened new doors for them.

For many travellers, this bridge is also a gateway. Beyond it, roads lead into the valleys and forests of eastern Arunachal Pradesh. Visitors heading into that state usually need a special travel permit, so plan ahead before you continue north. Others simply come to see the crossing, watch the light on the water, and turn back. Both are good reasons to stand here.

If you can, stay until the light starts to soften. Early morning and late afternoon turn the water silver and gold. The long line of piers throws soft shadows across the surface. Mist sometimes rests over the river at dawn, and the far bank fades into it. These are quiet hours, when the traffic thins and the wind is easier to hear.

Before you leave, look back along the deck one more time. Picture the ferries that once worked these waters, and the long waits on the riverbank. Then notice the cars and trucks moving across in minutes. Somewhere in the town behind you, a singer grew up listening to this river. As you walk or drive away, listen again... the wind, the water, and the steady hum of the road.
