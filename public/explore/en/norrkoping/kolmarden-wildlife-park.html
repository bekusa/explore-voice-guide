The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere behind the pine trees, a bird calls. It is not a Swedish bird. Then the wind comes up off the water, carrying the smell of resin, damp moss, and something warmer and wilder underneath. You are standing on a forested ridge above a long arm of the Baltic Sea. Here, in the middle of a Nordic woodland, animals from Africa, Asia and the Arctic live side by side. Welcome to Kolmården.

The name belongs to the landscape before it belongs to the zoo. Kolmården is a long, rocky, wooded ridge that runs along the northern shore of Bråviken, a deep inlet that reaches inland from the Baltic toward the city of Norrköping. For centuries this ridge was a hard place to cross. The forest was thick, the slopes were steep, and travellers between the provinces of Östergötland and Södermanland had to work their way over it. Today the same terrain shapes everything you see in the park.

That terrain is the first thing worth noticing. Most zoos sit on flat ground in a city. Kolmården climbs. Paths rise and fall over granite outcrops, between birch and pine, with sudden views down to the grey or blue water of the bay. The builders did not flatten the hill. Instead, they fitted enclosures into its folds. So as you walk, you are always going up or down, and the animals often appear above you or below you, rather than straight ahead behind a fence.

The park opened in the mid-1960s, during a period when Swedish families were gaining cars, holidays, and a new appetite for summer trips. The idea was ambitious for the time. It was not to be a small city menagerie but a large wildlife park in open country. Over the following decades it grew into one of the largest zoos in the Nordic countries. It became a fixture of Swedish summer, the kind of place many adults here remember visiting as children.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The rock under your feet has its own fame. Kolmården gives its name to a green-veined stone known as Kolmården marble, quarried in this area for a long time. Polished, it shows soft bands of green and white. It was used in fine Swedish interiors, including grand buildings in Stockholm. So the same ridge that now shelters tigers and giraffes once sent decorative stone to palaces and public halls.

Now to the animals. The park is organised into themed areas, each built around a part of the world. In the savanna section you may see giraffes, zebras and antelopes moving across open ground. There are big cats, including tigers, in their own landscaped area. There are animals of the north as well, which feel very much at home in this climate. Watch how differently each group handles the Swedish weather. Some seek the sun on warm rocks. Others clearly prefer a cool, grey day.

One of the best ways to understand the scale of this place is from above. A cable car runs over the safari area, and the small gondolas carry you slowly over the treetops and the enclosures. From up there the logic of the park becomes clear. You see how the fences follow the slopes, how water and woodland are used as natural borders, and how wide the territories really are. On a clear day, the bay of Bråviken spreads out behind it all.

Then there is the sea, or at least its creatures. Kolmården has long been known in Sweden for its dolphins. For many Swedes, the dolphin shows here were their first close view of these animals. The dolphins still draw crowds today, and the presentations tend to focus more on their natural behaviour, their intelligence, and how the keepers work with them. If you go, sit quietly for a while before the start. You can often hear the dolphins breathe at the surface... a short, sharp puff of air.

Here is the second surprise. Kolmården is not only a zoo. It is also home to a large wooden roller coaster called Wildfire. It rises out of the forest at the edge of the park, its timber frame pale against the dark pines. On a summer afternoon you will hear it before you see it. First, a low roll of wheels on track. Then, a wave of screams that carries across the hillside. Then, silence again, and the birds return.

For younger visitors, there is another familiar face. Bamse, the cartoon bear who is the strongest bear in the world in Swedish children's comics, has his own area here. Generations of Swedish children have grown up with Bamse and his friends, and the stories carry a gentle message about kindness and using strength to help others. If you see small children here suddenly very excited, this is usually why. For them, meeting Bamse matters as much as meeting a real bear.

A park like this also has a quieter side that most visitors never see. Modern zoos in Europe take part in shared breeding programmes for endangered species, and Kolmården works within that network. Behind the public paths there are keepers, vets and researchers who track the animals' health, diet and behaviour. When you watch an animal resting in the shade, remember that its daily routine has been planned with care, from the shape of its shelter to the timing of its meals.

The experience changes a great deal with the season and the weather. In high summer the paths fill with families, ice cream and the noise of the coaster. On a cooler, overcast day the park becomes calmer, and many animals become more active. Rain brings out the smell of the pine forest and makes the rocks shine. Wear good shoes. The hills are real hills, and you will feel them in your legs by the end of the day.

Before you leave, find a spot where the trees open toward the water. Stand still for a moment. Below you lies Bråviken, the long inlet of the Baltic. Around you is a Swedish forest of granite, pine and birch. And somewhere in that forest, an animal from very far away is lying in the shade, watching the same afternoon light that you are. As you walk back toward the gate, listen again. The strange bird is still calling.
