The guide

The story

Listen first. A wet, green hush. The soft crack of bamboo somewhere up the slope. Mud pulls gently at your boots. The air is cool and thin and smells of crushed leaves and damp soil. Mist moves across the hillside in slow sheets. For a moment it hides the peaks, and then it lifts. You are standing at the edge of Volcanoes National Park, in the far northwest of Rwanda, on the shoulders of a chain of sleeping mountains.

Those mountains are the Virunga volcanoes. The range stretches across the borders of three countries — Rwanda, Uganda, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The Virunga chain has eight major volcanoes, and five of them stand partly inside this park. Their names come up again and again in local conversation: Karisimbi, Bisoke, Sabyinyo, Gahinga, and Muhabura. Each one has its own shape. Learn to tell them apart, and the horizon becomes a map you can read.

Karisimbi is the giant of the group. It rises to roughly 4,500 metres, high enough that its summit sometimes turns white with hail or frost. Bisoke is smaller, with a crater lake sitting in its summit like a dark green eye. Sabyinyo has a jagged, broken crest. Locals often compare it to an old man's teeth. Muhabura is a near-perfect cone, and Gahinga sits low and rounded beside it. The far western Virunga volcanoes, across the border in Congo, are still active. These five have been quiet for a very long time.

Here is the first surprise. This landscape belongs to one of the oldest protected areas on the African continent. In 1925, colonial authorities created Albert National Park, which covered part of these mountains. It is widely described as Africa's first national park. The aim, even then, was to protect the gorillas living on these slopes. Over the following decades, borders shifted and new countries were born. The Rwandan section eventually became its own park, the one around you today.

And yes — the gorillas. Mountain gorillas live only in this volcanic region and in one other forest area nearby, in Uganda. They live nowhere else on Earth, and no zoo keeps them. Every mountain gorilla alive today lives in the wild. When people come to this park, most of them come for a single hour with a gorilla family. That hour is short. Many visitors later say it was the longest hour of their lives.

A trek usually begins in the early morning at the park headquarters near the village of Kinigi. Rangers divide visitors into small groups, and each group follows one habituated gorilla family. Habituated means the family has slowly become used to calm human visitors over years of patient contact. Trackers go ahead at dawn to find where the gorillas slept. Then the guides lead you up through farmland, across a stone wall, and into the forest. The trek can take less than an hour or most of a day.

The forest itself changes as you climb. Lower down, there is bamboo, tall and pale green, creaking in the wind. Young bamboo shoots are a favourite gorilla food in certain seasons. Higher up, you enter a strange, quiet woodland of Hagenia and Hypericum trees. Their twisted branches hang with moss and lichen, grey-green and dripping. Above that, on the highest slopes, the plants grow tough and low against the cold. Each band of forest feels like a different country.

Now the name you have probably been waiting for. In 1967, the American primatologist Dian Fossey set up a research camp high in the saddle between Karisimbi and Bisoke. She called it Karisoke, joining the two mountain names together. She spent years here, watching and recording gorilla families. She fought hard against poachers. Her book, Gorillas in the Mist, made these animals famous around the world, and a film of the same name followed in the late 1980s.

Fossey's story ended in tragedy. She was murdered at her camp in 1985, and the crime has never been fully explained. She is buried near Karisoke, beside many of the gorillas she studied, including one named Digit. Today, some visitors make a separate hike up to her grave and the remains of the camp. The trail is steep and muddy. At the top, simple markers stand in a quiet clearing among the trees.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a hopeful one. For much of the twentieth century, mountain gorillas seemed to be heading toward extinction. Poaching, war, and the loss of forest pushed their numbers very low. But careful protection worked. Ranger patrols, veterinary care, and community programmes all played a part. In 2018, the International Union for Conservation of Nature moved the mountain gorilla from critically endangered to endangered. That is still serious, but it is real progress.

Gorillas are not the only residents. Golden monkeys live in the bamboo zone here. They are small, quick, and coloured a bright orange-gold across the back. Treks to see them are usually easier and full of movement and noise. The park also shelters forest birds, buffalo, and elephants, though the elephants are shy and rarely seen. Listen for bird calls in the mist. The sound often carries far through the still, damp air.

The people around the park matter to its story as well. Each year, Rwanda holds a ceremony called Kwita Izina, which means giving a name. Newborn gorillas receive names, much as a Rwandan family names a new child. Part of the money from gorilla trekking also goes to communities living beside the park. This link is important. The farms you walk past on the way up are the neighbours the forest depends on.

So take a moment where you are. Look up at the volcanoes — maybe clear, maybe wrapped in cloud. Notice the line where the neat green fields stop and the wild forest begins. That line is easy to miss, but almost everything here depends on it holding. As you walk away, listen once more. Bamboo creaks. A bird calls. Somewhere up in that mist, a gorilla family is eating, resting, and simply carrying on.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book your gorilla trekking permit well before arriving.
Arrive early at Kinigi headquarters for group assignments.
Wear long sleeves and gloves against stinging nettles.
Pack waterproof layers; mist and rain arrive quickly.
Hire a local porter to help on muddy trails.
