The guide

The story

Step inside, and the air changes. Outside, the Deer Park holds birdsong, the murmur of pilgrims, and the soft slap of sandals on brick paths. In here it is cooler and quieter. The light falls evenly across pale stone. The first thing many visitors notice is the surface of that stone. Some of it shines almost like metal, polished so finely that it seems wet.

You are in Sarnath, about ten kilometres northeast of the old city of Varanasi. This small town holds a special place in the Buddhist world. Tradition says that here, after his enlightenment, the Buddha gave his first sermon to five former companions. Buddhists call that moment the first turning of the wheel of the Dharma, meaning his teaching. Almost everything in this museum came out of the ground just outside these walls.

The museum is one of the oldest site museums run by the Archaeological Survey of India, the government body that cares for the country's monuments. It opened in the early twentieth century to keep the finds close to where they were dug up. Look at the building's plan as you walk. It was designed to echo half of a Buddhist monastery, built around a central space. The architecture itself is a quiet nod to the ruined monasteries in the park next door.

Now, the reason most people come. In the main hall stands the Lion Capital of Ashoka. Ashoka was an emperor of the Mauryan dynasty who ruled much of the Indian subcontinent in the third century before the common era. After a bloody war, he turned toward Buddhism. He raised tall stone pillars across his lands, many carved with messages about moral conduct. One of those pillars stood here at Sarnath, and this sculpture once sat on top of it.

Take a moment with the four lions. They sit back to back, each one facing a different direction. Their manes fall in tight, flame-like curls. Their mouths are open, and the muscles of their chests are firm and alert. Below them runs a round band of stone called the abacus. On it, four animals walk in a circle: an elephant, a bull, a horse, and a lion. Between each animal turns a spoked wheel, the symbol of the Buddha's teaching.

Here is the detail many visitors miss. Look again at that glossy surface. The capital is carved from a single block of sandstone, quarried at Chunar, not far from Varanasi. Mauryan craftsmen polished their stone to a mirror-like finish. Scholars still discuss exactly how they achieved it. After more than two thousand years, the shine remains. Stand at an angle to the light, and you will see it catch along the lions' backs.

And a second surprise. You may recognise this sculpture from your pocket. In 1950, the newly independent Republic of India adopted the Lion Capital as its national emblem. You will find it on banknotes, coins, passports, and official seals. Usually, only three lions show in the flat version, because the fourth hides behind. In the original, the four lions were once crowned by a large stone wheel. That wheel broke long ago, and only fragments survive.

The capital was found during excavations in the first years of the twentieth century, broken from its pillar. The lower part of the pillar still stands in the archaeological park outside, behind a protective enclosure. If you visit the park after the museum, you can see the shaft where this masterpiece once rose. It helps to picture the whole thing standing there, high above the monks and pilgrims, facing the four directions.

Move on through the galleries, and the mood softens. The most loved piece after the lions is a seated Buddha from the Gupta period. The Gupta age, roughly the fourth to sixth centuries of the common era, is often called a classical moment in Indian art. Here the Buddha sits cross-legged, his hands raised in front of his chest in the gesture of teaching. His eyes are half closed. His face is calm, almost private.

Look below his seat. A small wheel is carved there, with a pair of deer on either side, and a row of tiny figures kneeling. These figures are usually read as the five companions who heard the first sermon, sometimes with a donor family. The deer point to the Deer Park, the setting of the story. Behind the Buddha's head rises a large halo, cut with delicate floral bands. Notice how thin and smooth the robe looks, almost transparent against the body.

Not everything here is the cream-coloured Chunar stone. Watch for sculptures in a deep red, speckled sandstone. That stone came from Mathura, an older centre of art to the west. One tall standing figure, a Bodhisattva, carries an inscription naming a monk called Bala as its dedicator. A Bodhisattva is a being who delays final release to help others reach enlightenment. Its strong shoulders and broad face feel very different from the gentle Gupta Buddha you just met.

The collection also reminds you that Sarnath did not belong to only one faith or one century. You will see Hindu deities among the Buddhist figures, along with carved railings, inscribed tablets, pottery, and decorated architectural fragments. Over many centuries, the monasteries here grew, were rebuilt, and finally fell into ruin. Each gallery holds a different layer of that long story. Take your time, and read the faces as much as the labels.

As you leave, the noise of the park returns. Somewhere a group of monks may be chanting near the great brick Dhamek Stupa. The smell of incense might drift across the lawns. Before you walk on, think of the lions you just stood beside. They have looked out in four directions for over two thousand years. Now, step outside, and look out with them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Expect to deposit phones and cameras before entering.
Visit the museum first, then walk the Deer Park ruins.
Find the broken Ashokan pillar shaft in the nearby park.
Allow quiet time at the seated Gupta Buddha.
Dress modestly; Sarnath remains an active pilgrimage site.
