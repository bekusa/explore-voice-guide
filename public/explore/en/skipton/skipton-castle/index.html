The guide

The story

Step through the gatehouse and the noise of the High Street falls away. Your footsteps change on the worn stone and the air feels cooler between the thick walls. Above the arch, carved in large stone letters along the parapet, is a single word: Desormais. It is old French for henceforth. It was the motto of the Clifford family, and it has looked down on this market town for centuries.

The word Skipton is usually explained as sheep town, from an old English name. That fits. This is the edge of the Yorkshire Dales, where wool and livestock shaped the local economy for generations. The castle stands at the top of the long High Street, beside Holy Trinity Church. Market stalls, shops and pubs run downhill from its gates. The town grew up at the foot of this fortress, and the layout still shows it.

The story begins soon after the Norman Conquest. Around 1090, a Norman baron called Robert de Romille raised a castle here. The first defences were most likely timber and earth, a quick way for the new rulers to hold land they had just taken. Over the following generations the wood gave way to stone. Look at the site itself and you can see why it was chosen.

Walk to the far side of the castle and the ground simply ends. A steep drop falls away to the gorge of Eller Beck, a small stream below. On this side no army could easily attack. The builders only needed to defend the town-facing approach. That is why the strongest towers and the great gatehouse face the High Street. The cliff did the rest of the work for free.

In 1310, King Edward the Second granted the castle to Robert Clifford. That moment shaped everything that followed. The Cliffords were a powerful northern family, and they held Skipton for centuries. They fought in the wars against Scotland and in the Wars of the Roses. Several Clifford lords died in battle. For a family so often at war, this castle was a secure home base in the north of England.

Now find your way into the inner courtyard, known as Conduit Court. This is the quietest and most personal space in the castle. Stone walls with Tudor windows rise on every side. In the centre grows a yew tree, its dark needles spreading across the courtyard. Tradition says Lady Anne Clifford planted it in 1659. Stand underneath and look up. The tree and the walls feel like they have grown old together.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Much of this courtyard was remodelled in the Tudor period. In the 1530s, Henry Clifford, the first Earl of Cumberland, added grander rooms. The work is linked to his son's marriage to Lady Eleanor Brandon, a niece of King Henry the Eighth. A royal connection demanded a finer home. So the medieval fortress gained more comfortable rooms, larger windows and space for a noble household.

Then came the English Civil War of the 1640s, when King and Parliament fought for control of the country. Skipton held out for the King. It became one of the last Royalist strongholds in the north. The siege dragged on for about three years. Cannon fire struck the walls, and the garrison inside held on until they finally surrendered in December 1645. They were allowed to march out with honour.

Parliament did not want the castle used against it again. Orders came to make it useless as a fortress, a process called slighting. The roofs were taken down and the defences weakened. Look closely at the towers as you walk around. Some of the stonework shows repair and rebuilding, a quiet record of that damage. Without a roof, a stone building does not last long in a Yorkshire winter.

The castle was saved by a remarkable woman. Lady Anne Clifford had fought for decades in the courts to win back her family's estates. When she finally inherited them, she began restoring her castles across the north, including Skipton. Locals tell a popular story about her. It is said that Cromwell allowed the work only if the new roofs were too weak to carry cannon. Whether that is true or not, the roofs went back on.

That restoration is a big reason why the castle survives so complete today. Many English castles are open ruins, with grass where the floors once stood. Here you can walk from room to room under a roof. You can climb stairs, look into the kitchen and the great hall, and peer down into the dungeon. It is easy to picture how people actually lived and worked inside these walls.

One more surprise waits in the grounds. Look for a small building called the Shell House. Its walls are decorated with seashells and coral, set into patterns. It dates from the seventeenth century, when wealthy families liked curiosities and decorative garden rooms. Imagine the effort of bringing shells to a town this far from the sea. It is a playful detail in a place built mainly for war.

Before you leave, walk back through Conduit Court and pause by the yew. Listen to the voices echo softly off the stone. Then step out under the gatehouse again and look up at that single carved word. Desormais... henceforth. Below you, the High Street carries on with its shops and market stalls, and the town that grew at this castle's feet goes about its day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk to the back walls to see the drop to Eller Beck.
Allow extra time to explore the roofed rooms at a slow pace.
Combine your visit with Holy Trinity Church next door.
Wear sturdy shoes for worn stone stairs and uneven floors.
Visit on a market day to see the High Street at its busiest.
