The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is the soft slap of water against a wooden landing, the creak of reeds, maybe a duck calling somewhere across the lake. Underfoot the gravel gives way to packed earth. In front of you, thatched roofs lean over rough plaster walls, and timber beams cross the facades. It looks like a quiet village in Normandy. But you are standing inside the grounds of Versailles, one of the most formal palaces ever built.

This is the Hameau de la Reine, the Queen's Hamlet. It was built for Marie Antoinette in the 1780s, a short walk from her private retreat at the Petit Trianon. Construction began in 1783. The architect was Richard Mique, the queen's favourite builder. The painter Hubert Robert, known for his romantic landscapes and ruins, helped shape how the whole scene should look. Together they did not design buildings so much as a living picture.

The idea was not entirely new. Rural fantasies were popular among the French aristocracy at the time. The Prince of Condé already had a small hamlet in the gardens at Chantilly, north of Paris, and the queen knew it. Writers of the period praised simple country life as more honest and more natural than court life. For a queen watched every hour of the day, a village of her own offered something rare — a place where the rules of the palace did not seem to apply.

Look closely at the walls. Many of these surfaces were made to look old on purpose. Painters and plasterers added false cracks, worn patches, and faded colours. The timber framing is partly decorative. Even when new, the hamlet was meant to look weathered, as if farmers had lived here for generations. It is a strange thought. You are looking at a ruin that was planned as a ruin from the very first day.

The largest building is the Queen's House, with its two parts joined by a wooden gallery. From the outside it looks rustic. Inside, the rooms were refined, with fine furniture, games, and space for small gatherings. A spiral of wooden balconies and stairs runs along the front. Along these galleries, notice the blue and white ceramic flowerpots. Many carry the queen's monogram. They are a quiet reminder of who this cottage really belonged to.

Now turn toward the water. The tall round building by the lake is the Marlborough Tower. It served partly as a lookout and partly as a place for fishing. Its name has a playful origin. According to a popular story, a nursemaid sang a well-known French song about the Duke of Marlborough to the queen's young son, and the tune became fashionable at court. Whether or not every detail is true, the song and the tower share the name.

Here is something most visitors miss. The hamlet was not only a stage set. It included a real working farm, set a little apart from the queen's buildings. There were cows, goats, sheep, and poultry. A farming family lived on site and managed the animals and fields. Milk, eggs, and produce were sent up to the queen's table. Today animals are often kept at the farm again, so you may hear a goat before you see it.

This leads to a famous image. In popular imagination, Marie Antoinette milked cows here dressed as a shepherdess. That picture is mostly legend. The queen did visit the dairy, and she enjoyed fresh milk and cream served in elegant settings. But the hard work was done by the farm staff. There were in fact two dairies — one for preparing milk and cheese, and one more decorative, for tasting and display, kept cool with marble surfaces.

Follow the path past the mill. Its wheel sits at the edge of a small stream. It was built mainly for charm, and it was not a serious grain mill. Nearby you will find smaller buildings, including a dovecote and a guardhouse. In total the hamlet holds about a dozen structures, spread loosely around the lake. Nothing here stands in a straight line. Compare that with the main palace gardens, where every path follows strict geometry.

That contrast is the whole point. Versailles was built by Louis the Fourteenth to show order and royal power. The hamlet follows a different style — the English landscape garden, where paths wind, water curves, and views open by surprise. Gardeners planted trees and shrubs to look as though they had grown wild. Walk slowly and notice how each turn of the path shows you the village from a new angle, like a series of paintings.

The peaceful mood did not last. In October 1789, during the first months of the French Revolution, crowds marched from Paris to Versailles. The royal family was forced to leave for the capital. The queen never came back to her hamlet. In the years that followed, the furniture was sold and the buildings slowly decayed. Later rulers, including Napoleon, ordered some repairs, but the village never returned to its original life.

Restoration work has continued in recent decades. The Queen's House was restored with private funding and reopened to visitors in 2018, after many years closed. Some interiors can now be seen, though access often depends on guided visits or special timed tickets. Even from outside, the restored buildings give a clearer idea of how the hamlet looked in its first years, with fresh thatch, clean timber, and painted walls.

Before you go, stand by the edge of the lake once more. Look at the reflection of the tower and the cottages in the still water. Listen for the ducks, the wind in the reeds, and the distant voices of other visitors. Two centuries ago, a queen came here to escape the eyes of a court. Today you can walk the same paths. As you leave, notice how the sounds of the hamlet fade behind you, and how the gardens slowly straighten back into lines.
