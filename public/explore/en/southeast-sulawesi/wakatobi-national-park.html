The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the hull of a small wooden boat, the water taps and slaps against the planks. The air smells of salt, diesel, and drying fish. Lean over the side, and the sea is so clear that the coral below seems close enough to touch, even when it lies several metres down. This is Wakatobi, a scatter of islands off the southeastern tip of Sulawesi, in Indonesia. Here the sea is not the edge of the place. The sea is the place.

Start with the name, because it holds a small puzzle. Wakatobi is not an old word. It is an acronym, built from the first letters of the four main islands. Wa for Wangi-Wangi. Ka for Kaledupa. To for Tomia. Bi for Binongko. Say it slowly — Wa, Ka, To, Bi — and you have the map in your mouth. Around these four lie many smaller islands, sandbars, and reefs. Together they form one of the largest marine parks in Indonesia.

The park was declared in the 1990s, and its protected area covers well over a million hectares. Most of that is water, not land. Later, UNESCO also recognised Wakatobi as a biosphere reserve. That status is meant to protect nature and also the people who live inside it. It is a key idea here. This is not an empty wilderness. Tens of thousands of people live on these islands, and many of them still depend on the reef for food every single day.

So why does this reef matter so much? Wakatobi sits inside a region scientists call the Coral Triangle. This is a stretch of ocean between Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, Papua New Guinea, and the Solomon Islands. It holds more kinds of reef-building coral than anywhere else on Earth. Marine biologists often describe it as the centre of ocean life. Wakatobi is one of its richest corners. Divers come here for the variety, not for one single famous creature.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. These islands also sit in a zone known as Wallacea. It is named after Alfred Russel Wallace, the nineteenth-century naturalist who studied how animals in Asia differ from those in Australia. Wallacea lies between those two worlds. Deep water channels kept many species apart for a very long time. The result is a mix of life you will not find in quite the same form anywhere else.

Now, look at the colours of the water. Over the shallow reef flats, the sea turns pale turquoise, almost white where the sand is close. Then, quite suddenly, it drops to deep, inky blue. That line is the edge of a wall, where the reef falls away into much deeper water. Snorkellers often float right along this boundary. On one side, you see sunlight on sand. On the other side, the blue simply continues down, further than the eye can follow.

Under the surface, the reef is crowded and busy. Hard corals grow in plates, branches, and rounded domes. Soft corals sway in the current. Small fish move in clouds — some bright orange, some electric blue. Look closely into the cracks, and you may spot a moray eel, or a tiny shrimp cleaning another fish. Turtles are often seen here. And sometimes, out in the blue, a larger shape passes by. Patience is the best tool you can bring.

Now lift your head and look toward the villages. Some are built on land, but some stand on stilts over the water itself. These belong to the Bajau, often called the sea people. For generations, the Bajau lived much of their lives on boats, moving across the seas of Southeast Asia. Many have now settled, but the sea still shapes their days. Children paddle small canoes before they can walk far. Fishing is not a hobby here. It is life.

On Kaledupa, there is a Bajau village called Sampela, built out over the shallows. Wooden walkways link the houses. Laundry hangs above the water, and boats are tied up under the floors like cars in a garage. If you visit, go slowly and quietly. People live here. A smile and a simple greeting open more doors than a camera. Ask before you photograph anyone, especially children.

Each main island has its own character. Wangi-Wangi is the busiest, with the main town and the airport. Kaledupa is green and quieter, and known for its hand-woven cloth. Tomia is popular with divers. And Binongko, the most remote of the four, has a surprising reputation. Its people are known across the region as blacksmiths. Locals say the sound of hammers on hot metal has rung out here for generations, as families forge machetes and blades for trade.

There is also serious science happening in these waters. For many years, international research teams have studied the reefs around the small island of Hoga, near Kaledupa. Students and scientists count fish, measure coral, and track how the reef changes. That work matters, because reefs everywhere are under pressure. Warmer seas can cause coral bleaching, when coral loses its colour and its food source. Overfishing and destructive fishing methods have also left scars in parts of the region.

This brings us to you, the visitor. What you do here has a real effect. Never stand on coral, even for a moment. Coral is a living animal, and a single footstep can break years of growth. Keep your fins high, and stay off the bottom. Choose a reef-safe sunscreen, or wear a long-sleeved rash guard instead. And if you buy fish or cloth or a carved souvenir, buy it from local hands. That keeps the money on the islands.

So take a moment now. Wherever you are standing — on a jetty, on a boat deck, on a strip of white sand — notice the sounds. A motor coughing somewhere in the distance. Children calling from a stilt house. Water lapping softly under the planks. The reef is below you, quiet and busy at the same time. When you leave, look back once at that line where turquoise meets deep blue. That edge is Wakatobi... and it is still here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Never stand on or touch coral while snorkelling.
Wear a rash guard instead of heavy sunscreen.
Ask permission before photographing people in Bajau villages.
Buy woven cloth and crafts directly from island makers.
Book boat transfers between islands well in advance.
