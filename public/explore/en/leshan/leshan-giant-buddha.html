The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, two rivers meet with a low and steady rush. The water is often brown with silt, and it folds over itself where the currents collide. The air is damp and smells of moss and wet sandstone. Then you lift your eyes, and a face as tall as a building looks calmly past you, out across the water. This is the Leshan Giant Buddha. He has watched these rivers for more than twelve hundred years.

The figure is carved straight out of a red sandstone cliff on Lingyun Hill, on the edge of the city of Leshan, in China's Sichuan province. He sits upright, hands resting on his knees, feet planted near the waterline. He stands about seventy-one metres tall. For more than a thousand years he was the tallest stone Buddha in the world, and he remains the largest carved in pre-modern times. Nothing here was assembled from blocks. The cliff itself became the statue.

The figure represents Maitreya, the Buddha of the future in Buddhist tradition. Maitreya is the teacher who is expected to come in a later age. In Tang dynasty China, he was a popular figure of hope and renewal. That matters here. This statue was not built to honour a past event. It was built to face forward, toward something still to come, and toward the rivers that people feared.

Those rivers are the key to the whole story. Here the Min River meets the Dadu River, and the Qingyi River joins nearby. In the eighth century, this meeting point was dangerous. Boats travelling through the region were wrecked in the turbulent water, and many lives were lost. A monk named Haitong decided to act. He believed that a great Buddha carved above the river could calm the water and protect the boatmen below.

Work began in the year 713, during the Tang dynasty. Haitong spent years gathering donations for the project. Locals tell a famous story about him. According to the legend, a corrupt official tried to seize the money Haitong had raised. The monk refused, and said the official could have his eyes before he took the Buddha's funds. Then, the tale says, he gouged out one of his own eyes to prove his resolve. Treat that as legend. But it shows how much this statue meant to the people of the region.

Haitong did not live to see the work finished. Carving stopped after his death, and for a time the cliff stood half shaped. Later, regional governors took up the project and funded it again. The statue was completed in 803. That means roughly ninety years passed between the first chisel strike and the last. Generations of carvers worked on one face of rock. Many of them must have started as young men and finished as old ones, or never finished at all.

Here is the first surprise. The plan may actually have worked — though not through any miracle. Historical accounts suggest that the huge quantity of stone cut from the cliff fell into the river below. That debris changed the riverbed and made the currents less violent. So the boatmen may really have been safer once the Buddha was carved. The protection was real. It simply came from engineering, not from the statue's gaze.

The second surprise is hidden inside the figure. The builders created a drainage system within the statue. Channels run behind the hair, the ears, the chest, and the folds of the robe. They carry rainwater away from the stone. Sichuan is wet and humid, and soft sandstone weathers quickly. Without these channels, the statue would likely have worn away long ago. Look closely and you may notice small openings that hint at this system.

Now look at the head. The hair is formed from rows of tight spiral curls, a traditional feature of Buddha images. The figure most often quoted is 1,021 of them, set into the stone one by one. The long earlobes are another classic sign, a reminder of the princely jewellery the historical Buddha gave up. The ears are so large that a person could stand comfortably inside one. Moss and small plants grow in the creases, softening the red stone with green.

For centuries, the Buddha did not sit in the open air. A large wooden pavilion, many storeys tall, once covered the statue and sheltered it from rain and sun. That structure was destroyed by fire and war in later centuries, long before modern times. Since then, the figure has faced the weather directly. Repeated restorations have repaired cracks, cleared plants, and cleaned blackened surfaces. Today the stone shows patches of different colours — traces of that long battle with time.

In 1996, the Giant Buddha was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list, together with nearby Mount Emei. Mount Emei is one of China's four sacred Buddhist mountains, and the two sites share a long religious history. On Lingyun Hill itself, there is still an active temple near the Buddha's head. You may hear the soft clink of incense burners, or see visitors bowing before small shrines. This is still a living place of faith, not only a monument.

If you want to reach the feet, you can take the steep stairway carved into the cliff beside the statue. It zigzags down the rock face in tight turns. The steps are narrow and often damp. As you descend, the scale changes. From above, you see the face. Halfway down, the chest and hands fill your view. At the bottom, you stand beside a single foot, and it is wider than a small car. Your own body feels very small here.

There is a third way to see him: from the water. Boats run past the cliff, and from the river you understand the original purpose. This is the view the boatmen had. Locals also point out something else from the river. The line of hills behind the city looks like a giant Buddha lying on his back, with the Giant Buddha sitting at his heart. It is a matter of imagination, but once someone shows you, it is hard to stop seeing it.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the rivers again. They are the same waters that frightened travellers when Haitong first looked at this cliff. Notice the moss in the curls of hair, the dark streaks where rain still runs, and the patient half-closed eyes. As you walk away, glance back once. The Buddha will still be looking out across the water, toward a future he was carved to wait for.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid long queues on the cliff stairway.
Wear shoes with good grip for the damp, steep steps.
Take a river boat to see the full figure at once.
Visit the temple on the hilltop before descending the stairs.
Bring a light rain layer; the cliff is often wet.
