The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Below you, the Ohře river runs over stones, low and steady. Above you, grey granite rises straight out of the rock it was built on. The castle and the cliff look like one piece of stone. The air near the water feels cooler, and in the early morning a thin mist often hangs between the trees on the far bank.

You are standing in Loket, a small town a short drive from the spa city of Karlovy Vary. The name means elbow in Czech. Look at the river and you will see why. The Ohře bends sharply around this rock, almost in a full loop, like an arm bent at the elbow. The town and its castle sit inside that bend, protected by water on nearly every side.

That shape decided everything here. A rock surrounded by a river is a gift to anyone who needs to defend a border. For centuries this was the western edge of the Bohemian kingdom, close to the German lands. Medieval writers gave the castle a proud nickname: the Lock of the Kingdom. Whoever held Loket controlled one of the main roads into Bohemia.

The oldest parts of the castle reach back to the twelfth century. Look for the square tower, the heaviest and most solid part of the complex. Its thick walls and small openings belong to an early style of building called Romanesque, the round-arched style that came before Gothic. Archaeologists have also found the remains of a small round chapel, a rotunda, one of the earliest stone buildings on the site.

Most of what you see today is Gothic, built and rebuilt over the following centuries. Gothic builders liked height, pointed arches, and larger windows. The castle grew outward and upward from that first tower. The result is not one neat design. It is a collection of walls, wings, and courtyards added by different owners for different needs. You can read that history in the changes in stone and in the uneven rooflines.

Now for a story most visitors miss. In the early fourteenth century, a small boy was held here against his will. He was the son of King John of Luxembourg, and his father kept him apart from his mother for political reasons. The child was only about three years old. That boy grew up to become Charles the Fourth, King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor... the ruler who later gave his name to Karlovy Vary itself.

Think about that for a moment. The spa town you probably came from carries the name of a man who spent part of his childhood as a small prisoner on this rock. Charles later returned to Loket under very different circumstances, as a king. He kept a close interest in the castle and in the region, and the town grew with royal support.

The castle has a darker chapter too. In the nineteenth century, the old fortress was turned into a prison, and it stayed a prison for well over a hundred years, into the middle of the twentieth century. Thick medieval walls built to keep armies out worked just as well to keep people in. Inside, an exhibition of medieval punishment and torture instruments shows the grim side of justice in earlier times.

Legend has it that a cruel burgrave once ruled here, a castle governor who treated the people of Loket without mercy. According to one popular local tale, he was cursed and turned into a lump of stone that fell from the sky. Locals connect the story with a real meteorite linked to the town, known as the Bewitched Burgrave. It is folklore, but it shows how people here explained a strange stone that fell from the heavens.

Loket also has a literary visitor. The German poet Johann Wolfgang von Goethe came to this area many times while taking the waters at the nearby spas. He loved Loket. It was here, in his old age, that he celebrated a birthday in the company of a young woman named Ulrike von Levetzow. He fell deeply in love with her. She did not accept his proposal, and the sadness of that ending shaped some of his late poetry.

Inside the castle, look out for the collection of porcelain. This corner of western Bohemia has a long tradition of porcelain making, with small factories in the towns and villages around Karlovy Vary. The white clay of the region made this possible. The pieces on display show how a local craft became an industry, from simple tableware to delicate painted decoration. It is a quieter room, and a good contrast to the prison cells.

When you step back outside, walk to the edge of the walls and look down at the town. The main square curves gently, lined with pastel houses and steep roofs. The whole town follows the shape of the rock. Film fans may recognise the square. Loket appeared in the 2006 James Bond film Casino Royale, along with several places in Karlovy Vary. Few people watching the film knew they were looking at a medieval town in Bohemia.

Take a final look before you leave. The river is still turning around the rock, just as it did when the first builders chose this place. Notice the colour of the stone in the current light, the sound of the water below, the swallows that circle the tower in summer. As you walk down toward the bridge, turn around once. From the far bank, you can see the whole elbow at once... the town, the castle, and the river holding them both.
