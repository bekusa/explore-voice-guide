The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you look up. Ferry horns, the slap of water against the wharf, the hollow rumble of trains crossing above Circular Quay. Salt is in the air, mixed with coffee from the cafés along the promenade. And in front of you stands a tall, cream-coloured building with strong vertical lines. It looks like it belongs to an older Sydney. Inside, though, it holds some of the newest art in the country. This is the Museum of Contemporary Art Australia.

Start with where you are standing. This is the western edge of Circular Quay, on the shore of Sydney Cove. Before any building rose here, this was the country of the Gadigal people of the Eora Nation. They fished these waters and lived along this foreshore for thousands of years. In 1788, the First Fleet arrived from Britain and anchored in this cove. A penal colony began a short walk from this spot. The museum sits on ground where two very different histories meet.

Now look at the older part of the building. It was not built as a gallery. It was built for the Maritime Services Board, the government body that managed the busy port of Sydney. Work began before the Second World War, slowed during the war years, and the building was finished in the early 1950s. Its style is a late form of Art Deco. Notice the strong vertical bands, the restrained ornament, and the sense of solid civic authority.

For decades, this was a place of shipping schedules, harbour pilots, and port paperwork. Staff inside could watch the vessels move in and out of the cove. Then, as the working port shifted away from the city centre, the building lost its first purpose. In 1991, it opened in a new life as a museum of contemporary art. A building made to run the harbour became a building made to question and reflect on the world.

Here is a story many visitors never hear. The museum grew partly from a gift made long before it existed. An Australian-born artist named John Power spent much of his life in Europe. When he died, he left a bequest to the University of Sydney to bring the latest international art to Australians. That collection, known as the Power Collection, became an important foundation for this museum. One artist's wish, made decades earlier, helped shape what hangs on these walls.

Now turn your attention to the newer section. In 2012, the museum opened the Mordant Wing, an extension designed by the Sydney architect Sam Marshall. Look at how it meets the older building. Instead of copying the Art Deco style, it uses stacked, offset boxes in pale and dark tones. The contrast is deliberate. Old and new stand side by side, and each makes the other easier to see.

Inside, the museum has no single permanent display that stays the same forever. Exhibitions change through the year. One visit might bring a large installation that fills an entire room. The next might show quiet photographs or a film playing in a darkened space. This is part of the idea. Contemporary art means art of our own time, so the museum keeps moving with that time. It is worth checking what is on before you climb the stairs.

The museum collection focuses on Australian contemporary art. A strong and central part of it is work by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander artists. Torres Strait Islanders are the Indigenous peoples of the islands between northern Australia and Papua New Guinea. Here, their art is shown as living, current practice, not as something from a distant past. Painting, sculpture, weaving, video, and new media all appear. Given the history of this shoreline, that choice carries real weight.

The Mordant Wing also holds the National Centre for Creative Learning. This is a space for workshops, school groups, and community programs. On many days, you may hear children and families making things, talking about art, and trying ideas for themselves. It is a reminder that this museum is not only for quiet looking. It also wants people to take part. If you are travelling with young people, ask the staff what activities are running.

Make time to go up. The museum has a rooftop level with a café and an outdoor terrace. From there, the view opens across the whole of Sydney Cove. To your right, the Harbour Bridge stretches over the water. Straight ahead, across the quay, the white sail shapes of the Opera House catch the light. Ferries cross back and forth below. Few places in the city put you so close to all three at once.

Here is a second detail worth noticing. From the rooftop, you can see that the museum and the Opera House face each other across the cove. One is a modest civic building from the mid-twentieth century. The other is a world-known performing arts centre. Yet both now serve culture. The harbour that once only carried cargo and convicts is now framed by two buildings dedicated to art. Stand still for a moment and take in that change.

When you leave, walk a little way into the streets behind the museum. This is The Rocks, one of the oldest parts of European Sydney. Narrow lanes, sandstone walls, and old pubs sit just a few minutes away. The museum works almost like a doorway between two worlds. On one side are the old colonial streets. On the other is the open water and the modern city skyline.

Before you go, look once more at the front of the building. Notice how the evening light changes the colour of the facade, from cream to a warmer gold. Listen again for the ferry horns and the trains. Think of the port officials who once worked behind these windows, and of the artists whose work now fills the rooms. Then let the harbour carry you onward, along the water, into the rest of your Sydney day.
