The guide

The story

Narrow lanes, worn sandstone, pub signs creaking above doorways. The Rocks sits pressed between the Harbour Bridge and Circular Quay, and it is where the modern city began. In January 1788, eleven British ships anchored in the cove below, carrying more than a thousand people, most of them convicts. The colony they started grew into modern Australia, and it started here.

Before the ships, this shore belonged to the Gadigal people. They fished the cove, gathered oysters and knew every fold of the sandstone. Within a year of the fleet's arrival, smallpox devastated Aboriginal communities around the harbour. Any honest walk through The Rocks begins with that memory: this is old ground, and its first story is not European.

The name is literal. Convicts hacked the first streets and houses out of a rocky sandstone ridge, and the stone they cut became walls, steps and warehouses, many still standing. Look closely at the older blocks and you can find grooves left by chisel and pick almost two centuries ago.

The oldest surviving house is Cadmans Cottage, a plain stone box near the water built in 1816. John Cadman, a convict transported for stealing a horse, rose to oversee the government boats and lived here with his family. The cottage once stood on the tide line. Land reclamation has since pushed the harbour meters away from its door.

For its first century, the neighborhood had a hard reputation. Sailors, publicans and traders crowded the lanes, and gangs known as larrikin pushes roamed after dark. Respectable Sydney kept its distance and built its mansions elsewhere, leaving The Rocks to the poor, the salty and the newly arrived.

In 1900, bubonic plague reached the waterfront. The government seized the area, demolished hundreds of buildings and cleansed whole streets in the name of hygiene. Two decades later, engineers cleared hundreds more homes to make room for the Harbour Bridge approaches. The neighborhood you see today is a survivor, twice cut back and still standing.

The 1970s brought the closest call of all. Developers planned to level much of The Rocks for office towers. Residents resisted, and the builders union led by Jack Mundey imposed what became known as green bans: union members refused to work on projects that destroyed heritage or community. The bans held, the plans shrank, and the old streets survived long enough to be protected.

Walk through the Argyle Cut to feel the convict era in your skin. This deep road canyon through the sandstone ridge was begun in 1843 by convict gangs with hand tools and finished years later with explosives. Stand in its shade, touch the cold stone, and look up at the traffic crossing far above.

Seek out the smallest lanes too. One passage, barely a shoulder wide, is called the Suez Canal, a joke about the rainwater and waste that once flushed down its slope. Nearby, Foundation Park keeps the exposed foundations of eight demolished houses terraced into the cliff, with oversized furniture marking rooms that no longer exist.

The pubs argue about age, and the argument is part of the fun. Three of them, the Fortune of War, the Hero of Waterloo and the Lord Nelson, each claim to be Sydney's oldest in a different way. Order something cold in any of them, look at the stone walls, and judge the atmosphere instead of the paperwork.

For ordinary lives rather than legends, visit Susannah Place, a terrace of four brick houses built in 1844. Families lived there continuously for about 150 years, and the museum keeps their wallpaper, kitchens and corner shop much as they were. The free Rocks Discovery Museum nearby displays objects dug from beneath these streets, from fishing hooks to clay pipes and children's toys.

On weekends, the lanes fill with white market tents. The Rocks Markets bring jewellers, photographers, soap makers and food stalls into the streets, and buskers work the corners. The smell of coffee and grilled food drifts between sandstone walls that once smelled of tar, rum and rope.

End at the water's edge at Dawes Point, under the great steel arch, or by the Overseas Passenger Terminal, where cruise ships tie up beside the cove. Across the water, the Opera House gleams. From this exact shoreline, a colony, then a city, spread across a continent. Stay until the lamps come on and the lanes go quiet. The stones underfoot remember all of it.
