The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The noise of the main street fades behind you, and the ground changes underfoot. Paving stones give way to packed earth and gravel. Ahead, a long portico of slender, pale pillars frames a garden. A narrow channel of water once ran down its middle. Most of Pompeii feels enclosed, made of walls and doorways. This place feels open. It feels like a country estate that somehow ended up inside a city.

You are standing in the property of a woman named Julia Felix. Her full name appears in Latin as Julia, daughter of Spurius, Felix. That detail matters. In a Roman world where public life belonged almost entirely to men, this huge plot belonged to a woman. She did not hide behind a husband or a guardian, either. She put her own name on a painted sign by the street, for everyone to read.

The estate is large. It fills an entire city block in the quiet eastern part of Pompeii, close to the amphitheatre and the great exercise ground called the Large Palaestra. Here the town was less crowded. There was room for gardens, vineyards and orchards. Julia's property mixed several things in one place: a private home, a public bath, shops, rental rooms, and open green space. Think of it less as a house and more as a small business complex.

Now for the first surprise. We know about her business because of a real advertisement. It was painted on an outside wall facing the street. In neat red letters, it offered spaces for rent on her property. It listed an elegant bath, shops, rooms above the shops, and upper-floor apartments. The lease would run for five years, starting in the middle of August. Scholars still debate one or two words in the text. But the message is clear. Julia Felix was a landlord, and she was looking for tenants.

Why would a wealthy woman rent out parts of her estate? Many historians connect it to an earthquake. In 62 CE, a strong earthquake shook the Bay of Naples and damaged much of Pompeii. Some public baths were out of action for years afterwards. Julia, it seems, saw an opportunity. Her private baths could serve paying customers from the town. It is a very practical response to disaster. Repair the damage, open the doors, and earn an income.

Walk toward the bath area if it is open during your visit. Roman baths followed a set route. Bathers moved through rooms of different temperatures, from warm to hot, and finished with a cold plunge. Look at the floors and lower walls. Romans heated rooms by sending hot air through hollow spaces under the floor and inside the walls. Even in ruin, you can often see the small brick stacks that once held the floors up above the hot air.

Come back to the garden. This is the heart of the estate. The portico on one side has rectangular pillars faced with marble. They are elegant and thin, almost modern in feel. Down the middle of the garden ran a long water channel. Romans called this kind of feature a euripus, after a narrow strait in Greece. Small bridges crossed it. Picture water reflecting the pillars, plants along the edges, and shade under the roof on a hot afternoon.

At one end of the garden is a dining room built for summer. Guests reclined on couches set around a central table, which was the Roman custom. Behind them, water could run down a small stepped cascade set into a niche. The sound of falling water would have cooled the room and covered the conversation. Pause here and imagine the scene. Lamps at dusk. Wine in bronze cups. The smell of wet stone and green leaves.

The second surprise is not on these walls at all. Some of the paintings from this estate are now in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples. Among them is a long series showing daily life in the forum of a Roman town. People buy shoes and cloth. A teacher appears to discipline a pupil. Someone reads public notices. These scenes are among the best images we have of ordinary Roman street life. They came from right here. The estate also produced fine still-life paintings of food and kitchen objects.

The story of the excavation is unusual too. Diggers working for the Bourbon kings of Naples uncovered the property in the 1750s. They removed paintings and objects they considered valuable. Then they covered much of the site again with earth. For about two centuries it stayed hidden. Archaeologists returned in the 1950s and cleared it properly. So the place you see today has been discovered twice. Each time, people understood it in a different way.

Think about the morning in 79 CE when Vesuvius erupted. We do not know what happened to Julia Felix herself. We do not know if she was still alive by then, or whether her advertisement found tenants. The ash covered the garden, the baths and the shops alike. What survived is a set of rooms, a few paintings, and one painted sign. From those pieces, we can still recognise a sharp and confident businesswoman.

Take a slow walk around the edges before you leave. Notice how much empty ground there is compared with the dense streets near the forum. Parts of this space were likely planted with fruit trees and vines. Archaeologists in Pompeii have studied root cavities and plant remains to understand these gardens. Look at the modern plantings and consider that the Romans valued this green space as much as any marble.

Stand under the portico one last time and look down the length of the garden. The pillars throw thin lines of shadow across the gravel. The noise of other visitors drifts in from the street. As you walk back toward the road, look at the outer walls. Somewhere on a wall like these, nearly two thousand years ago, a woman painted her name and her offer for the whole town to read.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit together with the nearby amphitheatre and Large Palaestra.
Come early to see the garden before crowds arrive.
Bring water and a hat for this exposed garden.
See the forum frescoes later at Naples Archaeological Museum.
Check on site which rooms are open during your visit.
