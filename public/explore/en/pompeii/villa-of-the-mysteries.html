The guide

The story

Leave the crowded streets of Pompeii behind. Walk out through the old city gate and down the road lined with tombs. The noise fades, the path softens, and the air smells of dry earth and pine. Ahead is a long, low villa of pale stone, sitting quietly on the slope. Inside one of its rooms is a painting so red, so close and so strange that people have argued about it for more than a century.

This is the Villa of the Mysteries. It stands just outside the walls of Pompeii, beyond the gate that once led toward Herculaneum. In Roman times this was a suburban villa, a country house on the edge of town. Its owners could look out over the slope toward the sea, which was much closer to the city then than it is today. The villa was a place of comfort, and later a place of work.

The house grew over a long period. Its earliest parts date to around the second century before the Common Era, when Pompeii was a prosperous Italian town under growing Roman influence. Later owners added terraces, porticoes and fine reception rooms. In its final years, part of the villa was turned to farming. A wine press was found here, and a reconstruction now shows how grapes were crushed. Luxury and labour lived side by side under one roof.

Then came the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in the year 79. Ash and volcanic debris buried the villa, along with the rest of Pompeii. Several victims died in and around the house, and plaster casts of their bodies were made from the hollows they left in the hardened ash. The same deposits sealed the painted walls. They kept the colours away from light and weather for well over 1,500 years.

The villa came back to light in the early twentieth century. Excavation began in 1909, on land that was then privately owned. Much of the house was cleared later, during the excavations of the late 1920s and early 1930s. When the main painted room was uncovered, it surprised everyone. Here was a single, continuous scene running around the walls, with figures almost the size of living people. Nothing quite like it had been found in Pompeii.

Now step into that room. The walls glow with a deep, intense red. Against this background, about thirty figures move across a painted stage, set on a narrow green ledge. Art historians call this kind of work a megalography, which simply means a painting of large figures. The style belongs to the middle of the first century before the Common Era. The figures are not crowded. Each one has space, and each one seems to be waiting for something.

What exactly is happening here? That is the great question, and the honest answer is that nobody knows for certain. The most popular reading links the scenes to the worship of Dionysus, the Greek god of wine, ecstasy and rebirth, whom the Romans called Bacchus. His cult had secret rites called mysteries. Only initiates took part. Because the rites were secret, few written descriptions survive. So scholars read the walls like a puzzle, and they do not always agree.

Follow the scenes slowly around the room. A boy reads from a scroll while a woman listens. Women prepare food and offerings. An old figure called Silenus, a companion of Dionysus, plays a lyre. Rustic woodland figures appear, one playing pipes. On another wall, a woman turns sharply away, her cloak billowing, as if she has seen something frightening. Her gesture is one of the most dramatic moments in the whole cycle.

At the centre of the main wall sits the god himself. Dionysus lounges across the lap of a woman, often identified as Ariadne, his mortal bride. Unfortunately the upper part of this scene is damaged, so their faces are not fully preserved. Nearby, a kneeling woman begins to lift a cloth from a tall object. Many interpreters believe it is a ritual symbol. Beside her stands a dark-winged female figure, raising a whip.

And the whip falls. On the next section of wall, a young woman kneels with her bare back exposed, her head resting in the lap of a companion. Close by, another woman dances naked, holding cymbals above her head. Pain and joy appear side by side. Some see a moment of ordeal and release in an initiation. Others suggest the paintings may show the preparation of a bride for marriage. Both readings have supporters.

One more figure watches it all. A dignified woman sits apart, wrapped in her cloak, calm and still. Some think she is the lady of the house, or a priestess, or the initiate after the rite is over. Notice how she seems to observe the scenes rather than take part. Here is a detail many visitors miss — several figures look straight out of the wall, toward the doorway. The painting watches you as you enter.

The rest of the villa deserves time too. Other rooms carry painted walls in a different mood, some with black backgrounds and fine, delicate decoration. Look at the thresholds, the floor patterns and the small changes in wall height. You can trace how the house was rebuilt and repaired over many years. The paintings were conserved and cleaned in a major restoration completed in the 2010s, which brought back much of their brightness.

Before you leave, stand for a moment in the red room again. Let your eyes move from one face to the next. These figures were painted by hands that never saw the eruption coming. They chose this red, this rhythm, these secret gestures. As you walk back up the road toward the city gate, past the tombs and the pines, you may still feel those painted eyes behind you — quiet, patient, keeping their mystery.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the tomb-lined road from the gate to reach it.
Visit early or late to avoid crowds in the red room.
Move clockwise around the frieze to follow the scenes.
Pause at the doorway to see figures looking toward you.
Allow extra time, as the villa sits far from central Pompeii.
