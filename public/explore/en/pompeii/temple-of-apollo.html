The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The paving here is uneven and worn smooth, and the dust has a pale, chalky colour. Above you, slender columns rise from the stone without a roof to hold. Somewhere close by you will hear footsteps and voices from the Forum. Inside this walled courtyard the sound is softer. You are standing in the Temple of Apollo, one of the oldest sacred places in Pompeii.

First, find your bearings. The sanctuary sits on the western side of the Forum, the great open square at the centre of the city. A tall wall separates the two spaces. On the other side of the temple stands the Basilica, where Pompeians settled lawsuits and did business. So this courtyard sat between commerce and law. People stepped in here to step away from the noise of the square.

Now the first surprise. This place is older than most of the Pompeii around you. Archaeologists have found offerings and pottery here from the archaic period, roughly the sixth century before Christ. That was long before Rome controlled this coast. Back then, Greek cities ruled the Bay of Naples. Through them, Apollo came to this small town. He was the Greek god of light, music, prophecy and healing.

The temple you see today is younger than that first shrine. Most of its present form dates to the second century before Christ. At that time Pompeii was a prosperous town, and its people still spoke Oscan, the local language of this region before Latin replaced it. Wealthy families spent money on public buildings to show their status. This rebuilt sanctuary was part of that wave of ambition, and its style looks firmly toward the Greek world.

Look at the layout. A covered walkway once ran all around the courtyard, held up by a long ring of columns. Visitors could walk in shade here, out of the strong Campanian sun. In the middle stands the temple itself, raised on a high platform called a podium. A broad staircase climbs to the front. At the top was the cella, the small inner room that held the image of the god.

In front of the steps you will find a stone altar. This was the real centre of worship. Most ceremonies took place outside, in the open air, where everyone could watch. Priests sacrificed animals here and burned offerings while the crowd stood in the courtyard. The god lived in the temple. The people met him at the altar. Picture smoke, music and the smell of roasting meat filling this enclosed space.

Now turn to the bronze figures along the colonnade. One shows Apollo as an archer, with his arm stretched out as if he has just released an arrow. Facing him is a bust of his twin sister, Diana, the goddess of the hunt. Here is something many visitors miss. The pieces you see are copies. The ancient originals are now kept safely in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples.

The copies still do an important job. They show you how the courtyard felt when it was full of art. Ancient Pompeians did not see pale, empty ruins. They saw painted plaster, coloured stone and dark, gleaming bronze. Statues stood between the columns and looked across at each other. Try to imagine colour on every surface. That image is much closer to the truth than the grey stone around you today.

Near the steps, look for a tall column with a sundial on top. A Latin inscription on it names local magistrates who paid for it. Telling the time was a public service, and this sanctuary was a public meeting place. There is another inscription in the floor of the cella. It is written in Oscan. That small line of text records the town's older, pre-Roman voice, still sitting inside a sanctuary that later became fully Roman.

Here is the second surprise. The sanctuary does not line up neatly with the Forum. Its walls sit at a slight angle to the great square next door. Many scholars think the builders added thick pillars along the shared wall to hide this. The awkward angle exists because the temple was here first. The Forum grew up later around it. So the city adjusted itself to the god, not the other way around.

The story does not end with a calm ancient town. In the year 62, a strong earthquake shook Pompeii. Temples, houses and public buildings were badly damaged. Repairs were still underway across the city seventeen years later. Then, in 79, Mount Vesuvius erupted. Ash and pumice buried this courtyard. When excavators uncovered it in the nineteenth century, they found signs that work on the sanctuary had never been finished.

Before you leave, walk to the northern side of the courtyard and look up. On a clear day, Vesuvius rises beyond the columns and the walls. It is the same mountain that buried this place and also preserved it. The people who worshipped here had no word for a volcano. To them it was simply a green mountain covered in vineyards. Standing here, you see both what they saw and what they could not know.

Take one more moment. Feel the warmth in the stone and notice where the shadows of the columns fall. Listen to the Forum beyond the wall. When you walk out, look back once at the altar in front of the steps. For more than six centuries, people came to this exact spot to ask a god for light, health and answers. Now you have stood here too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning when the courtyard is quiet and cool.
Combine your visit with the Forum and Basilica next door.
Bring a hat because the courtyard has little shade.
Stand at the northern end to frame Vesuvius behind columns.
Visit the Naples museum later to see the original bronzes.
