The guide

The story

Step through the doorway and the noise of the street drops away. The ground underfoot changes from rough volcanic paving to worn floor, cool even on a warm day. Ahead, light falls through an opening in the roof and lands on a shallow basin. Beyond that, columns stand in a quiet garden. This is the House of Menander, one of the largest and most finely decorated homes in Pompeii. Take a moment here before you walk further in.

The house sits in the eastern part of the city, in the district archaeologists call Regio One. It fills most of a city block. That fact alone tells you something about its owners. In a dense Roman town, space was money, and this family held a great deal of it. The plan follows a classic pattern. A narrow entrance opens into a formal hall, then a garden, then private rooms. Over many generations, the house grew as owners bought and joined neighbouring properties.

The main hall in front of you is the atrium. In a Roman house, this was the public stage. Clients came here in the morning to greet the master of the house, to ask favours, or simply to show loyalty. The opening in the roof let in light and rainwater. The water collected in the basin below and drained into a cistern. Look around the edges of the room. Small side rooms open off the hall, and some still hold traces of painted plaster.

One of those side rooms holds a surprise that many visitors walk past. Its walls show scenes from the Trojan War, the great Greek story of the siege of Troy. Among them, scholars identify the priest Laocoön and his sons attacked by serpents, and the moment Cassandra is seized. The panels are small and faded. But imagine guests waiting here, recognising every figure. These were the stories every educated Roman knew by heart, the way we might know a famous film.

Near the atrium you will also find the household shrine, called a lararium. Romans believed that protective spirits guarded the home and the family line. Here the household offered small gifts of food, wine, and incense, often every day. This shrine is unusual in its form, built more like a small temple than a simple painted niche. Pause beside it. This was the spiritual centre of daily life, where slaves and family members alike would have stopped to honour the gods.

Now walk on toward the garden. This is the peristyle, an open courtyard surrounded on all sides by columns. After the shaded atrium, the space feels wide and bright. Romans loved to bring nature inside their walls. Plants, fountains, and statues would once have filled this court. The columns were coated in plaster and painted, so imagine colour rather than bare stone. Listen for the birds that still gather here. In summer, the cicadas in the nearby pines can be very loud.

Around the garden, look for a set of recesses, set into the walls like small open rooms. In one of them is the painting that gave the whole house its modern name. A man sits calmly in a chair, holding an open scroll. Painted beside him is his name, Menander. He was a Greek playwright from Athens, famous for his comedies about ordinary people, love, and family trouble. He had lived roughly three centuries before this house was buried.

Why would a Pompeian family honour a Greek writer on their wall? Displaying Greek culture was a way to show learning and taste. Here is the second surprise. We do not actually know the real name of the family who lived here. The excavators simply named the house after the painting. A seal and other finds have led some scholars to link the house with the family of the Poppaei. That family is connected with Poppaea Sabina, the wife of the emperor Nero. But this link remains a theory, not a proven fact.

The house was excavated in the late 1920s and early 1930s, under the direction of the archaeologist Amedeo Maiuri. And in 1930, his team made one of the most famous discoveries in all of Pompeii. Below the bath area, in an underground space, they found a wooden chest. Inside lay a large collection of silver tableware. Cups, bowls, plates, and mirrors, more than one hundred pieces in total, had been wrapped and hidden away. Someone had stored them carefully, perhaps during building work, perhaps for safekeeping.

That treasure is no longer here. It is now kept in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples. If you visit the museum, you can see cups decorated with scenes in raised silver, some showing myths and others showing the labours of Hercules. Standing here, it is worth remembering what that silver means. Dinner in this house was a performance. Guests reclined on couches, ate from fine dishes, and admired the host's wealth with every course.

The house also had its own private baths. Few homes in Pompeii did, since most people used the public bath houses in town. Here the family could bathe in comfort without leaving home. The bath rooms still keep some of their floor mosaics, made of tiny black and white stones. Look for figures of swimmers and sea creatures. Notice too how the floors and walls were built to carry hot air, heated by a furnace, so that the rooms stayed warm.

Not every part of the house was elegant. Toward the back were service areas, where enslaved workers cooked, stored goods, and kept animals. These rooms are plainer, with rough walls and simple floors. They are a reminder that a house like this ran on the work of many people whose names we will never know. When the eruption of Vesuvius came in the year 79, people were still here. The remains of several victims were found inside the house during the excavations.

As you move through the rooms, notice how the house controls your view. From the entrance, your eye travels in a straight line, through the atrium, past the basin, and into the garden. This was designed on purpose. Every visitor saw the depth and wealth of the house from the very first step. Roman architects thought carefully about these lines of sight. Try standing at the door again before you leave, and you will see the effect clearly.

Before you go, find a quiet spot near the garden columns. Look up at the sky above the open courtyard. It is the same view the family had, framed by the same stone. Feel the warmth in the walls, and hear the voices of other visitors fade along the street outside. When you step back through the doorway, look down at the threshold under your feet. Nearly two thousand years of footsteps have worn it smooth.
