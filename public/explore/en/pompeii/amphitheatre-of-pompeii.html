The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. The arena floor is packed earth, soft and pale, lower than the town around it. Look up and the stone seats rise around you in a long oval, their edges worn smooth. Pine trees stand beyond the rim. On a quiet morning you can hear swifts, footsteps on gravel, and very little else. It is hard to imagine twenty thousand voices here, but that is roughly how many people this place was built to hold.

You are standing in the oldest surviving stone amphitheatre in the Roman world. It was built around 70 BCE, more than a century before the Colosseum in Rome. Many visitors assume the Colosseum came first, and that Pompeii copied it. In fact, the opposite is closer to the truth. This arena is one of the early experiments, built when the whole idea of a permanent stone venue for gladiators was still new.

The timing matters. Only a few years earlier, after a war with Rome, Pompeii had been turned into a Roman colony. Retired soldiers loyal to the general Sulla were settled here. They brought Roman tastes with them, and gladiator games were one of those tastes. This building was, in a sense, a gift to the newcomers. It announced that Pompeii was now a Roman town.

We even know who paid for it. An inscription names two local magistrates, Gaius Quinctius Valgus and Marcus Porcius. It says they built the venue with their own money and gave it to the colonists forever. Here is the surprise: the inscription does not call it an amphitheatre. The word did not exist yet in common use. The Pompeians simply called it the spectacula... the place of the shows.

Now look at how the building sits in the land. The builders did not raise a tall tower of stone. Instead, they dug the arena down below street level and piled the excavated earth into banks around it. The seats rest on those banks. This kept costs down and made the structure very stable. It also explains why the outside looks so low and heavy, almost like a fortified mound.

The location tells a story too. The amphitheatre sits in the far southeastern corner of the town, pressed against the old city walls. That spot was mostly open ground at the time. It also made sense for crowd control. Thousands of spectators, many from nearby towns, could arrive and leave without pushing through the narrow streets of the centre.

When you walk back outside, look for the great external staircases. Two double ramps climb the outer wall toward the upper seats. There were no internal corridors here like the ones in later arenas. People climbed up from outside and then walked down into their rows. The lower seats, closest to the action, were reserved for the most important citizens. The higher you sat, the lower your rank.

On hot days, the shows offered a comfort that was worth advertising. Painted notices on walls around Pompeii announced upcoming games, and some of them promised awnings to shade the crowd. Imagine canvas stretched overhead against this same southern sun. Other notices mentioned sprinkling, probably water to settle the dust. Organisers competed for public favour, and these small luxuries helped.

The most famous day in this arena was not a show at all. In 59 CE, a fight broke out between Pompeians and visitors from the neighbouring town of Nuceria. It began with insults, then stones, then blades. The Roman historian Tacitus records that many people died. The Senate in Rome punished Pompeii by banning gladiator games here for ten years. Think about that while you look at the seats... a whole town lost its favourite entertainment because of one afternoon.

A Pompeian painted that riot on a wall in his own house. The fresco shows this very amphitheatre from above, with fighting spreading across the seats, the arena, and the ground outside. You can even see the external staircases and the awnings. It is one of the very few ancient pictures of a real event in a real building. The painting is now kept at the National Archaeological Museum in Naples.

The arena walls themselves were once painted as well. When excavators uncovered the building in the early nineteenth century, they found colourful scenes on the low parapet around the arena, including animals and fighters. Exposed to sun and rain, those paintings faded away within years. Today the parapet is plain. What survives of them survives mostly in drawings made at the time of the dig.

The story did not end with the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE. In 1971, the band Pink Floyd filmed a concert here with no audience at all, only the crew and the empty stone seats. Decades later, music returned to this arena with live crowds. The acoustics of the bowl still carry sound well, which is easy to test. Speak quietly in the middle, and listen to how far your voice travels.

Before you leave, walk the edge of the arena slowly. Look for the low arched passages where fighters and animals entered. Notice the difference between the original stone and the newer repairs, which are often smoother and more regular. Some of the damage you see is from an earthquake that struck the region in the early 60s CE, years before the eruption. Pompeii was still repairing itself when the end came.

So take one last look around. The trees beyond the rim, the pale earth of the floor, the curve of stone where thousands once shouted for their favourites. As you climb out and turn toward the rest of the town, remember that people walked this same path home after the games, arguing about who fought best. The road under your feet is the one they used.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to reach the arena before the main crowds.
Walk to the far southeastern corner; it is a long way.
Bring water and a hat; the arena floor has little shade.
Climb the outer staircases for an overview of the whole oval.
See the riot fresco at the Naples Archaeological Museum.
