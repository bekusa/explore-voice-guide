The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Away from the main streets, the noise of the crowds drops away. You stand inside a high wall, in a small courtyard of brick and weathered plaster. The ground under your feet is packed earth and old paving. Above you, the sky is a clean rectangle framed by the ruins. This is the Temple of Isis in Pompeii — a sanctuary to a goddess who came to this town from very far away.

Isis was an Egyptian goddess. In Egypt she was honoured as a mother, a healer, and a protector of sailors and the dead. Over the centuries before Rome's rise, her worship travelled along the trade routes of the Mediterranean. It reached Greek islands, port cities, and eventually the Bay of Naples. Pompeii lay close to the sea and to busy harbours. Sailors, merchants, and enslaved people from the eastern Mediterranean all passed through. A foreign goddess found a real home here.

What made Isis so attractive to ordinary Romans? Her cult offered something personal. Followers went through an initiation. They believed the goddess cared for them as individuals, in this life and after it. Traditional Roman religion was often a public duty, linked to the state and to family rank. The worship of Isis felt closer and more emotional. Women, freed slaves, and foreigners could all take part. For many people, that was a powerful promise.

Look at the temple itself, standing in the centre of the courtyard. It rises on a high platform, called a podium, reached by a flight of steps. At the top was the inner room, where the statue of the goddess stood. Notice the small niches on either side of the front. They once held images or sacred objects. The shapes feel Roman, but the decoration once mixed Roman style with Egyptian symbols and scenes.

Around you runs a covered walkway, a portico, supported by columns. The columns are made of brick, covered with stucco, which is a smooth layer of plaster. On some of them you can still see traces of red paint on the lower part. Imagine the whole space painted in strong colours. The walls once carried scenes of Egyptian landscapes, priests, and sacred animals. Today they are bare, and the original paintings are elsewhere.

Here is a surprise most visitors miss. The best evidence for who rebuilt this temple is a stone inscription. In 62 CE, a strong earthquake struck Pompeii and damaged much of the town, including this sanctuary. The inscription says the temple was rebuilt from its foundations by Numerius Popidius Celsinus. He was six years old. In return, the town council welcomed this child as a member.

Of course, a six-year-old did not pay for the work. Historians believe the real money came from his father, Numerius Popidius Ampliatus. He was a freedman — a man who had once been enslaved. Roman law prevented freedmen from holding certain public offices. So the father used his wealth to open a door for his son. This temple is a story about faith. It is also a story about ambition and social climbing in a Roman town.

Now find the small roofed structure in the courtyard, near the altar. It looks almost like a tiny house, with a decorated front. This is the purgatorium, a place for purification. Inside, steps lead down to an underground basin. Scholars think it held sacred water, which represented the water of the Nile. For followers of Isis, the river Nile was holy. The flood that fed Egypt every year was linked to the goddess herself.

Behind the temple is a larger hall. Experts usually call it the ekklesiasterion, a Greek word for a meeting room. Here, the followers of Isis probably gathered for meals, meetings, and ceremonies. Sources from the Roman world describe daily rituals in this cult. Priests opened the temple in the morning and offered prayers. Music, chanting, and the rattle of a metal instrument called a sistrum may have filled this courtyard. Try to hear it in your mind.

Then, in 79 CE, Mount Vesuvius erupted. Ash and pumice buried this courtyard along with the whole town. The temple stayed hidden for about seventeen centuries. When excavators uncovered it in the 1760s, it was one of the first buildings at Pompeii to come back to light. Its paintings and statues were in remarkable condition. Much of what was found here was later moved to the National Archaeological Museum in Naples, where you can still see it.

The discovery caused a sensation across Europe. Travellers on the Grand Tour, the educational journey of wealthy young Europeans, came to see this exotic Egyptian shrine. One of these visitors was the young Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, who came to Pompeii as a boy in 1769. Years later, he wrote The Magic Flute, an opera full of Egyptian-style mysteries and priests of Isis. Some people suggest this visit inspired it. That idea is attractive, but it remains a guess, not a proven fact.

Stand still near the altar for a moment and look around. The walls are high. The space is enclosed and private. From the street, people could not easily see what happened inside. That was intentional. The cult of Isis had secrets, shared only with initiates. Compare this with the open public temples in the Forum. Here, the architecture itself creates a sense of mystery and belonging.

Just next door are other important spaces. Nearby lies the Large Theatre, and close by is an old exercise ground from the town's earlier Samnite period. The Samnites were the Italic people who lived here before Roman control. Walking between these sites, you can feel how many cultures shaped Pompeii. Greek, Italic, Roman, and Egyptian influences all sat side by side. This quiet courtyard holds a piece of that mix.

Before you leave, look once more at the steps of the temple. Countless feet climbed them, carrying prayers for health, for safe voyages, for a better life after death. Notice how the light falls across the brick and plaster. Listen to the sounds of today's visitors, mixed with birds and wind. As you walk back into the streets of Pompeii, remember the six-year-old boy, his ambitious father, and a goddess from the Nile.

What to look for

Red paint traces on the lower brick columns.
Small roofed purgatorium with steps down to the basin.
Empty niches flanking the front of the temple.
High enclosing wall that hides the courtyard from the street.
Steep steps rising to the temple's raised platform.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Naples Archaeological Museum to see the original paintings.
Come early to enjoy the courtyard before tour groups arrive.
Combine this stop with the nearby Large Theatre.
Bring water and a hat; the courtyard offers little shade.
