The guide

The story

Before you look up, look down. At the threshold, small cubes of black and white stone form the shape of a dog. It is chained, crouching, and baring its teeth. Beneath it, two Latin words are set into the floor: Cave Canem, which means beware of the dog. Almost two thousand years after the warning was laid, it still works. You notice it. You hesitate for half a second before you step inside.

This is the House of the Tragic Poet. It is one of the most quoted homes in Pompeii, though it is not one of the largest. It stands in the northwest part of the city, a short walk from the Forum and the Forum Baths. If you have walked past the grand villas elsewhere in the ruins, this house may feel modest. That is part of its value. It shows how a comfortable, well-off family lived in the final years of the town.

Those final years ended in the year 79, when Mount Vesuvius erupted. Ash and pumice buried Pompeii, and the city disappeared from view for centuries. This house came back into the light in the 1820s, during the excavations of that period. The diggers were struck at once by the quality of its decoration. Mosaics and wall paintings of Greek myths covered the rooms. News of the finds travelled quickly through Europe, and visitors soon came to see them.

The name of the house comes from one of those finds. It was not a sign or an inscription. Excavators found a floor mosaic showing a group of performers preparing for a play. One figure appears to direct them, and the actors handle masks and costumes. Early scholars took the central figure for a poet, and the name stuck. Today, many experts read the scene as a rehearsal for a theatrical performance. The original mosaic is now in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples.

Now look again at that dog. Its exact purpose is still debated, and here we must be honest. Some say it was a simple warning to strangers, because real guard dogs were common in Roman homes. Others suggest a more playful idea... that it warned guests to watch out for a small house pet underfoot. Nobody knows for certain. What is clear is that the image became famous. Copies of it now appear on doormats, postcards, and gate signs around the world.

As you move inward, you enter the atrium. This was the main hall of a Roman house, and the centre of public life for the family. Look for the shallow rectangular basin in the floor. It is called an impluvium. Above it, the roof once had an opening to the sky. Rainwater fell through the gap, collected in the basin, and drained to a cistern below. On hot days, the water also cooled the air in the room.

The walls around the atrium were once covered with large painted scenes from Greek legend, especially from the story of the Trojan War. One well-known panel showed Achilles and Briseis, the young woman taken from him during the war. Another showed the marriage of Zeus and Hera, the king and queen of the gods. Many of the best panels were cut from the walls in the nineteenth century and taken to Naples. That was common practice at the time. It protected the art, but it emptied the rooms.

So, as you stand here, use your imagination. The faded plaster you see was once bright red, yellow, and black. The style of painting in this house belongs to the last phase of Pompeian decoration. Scholars call it the Fourth Style. It mixes large central pictures with fanciful architecture, thin columns, and decorative borders. Look closely at the walls. In some places, small patches of colour still hold on, and they give a hint of the original effect.

Beyond the atrium is the tablinum. This was a kind of office or reception room, where the head of the household met clients and kept records. It sits on the main axis of the house. From the front door, a visitor could look straight through the atrium and the tablinum into the garden behind. Romans designed this long view on purpose. It showed off the depth of the house and the wealth of the owner. Stand in the entrance and you can still see the effect.

The garden at the back is called the peristyle. Columns once framed it on several sides, and it would have held plants, perhaps a few shrubs or flowers. In the back corner, look for a small shrine. This was the lararium, where the family made offerings to their household gods. Every Roman home had some form of this shrine. It was a quiet, daily ritual, much closer to ordinary life than the grand temples in the Forum.

Off the garden was a dining room, known as a triclinium. Diners reclined on couches here, leaning on one elbow as they ate. This room also carried painted scenes from myth. One of them showed Theseus leaving Ariadne asleep on an island, a story of love and abandonment. Another famous painting from this house, now in Naples, shows the sacrifice of Iphigenia, a princess offered to the gods before the Greek fleet sailed to Troy. Dinner here came with serious drama on the walls.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. This house also played a role in nineteenth century literature. The English writer Edward Bulwer-Lytton wrote a popular novel called The Last Days of Pompeii, published in the 1830s. He chose this house as the home of his hero, a young Greek named Glaucus. The book was a best seller. For generations of readers, this small house became the very picture of Roman life in Pompeii, and many travellers came looking for it.

Notice, too, the openings on either side of the main entrance. These were probably shops, facing the street and open to passing trade. In Pompeii, private homes and small businesses often shared the same walls. A family could live in comfort just behind a counter where goods were sold. Step back out to the street for a moment. Look at the worn paving stones, the raised pavement, and the stepping stones that let people cross without touching mud and waste.

Before you go, return to the threshold once more. Stand where the guest once stood, and look in past the dog toward the far garden. The roof is gone, the paintings have mostly left, and the voices are silent. But the plan of the house is still clear. As you walk on, listen to the crunch of gravel under your feet, and look at the doorways around you. Each one opens onto another life that stopped on the same day as this one.
