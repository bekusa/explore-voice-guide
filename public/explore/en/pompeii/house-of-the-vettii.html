The guide

The story

Step out of the sun and into the doorway. The air drops a few degrees. Under your feet the floor is worn smooth, and ahead of you the light changes. It falls from an open roof onto a rectangle of stone, and beyond that you can see green leaves, white columns, and walls painted a deep, glowing red. You are standing at the threshold of the House of the Vettii, one of the most carefully decorated homes in Pompeii.

Before you go further, look to your right, at the wall just inside the entrance. A painted figure stands there, holding a pair of scales. On one side of the scale is his own very large phallus. On the other side is a bag of money. This is Priapus, a Roman god of fertility and abundance. To a Roman visitor, the image was less shocking than it is to us. It was a protective symbol, and a cheerful boast about prosperity. The message was simple: good fortune lives here.

Who were the people behind that boast? Seal rings and inscriptions found in the house point to two men, Aulus Vettius Restitutus and Aulus Vettius Conviva. Historians believe they were freedmen, which means they were born into slavery and later gained their freedom. Conviva was a member of the Augustales, a priestly association often joined by wealthy former slaves. These were men who had climbed a long way. This house was their proof, painted on every wall.

Now walk into the atrium, the open central hall. In Roman homes, this was the public face of the family. Clients and visitors waited here in the morning to greet the master. Here the Vettii placed their strongboxes, heavy chests that held the family money. They did not hide their wealth in a back room. They put it where every guest would see it. It is a small detail, but it tells you a great deal about their ambitions.

The walls around you belong to what scholars call the Fourth Style of Pompeian painting. This was the last fashion in the city before the eruption. It mixes large fields of solid colour with painted architecture, fantasy columns, and framed scenes that look like pictures hung on a wall. Much of this decoration was likely painted after a powerful earthquake struck the region in 62 CE. The Vettii were redecorating in the latest style, only a few years before the end.

Move ahead into the peristyle, the garden surrounded by columns. This is the heart of the house. When archaeologists excavated it in the 1890s, they found the garden almost intact, with marble tables, bronze and marble statues, and a system of lead pipes for fountains. Water once ran from small figures into basins around the edge. Today's planting is a modern reconstruction, but it follows the plan of the original beds. Stand still for a moment and imagine the sound of running water here.

The Vettii were lucky in one way. Earlier excavations often stripped Pompeian houses of their objects and carried the best paintings to museums in Naples. Here, the excavators made a different choice. They left many of the frescoes and sculptures in place and put a roof back over parts of the house. That is why you can still see these paintings in the rooms they were made for. The house was also closed for many years of restoration, and it reopened to visitors in early 2023.

Now look for the large room that opens onto the garden, decorated with a black band of tiny figures. This is one of the surprises most visitors walk straight past. The figures are cupids, small winged boys, and they are at work. They make perfume. They press grapes and sell wine. They work as goldsmiths, and they clean and press cloth as fullers did. It is a charming picture of daily trades in a Roman town, painted as if children were running the city's businesses.

Other rooms around the garden hold larger mythological scenes. In one, the infant Hercules strangles two snakes sent to kill him, while his parents watch. In another, the Theban king Pentheus is torn apart by women in a religious frenzy, as punishment for opposing the god Dionysus. Another panel shows Dirce, tied to a wild bull. These were stories every educated Roman knew. By choosing them, the Vettii showed that they belonged to that cultured world.

A second room is often linked to the myth of Ixion. In Greek legend, Ixion offended the gods and was bound forever to a turning wheel. The scenes in these rooms are about power, punishment, and the danger of crossing the gods. They make a strange contrast with the playful cupids nearby. Perhaps the owners wanted dinner guests to have something serious to discuss over their wine.

Now walk toward the service area of the house, where the mood changes. Here is the kitchen, where bronze cooking pots were found still on the hearth. Nearby is a household shrine called a lararium. It shows the family's guardian spirits and a large painted serpent, a symbol of protection and fertility. Slaves cooked, cleaned, and prayed in this part of the house. It is plainer and darker than the garden rooms, and the difference is the point.

One more detail is easy to miss. Near the entrance, a short graffito names a woman called Eutychis and offers her services for a small sum. Scholars still debate what it meant and who wrote it. Close to the kitchen there is also a small room with erotic paintings. These traces remind us that this house held many lives, not only those of its proud owners. Some of the people who lived here had no say in how it was decorated.

Then, in 79 CE, Mount Vesuvius erupted. Ash and pumice buried the town, and the house of the Vettii disappeared under the ground for more than eighteen centuries. The paintings stayed in darkness, protected from the light that might have faded them. When the excavators uncovered them in the 1890s, the reds and yellows were still strong. Much of what you see today is that original colour, carefully cleaned and conserved.

Before you leave, stand once more at the edge of the garden. Look at the columns, the painted walls, and the strip of sky above the open roof. Two former slaves built this world to show that they had made it. As you walk back out to the street, notice the worn stone of the doorstep under your feet. Many people crossed it, almost two thousand years ago, carrying news, deliveries, and greetings, just as you do now.

Key facts

Owners were Aulus Vettius Restitutus and Aulus Vettius Conviva.
The two Vettii are believed to be freed slaves.
Excavated in the 1890s, frescoes largely left in place.
Decorated mainly in the Fourth Style of Pompeian painting.
Reopened in 2023 after a long restoration.
