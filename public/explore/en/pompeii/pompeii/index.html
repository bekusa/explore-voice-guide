The guide

The story

The cobblestones beneath your feet are rutted with grooves worn smooth by Roman cart wheels two thousand years ago. Stand still for a moment and listen — the silence here is almost physical, broken only by the wind moving through empty doorways and the distant voices of other visitors drifting across what was once a living city.

You are standing in Pompeii, a Roman town frozen mid-stride on an October morning in the year seventy-nine. Mount Vesuvius erupted that day, burying the city and its inhabitants under six metres of volcanic ash and pumice. What makes Pompeii extraordinary is not that it was destroyed — many ancient cities were — but that it was preserved. The ash that killed the city also sealed it, creating a snapshot of Roman life more complete than anything else that survives from the ancient world.

Walk along any street and you will see the city exactly as its inhabitants left it. Bakeries still have carbonized loaves of bread on their counters. Taverns display painted menus on their walls. Private homes preserve frescoes in colours so vivid they look weeks old, not millennia. The ash fell so quickly that people were caught in the middle of ordinary moments — eating lunch, sheltering in doorways, running toward the harbour. Plaster casts made from the voids their bodies left in the hardened ash show them in their final positions, hands raised, faces turned away from the falling sky.

The forum stretches before you, the political and commercial heart of the town. This open plaza was surrounded by temples, government buildings, and covered markets. Citizens gathered here to vote, to trade, to hear news from Rome. The columns that once supported a two-storey colonnade now stand as broken stubs, but you can still trace the layout — the Temple of Jupiter at the north end, the basilica where legal cases were heard along the western side, the macellum where meat and fish were sold to the east.

Pompeii was not a major city. It held perhaps twelve thousand permanent residents, a provincial market town on the Bay of Naples that thrived on wine production and the manufacture of garum, a fermented fish sauce Romans used the way we use salt. But its ordinariness is precisely what makes it valuable. This is not imperial Rome with its monuments to power and conquest. This is the daily texture of life in the Roman world — how people ate, how they worshipped, how they entertained themselves, how they raised children and ran businesses and fell in love.

The houses here reveal a social structure archaeologists are still unpacking. Wealthy families lived in large homes built around central courtyards, their walls covered in elaborate frescoes depicting mythological scenes or garden landscapes. Poorer residents crowded into small apartments above shops on the main streets. But wealth was fluid — some of the grandest houses belonged not to old aristocratic families but to freedmen, formerly enslaved people who had earned or bought their liberty and built fortunes in trade. The House of the Vettii, one of the most richly decorated homes in Pompeii, belonged to two brothers who had been slaves before becoming successful merchants.

The streets themselves are an engineering lesson. Notice how they are raised well above the level of the roadway, with stepping stones placed at intervals to let pedestrians cross without descending. Pompeii had no sewage system — waste and rainwater ran down the centre of the street in open channels. The stepping stones let you cross without wading through it, while the gaps between them were wide enough for cart wheels to pass. Those deep ruts you saw earlier came from decades of heavily loaded wagons wearing grooves into the volcanic paving stones.

The amphitheatre at the eastern edge of town is the oldest surviving Roman amphitheatre in the world, built around seventy years before the Colosseum in Rome. It seated twenty thousand people, nearly twice the town's population, drawing crowds from the surrounding countryside for gladiatorial games and animal hunts. In the year fifty-nine, a riot broke out here during a contest between Pompeii and the neighbouring town of Nuceria. The violence was so severe that the Roman Senate banned all games in Pompeii for ten years. Graffiti on the amphitheatre walls still records the names of favourite gladiators and the outcomes of specific fights.

The brothel, marked by an erotic fresco above its entrance, preserves a series of explicit paintings in each of its small rooms. These were not decorations but a menu, showing clients what services were available in a world where many customers and workers spoke different languages. The stone beds are narrow and hard, separated only by thin partitions. Names and messages scratched into the walls by clients and workers survive — some boastful, some melancholy, all intensely human.

The Garden of the Fugitives holds thirteen plaster casts of people who died together while trying to escape through an orchard at the southern edge of town. You can see adults and children, some clutching each other, some curled on the ground with their arms over their heads. The ash and gas that killed them moved faster than anyone could run. Most of Pompeii's population did escape — only about eleven hundred bodies have been found at the site — but those who hesitated, who went back for valuables or tried to shelter in place, had no chance once the pyroclastic surge arrived.

Archaeologists have been excavating Pompeii since 1748, and roughly a third of the city still lies buried. Every year brings new discoveries — frescoes, graffiti, skeletons, entire buildings emerging from the volcanic soil. Recent excavations have used new techniques to study the remains, revealing details about diet, disease, and social mobility that earlier digs missed. The work is painstaking and will continue for generations.

As you walk back toward the entrance, look around at the buildings one more time. The roofs are gone, the upper floors have collapsed, the paint has faded on exterior walls exposed to two thousand years of rain. But the bones of the city remain — the streets, the fountains, the threshold stones worn smooth by sandalled feet. Somewhere in Rome or Carthage or Alexandria, other cities were rising and falling on that October day in seventy-nine. Only this one was stopped mid-breath, waiting under the ash until we were ready to listen to what it had to say.

You are standing in a place where the ancient world is not a collection of facts in a textbook but a physical presence you can touch. The stones remember. The walls remember. Walk slowly as you leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early in the morning to avoid midday heat and crowds.
Wear sturdy shoes with good grip for uneven cobblestones.
Bring water and sun protection as shade is limited.
Allow at least three hours to see the main sites.
Download a detailed map before entering as signage is sparse.
