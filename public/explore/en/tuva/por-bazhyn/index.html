The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across open water, and reeds rustle along the shore. Somewhere a bird calls over the lake, and then the quiet comes back. Under your feet the ground is soft and uneven. It is not rock. It is old clay, packed and worn down over more than a thousand years. You are standing inside one of the strangest ruins in Inner Asia. It is a fortress on an island, in the middle of a mountain lake, far from almost everything.

This is Por-Bazhyn. It sits on a small island in Lake Tere-Khol, in the remote southeast of Tuva, close to the Mongolian border. The name is Tuvan, and it is usually translated as "clay house." That is a modest name for something so large. From the air the complex looks like a sharp rectangle laid on the water. From the ground it looks like long, low ridges of earth, with the sky open above them.

Look at the walls around you. They were not built from cut stone. They were made from rammed earth, which means layers of damp clay pressed hard between wooden frames until they set. Builders across Inner Asia and China used this method for centuries. When the walls were new, they stood much taller than they do today. Rain, frost and wind have slowly pulled them down into the rounded banks you see now.

So who built it? Most researchers link Por-Bazhyn to the Uyghur Khaganate. This was a powerful steppe empire of the eighth and ninth centuries, centred in what is now Mongolia. Here is the first surprise. Wooden beams from the site have been dated with tree rings, and the results point to the late eighth century. That is unusually precise for a ruin this old, in a place this far from any city.

The layout tells a story of planning and ambition. An outer wall encloses the whole island site. Inside, a central courtyard leads to a raised inner complex, where the main buildings once stood. Archaeologists found traces of roofs covered with clay tiles. Some of these were decorated, in a style close to Chinese architecture of the same period. So imagine this place in colour, with tiled roofs, timber halls and a gate facing the water.

Now comes the deepest mystery. Nobody knows for certain what Por-Bazhyn was for. Some scholars think it was a palace or a summer residence for a ruler. Others suggest it was a monastery. One idea links it to Manichaeism, a religion from Persia that the Uyghur rulers adopted for a time. Others still have proposed a fortress, a memorial, or even a place to watch the sky. Each theory has supporters. None has been proven.

Here is the second surprise. When archaeologists dug here, they found very few signs of daily life. There were few tools, few broken pots, and few hearths full of ash. For a building this large, that is strange. It suggests that people may have left Por-Bazhyn soon after it was finished, or perhaps never lived here fully at all. A huge project, built with great effort, and then... abandoned.

Why would anyone leave? Again, there are only theories. The Uyghur Khaganate collapsed in the ninth century, and a change of power may have ended the project. Some researchers have pointed to signs of earthquake damage in the walls. The region is seismically active, so this is possible. The honest answer is that the site keeps its secret. You are looking at a question, not a finished story.

The ruins have drawn serious attention in modern times. Soviet archaeologists studied the site in the mid twentieth century. A larger international project returned in the 2000s, with teams mapping the walls and excavating the inner buildings. Much of what we know about the tiles, the beams and the empty rooms comes from that later work. Some finds are now kept in museums elsewhere, so the island itself shows mostly earth and outline.

The land here is fragile in ways you cannot easily see. Beneath the surface lies permafrost, ground that stays frozen through the year. The lake level also changes over time, and water slowly eats at the island's edges. Researchers have warned that the shoreline and the walls are wearing away. Every visitor's footstep adds a little to that pressure. That is one reason to walk gently and stay on worn paths.

Take a moment to look beyond the walls. The lake stretches out around you, and low hills and mountains rise in the distance. This corner of Tuva is very thinly populated. Herders and their animals still move through the wider landscape, as people have done for a very long time. The builders of Por-Bazhyn looked out on a view much like this one. The setting has changed far less than the ruin.

Pause on the light, too. On a clear day it is sharp and high, and shadows fall hard along the tops of the walls. In the evening the low sun picks out every ridge and dip in the clay. That is when the shape of the old buildings becomes easiest to read. Clouds can roll in quickly over the mountains here, and the whole scene turns grey and silver within minutes.

Before you leave, stand still once more. Look along the line of the outer wall and follow it with your eyes to the corner. Feel the wind coming off the water. Somewhere under this clay lie the answers to questions nobody has solved yet. As you walk back toward the shore, notice how quickly the walls sink into the landscape behind you. From a short distance, they look almost like natural hills.
