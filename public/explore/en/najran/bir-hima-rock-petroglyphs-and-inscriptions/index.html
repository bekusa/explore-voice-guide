The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the loudest sound is often the wind moving across warm sandstone. The ground underfoot is a mix of gravel and fine, pale sand that shifts under each step. Around you, low rock outcrops rise from the desert in shades of rust, honey and dark brown. Look closely at those surfaces. They carry pictures and words, cut by human hands over thousands of years.

This is Bir Hima, in the Najran region of southwestern Saudi Arabia. The name tells you why people came. Bir means well in Arabic. In a landscape this dry, a reliable source of water decided where people stopped, rested and gathered. The wells drew herders, hunters and, later, long trains of traders with their camels. Many of them left a mark on the rocks before they moved on.

In 2021, UNESCO added this landscape to its World Heritage List under the name Hima Cultural Area. That decision recognised two things at once. First, a huge body of rock art that spans thousands of years. Second, a dense collection of ancient inscriptions in several scripts and languages. Together, they make this one of the most important open-air archives on the Arabian Peninsula. There is no roof and no glass case — just the rock, the sky and the record of travellers.

Why here? Bir Hima sat on one of the old caravan routes that crossed Arabia. Traders carried goods such as frankincense and myrrh — fragrant tree resins prized for temples and rituals across the ancient world. These caravans moved from the southern lands, in what is now Yemen, toward markets far to the north. For anyone heading out across the desert, a well like this was a place to fill every water skin. It was a final pause before a hard journey.

Now let your eyes adjust to the carvings themselves. Most were made by pecking. Someone struck the rock surface again and again with a harder stone. This chipped away the dark outer layer, which geologists call desert varnish. Underneath, the paler rock shows through. The contrast forms the image. Over centuries, new varnish slowly darkens the carvings again. So, as a rough guide, lighter images are often younger than darker ones.

The animals here tell a story of changing climate. You may find long-horned cattle, ibex with great curved horns, ostriches, and figures that some researchers read as lions or other large animals. Cattle need far more water than this desert offers today. Their presence suggests that, in some earlier periods, the land was greener and wetter. Here is something many visitors miss. The rock art is not only decoration. It is also a record of an environment that has disappeared.

Then come the people. Look for small human figures holding bows, spears or shields. Some stand in groups, some face animals, some seem to be hunting or fighting. Later, the camel takes over the scene. Domesticated camels changed life in Arabia, because they made long desert crossings possible. Watch how often the camel appears, sometimes with a rider. In many ways, that single animal explains why caravans could reach this well at all.

Alongside the pictures, you will see writing. This is the second treasure of Bir Hima. Scholars have recorded inscriptions in several scripts here. One of the most common is Musnad, the old South Arabian alphabet with its tall, angular letters. Others include forms of early North Arabian writing, and researchers have also identified Greek and Aramaic texts. Each script tells you something about who passed by, and where they came from.

Many inscriptions are short and personal. A name. A family line. A prayer or a simple note that someone was here. That idea may feel familiar. Travellers today sign guest books or post a photo. Thousands of years ago, a person rested by this well and cut their name into the stone. Some of these names have now outlasted the cities their owners came from. Stand in front of one, and you are reading a message meant for anyone who would follow.

A few inscriptions carry larger history. Near Bir Hima, scholars studied a text linked to Abraha, a sixth century ruler of southern Arabia. The inscription records a military campaign into central Arabia. Abraha is also the figure connected, in Islamic tradition, to the story of the Year of the Elephant. Historians still debate how the written record and that tradition fit together. But the stone itself is real, and it places a major regional power here, at these wells.

The writing also shows a slow change in language. Over time, scripts used in Arabia shifted, and some inscriptions in the wider Najran area belong to early stages of Arabic writing. For scholars, this matters a great deal. These rocks help them trace how the Arabic alphabet developed before it became the script of books and manuscripts. You do not need to read any of these letters to feel the weight of that. Just notice how many different hands, and different alphabets, share the same rock faces.

The wells still matter. Local herders have used water from this area for a very long time, and the site remains part of a living landscape. You may see camels grazing in the distance or tracks from vehicles of local families. This is not a museum frozen in one moment. It is a place where people have stopped for water across thousands of years, and in a quieter way, they still do.

As you explore, please treat the carvings gently. Do not touch them, trace them or pour water on them for a better photo. Skin oils and scratches damage the thin surface layer, and that damage is permanent. The light does the work for you. Early in the morning and late in the afternoon, low sun throws shadows into the pecked grooves. Faint figures that looked invisible at midday suddenly appear, sharp and clear.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment. Look along the line of the rocks, then out to the open desert. This view is close to what a caravan driver saw, after filling the water skins and checking the camels. Ahead lay a long, dry road. Behind lay a rock face covered in names and animals. As you walk back, look down at your own footprints in the sand — and notice how quickly the wind starts to smooth them away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon for raking light.
Carry plenty of water, shade is very limited.
Use a capable vehicle for the desert tracks.
Hire a local guide to locate the best panels.
Never touch or wet the carvings for photos.
