The guide

The story

Feel the ground first. Under your feet, the dark basalt of the Sahyadri hills is rough and pitted, warmed by the sun or slick after rain. A breeze comes up from the plain below, carrying the smell of wet grass and woodsmoke. Somewhere nearby a vendor calls out, a scooter hums past, and a temple bell rings once. This is Panhala. It is a fort, but it is also a small hill town, and people still live inside its walls.

You are standing high above the Kolhapur plain, in the Sahyadri range. That is the local name for the Western Ghats, the long chain of hills that runs down India's western side. From the edges of the plateau, the land drops away in steep, wooded slopes. On a clear day the fields below look like a patchwork. In the monsoon, clouds often drift across the walls themselves, and the whole fort can disappear into grey mist within minutes.

The story of this place begins long before the Marathas. Historians link the first major fortifications to the Shilahara dynasty, a regional ruling family. Their king Bhoja the Second is usually credited with building the fort in the late twelfth century. Over the following centuries, control passed from one power to another. The Yadavas held it, and later the Adil Shahi sultans of Bijapur, a kingdom to the east, made it one of their strongholds in this region.

The Bijapur period left some of the stone you can still see. The walls you walk beside today stretch for several kilometres around the plateau. In many places they follow the natural edge of the cliffs, so the hill itself does much of the defensive work. Builders only had to close the gaps. Look at how the masonry changes from one stretch to the next. Different rulers repaired and extended these walls, and the joins between their work are often visible.

Now, the chapter most visitors come for. In 1659, Shivaji Maharaj, the founder of the Maratha kingdom, took Panhala. To this day he is revered across Maharashtra. Holding the fort did not last easily. In 1660, an army under the Bijapur commander Siddi Jauhar surrounded the hill. The siege dragged on for months. Supplies ran low, the monsoon arrived, and Shivaji was trapped inside these walls with no clear way out.

What happened next is one of the most retold episodes in Maratha history. On a dark, rainy night, Shivaji slipped out of the fort with a small band of soldiers. They headed for Vishalgad, another fort to the west. According to the popular account, a barber named Shiva Kashid, who looked like Shivaji, dressed as the king and allowed himself to be captured. This bought the real Shivaji precious time. The deception was soon discovered, and Shiva Kashid was killed.

The pursuers came on fast. At a narrow mountain pass, the warrior Baji Prabhu Deshpande stayed behind with a small group of men to hold off the enemy. They fought until Shivaji reached Vishalgad. Baji Prabhu died of his wounds there, and the pass became known as Pavan Khind, which means roughly the sacred pass. Even today, groups of young people retrace the escape route on foot during the monsoon, walking through mud and rain to honour that night.

Let us come back inside the walls. One of the most impressive structures here is the Teen Darwaza, which means the Three Gates. It is a fortified entrance on the western side. Notice how the gates do not line up in a straight path. An attacker would have had to turn and slow down inside a confined space, under fire from above. Look up at the carved stonework over the arches. Even in a military building, someone took care over decoration.

Near the gate area you will find the Andhar Bavdi, often translated as the hidden well. It is a step well built into a multi-storey stone structure. From outside, it does not look like a water source at all. That was the point. In a siege, water is life, and a well that enemies could not easily find or poison was a serious advantage. Step inside and the air turns cool, and your footsteps echo off the damp stone.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Panhala also has enormous granaries, known as the Amberkhana. These thick-walled stone buildings stored grain to feed the garrison through long sieges. The largest is often called the Ganga Kothi, and two smaller ones carry the names of other sacred rivers. Stand inside one and look at the ceiling. The scale tells you how seriously the fort's builders took the threat of being cut off from the world.

The fort's importance did not end with Shivaji. In the early eighteenth century, the Maratha queen Tarabai made Panhala a seat of her power. She led the resistance against the Mughal Empire after her husband's death, ruling on behalf of her young son. This made the fort, for a time, the political heart of the Kolhapur branch of the Maratha state. Her palace still stands, and parts of it are now used for public offices and a school.

Another surprise comes from the twenty-first century. In 2025, Panhala became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing called the Maratha Military Landscapes of India. The listing groups together a set of forts that show how the Marathas used hills, coasts and terrain as part of their defence. So the walls around you are now recognised as part of a much larger system, a network of strongholds that once stretched across the region.

As you walk around, notice the mix of old and new. Snack stalls sell roasted corn and spiced tea beside centuries-old bastions. Families picnic on the grass. Monkeys watch for unguarded food. Small shrines are tucked into corners, with fresh flowers and red powder on the stones. This is not a sealed museum. It is a lived-in landscape, and that ordinary daily life is part of what makes the history feel close.

Before you leave, find a quiet stretch of wall and look out over the drop. Picture the rain on that night in 1660, the torches below, and a small group of men slipping away into the dark. Then notice what is in front of you now — the wind, the birds, the wide plain. As you walk back toward the gates, run your hand along the stone. It has outlasted every army that came here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in the monsoon to see clouds drift over walls.
Wear shoes with good grip for wet basalt paths.
Keep snacks hidden from the monkeys near picnic spots.
Arrive early on weekends to avoid local crowds.
Carry a light rain jacket during the wet season.
