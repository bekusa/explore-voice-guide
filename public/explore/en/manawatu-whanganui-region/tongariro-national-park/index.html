The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the wind moves through tussock grass with a dry, steady hiss. Underfoot, the ground crunches. It is not soil so much as broken pumice and ash, light grey and full of tiny air pockets. On a cold morning you may smell something sharp on the breeze. That is sulphur, rising from vents in the earth. You are standing on a volcanic plateau in the centre of New Zealand's North Island, and the ground beneath you is still active.

Welcome to Tongariro National Park. Three volcanoes rise above this plateau. Ruapehu is the broad, snow-capped giant to the south, and the highest point in the North Island, at close to 2,800 metres. Ngauruhoe is the steep, near-perfect cone in the middle. Tongariro itself is the oldest and most worn of the three, a cluster of craters and ridges to the north. Together they form the southern end of a long volcanic zone that runs up through the island toward the Bay of Plenty.

Here is the first thing many visitors do not know. This was New Zealand's first national park, and it began with a gift. In 1887, Te Heuheu Tukino the Fourth, a paramount chief of the Ngāti Tūwharetoa people, offered the sacred peaks to the Crown. His aim was protection. Land disputes and sales were spreading across the region, and he wanted the mountains kept safe from being divided and sold. The national park grew from that act. Few parks anywhere began this way.

For Ngāti Tūwharetoa, these mountains are not scenery. They are ancestors. The peaks carry tapu, a Māori idea that means sacred or set apart. Because of this, local iwi, or tribes, ask visitors not to climb to the very summits of Ngauruhoe and Tongariro. Many people still do, but more and more hikers now respect the request. If you walk here, it is worth keeping that in mind. You are a guest on land that holds deep meaning.

The mountains also carry stories. Locals tell one of Ngātoroirangi, a high priest and navigator who, according to tradition, arrived on the Arawa canoe from the ancestral homeland of Hawaiki. Legend has it he climbed Tongariro and began to freeze in the icy wind. He called out to his sisters across the ocean for fire. They sent it under the sea and through the land, and where it surfaced, the hot springs and geysers of the North Island were born. Another tale says these mountains once fought over the lovely Pīhanga, and the loser fled west.

That cultural bond shaped the park's place in the world. In 1990, UNESCO listed Tongariro as a World Heritage Site for its natural features. In 1993, it was listed again, this time for its cultural value to the Māori people. It was one of the first places on Earth recognised as a cultural landscape. That means the world officially accepted that the meaning of this land to its people matters as much as its rocks and lakes.

Now look at Ngauruhoe again. That symmetrical cone may feel familiar. Film fans know it as Mount Doom, because the mountain stood in for that volcano in scenes from The Lord of the Rings films. In reality, it is a young volcano, as these things go, built up layer by layer over a few thousand years. Its slopes are loose scree, small stones that slide underfoot. From a distance, the dark streaks down its sides show where old lava flows and ash have settled.

The volcanoes are not sleeping. Ruapehu holds a crater lake near its summit, and that lake has caused tragedy. On Christmas Eve in 1953, a wall of water, ash, and mud burst from the crater and rushed down the Whangaehu River. It weakened the rail bridge at Tangiwai just before a passenger train arrived. The train fell into the river, and 151 people died. It remains one of the worst disasters in New Zealand's history. Ruapehu erupted again in the mid-1990s, sending ash across much of the North Island.

Even Tongariro, the old one, still stirs. In 2012, the Te Māri craters on its northern slopes erupted after a long quiet period. Rocks were thrown across the landscape, and a mountain hut was damaged. Nobody was hurt. Today, scientists watch all three volcanoes closely, with instruments that track ground movement, gas, and temperature. Warning signs on the tracks are not decoration. They are part of living beside an active system.

Most people come here for one walk — the Tongariro Alpine Crossing. It runs for roughly twenty kilometres across the saddle between Tongariro and Ngauruhoe. It is often called one of the finest day walks in the country. The track climbs past old lava flows to the Red Crater, where the rock is rust red and steaming. Just beyond it lie the Emerald Lakes. Minerals from the volcano turn their water a bright green and blue. Further on, the calm Blue Lake sits in an old crater.

The crossing looks gentle in photographs. It is not. The weather can change in minutes. Sunshine at the start can become freezing wind, rain, or snow near the top. There is little shelter, and many people turn back each year. Here is a second detail visitors often miss. Below the mountains, the eastern side of the park holds the Rangipo Desert. It gets plenty of rain, but harsh winds and poor volcanic soil keep it bare and pale, like a desert in a wet land.

If you prefer a softer day, the park offers it. Near Whakapapa Village, shorter tracks lead through beech forest and across tussock to waterfalls. In winter, Ruapehu becomes a ski mountain, with fields on its northern and southern sides. And at the village, you will see the Chateau Tongariro, a grand old hotel built in the late 1920s. It sits in the open landscape with the volcano directly behind it, a strange and handsome sight.

Wherever you walk, notice the plants. Low tussock, small alpine flowers, and tough shrubs grow in thin, ashy ground. Some of these plants exist nowhere else in the world. You may also see purple heather, which was brought here long ago and has spread too far. Rangers work hard to control it. Stay on the marked tracks. The volcanic soil is fragile, and one footprint in the wrong place can last for years.

Before you move on, take one more look around. Feel the wind on your face. Notice the colours — grey ash, gold tussock, dark rock, and the white of Ruapehu's snow. Somewhere beneath you, heat is still moving through the earth, as it has for thousands of years. A chief gave these peaks away so they would stay whole. As you walk on, you carry a small part of that promise with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check the mountain weather forecast before starting the Alpine Crossing.
Pack warm layers and rain gear, even on sunny days.
Book a shuttle for the Crossing, since it is one-way.
Respect the iwi request to avoid the sacred summits.
Start the Crossing early to finish well before dark.
