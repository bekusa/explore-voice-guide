The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of the jetty at Kerr Bay and listen. The water laps against old timber posts. A bellbird calls from the beech trees behind you, a clear, ringing note. The air smells of cold stone and damp moss. And on a still morning, the lake holds the mountains upside down, so perfectly that it is hard to say where the real ridge ends.

This is Nelson Lakes National Park, at the northern end of the Southern Alps, near the top of New Zealand's South Island. It is not the largest park in the country, and it is not the most visited. But it brings together almost everything this island is known for. You will find deep glacial lakes, dense beech forest, alpine ridges and fast, clear rivers. Most visitors arrive through the small village of Saint Arnaud, beside the lake in front of you.

The lake you are looking at is Rotoiti. Its neighbour, a short drive to the west, is Rotoroa. In the Māori language, "roto" means lake. Rotoiti is usually translated as "little lake", and Rotoroa as "long lake". The names are simple and literal, and they fit. Rotoiti feels close and contained. Rotoroa stretches away into the hills, quieter and more remote, with fewer people on its shores.

Both lakes were shaped by ice. During the last ice ages, glaciers flowed down these valleys and carved them deep. When the ice melted, the glaciers left ridges of rock and gravel behind them, called moraines. These ridges acted like natural dams. Meltwater filled the basins behind them, and the lakes were born. Look at the gentle, wooded rise near the village. That shape is part of the story of the glaciers.

There is an older story too. According to Māori tradition, the great lakes of the South Island were dug by the ancestor Rākaihautū, using a digging stick called a kō. Some versions of the tradition place Rotoiti and Rotoroa among his work. This is a story of origins, not a geological record. But it carries something true. These lakes have been named, travelled and valued for many generations, long before any park boundary was drawn.

Now for a surprise most visitors never hear about. A major crack in the Earth's crust runs close to where you stand. It is called the Alpine Fault. It marks the boundary between two tectonic plates, the Pacific and the Australian. To the northeast, the long, straight Wairau Valley follows this fault line. The mountains around you are still rising, very slowly, because these two plates keep pushing against each other. The landscape here is not finished.

Look closely at the trunks of the beech trees along the lake. Many of them are black, as if they were burned. They were not. The black coating is a sooty mould. It grows on a sweet liquid called honeydew, which tiny insects living in the bark produce. In sunlight, you may see fine threads with shining drops at their tips. That honeydew is food for tūī, bellbirds and the large forest parrot called the kākā.

That brings us to the second surprise. The forest around Lake Rotoiti is part of one of the longer-running conservation projects on mainland New Zealand. It is known as the Rotoiti Nature Recovery Project. For years, teams have trapped introduced predators such as stoats, rats and possums. These animals arrived with people and do great harm to native birds. Along the trails, you will see small wooden boxes near the ground. Those are traps, and they are part of the reason the birdsong here is so loud.

Back at the jetty, keep watching the water below the boards. Sometimes long, dark shapes move slowly in the shallows. These are longfin eels, native freshwater fish that can live for a very long time. They are a protected species and an important traditional food for Māori. Please do not feed them. Human food is not good for them. Just watch quietly, and you may see one rise towards the light.

If you want height, the park offers it quickly. The Saint Arnaud Range rises behind the village, and a track climbs through the beech forest to the bushline. Above the trees, the world changes. You find tussock grass, loose rock, and wide views over both lakes. Mount Robert, on the far side of Rotoiti, has a popular loop to its ridge. On a clear day, the views stretch far along the mountains.

For experienced trampers, the park is also a gateway to longer journeys. The Travers–Sabine Circuit follows river valleys deep into the mountains and crosses a high alpine pass. Another route leads to Lake Angelus, a small tarn high on the ridge, with a hut beside it. These are serious alpine trips. The weather here can change within an hour, and snow can fall in any season. Always check conditions with the visitor centre before heading out.

You do not need to climb to feel the place, though. Short walks along the lakeshore pass through forest so mossy that the ground feels soft underfoot. Ferns curl beside the track. Small streams run clear over grey stones. In the early morning, mist often sits on the lake and lifts slowly as the sun reaches the water. In the evening, the light turns gold on the peaks while the shore falls into shadow.

One more practical note, in the spirit of honesty. This park is home to sandflies, small biting insects that gather near the water. They are most active when the air is still. Insect repellent and long sleeves help a great deal. Locals tend to treat them as part of the price of a quiet lakeside evening, and after a while, so will you.

Before you leave, take one last look across Rotoiti. Notice the line where the dark beech forest meets the pale rock above. Notice the stillness of the water, and the sound of birds that are here partly because people chose to protect them. As you walk back towards the village, listen for the bellbird again. It is probably still calling, somewhere in the trees just behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Kerr Bay jetty early for still morning reflections.
Bring insect repellent for sandflies near the lakeshore.
Check weather at the visitor centre before alpine walks.
Pack warm layers, since snow can fall in any season.
Watch the eels quietly, but never feed them.
