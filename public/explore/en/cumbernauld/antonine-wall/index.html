The guide

The story

Start with the ground under your feet. It is soft, uneven, and green, and it rises and falls in a long, deliberate line. Listen for the wind moving through the grass and the trees. Nothing here looks like a wall at first. There are no towers or battlements. And yet you are standing beside the most northerly frontier the Roman Empire ever built across Britain. The shape in the turf is the clue. Every hump and hollow along this line was made by people.

The wall takes its name from the emperor Antoninus Pius. He came to power in the second century, after the reign of Hadrian. Hadrian had already built his own famous wall far to the south, in what is now northern England. Antoninus decided to push the frontier further north. Around the early 140s, Roman soldiers began work on a new barrier. It ran across the narrow waist of Scotland, between the Firth of Forth in the east and the River Clyde in the west.

That waist matters. Look at a map of Scotland and you will see the two great estuaries reach towards each other here. The gap between them is about sixty kilometres, the shortest crossing anywhere in the country. The Romans were practical engineers. They chose the line that needed the least building and gave them water access at both ends. Cumbernauld sits close to the middle of that line, on high ground with long views to the north.

Here is the first surprise. Unlike Hadrian's Wall, this frontier was not mainly built of stone. The soldiers laid a stone base, and on top of it they stacked blocks of cut turf. The turf rampart rose well above head height, and it probably carried a timber walkway along the top. Over the centuries the turf slumped and softened. That is why the wall today looks like a long grassy bank, not a line of masonry.

Now turn and look north. In front of the rampart the Romans dug a huge ditch. In many places it was wider than a two-lane road and deep enough to hide a standing man. The earth from the ditch was thrown out on the far side, making an extra mound. Anyone approaching from the north had to cross that mound, drop into the ditch, and then climb towards the rampart. Along much of this stretch, the ditch is still the clearest feature you can see.

Behind the wall, to the south, ran a road. Historians call it the Military Way. It let soldiers and supplies move quickly between the forts. Those forts were spread along the whole line, more than a dozen of them, with smaller fortlets in between. Near Cumbernauld there were forts at places such as Westerwood and Castlecary. Castlecary is unusual, because its defences were built in stone rather than turf. Today, little of these forts shows above ground, but their outlines have been traced by archaeologists.

Who actually built all this? The answer is carved in stone. Soldiers from three Roman legions, the Second, the Sixth, and the Twentieth, did the heavy work. Each team finished a measured length of wall, and then set up a carved slab to record it. These are known as distance slabs. They show the name of the legion, the length completed, and often scenes of Roman victory. Nothing quite like them survives from any other Roman frontier. Many of the best examples are now in the Hunterian Museum in Glasgow.

Think about the life of a soldier posted here. Many came from far across the empire. They faced rain, cold winds, and short winter days. They cut turf, dug ditches, and stood watch over the hills to the north. Archaeologists have found traces of their daily life along the line: pottery, coins, shoes, tools, and the remains of bathhouses. A bath was one of the comforts of home that the Romans carried with them, even to the edge of their world.

And here is the second surprise. All that effort did not last long. Within roughly twenty years, the Romans pulled back to Hadrian's Wall. The reasons are still debated. Perhaps the new frontier was too hard to hold. Perhaps soldiers were needed elsewhere. There was a short return in the early third century, under the emperor Septimius Severus, but it did not become permanent. So this long line in the grass marks a frontier that was held for barely one generation.

After the Romans left, the wall slowly faded into the landscape. Farmers ploughed across it. Local people reused its stones in buildings and field walls. For centuries, many locals called parts of it Grim's Dyke, a name that suggests they had forgotten who built it. Antiquarians began to study the line seriously in later centuries. Today, it is protected, and since 2008 it has been part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, together with Hadrian's Wall and other Roman frontiers in Europe.

Walking here today is a quiet experience. You share the path with dog walkers, runners, and families. Some stretches pass through woodland, others across open fields or along the edges of modern housing. The town of Cumbernauld grew up in the twentieth century, but the Roman line is older than any building around it by many centuries. That contrast is part of the pleasure. Modern roads and rooftops sit only a short walk from a frontier that is nearly two thousand years old.

If you have time, follow the line east or west. Further along, at sites such as Croy Hill and Bar Hill to the west, or Rough Castle to the east, the remains are easier to read. In some places you can see the rampart, the ditch, and the outline of a fort together. On clear days the high points give wide views across the Kelvin valley to the hills beyond. From up there, you can understand why the Romans chose this ground.

Before you move on, look again at the bank and ditch in front of you. Notice how straight the line runs, and how it follows the rise of the land. Every metre of it was shaped by hand, with spades and baskets, by men a long way from home. The turf is quiet now. Grass and moss cover the work. But as you walk away, you may find yourself reading the landscape differently... noticing every long, low ridge, and wondering who made it.

Look closer

What to look for

The long grassy bank of the slumped turf rampart.
The deep ditch running along the northern side.
The low mound of spoil beyond the ditch.
Traces of the Military Way road south of the rampart.
Long views north from the higher stretches of the line.
Before you go

Practical tips

Wear waterproof boots, since the ditch and banks are often muddy.
Visit Croy Hill or Bar Hill for clearer remains.
See the distance slabs at the Hunterian Museum in Glasgow.
Bring a layer, as wind crosses the high ground quickly.
Walk the ditch edge carefully after rain.
