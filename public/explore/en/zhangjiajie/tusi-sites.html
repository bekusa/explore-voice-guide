The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below, water is running over stone. The air is damp and green, and the ground underfoot is not smooth pavement but rounded river pebbles, pressed into the earth in long, patient lines. This is the quiet of the Wuling Mountains, the same folded, forested country that gives Zhangjiajie its famous sandstone pillars. Here, though, the story is not written in rock towers. It is written in walls, terraces and paths left behind by people who governed these valleys for centuries.

The Tusi Sites are a group of three places in the mountains of central and southwest China. Together they were added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2015. The site closest to Zhangjiajie is Laosicheng, in Yongshun County in western Hunan, a drive from the city through river valleys and small towns. The other two are Tangya in Hubei and Hailongtun in Guizhou. Each one is a ruin. Each one is a piece of the same unusual system of rule.

So what does the word Tusi mean? Roughly, it means a native chieftain, a local leader appointed by the imperial court. From around the Yuan dynasty, the era of Mongol rule over China, and through the Ming and Qing dynasties, emperors faced a problem. These mountains were steep, remote and home to many peoples who were not Han Chinese. Sending outside officials was slow and costly. Instead, the court gave titles to local ruling families and let them govern their own lands.

The arrangement was a bargain. The chieftain family kept its power, its customs and its hereditary line. In return, it paid tribute, kept order and sent soldiers when the empire asked. The court gained loyalty without conquest. The local family gained recognition and protection. For a long stretch of history, this balance held the borderlands together. The sites you are visiting are the physical remains of that bargain... the seats of power where it was lived day by day.

At Laosicheng, the ruling family was the Peng clan, and the people they led were largely Tujia. The Tujia are one of China's recognised ethnic groups, and many still live in these mountains today. The name Laosicheng simply means something like old ruler's city. For generations, this riverside settlement was the political heart of a chieftain domain. It held residences, halls, workshops and streets, all wrapped by the hills and the Lingxi River.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Very little of what you see is tall. There are no grand palaces left standing. What survives is mostly at ground level — wall foundations, stone drains, gateways, terraces and those pebble-paved lanes. Archaeologists have uncovered much of the layout by excavation. So the experience is less like walking through a palace and more like reading a floor plan written in stone. You have to imagine the timber buildings that once rose above it.

That timber matters. Traditional buildings in this region were often wooden, raised on stone bases, with dark tiled roofs. Wood burns, rots and is replaced. Stone stays. This is why the pebbled paths and walls carry the memory of the place, while the upper storeys have vanished. As you walk, notice how the stones are laid. Some lanes show careful patterns. Others show repairs, where newer stones fill old gaps. The ground itself shows centuries of use.

Now think about the shape of the landscape. These sites were not built on open plains. They were set into valleys, beside rivers, or on high ridges that were hard to reach. That choice was about power and defence. A chieftain needed water, farmland and a view of anyone approaching. The mountains gave all three. Standing here, look at the slopes around you and picture the terraced fields, the footpaths and the watchful eyes that once covered them.

The second surprise comes from the Guizhou site, Hailongtun. Unlike Laosicheng, it is a mountaintop fortress, ringed by walls and gates. It belonged to the Yang family, rulers of a domain called Bozhou. Their story ended in violence. Around 1600, the chieftain rebelled against the Ming court, and imperial armies besieged and took the fortress. It is a reminder that the Tusi bargain could break. When a local ruler grew too strong or too defiant, the empire answered with force.

In the end, the whole system faded. During the Qing dynasty, especially in the early eighteenth century, the court pushed a reform that replaced many hereditary chieftains with appointed officials. In Chinese this policy is known as gaitu guiliu, which roughly means changing native rule into regular rule. Over time, the old seats of power lost their purpose. People moved, buildings decayed, and places like Laosicheng became quiet villages and fields above buried foundations.

Yet the culture of these valleys did not disappear with the chieftains. In the wider region around Zhangjiajie and Xiangxi, Tujia traditions are still visible. You may see woven brocade with bold geometric patterns, sold in markets and towns. You may hear about the hand-waving dance, a group dance performed at festivals. You may see wooden stilted houses clinging to hillsides. These living traditions help explain why UNESCO valued the sites. They connect the ruins to people who still live nearby.

Why does this matter beyond China? The Tusi Sites show one way a large state tried to rule diverse peoples without erasing them. It was not equality, and it was not always peaceful. But it was a long experiment in shared authority, lasting many centuries. Historians around the world study such systems, because empires everywhere faced the same question. How do you hold distant mountains and different cultures together? Here, the answer was partly written in these stones.

As a visitor, take your time. The site rewards slow walking rather than quick photographs. Stop at the edge of a terrace and look down toward the river. Follow a drain channel with your eyes and imagine rain running through it hundreds of years ago. Sit on a low wall, if it is permitted, and let the sounds settle around you. Birds, water, a distant motorbike, a farmer's voice. The place is small in height but wide in time.

Before you leave, look once more at the pebbles under your feet. Each one was carried from the river, set by hand and walked on by chieftains, servants, soldiers and farmers. Today it is your turn. Notice the moss in the cracks, the shine of the stones after rain, the green ridges closing the view. When you return to Zhangjiajie and its stone pillars, remember these quieter stones too. They carry a human story that the mountains kept safe.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan a full day trip from Zhangjiajie to reach Laosicheng.
Wear shoes with good grip for damp pebble paths.
Bring rain gear because mountain weather changes quickly.
Visit after Laosicheng's small exhibition to understand the ruins better.
Combine the trip with a Tujia village or local market.
