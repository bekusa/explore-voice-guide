The guide

The story

Feel the heat rise off the ground first. The desert floor here is pale and gritty, and the light is hard and white for most of the day. A dry wind moves across the plain and carries fine dust against your skin. Out of this flat, sunlit emptiness rise stones cut by human hands more than two thousand years ago. You are standing in the old heartland of Saba, the kingdom many people know by another name — Sheba.

This is not a single monument. It is a group of places spread across the Marib area and the nearby site of Sirwah. Together they include the remains of a great dam, temples to an ancient god, and the traces of walled cities. UNESCO added these landmarks to its World Heritage List in 2023. At the same time, it placed them on its list of heritage in danger. That pairing tells you something important about this place before you see a single stone.

So who were the Sabaeans? They were one of several kingdoms that grew up in southern Arabia during the first millennium before Christ. Their wealth came from two things — water and incense. Frankincense and myrrh are fragrant tree resins that people burned in temples across the ancient world. Caravans carried them north from this corner of Arabia toward the Mediterranean. Marib sat close to that route, and it grew rich on the trade.

But wealth needs food, and food here needs water. Marib lies at the edge of a desert, where rain is rare. Several times a year, though, storms in the mountains to the west send sudden floods rushing down a valley called the Wadi Dhana. The Sabaeans learned to catch these floods. They did not try to store them in a lake. Instead, they built a long earthen barrier and stone sluices to turn the floodwater aside and spread it across the fields.

This is the great Marib Dam, and its surviving parts are one of the most impressive engineering works of the ancient world. Look for the stone structures at the northern and southern ends. These are the sluice works, built from large, carefully dressed blocks fitted closely together. Water passed through them into canals and then out across two wide irrigated areas. In its day, this system fed oases of fields and orchards in the middle of the desert.

Here is the first surprise. The dam was not built once and then left alone. It was repaired, raised, and rebuilt over many centuries. Inscriptions record major repairs carried out by later rulers. Then, around the late sixth century of the common era, the dam failed for the last time and was not restored. The collapse is remembered in the Quran as the Flood of al-Arim, in a chapter that carries the name Saba. For many people, that ending became a lesson about the fall of a proud civilization.

Now turn your attention to the temples. The main god of the Sabaeans was Almaqah, often linked with the moon, although scholars still debate his exact nature. The largest sanctuary to him near Marib is the Awwam Temple. Local people call it Mahram Bilqis, the sanctuary of Bilqis. Bilqis is the name Arabic tradition gives to the Queen of Sheba. This is a tradition and a local name, not proof that she worshipped here.

The Awwam Temple is unusual in shape. Its great outer wall forms a long oval, which is rare for temples in this region. Near the entrance stand tall square stone pillars, some still upright, partly buried by drifting sand over the centuries. Archaeologists have found many inscriptions here, cut in the old South Arabian script. These texts recorded offerings, building works, and the deeds of rulers, and they are a major source for Sabaean history.

A short distance away stands the Barran Temple. Locals call it Arsh Bilqis, the throne of Bilqis. Its most famous feature is a row of tall, slender stone pillars rising straight out of the plain. Five stand complete, and a sixth is broken. Each one is cut from a single piece of stone. In photographs they look delicate. In person, against that hard blue sky, they feel almost impossibly tall and thin.

The second surprise is in the writing itself. The South Arabian alphabet, sometimes called Musnad, has crisp, angular letters that look almost modern. Sabaean stone carvers cut them with great precision, often in straight, even lines. If you see inscribed blocks, look closely at how sharp the edges are. Some of these letters have kept their clean outlines for well over two thousand years in a harsh desert climate.

To the west lies Sirwah, the other part of this World Heritage group. It was an important Sabaean town with its own large temple to Almaqah. German archaeologists have worked there and studied a long inscription that records the military campaigns of an early Sabaean ruler. Taken together, Marib and Sirwah show how this kingdom organised its religion, its water, and its political power across a wide landscape.

All of this is fragile. Yemen has gone through years of conflict, and the Marib area has seen fighting. Some historic structures, including parts of the old town and the area around the dam, have suffered damage. Sand, wind, and a lack of protection add slow pressure every year. This is why UNESCO listed these places as endangered on the same day it recognised their value. The stones have survived empires, floods, and time. Their future now depends on care.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to the wind moving across the open ground, and notice how quiet it is. Then look at the cut stone nearest to you — the tool marks, the tight joints, the letters if you can find them. Someone shaped that block by hand, for a god, for a harvest, for a kingdom that fed itself from sudden floods. As you walk away, look back once at the pillars against the sky.
