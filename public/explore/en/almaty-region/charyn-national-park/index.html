The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the ground. Flat, dry steppe runs out in every direction, pale and dusty under a wide sky. Then, almost without warning, the earth simply drops away. A deep gash of red, orange and ochre stone opens at your feet. A dry wind moves through the scrub, and somewhere far below, too faint to hear at first, a river is running.

This is Charyn National Park, in the Almaty Region of southeastern Kazakhstan. It lies roughly two hundred kilometres east of the city of Almaty, not far from the border with China. The heart of the park is Charyn Canyon, a long, winding gorge cut by the Charyn River. Many travellers call it a smaller cousin of the Grand Canyon in the United States. That comparison helps, but the place has its own character, and it rewards a slow look.

Start with the rock itself. The walls are sedimentary stone, built up layer by layer from sand, clay and gravel over millions of years. Look closely and you can see the bands — some rust red, some cream, some almost purple in shadow. Each band marks a different period of deposit. Wind, frost and water then went to work on these soft layers. What they left behind is the shape you see today.

The river is the sculptor here. The Charyn rises in the Tian Shan mountains to the south, the great range that runs across Central Asia. It carries snowmelt and rain down through the steppe and eventually joins the Ili River. Over a very long time, the water cut down through the stone. Seasonal floods and fierce temperature swings broke the walls into towers, ledges and narrow side gullies.

The most visited section is called the Valley of Castles. The name makes sense the moment you see it. Tall columns of stone stand along both sides of a dry channel, with rounded tops, sharp ridges and deep vertical grooves. With a little imagination, they look like turrets, battlements and fortress walls. A trail leads down from the rim through this corridor of stone toward the river, and most visitors walk it.

As you walk, the temperature changes. Up on the rim, the steppe wind is open and constant. Down between the walls, the air can feel still and hot, especially in summer, when the stone holds the sun's heat. Then, near the bottom, you may feel a cooler breath coming off the water. The sound changes too. The wind drops away, footsteps echo, and finally the river becomes a steady rush.

Here is something many visitors miss. This canyon is not only about stone. Along parts of the Charyn River grows a rare grove of Sogdian ash trees. Scientists describe these trees as a relic — a survivor from a much warmer and wetter age in this region's distant past. When the climate dried out, most of these forests disappeared. In the sheltered, watered floor of the canyon, a remnant held on.

That grove is one of the main reasons the area is protected. Kazakhstan created the national park in 2004 to guard the canyon, the river and the ash forest together. The protected land covers far more than the famous viewpoints. It includes stretches of steppe, desert plains and other canyon sections that few tourists ever reach. So the Valley of Castles is only a small window into a much larger landscape.

The second surprise is the colour. The canyon does not look the same twice in one day. In the flat light of midday, the rock can seem dusty and pale, almost washed out. In the early morning and late afternoon, the low sun turns the walls deep red and gold, and long shadows pick out every groove and ledge. If you can, stay long enough to see that shift. It changes the whole mood of the place.

Life here is tough, but it is present. Look along the rim and you will see low, hardy shrubs and grasses adapted to heat, cold and very little rain. Birds of prey often circle above the gorge, riding the warm air that rises from the stone. Small lizards dart across the path on hot days. Most animals stay hidden in the heat of the afternoon, so quiet watching at the edges of the day gives the best chances.

For people, this has long been a land of passage. The wider region was home to nomadic herders for centuries, moving livestock between summer and winter pastures. Today, the visitor experience is simple. There are viewpoints along the rim, the main trail down through the Valley of Castles, and a small rest area near the river where people pause before the climb back. Respect the edges. The soft rock can crumble, and there are no rails along much of the rim.

Take a moment, before you leave, to stand at one of the high points. Look along the line of the gorge as it bends away into the distance. You are looking at a story told very slowly — layers laid down over millions of years, then carved out by one patient river. The same water you heard below is still at work, still cutting, a little each season.

As you walk back toward the steppe, notice how quickly the canyon disappears behind you. A few steps from the edge, the land looks flat again, as if nothing were there at all. The dust settles on your shoes. The wind picks up. And somewhere behind you, out of sight, the red walls are catching the light and the river is still running.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Carry plenty of water for the climb back up.
Wear sturdy shoes with grip for loose, sandy trails.
Stay back from unfenced edges where the rock crumbles.
Wait for late afternoon light to see the reddest walls.
