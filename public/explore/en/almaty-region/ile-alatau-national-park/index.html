The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere below you, meltwater is running over stone. The air carries the sharp, clean smell of spruce resin, and it is noticeably cooler than the city you left behind. Look up, and the ridges climb in layers, from dark forest to bare rock to white snow. This is Ile-Alatau National Park, and Almaty sits right at its feet.

The park stretches along the northern slopes of a mountain range called the Trans-Ili Alatau. This range is the northernmost arm of the Tian Shan, the great mountain system that runs across Central Asia into western China. The name Tian Shan is usually translated as the Heavenly Mountains. Kazakhstan created the national park in 1996 to protect these slopes, their forests, and the water that flows down from them.

Here is the first surprise. Few major cities sit this close to true high mountains. From central Almaty, a short drive takes you up through foothills and into gorges. Within an hour, you can stand among peaks with permanent snow. On clear mornings, the whole range rises behind the city's apartment blocks like a wall. Almaty has always lived in the shadow of these mountains, and it depends on them.

The park is not one single place. It is a chain of gorges, each cut by its own river. The water runs north, out of the mountains and down toward the steppe. Every gorge has its own character. Some are wide and grassy, with picnic spots and small cafés near the bottom. Others are narrow, steep, and quiet, with trails that climb for hours. Wherever you entered today, you are standing in one of these river valleys.

Now look at the trees around you. The tall, dark, narrow conifers are Tian Shan spruce, also called Schrenk's spruce. Their slim shape is no accident. A narrow crown sheds heavy snow before the weight can break the branches. These spruces cover the steep, shaded slopes and give the mountains their deep green colour. On sunny slopes facing south, you will often see open grassland and shrubs instead.

The second surprise grows lower down, in the foothills. This region is home to wild apple trees, a species called Sievers apple. Scientists consider these forests a key ancestor of the apples sold in markets all over the world. The city's old name, Alma-Ata, is often linked to the Kazakh word for apple. Historians debate the exact meaning, but locals are proud of the connection. In autumn, wild apples still fall in some foothill groves.

The mountains are also home to animals that most visitors never see. The snow leopard lives in the high, rocky zones of the park. It is rare, shy, and almost impossible to spot. Siberian ibex move across the cliffs, and brown bears live in the remote valleys. Closer to the trails, you are more likely to hear birds or see small rodents near the rocks. Keep your voice low, and the mountain feels much more alive.

Water shapes everything here. High above the forest, glaciers feed the rivers that run down through the gorges. One of them, the Tuyuksu Glacier, has been studied by scientists for many decades. It is one of the most closely watched glaciers in Central Asia. The records show a clear pattern. Like many glaciers around the world, the ice here has been shrinking, and that matters for a city that drinks this water.

That water can also be dangerous. Heavy rain or a burst glacial lake can send mudflows down the gorges. In Kazakh and Russian, these are called sel. A mudflow is a fast river of mud, boulders, and broken trees. The large dam above the Medeu skating rink was built during the Soviet era to protect Almaty from exactly this threat. When you see concrete barriers in a valley, they are often part of this defence system.

If you visit Big Almaty Lake, you will notice its colour first. The water is a milky turquoise, coloured by fine rock dust carried down by glacial streams. The lake sits high in the mountains, surrounded by steep slopes and sharp peaks. It is also part of the city's water supply. That is why swimming is not allowed. The colour changes through the day and through the seasons, so no two visits look quite the same.

The park also has a scientific side that many people miss. Near Big Almaty Lake stands an astronomical observatory, placed here for the thin, clear mountain air. Further up the road, researchers have studied cosmic rays, the particles that arrive from deep space. It is a strange contrast. Shepherds and hikers share these slopes with telescopes and research stations. The mountains have long attracted both people who work the land and people who study the sky.

The weather here changes fast. A warm, sunny morning in the city can become a cold, windy afternoon at altitude. Clouds can fill a gorge in minutes and then lift again just as quickly. Higher up, the air is thinner, so even a gentle climb can leave you short of breath. Walk slowly, drink water, and pay attention to the sky. The mountain sets the pace here, not the visitor.

Before you move on, take one more look around. Notice the layers again: the river at the bottom, the spruce forest on the slopes, the bare grey rock, and the snow at the top. Each layer feeds the one below it. The water rushing past you started as snow high above, and much of it will end up in the taps of Almaty. As you walk back down, listen for the river. It will follow you most of the way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a warm layer even on hot city days.
Start early to avoid afternoon clouds in the gorges.
Walk slowly at altitude to avoid breathlessness.
Expect a park checkpoint at many gorge entrances.
Carry water and snacks for longer trail walks.
