The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves through the oak and beech on the slopes. Somewhere below, a stream runs over stones. The air here smells of damp earth and woodsmoke, and the light falls softly across the green folds of the Cárdenas valley. This quiet corner of La Rioja, in northern Spain, holds two monasteries, one high on the hillside and one on the valley floor. Together they carry one of the most surprising stories in Europe: the story of how a language first appeared in writing.

The two monasteries have simple names. Suso means "upper", and Yuso means "lower". Suso clings to the mountainside, small and old, partly cut into the living rock. Yuso spreads across the valley below, large and grand, with long stone wings and a tall tower. In 1997, UNESCO added both monasteries to its World Heritage List. The reason was not only their buildings. It was also what happened inside them, in quiet rooms where monks bent over manuscripts by candlelight.

The story begins with one man. His name was Emilian, or Millán in Spanish. He lived in the sixth century, when much of the Iberian Peninsula was ruled by the Visigoths, a Germanic people who settled here after the fall of Rome. According to tradition, Millán was a shepherd who turned to a life of prayer. He lived as a hermit in the caves on this hillside. Followers gathered around him, and a small religious community slowly grew.

Tradition says that Millán lived to a remarkable age, well past one hundred years. Treat that number with some caution — it comes from a life of the saint written by a bishop who admired him. What is clear is that his reputation spread far beyond this valley. After his death, people came to pray at his tomb. The caves where he lived became the heart of the upper monastery, and pilgrims kept climbing this slope for centuries.

If you visit Suso, you will notice how the building seems to grow out of the hill. Parts of the church are carved directly into the rock, and the caves still form small chapels. Look at the arches. Some of them have a rounded shape that pinches inward at the base, like a horseshoe. This style is often called Mozarabic, which refers to Christians who lived under Muslim rule and carried its artistic influences north. Later builders added Romanesque work, the solid round-arched style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries.

Now comes the first surprise. In this monastery, or in the library connected to it, a monk once read a Latin text and found some words difficult. So he wrote small notes in the margins and between the lines, explaining the Latin in simpler words. These notes are called the Glosas Emilianenses, the Emilian glosses. Some of them are written in an early form of the Romance speech that would later become Spanish. Others are written in Basque. They are among the oldest known written traces of both.

Think about that for a moment. A student or teacher, probably in the tenth or eleventh century, simply wanted to understand a hard passage. He had no idea that his quick notes would become famous. Today, Spanish speakers often call this valley the cradle of their language. Scholars still debate the exact dating and the precise place of writing. The original codex is no longer here. It is kept in Madrid, at the Royal Academy of History, but the story stays rooted in this valley.

The second surprise is a poet. In the thirteenth century, a cleric named Gonzalo de Berceo was connected with Suso. He came from the nearby village of Berceo, and he wrote long religious poems in the Castilian language, not in Latin. He is the first poet in Castilian whose name we know. Among his works is a life of Saint Millán himself. So the same hillside gave Spanish both some of its first written words and its first named poet.

Why are there two monasteries? Here, history and legend mix. A popular tale says that in the eleventh century, a king of Navarre wanted to move the saint's remains to another place. The relics were loaded onto a cart pulled by oxen. When the cart reached the valley floor, the oxen stopped and refused to move. The monks took this as a sign, and they built a new monastery on that very spot. That is the legend. Historians see a more practical story of growth, space, and royal support.

The lower monastery of Yuso that you see today is mostly much later than its medieval beginnings. Most of the present building dates from the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries. Its size and order have earned it a nickname: the Escorial of La Rioja, after the vast royal monastery near Madrid. Notice the long, regular facades, the heavy stone, the sense of discipline in every line. This is Renaissance and Baroque architecture, built to express power and permanence.

Inside Yuso, the visit usually leads through a church, cloisters, and rooms with paintings and carved wood. One of the most memorable spaces is the library and archive. Here you may see enormous choir books, so large that several monks could read the music from one book at the same time. Their pages are made of parchment, which is prepared animal skin. Monks still live in Yuso today. They belong to the Augustinian Recollects, a religious order that has cared for the monastery since the late nineteenth century.

A few practical thoughts for your visit. Both monasteries are usually seen with a guide, and the visits run at set times. The upper monastery has limited space, and access is often by a short shuttle from below, so it is wise to book ahead. Bring a light jacket, because the valley can feel cool even in summer. Comfortable shoes help on the old stone floors. And give yourself time to sit outside. The landscape here is part of the experience.

Before you leave, look up at the hillside once more. Somewhere above the trees is Suso, small and quiet, with its caves and horseshoe arches. Below you is Yuso, with its great walls and its living community of monks. Between them runs about fifteen centuries of prayer, study, and ordinary human effort. As you walk back toward the road, listen again to the stream and the wind. They sound much the same as they did when a monk first wrote a few simple words in the margin of a book.
