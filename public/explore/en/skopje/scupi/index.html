The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through the tall grass, and somewhere nearby the Vardar River runs low and steady. Under your feet the ground is uneven: packed earth, loose gravel, and here and there the hard edge of a cut stone block. This quiet field on the northwestern edge of Skopje does not look like a city. But for several centuries, it was one. You are standing among the remains of Scupi, the Roman town that came long before modern Skopje.

Take a moment to look around before the history begins. To one side, the slopes rise gently toward low hills. In the other direction, the apartment blocks and roads of the modern capital sit on the horizon. Between them lie foundations, broken columns, and the outlines of walls, many only knee-high. The scale is easy to miss at first. Scupi spread across a wide area, and much of it still lies under the soil around you, not yet excavated.

The story starts before Rome. This area was home to the Dardanians, a Balkan people who held the upper Vardar valley in the centuries before the Romans arrived. Their settlement here gave the place its name. Scholars still debate the exact origin of the word. What is clear is that the Romans did not choose an empty spot. They took over a site that already had a long history and a good position on a river crossing.

That position mattered. The Vardar valley is a natural corridor through the mountains. It links the Aegean coast in the south with the Danube lands in the north. Armies, traders, and messengers all used this route. A town here could watch the road, collect taxes, and supply soldiers. So when Rome brought the central Balkans under control, Scupi became an important outpost on one of the main roads through the peninsula.

In the late first century, the town received the status of a Roman colony. Its official name was Colonia Flavia Scupinorum. The word Flavia refers to the Flavian family of emperors, who ruled Rome in that period. A colony was more than a garrison. It was usually settled by retired soldiers, who received land in return for their service. Those veterans brought Latin, Roman law, and Roman habits of building with them. Inscriptions found here are mostly in Latin, which tells you how Roman this town became.

Now imagine the town at its peak. Streets were laid out in a grid. There were public baths, where people washed, exercised, and gossiped. There were temples, workshops, houses with painted walls, and a forum for business and politics. Water came in through channels, and drains carried waste away. Most of this is now only foundations. But when you trace a line of stones with your eyes, you are following a wall that once held up a roof.

Here is the first detail that surprises many visitors. Scupi had a theatre. It was built in the second century, and archaeologists have uncovered its curved seating area. Rows of spectators once sat here to watch plays, music, and public ceremonies. If you find the theatre area, stand at the bottom of the curve and speak in a normal voice. Even in its ruined state, the shape still shows how Roman builders thought carefully about sound.

Over time, the empire changed, and Scupi changed with it. In late antiquity, the town became the capital of the province of Dardania. Christianity spread through the region, and the town gained churches. Excavations here have found the remains of an early Christian basilica, a long hall-shaped church with a central aisle. Look for the long rectangular foundations. They mark a time when the temples of the old gods gave way to a new faith.

The end came suddenly. In 518, a powerful earthquake struck the region and destroyed Scupi. Ancient writers recorded the disaster, and the damage is still visible in the ruins. Walls collapsed, and buildings were not rebuilt in the same way. After the earthquake, the population moved, and life gradually shifted to the area where Skopje stands today, across the river near the fortress hill. So, in a real sense, the modern city is the child of this broken one.

Here is the second surprise. The name did not die with the town. Scupi slowly became Skopje. The sound of the old Latin and Dardanian name still lives in the name of the capital you are visiting. Every time someone in Skopje says the name of their city, they repeat a word that was spoken in this field almost two thousand years ago. Few capitals carry their origins so openly.

For centuries after the earthquake, the site was largely farmland. Locals ploughed over stones and sometimes found coins, pottery, or carved fragments. Systematic archaeological work began in the twentieth century, and it continues in seasons today. Finds from the site include sculptures, inscriptions, tombs, and everyday objects. Many of the best pieces are now kept in museums in Skopje. The field you see is only part of the picture. The rest sits in glass cases across town.

As you walk, pay attention to the materials. You will see rough local stone, neatly cut blocks, and thin layers of Roman brick set into the walls. Builders often mixed stone and brick in alternating bands, which made walls stronger and easier to level. In some places, newer stone has been added to protect or stabilise the ruins. Look closely and you can often tell the old work from the modern repair by colour and texture.

The site rarely feels crowded. On many days, you may share it with only a few other visitors, a guard, or archaeologists working under the sun. That quiet is part of the experience. There is no loud soundtrack here, only birds, wind, and traffic far away. It is easy to stand still for a few minutes and picture carts on the road, voices from the baths, and the noise of a busy market.

Before you leave, look once more across the field toward the modern city. One town ended here in a single morning of shaking ground. Another grew up a short distance away and kept its name. The stones near your feet are what remains of that first chapter. As you walk back to the road, notice the edges of buried walls in the grass. Scupi is still here, just below the surface.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and loose gravel.
Bring water and a hat, as shade is limited.
Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Pair your visit with Skopje's archaeological collections in town.
Take a taxi, since the site is outside the centre.
