The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Up here on the slopes of Mount Vodno, the noise of Skopje drops away. You hear wind in the pines, perhaps a dog in the village below, and gravel shifting under your shoes. The air is cooler than in the city, and it smells of resin and dry grass. In front of you stands a small church of brick and stone, with five modest domes. It does not look like much from outside... but inside is one of the most important painted rooms in medieval Europe.

This is the Church of Saint Panteleimon, in the village of Gorno Nerezi. An inscription records that it was built in 1164. That makes it more than eight and a half centuries old. The church was the heart of a small monastery. Today it still sits inside a quiet walled compound, with later residential buildings around it. From the terrace, the whole valley of the Vardar River opens out below, and the city looks surprisingly far away.

The man who paid for this church was no local landowner. He was Alexios Angelos Komnenos, a member of the ruling family of the Byzantine Empire. The Byzantine Empire was the eastern Roman state, governed from Constantinople, the city we now call Istanbul. Alexios was a grandson of the emperor Alexios the First Komnenos. So a prince from the imperial capital chose this remote hillside for his foundation. That choice explains much of what you are about to see.

Look at the building itself before you go in. The plan is what historians call a cross-in-square. Imagine a square, with a cross shape inside it, and a dome over the centre where the arms meet. Here there are four smaller domes as well, one over each corner. The walls mix stone blocks with bands of thin red brick. This alternating pattern was a common Byzantine way of building. It is practical, and it also gives the walls a warm, striped texture in the afternoon light.

Now step inside, and give your eyes time to adjust. The interior is dim, and the walls are covered with painted figures. These are frescoes. A fresco is painted onto wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall as it dries. The painters worked fast, section by section. Their names are not recorded. Most scholars believe they came from Constantinople, brought here by the patron. The quality of the work points to artists trained at the highest level of the empire.

Here is the first surprise. Not everything you see is from 1164. A strong earthquake in the sixteenth century damaged the upper parts of the church, including the domes. Those areas were repainted afterwards, in a later and simpler style. The lower walls, however, kept much of the original twelfth-century painting. So as you look around, you are really looking at two different centuries, side by side. Try to find where the older, finer brushwork ends and the later work begins.

The most famous scene here is the Lamentation. It shows the moment after Christ has been taken down from the cross. His mother, the Virgin Mary, holds his body and presses her face against his. Her expression is raw grief. Saint John bends to hold Christ's hand. Other figures gather around in sorrow. Earlier Byzantine art often showed holy figures as calm and distant. Here, the painter shows a mother who has lost her son, and nothing else matters to her.

This is why art historians travel to Nerezi. The emotion in these faces is unusual for its time. The bodies have weight. The gestures are human. Many scholars see this church as one of the clearest examples of a new, more expressive style in late Byzantine painting. Some have argued that this kind of feeling later influenced Italian painters, more than a century before Giotto. Whether you accept that link or not, the scene is easy to feel without any explanation.

Near the Lamentation, look for the Deposition, the scene where Christ's body is lowered from the cross. Watch how the figures strain under the weight. Notice the folds of their robes. The painters used fine lines to show cloth moving with the body underneath. Look also at the faces of the standing saints on the lower walls. Each one has a slightly different look — some stern, some gentle, some tired. They feel less like symbols and more like individual people.

The church is dedicated to Saint Panteleimon. In Christian tradition, he was a doctor who lived in the late Roman period and died as a martyr for his faith. Tradition says he treated the sick without asking for payment. For that reason, he became a patron of physicians and of healing. Near the altar area, look for his image set inside a carved stone frame. The stone screen in front of the altar is another detail that many visitors walk past too quickly.

This stone screen is called a templon. It separated the sanctuary, where the priests performed the liturgy, from the space where ordinary worshippers stood. Later Orthodox churches usually filled this space with a tall wall of painted wooden icons. Here, you can still see the older arrangement in carved stone. The marble carving is delicate, with plant shapes and fine borders. It is a small survivor, and it helps you picture how the church looked during services in the twelfth century.

The frescoes did not reach us by accident. Over the centuries, layers of later paint, soot from candles, and damage from damp covered much of the original work. Twentieth-century conservators carefully cleaned and studied the walls. Their work made the twelfth-century painting visible again. This is also why the church asks visitors to be gentle. Flash light, warm breath, and crowds all affect old plaster. Speak quietly, keep your hands away from the walls, and the paintings will last for future visitors too.

Before you leave, walk back outside and stand on the terrace for a while. Turn around and look at the church once more. Now that you know what is inside, those plain brick walls feel different. Then look down at Skopje, spread across the valley. A prince from Constantinople once looked at almost the same view. As you walk away, listen again for the wind in the pines... and carry that grieving mother's face with you, down the hill.
