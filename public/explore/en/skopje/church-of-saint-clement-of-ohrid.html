The guide

The story

Pause for a moment and look up. Above you, curved roofs rise one over another, like a cluster of shallow domes resting on a ring of arches. The traffic of Skopje hums nearby, but this building has its own slow rhythm. Its surfaces are smooth and pale, and the light slides across them as the clouds move. This is the Church of Saint Clement of Ohrid, the cathedral church of the Macedonian Orthodox Church.

At first glance, it may not look like the churches you expect in the Balkans. There are no small medieval walls of brick and stone here, no narrow windows darkened by centuries of candle smoke. Instead, you see a bold, rounded form from the late twentieth century. It is a modern building that speaks a very old language — the language of the Byzantine dome. Byzantine here means the Eastern Christian tradition that grew from Constantinople, the city we now call Istanbul.

To understand why this church looks the way it does, it helps to understand the city around it. In 1963, a powerful earthquake destroyed much of Skopje. Many people died, and a large part of the old centre was lost. In the decades that followed, architects from across Yugoslavia and beyond rebuilt the city. They often chose concrete, strong geometry, and forms that looked to the future. This cathedral belongs to that era of rebuilding.

Construction stretched across the 1970s and 1980s, and the church was consecrated in 1990. The design is credited to the Macedonian architect Slavko Brezoski. His challenge was unusual. He had to create a cathedral that felt rooted in Orthodox tradition, yet fitted a modern city. His answer was this gathering of domes and arches, which is traditional in spirit but clearly new in shape.

Now let us meet the man whose name the church carries. Saint Clement lived in the ninth and early tenth centuries. He was a disciple of the brothers Cyril and Methodius, the missionaries who brought written Slavic language to the peoples of Eastern Europe. After their deaths, Clement continued their work. He travelled south and settled in the region of Ohrid, a lakeside town in the southwest of today's North Macedonia.

In Ohrid, Clement taught. Tradition says he trained thousands of students — priests, teachers, and scribes — who carried literacy into villages across the region. This is the work historians link to the Ohrid Literary School. Clement is also closely connected with the development of early Slavic scripts, the ancestors of the Cyrillic alphabet you see on signs all around Skopje. Here is a detail many visitors miss: every street sign nearby is, in a sense, part of his legacy.

Clement died in Ohrid in the early tenth century and was buried there. Over time, he became one of the most loved saints in Macedonian tradition. People see him as a teacher of the nation, not only as a churchman. So it makes sense that the main cathedral in the capital carries his name. The building honours a man of books and schools, not a warrior or a king.

There is a second story inside this building — a story of church independence. In 1967, the Macedonian Orthodox Church declared itself autocephalous, which means self-governing, separate from the Serbian Orthodox Church. For decades, other Orthodox churches did not recognise this step. Only in 2022 did the Serbian Church and the Ecumenical Patriarchate in Istanbul move to accept the Macedonian Church back into full communion. For many local believers, this cathedral stood through all those years as a symbol of patience.

If the doors are open, step inside. The atmosphere changes quickly. The noise of the street falls away, and the air often carries the soft, sweet smell of beeswax candles. Look for the iconostasis, the screen of painted icons that separates the altar from the main hall. In Orthodox churches, this wall of images is the visual heart of the space. Worshippers stop before it, cross themselves, and kiss the icons.

Next, lift your eyes to the walls and the curves of the ceiling. Frescoes and icons cover much of the interior. They follow the traditional order of Orthodox painting. Christ usually appears high above, saints stand lower down, and scenes from the Gospels fill the spaces between. Even in a modern building, the painters followed rules that are many centuries old. Notice how the curved surfaces wrap the images around you, so you feel surrounded rather than simply watching.

A small note on manners — this is a living cathedral, not only a monument. Services take place here, and on major feast days the space fills with people, singing, and incense. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Speak quietly. If a service is under way, stand toward the back and take your time. Many visitors find that a few silent minutes here teach more than any guidebook can.

Back outside, walk slowly around the building if you can. Watch how the domes seem to change from each angle. From one side they look heavy and protective, like a shell. From another side they look light, almost lifted from the ground. In the early morning or late afternoon, low sunlight catches the curves and gives them depth. At midday, the surfaces look flatter and brighter.

This is the second surprise of the place. Many travellers come to Skopje to see the newer statues and classical facades of the city centre. They walk past this cathedral without a second look. Yet this building may tell the story of modern Skopje more honestly than many newer monuments. It comes from the years of rebuilding after disaster, and it carries the name of a teacher from eleven hundred years ago.

Before you go, look once more at the domes against the sky. Listen to the city around you — cars, voices, perhaps a church bell. Think of Clement, teaching his students by a lake in Ohrid, and of the letters he helped to spread. As you walk away, notice the Cyrillic signs above the shops and on the street corners. They are a quiet reminder that his work is still all around you.
