The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Car horns echo off the hills. Somewhere a café radio crackles, and the air carries the dry, resinous smell of pine and olive from the slopes around the town. This is Souk Ahras, in the far northeast of Algeria, close to the Tunisian border. Under these streets, and under the soil of these green hills, lies an older town. Its name was Thagaste.

If the name sounds unfamiliar, the man born here will not. In the year 354, a boy named Aurelius Augustinus was born in Thagaste. The world knows him as Saint Augustine. He became one of the most influential thinkers in the history of Christianity, and of Western philosophy as a whole. His ideas about time, memory, free will and the inner life still shape how millions of people think. And it all began in this upland town, far from the great cities of the Roman world.

In Augustine's day, Thagaste belonged to the Roman province of Numidia, in North Africa. It was a municipium, which means a town with recognised Roman civic status, its own local council and its own leading families. Before Rome, this land was home to Numidian peoples, the ancestors of the Amazigh, or Berbers, who still live across North Africa today. Many historians think Augustine himself had local roots. He was African in the fullest sense — a son of these hills who wrote in Latin.

His family was not rich, but it was respectable. His father, Patricius, was a modest landowner and a member of the town council. He was a pagan for most of his life and was baptised shortly before his death. His mother, Monica, was a devout Christian. Her patience and stubborn faith run through her son's writing. Today she is also venerated as a saint, and many towns around the world carry her name.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Augustine wrote about his childhood in Thagaste with unusual honesty. In his book Confessions — a long, personal address to God, and one of the first true autobiographies — he describes a night when he was about sixteen. He and a group of friends crept into a neighbour's orchard and stole pears. They were not hungry. They threw most of the fruit to the pigs. He did it, he wrote, simply because it was forbidden.

That small theft, in a small town, became one of the most studied episodes in Western literature. Augustine used it to ask a hard question: why do people choose to do wrong, even when they gain nothing from it? Philosophers and theologians have argued about his answer for sixteen centuries. So as you walk these streets, remember that somewhere near here was a pear tree, a boundary wall, and a teenager who later turned guilt into philosophy.

Thagaste could not hold a mind like his for long. Augustine studied first at nearby Madauros, a Roman town to the south, which was also the home of the writer Apuleius. Then he went on to Carthage, the great port city on the coast of today's Tunisia. Later came Rome and Milan, where he taught rhetoric — the art of persuasive speaking — at the highest level. In Milan, after years of searching, he converted to Christianity.

And then — a surprise. After his conversion, Augustine did not stay in the imperial capitals. He came home. Back in Thagaste, he sold much of his inherited property and gathered a small group of friends. Together they lived a simple, shared life of prayer, study and conversation. Many scholars see this little community as an early seed of the monastic rule that later carried his name across medieval Europe. Orders of monks, friars and nuns still follow it today.

His time at home was short. On a visit to the coastal city of Hippo Regius, near modern Annaba, the local congregation pressed him to become a priest. He later became its bishop and served there for the rest of his life. But Thagaste kept its link to him. His close friend Alypius, another son of the town, became bishop of Thagaste. The two men shared letters, councils and arguments for decades.

Now look around you with this story in mind. You will not find a grand Roman forum here, or rows of standing columns like those at some other Algerian sites. Modern Souk Ahras grew over the ancient town, and most of Thagaste lies below houses, roads and gardens. What survives is quieter: scattered stones, inscriptions and fragments, some kept in local and national collections. The past here is less a monument and more a layer under daily life.

One living link draws many visitors. In the town there is a very old olive tree, gnarled and split with age. Locals call it Saint Augustine's olive tree. According to a popular tradition, the young Augustine sat beneath it to think and pray. No document can prove this, and the tree's true age is debated. But people come anyway, from Algeria and from abroad, to stand in its shade. The tradition itself shows how deeply this town holds on to its most famous son.

The landscape is part of the story too. This is high, rolling country, cooler and greener than many people expect from North Africa. Forested hills, cork oaks and farmland surround the town. Not far away rises the Medjerda, one of the major rivers of the Maghreb, which flows east into Tunisia. In Roman times, these valleys produced grain and olive oil. The farms you see today follow, in many places, the same old patterns of soil and water.

So take a last look before you move on. Notice the light on the hills, the people walking to the market, the old stones set into newer walls. Sixteen centuries ago, a restless boy walked here too, under the same sky, and asked questions he would spend his whole life trying to answer. As you leave Thagaste behind, you might carry one of those questions with you — and see where it leads.
