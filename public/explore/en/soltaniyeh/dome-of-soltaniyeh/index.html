The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Out here on the open plain, the wind carries the dry smell of farmland and dust. Then you lift your eyes, and the dome fills the sky. It is pale blue, round and heavy, set on a body of warm tan brick. There is almost nothing around it to compare it to. That emptiness is part of the story. Seven centuries ago, this quiet ground was one of the great capitals of Asia.

What you are looking at is the Mausoleum of Öljaitü, better known as the Dome of Soltaniyeh. It rises in the north-west of Iran, in Zanjan Province. It was built in the early fourteenth century, during the time of the Ilkhanate. The Ilkhanate was a Mongol state, founded by the descendants of Genghis Khan, that ruled Iran and its neighbours. The Mongols arrived as conquerors. Within a few generations, many of their rulers had become Muslim patrons of Persian art.

Öljaitü was one of those rulers. He was a Mongol khan who also took the Muslim name Muhammad Khodabandeh. His father's generation had chosen this plain as a royal site, and Öljaitü turned it into a true capital. The name Soltaniyeh means, roughly, the imperial city. Palaces, markets and mosques once spread out from here. Today, the mausoleum is the great survivor... the one building tall enough to remind you what was lost.

Look at the shape of the building. The main body has eight sides. An octagon was a popular form for tombs in the Persian world, because it moves the eye smoothly from square ground to round dome. On top of this octagon sits the dome itself. It is counted among the largest brick domes ever built anywhere. Builders achieved it without steel, without concrete, and with brick and mortar alone.

Here is something many visitors miss. The dome is not one single layer. It is built with two shells, an inner one and an outer one, joined together. This technique makes a large dome lighter and stronger at the same time. Later architects across the Islamic world used versions of this idea. Some historians draw a line from Soltaniyeh to famous domes built much later, including in Mughal India, though how direct that influence was is still debated.

Now let your eyes travel around the base of the dome. You will see the remains of slender towers. Originally, eight minarets ringed the dome, one at each corner of the octagon. Imagine them complete, rising like a crown of thin spires. Time and earthquakes have taken their upper parts. What remains are stumps, but they still show the plan. Once you notice them, the whole silhouette of the building changes in your mind.

The blue you see on the dome is glazed tile. In bright sun, the turquoise looks almost white. Under cloud, it turns deep and cool. Below it, the brickwork carries patterns made by setting bricks at different angles and depths. These patterns catch shadow as the sun moves. In the morning and late afternoon, the walls look carved. At midday, they flatten into a single warm colour.

There is a famous story attached to this building. According to an often-repeated account, Öljaitü first planned the mausoleum for someone else. He had embraced Shia Islam, and the story says he hoped to bring here the remains of Imam Ali and his son Husayn, the most revered figures of the Shia faith. Their shrines are in Iraq. The plan, if it existed, never happened. In the end, the building became Öljaitü's own tomb.

When you step inside, the scale changes everything. Sound travels up into the dome and comes back softened. The light falls from high windows and lands in pale patches on the floor. For many years, visitors have also seen scaffolding in the interior. Restoration here has been long and careful work. Try not to see the scaffolding as a problem. It shows how much effort it takes to keep a building like this standing.

The interior decoration has two layers of history. The first scheme used brick and glazed tile. Not long after, much of it was covered with painted plaster in a new decorative programme. Scholars often connect this change to shifts in the ruler's religious life, though the exact reasons are still discussed. In places, you can see both layers side by side. Look closely where plaster has fallen away. The older surface shows through underneath.

If the upper galleries are open, they are worth the climb. The vaulted ceilings there carry painted and carved patterns in soft reds, blues and ochres. Up close, you can see the work of individual hands, small irregularities in line and colour. From the gallery, you also get a view out over the plain. It helps you picture the lost city spread out below, its streets and gardens now turned to fields.

After Öljaitü died, the Ilkhanate did not last long. Power shifted, and the capital lost its purpose. Over the following centuries, the city slowly faded. Wars and earthquakes damaged what remained. Yet the mausoleum held. In 2005, it was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List, recognised as a key monument of Persian architecture. The modern town around it is small, and that quiet suits the building well.

Before you leave, walk a slow circle around the outside. Watch how the octagon turns each face to the light in turn. Notice the stumps of the minarets, the patterned brick, the blue curve above. Listen again to the wind across the plain. Seven hundred years ago, a Mongol khan stood somewhere near here and imagined forever. What he left behind is this one great dome, still holding up the sky.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk a full circle outside before going in.
Visit in early morning or late afternoon for raking light.
Climb to the upper galleries if they are open.
Bring water and sun protection for the open plain.
Allow time to sit inside and listen to the acoustics.
