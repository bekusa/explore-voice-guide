The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind the high stone walls, the traffic of central Malta fades into something softer. There is the crunch of gravel underfoot, the splash of a fountain, and the dry rustle of palm fronds overhead. The air here feels a few degrees cooler than the streets outside. Shade does that on an island where summer sun can be fierce. You are standing in the grounds of San Anton Palace, in the village of Attard, near the geographic heart of Malta.

The palace began as a country retreat. In the early seventeenth century, a knight named Antoine de Paule built a house here, among the farmland of central Malta. He belonged to the Order of Saint John, the military and religious brotherhood that ruled Malta for more than two centuries. When de Paule later became Grand Master, the head of the entire Order, his modest retreat grew to match his new rank. The name San Anton comes from Saint Anthony, a link to the man who built it.

Why would a ruler want a house out here? Think about the alternative. Valletta, the fortified capital, is a dense grid of stone streets packed inside massive walls. In the heat of summer, it could feel close and airless. Attard offered space, cooler breezes, and gardens. The Grand Masters came here to rest, to hunt, and to escape the formality of court life. The house became, in effect, a summer palace for the rulers of the island.

As you walk, notice the stone around you. Almost everything here is built from Maltese limestone, the soft, honey-coloured rock that forms much of the island itself. When it is freshly cut, the stone is pale and easy to carve. Over time, it hardens and darkens with weather. In the late afternoon, it can glow almost golden. This same stone builds the churches, the farmhouses, and the walls of every village you pass through in Malta.

Here is something many visitors miss. The palace played a part in one of the most dramatic moments in Maltese history. In 1798, Napoleon's forces took Malta from the Knights. Within months, the Maltese people rose against the French. The French retreated behind the walls of Valletta and the harbour towns, and a long siege followed. During those years, San Anton served as a centre for the Maltese insurgents and their British allies. A quiet country house became a place of war councils.

That episode opened a new chapter. After the French left, Malta came under British rule, and it stayed that way until independence in 1964. San Anton Palace became a residence for the British governors of the island. Sir Alexander Ball, a naval officer who supported the Maltese during the siege, is closely linked with the palace and its gardens. Over the following century and more, the house welcomed admirals, officials, and visiting royalty.

Today, the palace has yet another role. It is the official residence of the President of Malta. That is why the building itself is generally closed to the public. You may see guards at the entrance, and parts of the grounds are kept private. But the gardens are a different story. They have been open to the public since the late nineteenth century, and Maltese families have walked these paths for generations.

So let us turn to the gardens, because this is where the place truly opens up. Walk slowly along the main paths. You will find tall palms, old evergreen trees, and flowering shrubs set between stone borders. Some of the trees are very old, with thick trunks and wide canopies. On an island with little natural woodland, mature shade like this is rare and precious. Malta's landscape is mostly open, rocky, and terraced, so a garden like this feels almost like an oasis.

Now look for the water. Ponds and fountains are scattered through the grounds, many framed by carved stonework. Ducks often paddle in the ponds, and children gather at the edges to watch them. Water has always been a luxury in Malta, which has no permanent rivers. A garden full of fountains was a clear statement of wealth and power in the age of the Knights. Today, it is simply a pleasure to sit beside.

As you wander, keep an eye out for small monuments and busts placed along the paths. Some honour figures from Malta's past. Others mark visits or anniversaries. Read the inscriptions when you can. They are often in English, Maltese, or Latin, and together they show how many layers of history meet in this one walled garden. The Maltese language itself, by the way, is a Semitic language written in the Latin alphabet, with many Italian and English loanwords.

Step back now and consider the village outside the walls. Attard is one of the so-called Three Villages, together with its neighbours Balzan and Lija. In the age of the Knights, wealthy families followed the Grand Masters here and built their own country houses and gardens. You can still see some of these grand homes along the old streets. The whole area carries the feeling of a retreat, quieter and greener than the busy harbour towns.

A second surprise, if you look closely at the palace itself. The building is not one grand design made all at once. It grew over time, as different Grand Masters and governors added rooms, terraces, and decorations. Look at the facades you can see from the garden paths. Notice where the stonework changes, where windows differ in style, or where one wing meets another. These small shifts are traces of many hands, working across centuries.

Before you leave, find a bench in the shade. Let your eyes rest on the stone, the leaves, the light on the water. Somewhere behind these walls, a head of state is going about the business of a modern country. Out here, a child is feeding ducks, and an older couple is walking the same loop they have walked for years. Notice the sound of the fountain once more as you head for the gate... and the warmth of the stone streets waiting for you just outside.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the gardens in the morning for cooler, quieter paths.
Bring water on hot summer days, shade is welcome.
Expect the palace interior itself to be closed to visitors.
Combine your visit with a walk through Balzan and Lija.
Respect guarded areas near the presidential entrance.
