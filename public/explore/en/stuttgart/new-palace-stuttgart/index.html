The guide

The story

Stand on the open gravel of the Schlossplatz for a moment and listen. Fine stones shift under your shoes. Fountains splash somewhere to your side. Trams and voices drift in from the shopping streets. In front of you, a long pale façade opens its arms around a deep courtyard. Its stone has the warm colour of sand in late afternoon. This is the New Palace, the Neues Schloss, and it has watched over the centre of Stuttgart for more than two centuries.

The word "new" here is relative. Just behind you, to the south, stands the Old Palace, a heavy fortress with round towers and medieval roots. By the eighteenth century, that old castle felt cramped and outdated to the rulers of Württemberg. Princes across Europe were building palaces in the style of Versailles. They wanted wide courtyards, grand staircases, and long suites of rooms for ceremony. Stuttgart's duke wanted the same.

That duke was Carl Eugen of Württemberg. He began this palace in the 1740s, while he was still a very young ruler. He was ambitious, extravagant, and often in conflict with his own subjects. The first designs came from the architect Leopoldo Retti. After Retti's death, the French architect Philippe de La Guêpière shaped much of what followed. His touch shows in the calm, elegant proportions — a French restraint laid over German Baroque, the ornate style of the age.

Here is the first surprise. Carl Eugen started this palace, but he did not stay to enjoy it. His relations with the city and the local estates, the powerful assemblies that controlled taxes, grew bitter. For years he moved his court to Ludwigsburg, north of Stuttgart, and to other residences. Building here slowed and stalled. A fire also damaged part of the palace during construction. The result is a building that took around sixty years to complete. It was only finished in the early nineteenth century.

By then, the world had changed. The French Revolution and Napoleon had redrawn the map of Europe. Württemberg was no longer a duchy. In 1806 it became a kingdom, and its ruler, Friedrich, became its first king. A king needed royal rooms. So the interiors were completed in the newer Classical style, cleaner and more severe than Baroque. The architect Nikolaus von Thouret designed many of these rooms. The palace began as a duke's dream and ended as a king's residence.

Look now at the shape of the building. Three wings frame a courtyard of honour, open toward the square. This layout was a stage. Carriages rolled in, guests stepped down, and the whole façade surrounded them with power and order. Notice the central section, which steps forward from the wings. Notice the columns and the balustrade along the roofline. The design guides your eye toward the centre, where the ruler once received his visitors.

Now turn around and take in the square itself. The Schlossplatz was once part of the palace grounds. Today it is the open living room of Stuttgart. In the middle rises a tall column. This is the Jubilee Column, raised in the nineteenth century to mark twenty-five years of rule by King Wilhelm the First. At its top stands a figure of Concordia, the Roman goddess of harmony. On warm days, people lie on the lawns beneath her and eat ice cream.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a hard one. The palace you see is largely a reconstruction. During the Second World War, Allied bombing raids struck Stuttgart again and again. In 1944, the New Palace burned out. Most of the interiors were lost. For years after the war, only blackened walls stood here. Many people argued that the ruin should simply be demolished. A palace for kings seemed an odd thing to rebuild in a city that needed homes.

After long and heated debate, the decision went the other way. The state government chose to rebuild. Work ran through the late 1950s and early 1960s. The outer shell was restored to its historic appearance. Inside, however, the palace received a new and practical life. Today it houses offices of the government of Baden-Württemberg, including state ministries. So when you look at those tall windows, imagine desks and meeting tables behind them, not thrones.

A few historic rooms were recreated for ceremony. The most important is the Marble Hall, used for state receptions and official events. Because the palace is a working government building, its interiors are usually closed to casual visitors. Occasionally there are guided tours or special open days. If you want to see inside, check with the local tourist office before you plan around it. For most visitors, the palace is an outdoor experience — and that is no loss.

The façade rewards slow looking. Walk toward the courtyard and watch how the light changes on the stone. In the morning, the eastern side catches the sun. In the evening, the façade can glow a soft gold. Look up at the sculpted figures and trophies along the roofline. Look at the wrought-iron details near the gates. Much of this is careful postwar work, made to match the old drawings and photographs.

Behind the palace, the city opens into green space. The Schlossgarten, the palace garden, stretches north-east toward the river Neckar. It is a long chain of parks, lawns, and ponds. Locals walk dogs, ride bikes, and jog there. Across the square stands the Königsbau, a long building with a row of columns, built for the king in the nineteenth century. Today it holds shops and cafés. Together, these buildings frame the square on every side.

It is worth thinking about how this space is used now. On summer evenings, the square fills with concerts and festivals. In winter, the Christmas market spreads into the nearby streets. Students sit on the palace steps with coffee. Children chase pigeons across the gravel. A building made to impress a royal court now serves as a backdrop for ordinary city life. That shift may be the most interesting story this place tells.

Before you move on, take one more look at the long façade. Remember the young duke who began it, the king who finished it, and the fire of 1944 that nearly erased it. Then notice what is around you right now — the fountains, the column, the people on the lawns. As you walk away, listen again to the gravel under your feet. It is the same sound that carriages once made, rolling up to the palace door.
