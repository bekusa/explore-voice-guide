The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Inside this building there is a soft, steady hum of air and footsteps on smooth concrete. Ahead of you the floor curves away, gently and endlessly, like the inside of a shell. Light comes in through angled panes of glass. Somewhere above, polished chrome catches it and sends it back. Welcome to the Mercedes-Benz Museum in Stuttgart, a building that tells the story of the automobile from its very first breath.

First, a word about where you are standing. The museum sits in Bad Cannstatt, on the eastern side of Stuttgart, close to the Neckar River. Next door is the football stadium, and not far away is the Mercedes-Benz factory at Untertürkheim. This is a working landscape of engines, roads and rail lines. The museum was not placed in a quiet park. It was placed right beside the industry that it celebrates.

The building opened in 2006. It was designed by the Dutch architecture firm UNStudio, led by Ben van Berkel and Caroline Bos. From outside, it looks like a silver, rounded form with ribbons of glass. Its plan is based on a cloverleaf, a shape with three rounded lobes around a central core. Inside, two spiral paths wind around each other, like the twisted strands of a DNA molecule. That idea of a double helix shapes your entire visit.

Here is the first surprise. You do not climb this museum. You start at the very top. Silver lifts, shaped a little like capsules, carry you straight up through the central atrium. Then you walk down, floor by floor, through more than a century of history. Gravity does the work. By the time you reach the ground, you have travelled from the age of the horse to the age of electric power.

And the horse is exactly where the story begins. When the lift doors open, the first figure you meet is not a car at all. It is a life-size white horse. For thousands of years, horses set the speed of human travel. The museum reminds you of that before it shows you a single engine. It is a quiet, clever start. Everything after this room is about replacing that animal.

The next rooms hold the inventions that changed everything. In the 1880s, two separate teams worked on the problem in southern Germany. In Mannheim, Karl Benz built a three-wheeled motor car and patented it in 1886. Near Stuttgart, Gottlieb Daimler and his partner Wilhelm Maybach built light, fast-running engines. They put them into a wooden motorcycle, a carriage, even a boat. Remarkably, the two inventors worked only a short distance apart, yet they never became partners.

Their companies came together much later. In 1926, Daimler's company and Benz's company merged. But where does the name Mercedes come from? It did not come from either founder. Around 1900, a wealthy businessman and car dealer named Emil Jellinek ordered powerful new cars from the Daimler company. He raced them under the name of his young daughter, Mercedes. The name stuck. So a girl who never designed a car gave her name to one of the best-known brands on Earth.

As you walk down, notice that the museum offers two routes. The first route is called the Legend rooms. They follow history in order, decade by decade. The second route is called the Collection rooms. They group vehicles by theme, such as travel, cargo, rescue and celebrity. You can switch between the two paths at several points along the spiral. Many visitors follow one route down, then go back up and follow the other. Take your time. The ramps are long, and the slope is kind to tired legs.

The Legend rooms do not avoid the difficult chapters. The years of the First and Second World Wars are part of this story. During the Nazi period, the company built military vehicles and engines, and it used forced labour in its factories. The museum addresses this directly in its displays. It is a sober moment in a building full of shining paint. Pause here. This section shows that industry and history cannot be separated so easily.

Further down, the mood lifts again. You will see the elegant cars of the 1950s, including the 300 SL with its doors that open upwards like the wings of a bird. People call it the Gullwing. Nearby are buses, trucks and fire engines. In the Collection rooms you may also find vehicles linked to famous owners, including a car used by a pope. Look closely at the dashboards, the stitching on the seats, and the shape of the steering wheels. Each era has its own handwriting.

Now the second surprise. Near the end of your descent, the floor itself begins to tilt. The final Legend room is about motor racing, and here the cars stand on a steep, banked curve, like the edge of a racing track. These are the Silver Arrows, the famous silver racing cars. Legend has it that in 1934 mechanics scraped the white paint off one car overnight to bring it under the weight limit, leaving bare silver metal. It is a wonderful story, though historians still debate whether it really happened that way.

Before you leave, look up from the lowest level. From here you can see the full height of the central atrium. The concrete walls twist and fold above you. Daylight falls in through the triangular glass. The building has no right-angled corridors, no simple boxes. Even the walls lean. It feels less like a warehouse for cars and more like the inside of a machine, with you as the moving part. Notice how quiet it is, despite all the engines around you.

Step outside now and turn around. The silver shell of the museum reflects the sky and the traffic on the nearby roads. Cars pass on the highway, trains run along the valley, and the factory continues its work not far away. The story you just walked through is still moving all around you. Listen to the traffic for a moment. Every one of those engines is a distant descendant of the machines you saw upstairs.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the lift to the top, then walk down.
Follow the Legend rooms first for chronological history.
Return upward later to explore the themed Collection rooms.
Wear comfortable shoes for the long, gently sloping ramps.
Combine your visit with a walk along the Neckar River.
