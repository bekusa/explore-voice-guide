The guide

The story

Start with your hands. Reach out and touch the wall in front of you — layered bands of warm sandstone and pale travertine, laid in stripes like the layers of a cake. Now look up. Fat tubular railings in bright pink and electric blue run along the ramps. They look almost like toys. This is not what most people expect from a state art gallery, and that is the point.

You are standing at the Staatsgalerie Stuttgart, the state gallery of Baden-Württemberg. It is really two museums joined together. One is a calm neoclassical building from the nineteenth century. The other is a playful, argumentative design from the 1980s. Between them, they hold one of the most important art collections in Germany. They also tell a story about how a city decided to show its art to the public.

Let us begin with the older half. The original gallery opened in 1843, during the age of the Kingdom of Württemberg. Its architect was Gottlob Georg Barth. He gave it the language of classical antiquity — columns, symmetry, a sense of order and restraint. In the early nineteenth century, a building like this said something clear. Art belonged to the educated public, not only to palaces. The kingdom wanted its capital to look cultured and modern.

When you walk around the old wing, notice how quiet the facade feels. It is low, balanced, and polite. It does not shout. Inside, the rooms follow a traditional rhythm, one gallery opening into the next. This is the way museums were imagined for a long time. You moved in a line, from room to room, as if reading the chapters of a book.

Then, more than a century later, the gallery needed space. A competition was held for a new building. The winners were the British architect James Stirling and his partner Michael Wilford. Their design opened in 1984. It caused real debate. Some critics loved it. Others found it strange, even rude. Today it is often named as one of the key buildings of postmodern architecture — a movement that mixed history, humour, and bright colour in deliberate ways.

Here is the first surprise. Look at the base of the wall near the car park entrance. A few stone blocks seem to have fallen out of the facade. They lie on the ground as if the building were already a ruin. Behind the gaps, you can see the openings. It is a joke. Stirling was teasing the idea of classical grandeur. The wall looks heavy and ancient, but the stone is a cladding over a modern structure.

Now follow the ramps up and into the heart of the new building. At its centre is a round courtyard, open to the sky. It recalls the rotunda of a classical museum — but without a roof. Plants climb the walls. Sound changes in here. The traffic noise softens, and you hear footsteps and voices bouncing around the circle. Many visitors miss this place entirely because they head straight inside.

And here is the second surprise. A public footpath winds through the gallery grounds and around this rotunda. You do not need a ticket to walk it. Stirling wanted the building to be a part of the city, not a fortress. People use it as a shortcut between the streets above and below. The museum lets the city pass through it, every day.

Look also at the entrance facade. A wall of green glass curves in and out like a wave. Next to it, the stone stays solid and straight. The two materials argue with each other. Stirling liked that kind of tension — old against new, heavy against light, serious against playful. Even the colours on the railings and doors were chosen to stand out against the stone, not to blend in.

Inside, the collection moves across many centuries. There are paintings from the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance. One much-loved work is Rembrandt's early painting of Saint Paul in prison, showing the apostle deep in thought, lit by a single beam of light. Hans Memling's Bathsheba is here too. These older works mostly live in the classical wing, which suits their quieter mood.

The modern collection is just as strong. The gallery holds a significant group of works by Pablo Picasso. It also shows German art of the twentieth century. Look for the figures from the Triadic Ballet by Oskar Schlemmer, an artist closely linked to Stuttgart and to the Bauhaus, the famous German school of design. His costumes turn dancers into geometric shapes — spheres, cones, and spirals. They look like sculptures, and they were made to move.

As you walk, pay attention to the light. In the new wing, many galleries receive daylight from above. The light is soft and even, and it changes through the day. On a grey Stuttgart afternoon, the rooms feel calm and cool. On a bright morning, the colours of the paintings seem warmer. The architecture is loud outside, but inside it often steps back and lets the art speak.

The gallery sits on Konrad-Adenauer-Straße, a busy road through the city centre. Nearby you will find the opera house and the long green park of the Schlossgarten. This stretch is sometimes called Stuttgart's cultural mile. After your visit, a walk in the park gives your eyes a rest. You can sit under the trees and think about what you have seen.

Before you leave, stand back from the building for a moment. Look at the stripes of stone, the pink railings, the green wave of glass, the old columns of the earlier wing just beside them. Two centuries of ideas about art sit side by side here. Listen to the traffic, the footsteps on the ramp, a door closing somewhere. Then take the path down through the courtyard — the same way the city walks through it every day.
