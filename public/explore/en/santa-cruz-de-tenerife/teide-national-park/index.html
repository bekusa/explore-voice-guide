The guide

The story

Listen to the ground first. Under your shoes, loose volcanic gravel crunches and shifts like coarse sugar. The air is thin and dry, and it smells faintly of warm stone and resin. The light up here is hard and clear, with almost no haze to soften it. Look around and the colours keep changing: rust red, charcoal black, pale ochre, and here and there a streak of green. You are standing more than two thousand metres above the Atlantic, inside the crater of an enormous volcano.

This is Teide National Park, high in the centre of Tenerife, the largest of the Canary Islands. At its heart rises Mount Teide itself, a smooth grey cone with a pale summit. It reaches about 3,715 metres above sea level. That makes it the highest mountain in all of Spain. Spain's tallest peak is not on the mainland at all. It stands on an island off the coast of Africa, far closer to Morocco than to Madrid.

Here is the first surprise. The height you see is only part of the story. Teide's base sits deep under the ocean, thousands of metres below the waves. Measured from the sea floor, it counts among the tallest volcanoes on Earth. Most of the mountain is hidden underwater. What you see above the clouds is only the tip of a vast pile of rock that the island has built over a very long time.

The wide, flat plain around you has a name: Las Cañadas. It is a caldera, which means a huge basin left behind when an older volcano collapsed or slid away. Geologists still debate exactly how it formed. Some point to giant landslides into the sea. Others point to the collapse of an emptied magma chamber. Either way, the high cliffs that ring the plain mark the edge of that lost mountain. Teide grew later, inside the scar.

Teide is not extinct. It is a dormant volcano, and scientists monitor it closely. Its neighbour, the broad, lower peak called Pico Viejo, which means Old Peak, last erupted in 1798. That eruption opened vents on its flank, known today as the Narices del Teide, or the Nostrils of Teide. Look for dark, fresh-looking lava tongues on the western slopes. Near the summit of Teide itself, small vents still release warm gas that smells of sulphur.

Long before Spanish ships arrived in the fifteenth century, the Guanches lived on this island. They were the indigenous people of Tenerife, and they called the mountain Echeyde. According to their legend, a demon named Guayota lived inside the volcano. In one popular tale, Guayota captured the sun and hid it in the mountain. The supreme god rescued the light and sealed the demon inside, and the summit became his stopper. Locals still tell this story to visitors today.

The second surprise is about protection. Spain declared this landscape a national park in 1954, which makes it one of the oldest in the country. In 2007, UNESCO added it to the World Heritage List. UNESCO recognised both its natural beauty and its importance for understanding how oceanic islands grow. Today the park is one of the most visited national parks in Europe. Many thousands of people come here every year, yet the land still feels empty and quiet.

Now turn toward the Roques de García, a line of tall, broken rock towers near the main road. These are hard volcanic remains that resisted erosion while softer rock wore away around them. The most famous is Roque Cinchado, a tower that is narrow at the base and wide at the top. It looks almost ready to topple. For years, its outline appeared on the Spanish one thousand peseta banknote, so older Spanish visitors often recognise it straight away.

Life here is tough, and the plants show it. Summer days are hot and bright, winter nights can bring frost and even snow, and rain is rare. Many species grow nowhere else in the world. The most striking is the tajinaste rojo, a plant that sends up a tall, cone-shaped spike of small red flowers in late spring. Another rare treasure is the Teide violet, a tiny purple flower that grows high on the volcano's loose slopes.

On many days, a white sea of clouds spreads below the park. Steady trade winds push moist air against the north of the island. The air rises, cools and forms a thick layer of cloud along the middle slopes. Above that layer, the sky is often clear and blue. On a good day you can see the peaks of other Canary Islands rising out of the haze, such as La Palma, La Gomera and El Hierro.

That clear, dry air brings visitors of another kind. Astronomers prize this region for its steady, dark skies. On the ridge at the edge of the park stands the Teide Observatory, a cluster of white domes and towers that study the sun and the night sky. After sunset, the darkness here is very deep. Stars appear in numbers that city dwellers rarely see, and the Milky Way often stretches clearly across the sky.

You can climb higher. A cable car carries visitors up the cone to a station called La Rambleta, a short distance below the summit. From there, marked trails lead across the slopes toward Pico Viejo and a viewpoint. The final path to the very top is protected, and you need a free permit, booked well in advance, to walk it. Remember that the air near the summit holds much less oxygen. Move slowly, drink water, and listen to your body.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Feel the dry wind on your face and the faint warmth rising from the dark stone. Look at the long shadow that Teide casts across the plain, especially late in the day. Notice how the colours of the rock shift as the sun moves. Every slope around you was once molten. As you walk back over the crunching gravel, remember that this island is still being made, slowly, beneath your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the summit trail permit well in advance online.
Bring warm layers; summit temperatures drop sharply.
Wear strong sun protection; high-altitude sunlight burns fast.
Walk slowly near the summit to manage thin air.
Stay after sunset for some of Europe's darkest skies.
