The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, the forest speaks in sounds. A sharp, repeated bark rises from somewhere in the trees. That is a spotted deer, and it is warning the herd. Dry sal leaves crackle under hooves you cannot see. Somewhere far off, a peacock calls, high and urgent. Here, the jungle tells you what is happening long before it shows you. Your guide and driver are listening too. In this place, an alarm call can mean a tiger is moving.

You are standing in Jim Corbett National Park, in the foothills of the Himalaya in the Indian state of Uttarakhand. The park spreads across parts of Nainital district and the neighbouring Pauri Garhwal district. Most visitors arrive through the small town of Ramnagar, which serves as the gateway. From there, dirt tracks lead into a landscape of tall forest, open grassland, river beds full of stones, and low ridges. Around you are the outer hills, the first steps of the mountains that rise further north.

Here is the first surprise. This is the oldest national park in India. It was established in 1936, but under a different name. It was first called Hailey National Park, after Sir Malcolm Hailey, the British governor of the region at the time. After India became independent, the park was renamed Ramganga National Park, after the river that runs through it. Then, in the 1950s, it received the name it carries today. That name honours a man with a very unusual life story.

Edward James Corbett, known to everyone as Jim, was born in Nainital in 1875. He grew up in these hills. He knew the forest paths and the birdsong, and he could read animal tracks in the dust. In the early twentieth century he became famous as a hunter of man-eaters. These were tigers and leopards that had begun to kill people in the villages of the Kumaon hills. Local communities asked him for help, and he tracked these animals on foot, often alone, for weeks at a time.

And here is the turn in his story. The man who hunted tigers became one of their strongest defenders. Corbett came to understand that many man-eaters were old or injured animals. Some had wounds from bullets or porcupine quills, and they could no longer hunt their natural prey. He began to argue that India's tigers and forests needed protection. He supported the creation of this park. He also wrote books, including Man-Eaters of Kumaon, which are still read around the world. Late in life he moved to Kenya, and he died there in 1955.

The second surprise concerns the tiger itself. In 1973, India launched Project Tiger, a national programme to save the species from disappearing. The programme began here, in this park. Today the wider area is managed as the Corbett Tiger Reserve. It is known for one of the highest concentrations of wild tigers in the country. Even so, seeing one is never guaranteed. Tigers are quiet and patient, and they prefer to stay hidden. Many visitors leave without a sighting. They still leave with something real.

That is because the tiger is only one part of this place. Asian elephants move through the forest in family herds. Sometimes they cross the tracks directly in front of the jeeps. You may see four kinds of deer: the spotted chital, the large dark sambar, the small hog deer, and the barking deer. Leopards live here too, although they are even harder to find than tigers. Wild boar dig in the soft earth near the water. Langur monkeys watch from the branches, and they often give the first warning when a predator comes close.

Now look at the trees around you. Much of the forest is sal, a tall hardwood with straight trunks and broad leaves. In the dry season the fallen sal leaves cover the ground like brown paper. This is why every footstep can be heard. Between the forest blocks lie wide grasslands. Locally these are called chaurs. The grass can grow taller than a person. Herds of deer graze at the edges, and this is often where predators come to hunt.

Water shapes everything here. The Ramganga River runs through the heart of the park. Its banks are wide and stony, and its pools are deep and green. If you look carefully at the sandbanks, you may see crocodiles. There are two kinds. The mugger is the broad-snouted marsh crocodile. The gharial is a rare reptile with a very long, thin snout, which it uses to catch fish. Near the western side, a dam created a large reservoir. In winter, migrating birds gather on its shores.

Birds are a reason to come here by themselves. Birdwatchers have recorded several hundred species in and around the park. Hornbills fly heavily between the fig trees. Kingfishers wait on low branches above the river. Eagles and vultures circle on the warm air above the ridges. In the early morning, the forest fills with calls that change every few minutes. Even when the large animals stay hidden, the birds keep the forest full of life and sound.

The park is divided into several zones, and each has its own character. Dhikala is the best-known zone. It sits deep inside the park, beside the river and the open grasslands. Its old forest rest house has hosted visitors for generations. Other zones, such as Bijrani, Jhirna, and Dhela, are closer to the edges of the park. They are easier to reach for a single safari. Your experience will depend on which zone you visit, the season, and a good measure of luck.

A safari here follows a simple rhythm. You set out in an open jeep in the cool early morning or the late afternoon. Most of the time is spent in quiet waiting. The driver stops, switches off the engine, and listens. Everyone looks for movement in the grass or fresh pugmarks, the paw prints of a big cat, in the dust of the track. Patience matters more than speed here. The forest rewards people who stay still and stay quiet.

Before you leave the area, consider a short drive to Kaladhungi, south of Nainital. Corbett's family home there has been turned into a small museum. It holds personal belongings and photographs from his life in these hills. It is a quiet place. It helps you understand how one man could move from hunting tigers to protecting them.

For now, though, take one more moment where you are. Notice the light coming through the sal leaves and the dust in the air. Listen for one more alarm call from the trees. Somewhere close by, perhaps, an animal is watching you. As you head back towards Ramnagar, keep listening. The forest does not stop talking just because you are leaving.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book safari permits well ahead, especially for Dhikala.
Choose early morning drives for cooler air and animals.
Wear muted greens or browns, avoid bright colours.
Bring binoculars for birds along the Ramganga River.
Visit Corbett's former home museum at Kaladhungi.
