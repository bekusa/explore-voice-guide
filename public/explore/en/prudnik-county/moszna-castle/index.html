The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes as you come up the long, tree-lined approach. Then the trees open, and the castle appears all at once. It is a white mass of walls, gables and pointed roofs, crowded with small towers that seem to multiply the longer you look. In the morning the pale walls take on a soft, creamy colour. On grey days they look almost silver against the sky. Take a moment here before you walk closer. This first view is the one the builders wanted you to have.

You are standing at Moszna Castle, in the countryside of Prudnik County, in southern Poland's Opole region. This corner of the country is called Upper Silesia. For centuries it passed between Bohemian, Habsburg and Prussian rule, and German was widely spoken here until 1945. That layered past matters, because the castle in front of you is not one building from one moment. It is several buildings joined together, each one added by a different generation with different ideas of what a grand house should look like.

Start with the centre. The middle section is the oldest part of the castle, and it is Baroque in character. Baroque is the style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, with balanced, symmetrical façades and a sense of calm order. Look at how this central block sits evenly between the two wings. It feels steady and formal. Now let your eyes move left and right, and you will see that the calm does not last. The wings on either side belong to a much later, more theatrical age.

The family most connected with this place is the Tiele-Winckler family. In the second half of the nineteenth century, the estate was bought by Hubert von Tiele-Winckler. His fortune came from the coal mines and heavy industry of Upper Silesia, one of the great industrial regions of Europe in that era. Money from the mines paid for the elegant life you see reflected here. It is worth keeping that contrast in mind. The white towers above you were built on wealth dug from deep, dark shafts not very far away.

Then came a turning point. In the 1890s, a fire badly damaged part of the building. Instead of simply repairing what was lost, the family chose to expand. Over the following years, two new wings were added. The eastern wing was built in a Neo-Gothic style, which borrows the pointed arches, spires and vertical lines of medieval churches. The western wing came later, in a Neo-Renaissance style, which looks back to the decorated palaces of sixteenth-century Europe. So when you walk along the façade, you are walking through three different centuries of taste.

Now, the towers. You will often hear that Moszna has 99 turrets and 365 rooms — one room for every day of the year. It is a lovely idea, and it is repeated in many guidebooks and by local guides. But treat it as a cherished local tale rather than an exact count. The numbers sound almost too perfect, and that is part of their charm. Whatever the true figure, try counting the towers you can see from where you stand. Most visitors give up somewhere in the twenties.

There is an older legend, too. Some local stories claim that a medieval stronghold once stood on this site, linked to the Knights Templar, the crusading order of warrior monks. There is no firm evidence for this, and historians treat it as folklore. Still, it shows how people have always looked at this building and imagined something ancient and mysterious behind it. In truth, the fairy-tale look you see is mostly a deliberate creation of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — a romantic dream built in stone.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Neo-Gothic and Neo-Renaissance wings were not trying to fool anyone into thinking they were genuinely medieval. This was a fashion of the time. Wealthy families across Europe built houses that mixed historical styles, almost like a collection. Walk slowly and compare the windows on each wing. On one side you will see pointed, church-like shapes. On the other, you will find squarer frames and richer decoration. The building is a kind of open-air catalogue of European architecture.

The twentieth century brought hard change. At the end of the Second World War, the German owners left, and the region became part of Poland. The castle was occupied by soldiers, and like many great houses in this part of Europe, it suffered damage and looting. In the decades that followed, it was used for very different purposes, including as a place of medical treatment and recovery. Today it serves as a cultural centre, hosting concerts, events and visitors. Parts of the building also offer overnight stays.

Step inside if you can. The interiors carry the same mix of styles as the exterior. You will find carved wood, decorative ceilings and grand staircases. Some rooms have been restored, while others are simpler, so expect a contrast between richly finished spaces and plainer corridors. Listen to the sound of your footsteps on the floors. In the larger halls, voices carry and echo. On concert evenings, music fills these rooms, and you can imagine how they sounded when the house was full of guests.

Now turn your attention outside, to the park. The castle stands within wide grounds of old trees, lawns and ponds. In spring, the park is known for its flowering azaleas and rhododendrons. When they bloom, whole beds turn pink, red, orange and white, and the air carries a light, sweet scent. The castle holds a popular azalea festival in this season, with music and crowds. If you visit at another time of year, the park is quieter, and the water reflects the white towers.

Walk to the edge of one of the ponds and look back. This is one of the best places to understand the whole composition. From here, the castle's three parts read as a single picture. The towers rise above the trees, and their reflection sits on the surface of the water. Notice the birds on the ponds and the sound of wind moving through the tall trees. Away from the main entrance, the grounds become surprisingly calm, even on busier days.

The surrounding countryside is worth a thought, too. Moszna is a village, not a city, and the land around it is farmland and open fields. The area also has a long tradition of horse breeding, and you may see horses in the paddocks as you drive or walk nearby. This rural setting is part of the castle's character. It was built as a country seat — a place to escape from the noise and smoke of the industrial towns where the family's money was made.

Before you leave, stand once more in front of the main façade. Look at the steady Baroque centre, and then at the two wings that grew from it. Think of the fire that could have ended the story, and the decision to build bigger instead. As you walk back down the avenue, listen again to the gravel under your feet. Turn around once, near the trees. The towers are still there, white against the sky — a house that never stopped being added to.
