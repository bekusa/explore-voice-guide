The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your feet. Old Bourges sits on a low rise, and the streets slope gently away in almost every direction. Below, out of sight, lie water and soft marshland. Above you, the towers of the cathedral rise into the sky. That gentle slope is the oldest thing in this city. More than two thousand years ago, it carried a Gallic town called Avaricum.

Avaricum was the main stronghold of a Celtic people called the Bituriges. Their name is often translated as something like 'kings of the world'. Whatever it meant, it sounds like a people sure of their place. They controlled rich farmland in the heart of what is now France. They were known for working metal, especially iron. Their town on this hill was a busy, wealthy place, a centre of trade long before any Roman arrived.

We know Avaricum mostly through the words of its enemy. Julius Caesar wrote an account of his wars in Gaul, and he spends many pages on this town. He calls it one of the most beautiful cities in all of Gaul. That is unusual praise from a man who came to conquer it. It tells you how this hilltop looked to an outsider: well built, well defended, and worth fighting for.

The year was 52 BCE. Much of Gaul had risen against Rome under a young leader named Vercingetorix. His plan was harsh but clever. He wanted the Gauls to burn their own towns and farms, so the Roman army would find no food. Dozens of settlements went up in flames. But when it came to Avaricum, the Bituriges begged him to spare it. They argued that the marshes and rivers made it almost impossible to take.

They were not wrong about the landscape. Rivers and wetlands surrounded the town on most sides. Today the Yèvre and the Auron still run through Bourges, and the marshes to the east are still worked as small market gardens. In Caesar's time, only one narrow approach led to the walls on firm ground. Vercingetorix gave way, and the town was spared from fire. It was a decision with terrible consequences.

The defences of Avaricum were remarkable in their own right. Caesar describes a type of wall that historians now call the 'murus gallicus', which simply means 'Gallic wall' in Latin. Long timber beams were laid in the earth and filled with rubble, then faced with stone. The stone protected the wood from fire. The wooden frame protected the stone from battering rams. It was strong, flexible engineering, and Caesar clearly respected it.

So the Romans built instead of breaking. On that single dry approach, Caesar's soldiers raised a huge siege terrace, a ramp of earth and timber that climbed toward the top of the wall. They worked through cold, wet weather and short supplies. The defenders fought back hard. According to Caesar, they dug tunnels under the ramp, set it on fire, and raised their own towers higher and higher. The siege lasted for several weeks.

The end came in the rain. Caesar writes that during a heavy downpour, the guards on the wall grew less watchful. He ordered his men to attack quietly, and they reached the ramparts before the alarm spread. What followed was a massacre. Caesar himself claims that almost the entire population died, and that only a few hundred escaped to Vercingetorix. His numbers may be exaggerated, but the story is grim in any version.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The siege of Avaricum was not the end of the revolt. Later that same year, the Gauls would make their famous last stand at Alesia, far to the east. Avaricum was an earlier chapter in that story. Yet it shaped what came next. It showed the Gauls that even a strong town, on good ground, could fall to Roman patience and engineering.

The town did not disappear. Under Rome, Avaricum was rebuilt as a Gallo-Roman city, with stone buildings, roads, and public spaces. In late antiquity it became an important regional capital. Around that time, as the empire felt less secure, the people built a new stone wall around the high ground. That wall was smaller than the old Gallic town, but it was solid. And here is the second surprise: parts of it are still standing.

As you walk through the old centre, watch for sections of very old masonry under later houses. In places, you can see bands of flat red brick laid between rows of small stone blocks. That pattern is a common sign of late Roman building. Medieval builders often used the Roman wall as a foundation, so the ancient line hides in plain sight. Some later towers sit directly on Roman work.

To see objects from Avaricum up close, the Musée du Berry in the old town holds a strong collection of Gallo-Roman finds. Many are carved funerary stones showing ordinary people: craftsmen with their tools, families, travellers. They are a gentle contrast to Caesar's war story. They show the daily life that continued on this hill once the fighting stopped, for centuries, under a new name and a new rule.

The name itself changed slowly. Over time, the people here came to be called after the Bituriges rather than the old town name, and that tribal name softened, century by century, into 'Bourges'. So every time someone says the name of this city, they are still speaking of that Celtic people. The marsh, the rivers, and the rise of the ground are the same ones Caesar saw.

Look around you once more. The half-timbered houses, the cathedral, the cafés are all much later. But the shape of the hill is the shape the Bituriges defended. As you walk downhill toward the water, notice how the streets fall away. Somewhere along that slope, Roman soldiers once dug, built, and waited in the rain.
