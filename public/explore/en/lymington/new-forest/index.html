The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beneath the wind there is a low, steady tearing sound. It is a pony pulling at short grass, close to the ground, the way it has been done here for centuries. The air smells of peat and gorse, sweet and a little like coconut when the yellow flowers are out. Underfoot the ground gives slightly. This is the New Forest, and it begins almost at the edge of Lymington.

The first surprise is in the name. Much of the New Forest is not forest at all. Large stretches are open heathland, with heather, gorse and bracken under a wide sky. Woodland comes and goes in patches. Some are ancient oak and beech, some are newer plantations of conifer. Between them run boggy valley mires and slow streams. The word forest once meant something different. It meant land set aside for royal hunting, not land covered in trees.

And new is relative. William the Conqueror, the Norman duke who took the English crown in 1066, claimed this land as a hunting ground soon after his conquest. It already appears as a royal forest in the Domesday Book of 1086. That great survey recorded who owned what across England. So this forest has been called new for more than nine hundred years. Under forest law, the deer belonged to the king, and harsh penalties protected them.

Those hunting rights carry one of England's best-known royal mysteries. In the year 1100, William's son, King William the Second, was killed by an arrow while hunting in the forest. He was known as Rufus, probably because of his red face or hair. Chroniclers named a nobleman called Walter Tyrrell as the archer. Whether it was an accident or a murder has been argued ever since. A memorial called the Rufus Stone marks the place where tradition says he fell.

Now look at the animals around you. The ponies, the cattle, even the occasional donkey are not wild. Every one of them has an owner. These owners are called commoners. They hold ancient rights, attached to their property, to let animals graze on the open forest. This right of common pasture is the reason the landscape looks the way it does. The grazing keeps the heath open and the lawns short, like a living lawnmower.

Pasture is only one of the old rights. There is estovers, the right to collect firewood. There is turbary, the right to cut peat for fuel. And there is mast, the right to put pigs out in autumn. Locals call this season pannage. The pigs wander under the oaks and eat fallen acorns. This matters, because green acorns can poison ponies and cattle. If you are here in autumn, you may meet a pig on the road.

Who keeps order in all this? The answer is the Verderers, a body with medieval roots that still meets today. Their court sits in the Queen's House in Lyndhurst, the forest's main village. Under them work the Agisters. These are local people who ride or drive across the forest, checking on the animals. They attend accidents, round up ponies and collect fees from owners. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The Agisters cut a pattern into a pony's tail. It shows which area the pony belongs to, and that its fees are paid.

The pony itself is a breed of its own, the New Forest pony. It is sturdy and sure-footed, built to live outdoors all year. In autumn, the ponies are gathered in round-ups called drifts. Riders sweep across the heath and bring them into pens. There, foals are checked and marked, and some are sold. Watch how the ponies move. They know the roads, the shade and the water. Many will not move aside for a car. That is why cattle grids mark the forest edges, and why drivers here go slowly.

The woods hold their own life. Fallow deer are the most common, often seen in small groups at the edge of the trees. Red deer, roe deer and sika deer live here too. In the older woods, some beech and oak trees have been pollarded in the past. That means their tops were cut, and many branches grew out from one thick trunk. Rotting wood is left where it falls. It feeds beetles, fungi and birds. Stand still, and you may hear a woodpecker drumming somewhere above.

The heath is one of the rarer habitats in Europe. Lowland heath like this once covered much more of southern England. Most of it was lost to farming and building. Here it survives, because the commoning system never stopped. In spring and summer, ground-nesting birds raise their young among the heather. That is why some areas ask you to keep dogs on a lead. Look closely at the wet ground, too. Tiny sundew plants grow in the bogs and catch insects on their sticky red leaves.

Lymington sits on the southern edge of all this, where the forest meets the sea. The town looks out over the Solent, the strait that separates mainland England from the Isle of Wight. For centuries the marshes along this coast were used to make salt, by evaporating seawater. Today the harbour is full of yachts, and a ferry crosses to the island. In a short drive you can go from salt marsh to open heath to deep woodland. Few places in England change so quickly.

In 2005 the New Forest became a National Park. That gave the landscape new protection. But it did not replace the old system. The Verderers, the Agisters and the commoners still shape daily life here. Pressure is real, though. Millions of visits each year bring traffic, litter and disturbance. Ponies are hurt on the roads. Feeding them, even with an apple, teaches them to approach people and cars. It is against the forest bylaws, and locals take it seriously.

So walk slowly, and keep a respectful distance. Give the animals space, especially mares with foals. Stay on the main tracks when the ground is wet, because the mires can be deeper than they look. Early morning and late evening are the quietest times. Mist lies low over the heath, and the deer come out of the trees. Bring good shoes. Even in summer, many paths are muddy and uneven.

Before you go, look once more at the open ground in front of you. The short grass, the scattered gorse, the line of trees in the distance. None of it is untouched wilderness. It is the work of animals and people, over hundreds of years, following rules first written for a Norman king. Listen again for that soft sound of grazing. It is the same sound this land has known for nine centuries... and it will still be here when you walk away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Never feed or touch the ponies, even with fruit.
Drive slowly; animals roam freely across unfenced roads.
Visit at dawn or dusk to see deer at the woodland edge.
Wear waterproof boots; heath paths and mires stay boggy.
Keep dogs on leads during the ground-nesting bird season.
