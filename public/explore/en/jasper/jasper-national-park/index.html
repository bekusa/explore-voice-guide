The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere close, a river runs over stones, steady and cold. The air carries the sharp smell of spruce and wet rock. Above you, the mountains rise in grey and white layers, and the light changes on their faces as clouds move past. This is Jasper National Park, in the Canadian province of Alberta. Here, the scale of the land arrives slowly. It takes a while to understand how far you can actually see.

Jasper is the largest national park in the Canadian Rockies. It covers more than eleven thousand square kilometres of peaks, valleys, glaciers, lakes and forest. That is larger than some small countries. Most visitors see only a thin line of it, along the roads and the rail line that follow the valley floors. The rest is backcountry. It is wild ground, where trails run for days without passing a building.

People were here long before the park existed. Several Indigenous nations lived in, hunted in, and travelled through these valleys for thousands of years. The mountain passes were routes for trade and movement. The modern park often describes this land as a shared homeland, and in recent years Parks Canada has worked more closely with Indigenous communities on how the land is cared for. Their history in this landscape is far older than any name on a map.

The name Jasper comes from the fur trade. In the early nineteenth century, a trading post in the Athabasca valley became known as Jasper's House. The name came from Jasper Hawes, a clerk who ran it. The post was a small, remote stop on the long routes that fur traders followed across the mountains. That modest name outlived the post. Today it belongs to a town, a park, and a landscape of enormous size.

The park itself was created in 1907, at first as a forest park. The railway soon followed. In the early twentieth century, rail lines were pushed through the Yellowhead Pass to the west, and the town grew up around the tracks. You can still hear the trains today. Long freight cars roll through the valley, and the deep sound of the horn moves between the mountain walls. That sound has belonged to Jasper for more than a century.

Here is something many visitors miss. Jasper is not only a daytime park. In 2011 it was named a Dark Sky Preserve, one of the largest in the world. Lighting in the area is managed to keep the night sky dark. On a clear night away from town, the Milky Way crosses the sky as a pale band. In some seasons, and with some luck, the northern lights appear as green curtains above the ridges.

Jasper is also part of a larger World Heritage Site. Together with neighbouring parks such as Banff, it forms the Canadian Rocky Mountain Parks, listed by UNESCO in 1984. UNESCO is the United Nations agency that recognises places of outstanding value to all humanity. The listing honours the mountain scenery, but also the glaciers, the caves, the fossil beds, and the living systems that connect them. It is a whole landscape, not a single view.

To the south, the park reaches the Columbia Icefield, one of the largest bodies of ice in the Rocky Mountains. The Athabasca Glacier flows down from it toward the road. Markers along the approach show where the ice once reached, and the distance between them is sobering. The glacier has pulled back a long way within living memory. Standing there, you can feel the cold air that pours off the ice, even on a summer day.

The water in this park behaves in surprising ways. Medicine Lake is the strangest example. Each year, in summer, it fills with meltwater. Then, in autumn, it can shrink to almost nothing — a few channels running across a wide, muddy flat. The water does not vanish into the air. It drains through holes in the limestone below, into an underground river system. For a long time, people had no idea where the water went.

Some of that hidden water appears again in Maligne Canyon. Here, the river has cut a narrow, deep slot through the limestone. Bridges cross the gap, and you can look straight down into the dark, wet rock. In places, springs pour out of the canyon walls. In winter, the canyon freezes, and the walls hang with columns of blue and white ice. Guided walks lead people along the frozen floor of the gorge.

Wildlife is part of daily life here. Elk graze on open meadows and sometimes on lawns near town. Bighorn sheep stand on rocky slopes beside the road. Black bears and grizzly bears live in the park, along with wolves, moose and mountain goats. A small number of caribou survive in the high country, and their herds are closely protected. In autumn, the elk rut fills the valleys with a high, eerie bugling call, and the males become dangerous to approach.

The park has also lived through hard years. In the summer of 2024, a large wildfire reached the town of Jasper. Many homes and businesses were lost, and large areas of forest burned. The community has been rebuilding since then. As you travel, you may see blackened trunks on some slopes. You may also see new green growth beneath them. Fire has always been part of this forest, but that summer marked the town in a deep way.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. Notice the colour of the water, if there is water nearby — often a milky blue-green from fine rock flour ground by glaciers. Notice the line where the trees stop and bare rock begins. Listen again for the river, for wind in the spruce, perhaps for a distant train. These mountains will look slightly different every hour you spend here. Let them change, and walk on slowly.

Before you go

Practical tips

Keep at least one hundred metres from bears and wolves.
Give rutting elk wide space during the autumn season.
Drive slowly at dawn and dusk when animals cross roads.
Pack warm layers even in summer near the icefield.
Head away from town lights for clear night skies.
