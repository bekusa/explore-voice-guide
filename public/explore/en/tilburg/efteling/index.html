The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere close by, a gentle voice is calling out the same two words, over and over, from inside a fat little figure with an open mouth. Gravel crunches underfoot. Tall trees close in overhead, and the light turns green and soft. The air smells of damp leaves and woodsmoke. You have just walked into Efteling, in the village of Kaatsheuvel, a short drive north of Tilburg.

Efteling is a theme park, but it does not feel like most theme parks. There are no hard edges here. There are no shouting brands. Paths wind through old woodland. Roofs sag and curl as if they grew there. Even the rubbish bins have personalities. This place belongs to the Dutch in a very personal way. Many grandparents walking these paths first came here as children, holding the hands of their own grandparents.

Here is the first surprise. Efteling opened in 1952. That is several years before Disneyland opened in California. So this is not a European copy of an American idea. It grew from its own roots, in its own forest, with its own very Dutch sense of storytelling. It started out small and quiet. In those first years, it was mostly a nature park with a playground, a sports field, and a modest walk through fairy tales.

The people behind it came from very different worlds. A local mayor wanted a place for the region's families to enjoy. A filmmaker, Peter Reijnders, brought a sense of how to stage a scene. And then there was the illustrator Anton Pieck. In the Netherlands, his name is instantly familiar. His drawings show crooked houses, warm lamplight, and cosy, cluttered rooms full of old objects. That gentle, old-world style became the visual language of the whole park.

The heart of it all is the Fairytale Forest. This is where Efteling began, and it is still the place to slow down. Small scenes sit among the trees, each one telling a well-known story. You might find Little Red Riding Hood, or Sleeping Beauty breathing softly in her tower. Some scenes move. Some talk. Some simply wait for you to lean in. Children press against the railings. Adults often go quiet, because many of them remember these exact scenes from long ago.

Now, about that voice you heard at the start. It belongs to Holle Bolle Gijs. He is a greedy, round-bellied figure who works as a rubbish bin. Feed him a scrap of paper, and he thanks you. In Dutch, he calls out for paper, again and again. It is a simple trick, but it works. Children hunt for litter just to hear him speak. Watch a group of kids near one, and you will see a park kept clean by pure delight.

After Anton Pieck, another designer shaped the look of Efteling for decades. His name was Ton van de Ven. He took the softness of the fairy tale forest and carried it into much larger attractions. Under his hand, the park grew from a woodland walk into something much bigger. Yet the buildings kept their hand-made feeling. Look at a roof line here, and it rarely runs straight. Look at a window frame, and it often leans a little, like a house from a picture book.

In 1986, Efteling opened Fata Morgana. It is a slow boat ride through an imagined city from the Thousand and One Nights, the old cycle of Middle Eastern tales. You drift past markets, palaces, and dark corners full of figures. Then, in 1993, came Droomvlucht, which means dream flight. You float above a world of elves, castles, and glowing forests. For many Dutch families, these two rides are the true soul of the park. They are not fast. They are dreamy, and that is the point.

But Efteling also has a wilder side. In 1981, it opened Python, a steel rollercoaster with loops that roars over a lake. Later came wooden coasters and sharper thrills. One newer ride, Baron 1898, drops riders straight down into a dark mine shaft. It is wrapped in a dark legend about a greedy mine owner. Even the scariest rides here come with a story. The park rarely builds a machine just for speed. It almost always builds a tale first.

This mix is the second surprise. Efteling manages to be gentle and thrilling at the same time. A toddler can spend a full day in the forest and never see a rollercoaster. A teenager can do the opposite. And somehow, the two worlds sit side by side without clashing. Part of the reason is the trees. The woodland hides one area from the next. You walk around a corner, and the mood changes completely, like turning a page.

The park also changes with the seasons. On cool days, the paths fill with the smell of fresh pancakes and hot chocolate. In winter, Efteling often opens a winter season, with fires, lights, and frosty air over the ponds. Evenings bring a different mood. Lamps glow in the windows of the little houses. The water shows reflections of towers and roofs. Many visitors say the park feels most like itself when the light is low.

Water plays a large role here. Lakes and ponds run through the grounds. Boats glide across them. Fountains rise and fall in timed shows, set to music. Even in the Fairytale Forest, you hear trickling streams and splashing wells. The sound of water softens the crowds. It also cools the air on warm summer afternoons. Stand near a pond for a moment, and the voices around you seem to fade a little.

What makes Efteling special is not one ride or one building. It is the care in the small things. A mushroom that plays music. A tree with a face. A carved door handle shaped like an animal. Many visitors walk straight past these details. Others come back year after year, just to find something they missed. The park rewards slow walking, and it rewards curiosity, at any age.

So before you move on, pause for a moment. Look at the path in front of you, and the way it bends out of sight. Listen for the rustle of leaves, the splash of water, a child laughing somewhere in the trees. And if you hear that small voice calling for paper... perhaps find a scrap in your pocket. Feed him. Then walk on, slowly, and see what the forest shows you next.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Fairytale Forest early, before families arrive.
Stay into the evening when lamps light the houses.
Wear comfortable shoes for long woodland gravel paths.
Bring scraps of paper for Holle Bolle Gijs.
Check the park app for current ride queue times.
