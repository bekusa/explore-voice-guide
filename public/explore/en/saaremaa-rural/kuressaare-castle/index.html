The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here on the edge of the moat, the wind comes in off the Baltic and moves through the reeds. Ducks paddle across dark water. And above them rises a square block of pale grey stone, smooth and plain. It looks almost too complete to be real. This is Kuressaare Castle, on the island of Saaremaa, off the west coast of Estonia. Many people call it the best-preserved medieval stone castle in the Baltic states.

That pale stone is dolomite, a hard local rock. Saaremaa sits on thick beds of it, and islanders have quarried it for centuries. It is cut into neat blocks and laid in tight courses. In soft afternoon light it can look almost silver. On a grey day it turns the colour of wet ash. Stand close and run your eyes across the surface. You will see small fossils and fine layers in some blocks. These are traces of an ancient sea floor.

Who built a fortress this serious on a quiet island? The answer is the Church. In the Middle Ages, this part of the Baltic was ruled by crusader powers. They were German-speaking knights and bishops who came here in the thirteenth century. They conquered the local Estonian population by force. Saaremaa and the nearby western mainland formed the Bishopric of Ösel-Wiek. Ösel is the old German name for Saaremaa. Kuressaare became the bishop's main seat, his home and his stronghold.

The stone castle you see took shape mostly in the fourteenth century. Its plan follows a model that was common in the Baltic lands. Historians call it a convent castle. It is a compact square of wings around a small central courtyard. The design is part monastery and part fortress. Clergy could live and pray here behind thick walls. The building was defensive, but it was also a place of daily routine. There were hours of prayer, shared meals, and a strict order to the day.

Now look up at the two towers. They are not twins. The slimmer, taller one is the watchtower, known as Tall Hermann. From the top, a lookout could see ships approaching across the flat island and the sea. The heavier tower is the defence tower. It is built to absorb attack and hold out. Together they give the castle its outline, a block with one sharp point and one stout shoulder.

Here is something many visitors miss. The building in front of you is only the heart of the fortress. Walk around the grounds and notice how the land rises and falls. There are earthen ramparts, star-shaped bastions and a wide moat. These were added later, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. By then cannons had changed warfare. High stone walls were no longer enough. Engineers built low, angled earthworks to absorb cannon fire and give defenders clear lines of fire.

Those later works also mark a change of rulers. In the mid sixteenth century, the old church states of the Baltic collapsed in war. The bishop sold his lands to Denmark. In the seventeenth century, Saaremaa passed to Sweden. Then, after the Great Northern War in the early eighteenth century, the island became part of the Russian Empire. Each power left marks on the defences. The medieval core stayed largely as it was, and that is one reason it survives so well.

There is a darker story connected to the castle. According to a popular tale, workers once opened a sealed chamber inside the walls. They found a skeleton there, some say still in old clothing. The local legend grew from that discovery. It says a knight was walled up alive as punishment, perhaps for plotting against the bishop. Nobody knows if this is true. But guides on the island still tell the story, and many visitors look for that small room.

Inside, the castle is now home to the Saaremaa Museum. As you move through the building, notice the vaulted ceilings. Their stone ribs meet overhead like branches. The rooms that once served the clergy are large and quiet: halls for meetings, a refectory where the community ate, and rooms for sleeping. Narrow stairways twist inside the thickness of the walls. The steps are worn smooth, so watch your footing.

The museum's collections cover the island's long story. That story goes far beyond bishops and knights. Saaremaa has been home to farmers, fishermen, sailors and smugglers. It has known foreign rule many times, including the Soviet period in the twentieth century. During those decades the town even had a different name. It was called Kingissepp, after a Soviet revolutionary. Kuressaare got its old name back as Estonia moved toward independence again.

Step back outside and look at the town around you. Kuressaare is small, low and green. In the nineteenth century it became a spa resort. Visitors came here to bathe in healing mud from the coast. The castle grounds turned into a park with shady paths and benches. People came to walk, not to fight. Today you can still feel that slow, leisurely mood. On summer evenings, families walk along the moat and children throw bread to the ducks.

Before you go, try one more view. Walk to the far side of the moat and turn back toward the castle. From there, the plain walls and the two towers line up against the sky. Notice how few windows face outward. The builders did not make it to impress anyone. It was made to last, and it has. Wars, empires and border changes came and went around it. The walls stayed in place.

So take a last breath of that sea air. Listen to the water against the bank and the wind in the trees on the ramparts. When you walk back toward the town, look over your shoulder once. The grey tower of Tall Hermann will still be there above the rooftops. Lookouts once stood on that tower and watched the same flat horizon.
