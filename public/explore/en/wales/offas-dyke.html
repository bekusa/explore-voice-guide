The guide

The story

Put your hand flat on the turf. Under the grass is a long, rounded ridge of earth. It rises slightly above the land around it and runs away from you in both directions, over the hill and out of sight. Sheep have grazed it smooth. The wind comes across the open ground and moves the bracken. At first, it looks like a natural fold in the hillside. It is not. People built this bank by hand, more than a thousand years ago.

This is Offa's Dyke. In Welsh it is called Clawdd Offa. It is a great earthwork, a bank of soil with a ditch beside it, and it follows the borderland between Wales and England. It is one of the longest ancient monuments in Britain. You are standing on a line that people have argued over, crossed, defended and walked for a very long time.

The name comes from Offa, king of Mercia. Mercia was a powerful Anglo-Saxon kingdom in the English Midlands. Offa ruled for nearly forty years in the second half of the eighth century. In that time he became one of the strongest rulers in early medieval Britain. To his west were the Welsh kingdoms, among them Powys. Relations along this frontier were often violent, with raids and counter-raids on both sides.

How do we connect the dyke to Offa? The key source is a Welsh monk named Asser. Late in the ninth century, he wrote a life of King Alfred the Great. In it, he mentions a king called Offa who ordered a great dyke built between Britain and Mercia, from sea to sea. That one sentence, written about a century after Offa's death, is the foundation of the name you see on the maps today.

Now look at the shape of the earthwork. Along most of its length, the ditch lies on the western side, facing Wales. The bank rises behind it on the eastern, Mercian side. This tells us something important. The builders were looking west. Whoever stood on top of this bank had a clear view into Welsh territory. In many places, the line was set out to take advantage of the slope and give that wide view.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Historians still do not agree on exactly what the dyke was for. Some see it as a defensive barrier, perhaps once topped by a timber fence or patrolled by watchmen. Others see it as a statement of power, a way to mark where Mercian control began. It may have controlled the movement of people and cattle. It may have done all of these things at once.

And here is a second surprise. The dyke is not one unbroken wall. There are long stretches where no bank survives, or where it may never have been built. Some gaps fall where rivers or steep ground did the job instead. Archaeologists have also debated whether every section really belongs to Offa's time. Recent excavations have raised the possibility that some parts are older, or were built in different phases. The story is still being written.

The dyke also has a neighbour. In north-east Wales, a second earthwork called Wat's Dyke runs roughly parallel, a little to the east. It is shorter, and its date is also debated. Having two great banks so close together shows how much effort early rulers put into this border. It also reminds us that the frontier between Welsh and English land moved over the centuries. It was never a fixed line.

Over time, the dyke gathered stories. According to one popular tale, a Welshman found on the English side of the dyke could lose his ears, and an Englishman found on the Welsh side could lose his life. There is no reliable evidence for this. It is folklore, not law. But the story shows how strongly people felt about this line in the land, long after the Mercian kingdom had gone.

The dyke has also left its mark on place names. The town of Knighton, in Powys, has the Welsh name Tref-y-Clawdd. That means the town on the dyke. The earthwork passes right beside it. In many farms and villages along the border, the dyke is simply part of the landscape. Hedges grow along it. Field walls meet it. Lanes cross it, and many local people walk past it every day without a second thought.

Today, most visitors meet the dyke through the Offa's Dyke Path. This is a long-distance national trail that opened in 1971. It runs the full length of the border country, from the Severn estuary near Chepstow in the south to the sea at Prestatyn in the north. The path does not follow the earthwork everywhere. But in many sections, especially in the hills, you walk directly beside the old bank or along its top.

The walking changes a great deal along the way. In the south, the route passes above the Wye Valley, with woodland and river views. In the middle, the hills of the border open up, and some of the best-preserved stretches of the dyke cross high, quiet ground. In the north, the land rises toward the Clwydian hills before dropping to the coast. Walkers who do the whole route usually take around two weeks.

The monument is protected, so please treat it gently. Keep to the path where it is marked, and do not climb or dig into the bank. Erosion from many feet is a real problem in popular sections. Gates and stiles cross farmland, so close every gate behind you. Sheep and cattle often graze right on the earthwork. The dyke has survived more than a thousand years of weather. With a little care, it can survive many more.

So take one more look along the line of the bank. Notice how it bends to follow the slope, and how the ditch still holds a little shadow on its western side. Somewhere far behind you, there was a king who wanted this frontier marked. Ahead of you, the hills of Wales go on into the distance. As you walk on, keep an eye on the ground beside you. The dyke is often right there, low and green, quietly following you.
