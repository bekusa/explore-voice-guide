The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. You may hear wind moving through dry grass and insects in the scrub. The ground under your feet is uneven, with low stones breaking through the soil. Around you, gentle hills roll away toward the plain of Thessaly. Nothing here rises high. There are no columns and no marble. Yet this quiet hilltop is one of the oldest farming villages in Europe, and people lived on it for thousands of years.

Welcome to Sesklo. The site lies a short drive west of Volos, in the low hills between the coast and the Thessalian plain. Archaeologists date the first settlement here to around the seventh millennium before Christ. That is roughly nine thousand years ago. It is several thousand years older than the palaces of Mycenae, and older than the first pyramids of Egypt. When you stand here, you stand on one of the earliest chapters of European village life.

Let us think about what that means. The people who settled this hill were Neolithic farmers. The word Neolithic means the New Stone Age, the period when humans first began to grow crops and keep animals instead of only hunting and gathering. Here they grew cereals and pulses. They raised sheep and goats. They made tools from stone and bone. Slowly, a group of houses on a hill became a permanent community.

The settlement was first excavated in the early twentieth century by the Greek archaeologist Christos Tsountas. He was one of the pioneers of prehistoric archaeology in Greece. He uncovered house foundations and large amounts of pottery, and his work showed that Thessaly held some of the deepest prehistory in the Aegean world. Later, in the second half of the twentieth century, the archaeologist Dimitrios Theocharis returned to the site and greatly expanded our picture of it.

One of the most important findings is that Sesklo was larger than the hill you see first. Archaeologists divide the site into two parts. The first is the raised area often called the acropolis, which simply means the high town. The second is a much wider settlement that spread across the surrounding ground. Much of that outer area is not dug out, so a large part of Sesklo still lies under the fields. Some estimates suggest that a few thousand people may have lived here at its peak.

Now look at the stone foundations around you. These low walls are the bases of houses. The builders laid stone at the bottom, and above it they raised walls of mudbrick, made from clay mixed with straw and dried in the sun. The roofs were probably made of timber and reeds. Mudbrick does not survive well, so only the stone footings remain. When you see these outlines, try to picture walls above them, rooms, smoke, and the smell of cooking fires.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. On the acropolis stands the outline of a building with a particular plan — a main room entered through a porch at the front. Archaeologists call this layout a megaron. Thousands of years later, the same basic form appears at the heart of Mycenaean palaces, and even in the plan of early Greek temples. The building at Sesklo is often described as one of the earliest examples of this shape on Greek soil.

The name of this village is also the name of a whole culture. Archaeologists use the term Sesklo culture for the Middle Neolithic period across Thessaly and nearby regions. The clearest sign of it is the pottery. Potters here made fine vessels with a light surface painted in red designs — zigzags, triangles, and flame-like patterns. Pieces of this painted ware have been found at many sites, which shows that ideas and skills travelled between villages.

The people of Sesklo also made small clay figurines. Many of them show human figures, often women, sometimes seated. Nobody knows for certain what they meant. Some scholars link them to ideas of fertility and household life. Others are more cautious and say we simply do not know. It is worth holding both views. These small objects show that people here made images of themselves, and that they thought about more than food and shelter.

If you want to see these finds, many of them are kept in the Athanasakeion Archaeological Museum in Volos. Seeing the pottery and figurines there gives life to the low walls you see here. The site and the museum work well together. The hilltop gives you the space and the landscape. The museum gives you the faces, the colours, and the craft of the people who lived on it.

Sesklo did not last forever in the same form. Toward the end of the Middle Neolithic period, around the late sixth millennium before Christ, the settlement suffered a major destruction by fire. Life on the hill did continue later, but on a smaller scale. By then a new pattern of village life was growing nearby, at the site of Dimini, closer to the coast. Archaeologists use the name Dimini culture for this later stage.

That second surprise is worth a pause. Sesklo and Dimini are only a few kilometres apart, and together they tell a long story of change in one small corner of Greece. If you have time, visit both on the same day. Sesklo shows the older, open village. Dimini shows a later settlement with rings of stone walls. Seeing them one after the other makes the passage of time feel very real.

Before you leave, walk slowly to the highest point and turn in a full circle. Notice the shape of the hills, the line of the plain, and the direction of the sea toward Volos. The first farmers chose this place for reasons you can still feel — good soil, open views, and shelter. The grass moves in the wind, the stones sit low in the earth, and the same sun crosses the same hill. As you walk back down, you follow paths that people have walked for nine thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with nearby Dimini for a fuller story.
See the finds at the Athanasakeion Archaeological Museum in Volos.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and loose stones.
Bring water and a hat — shade is limited.
Visit early morning for softer light and cooler air.
