The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Behind you, the traffic of Volos hums along the waterfront. In front of you, the Pagasetic Gulf lies flat and pale, a wide sheltered bay held in by low hills. The air carries salt, a little diesel from the harbour, and on warm days the smell of pine drifting down from the mountain. This is the ground that ancient Greeks called Iolcus, a name that has lasted far longer than any wall built here.

Iolcus is two places at once. One is a real Bronze Age settlement, traced by archaeologists in and around modern Volos. The other is a place of myth, one of the most famous starting points in all of Greek storytelling. The two overlap but never fully match. As you walk here, it helps to keep both in mind, the town of pottery and foundations, and the town of heroes, kings and a ship called the Argo.

Let us begin with the legend, because it is the reason most people know the name. According to the myth, Iolcus was ruled by a king named Aeson. His half-brother Pelias took the throne from him. Fearing for the life of Aeson's young son, the family sent the child away in secret. That boy was Jason, and the story says he was raised in the forests of the mountain that rises behind this city.

That mountain is Pelion, and you can see its long green ridge from almost anywhere in Volos. In myth, Pelion was the home of the centaurs, creatures who were half human and half horse. The wisest of them, Chiron, was said to be a teacher of heroes. Legend has it that he taught Jason. Look up at those slopes now. The villages and chestnut woods up there still carry Chiron's name in local stories, shop signs and festivals.

When Jason grew up, the tale continues, he came down from the mountain to claim his father's throne. On the way he crossed a flooded river and lost one sandal in the current. Pelias had been warned by an oracle to fear a man wearing only one shoe. So when this stranger walked into Iolcus half barefoot, the king knew his danger had arrived. It is one of the most memorable small details in Greek myth.

Pelias did not fight the young man directly. Instead, he set him an impossible task. Jason must sail to the far edge of the known world and bring back the Golden Fleece, the golden wool of a magical ram. The land where the Fleece was kept was Colchis, on the eastern shore of the Black Sea. Here is a surprise for many visitors... Colchis lies in what is today western Georgia, thousands of kilometres from where you stand.

For the journey, the legend says, a great ship was built from the timber of Pelion. It was called the Argo, and its crew became known as the Argonauts, the sailors of the Argo. Among them, in various versions, were heroes such as Heracles and Orpheus. They set out from this gulf. So the calm water in front of you is, in the story, the launch point of the first great sea voyage in Greek imagination.

Now for the history beneath the myth. Archaeologists have found evidence that this area was settled for thousands of years, long before classical Greece. Several sites around the edge of the gulf have produced Bronze Age remains. Near Dimini, a few kilometres west of the city centre, excavations uncovered a substantial Mycenaean settlement. Mycenaean refers to the Greek civilisation of the late Bronze Age, the world that Homer's heroes were later imagined to belong to.

Inside Volos itself, attention has long focused on a low hill in the old quarter known as Palia, close to the waterfront. People here often call it Kastro, which simply means castle. Digging on and around this hill has revealed layers of occupation reaching back to prehistoric times. Many scholars connect it with ancient Iolcus. Others point to Dimini or nearby sites. The honest answer is that the exact location is still discussed, and Iolcus may have been spread across more than one place.

That uncertainty is part of the appeal. In Palia, later history sits right on top of the earlier layers. The hill was fortified again in Byzantine and Ottoman times, and pieces of old walling still appear between modern buildings. Look at how the street level rises and falls. Small changes in height often mark where buried remains lie. A modern city grew over the ancient one, so Iolcus survives mostly underground, beneath apartments, workshops and quiet lanes.

If you want to see what came out of the ground, the Athanasakeion Archaeological Museum in Volos is the place to go. It holds finds from Dimini and Sesklo, another important prehistoric site nearby, along with painted grave stelai, which are carved and painted stone markers for tombs. Seeing the pottery and tools up close makes the myth feel more human. These are objects used by real families, people who also looked out across this same gulf every morning.

It is worth noticing how strongly the city still holds on to its legend. The Argo and Jason appear in names, statues and souvenirs along the waterfront. Ferries leave from the harbour for the Sporades islands, so ships still depart from here toward the open sea. Locals are proud of the connection, and you may hear older residents tell the sandal story to children. Here the myth is not only on a museum label. It is still part of the city's everyday life.

Before you move on, turn once more toward the water. Notice the boats tied along the quay, the light on the surface, and the shape of Pelion behind the rooftops. Somewhere under your feet lie the traces of a Bronze Age town. Somewhere in the stories people tell here, a young man still walks in with one sandal. As you leave, listen for the harbour sounds, and look at the gulf where the Argo set out.

Practical tips

Visit the Athanasakeion Archaeological Museum to see local finds.

Come near sunset for soft light over the gulf.
Drive up Pelion afterwards to explore the Chiron legends.
