The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the wind moves across open fields, and somewhere in the distance you may hear the low hum of machinery. Underfoot, the ground feels ordinary, soft earth and dry grass. But just below the surface lies a Roman city. It once held tens of thousands of people, and archaeologists are still uncovering it, one trench at a time.

This is Viminacium. In the Roman era it stood near the place where the Mlava River meets the Danube, on the northern edge of the empire. Today the site sits close to the town of Kostolac in eastern Serbia. Across the great river, the flat farmland of the Banat region stretches toward the horizon. For the Romans, this river was more than scenery. It was the frontier itself, the line between the empire and the peoples beyond it.

Viminacium began as a military base. A Roman legion, the Seventh Claudia, was stationed here. A legion was a professional army unit of several thousand soldiers. Wherever soldiers settled, others followed. Traders, craftsmen, families and veterans built homes outside the fortress walls. Over time that civilian settlement grew into a true city, and the city grew in importance.

It became the capital of the province of Moesia Superior, or Upper Moesia, which covered much of what is now Serbia. Under the emperor Hadrian, in the second century, Viminacium received the rank of a municipium. That was a formal Roman town status with its own local government. Later, in the third century, it was raised to a colonia, the highest civic rank a provincial city could hold.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Viminacium had its own mint. From the year 239, coins were struck here in the city's name. These coins often show a female figure who represents the province, standing between a bull and a lion. The animals stand for the two legions of the region. Coins from Viminacium appear in museum collections far from Serbia, so this small field once sent its name across the Roman world.

Now look around and imagine the scale. The city covered a large area of this plain, with streets, baths, workshops and public buildings. Most of it is still underground. What you see today are selected areas that archaeologists have opened and protected. Some sit under modern shelters that keep out the rain and frost. In many ways the excavation itself is the attraction. You are walking through a working research site, not a finished museum.

One of the most important discoveries here is the amphitheatre. Archaeologists identified it only in recent decades, and that surprised many scholars. In an amphitheatre, crowds watched gladiator fights and shows with animals. Here, much of the structure was built in wood on stone foundations, and parts have been reconstructed so you can understand its shape. Stand inside the arena and look up at the seating. It gives you a sense of the noise and the crowd that once filled this space.

The dead of Viminacium have told archaeologists almost as much as the living. Thousands of graves have been excavated around the city, making this one of the largest Roman burial grounds studied anywhere. Some tombs have painted interiors. Their walls carry images of figures, plants and decorative patterns in red, ochre and other earth colours. These paintings are fragile, so they are carefully protected. If you have the chance to see one, take a moment with it. These are private images, made for people who never expected strangers to see them.

Nearby stands the site of a large mausoleum. Some researchers have suggested that it may be connected with a Roman emperor, but this remains a theory, not a proven fact. Treat it as an open question. It is a good reminder that archaeology is often a conversation between evidence and careful guesswork. At Viminacium, new finds can still change that conversation.

Now for the second surprise, and it is far older than Rome. In the coal mine next to the site, workers found the skeleton of a mammoth. It was discovered in 2009, and it is remarkably well preserved. Staff gave the mammoth the nickname Vika. The skeleton is displayed close to the Roman remains, so a single visit covers both ice age animals and imperial history. Children usually remember this part best. Many adults do too.

That coal mine and the nearby power plant are part of the story of the site. Large-scale industry in this area has brought many finds to light. It has also created real pressure to record and protect the ancient remains before the land changes. So when you hear machines in the distance, you are hearing a modern landscape working right beside an ancient one. The two exist side by side here, and that tension shapes how the site is studied.

Viminacium's long life ended in violence. In the fifth century, the Huns attacked the Danube frontier, and the city was destroyed. Later, in the sixth century, the Eastern Roman Empire tried to restore parts of the region. But the city never returned to its former size. The river kept flowing, the fields grew over the ruins, and for centuries people farmed above the streets without seeing them.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look at the ground between the excavated areas. Every flat patch of grass may hide a wall, a road or a grave. The open trenches show only a small part of the city. Somewhere under your feet there are rooms no one has entered for more than 1,500 years. As you walk back, listen again to the wind across the fields. It is the same wind the legionaries felt here on the edge of the empire.
