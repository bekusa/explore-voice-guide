The story

The wind arrives first. It moves through knee-high steppe grass with a dry hiss, bends the seed heads, and carries the smell of dust and distant snow. Ahead, scattered across an empty plateau, stand churches — alone, roofless, enormous — like ships left behind by a vanished sea. A gorge cuts the horizon, and beyond it rise the hills of another country. This silence was once one of the loudest cities in the medieval world.

Welcome to Ani, the ruined capital of medieval Armenia. A thousand years ago this plateau held palaces, markets, caravanserais, and so many chapels that chroniclers called it the city of one thousand and one churches. The number was a boast, not a census — but standing here, surrounded by domes and broken apses in every direction, the boast feels earned.

The city's great age began in 961, when King Ashot the Third of the Bagratid dynasty moved the Armenian capital here. His successors raised a double ring of walls — King Smbat's mighty towers still guard the northern approach, and you enter through them today. Behind those walls the city swelled. Some estimates put the population near one hundred thousand at its height, though scholars still argue the figure. What is certain is that Ani ranked among the great cities of its age.

The wealth came from movement. Ani sat on a branch of the Silk Road, where caravans crossed between Byzantium, Persia, and the lands beyond. Silk, spices, silver, and ideas passed through its gates and paid its tolls. Medieval writers compared the city to Constantinople and Baghdad — flattery, perhaps, but flattery that a mud-street town would never have received.

Walk toward the largest silhouette on the plateau: the cathedral. It was completed in 1001 under the architect Trdat — a master so respected that when an earthquake cracked the dome of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, the Byzantines called him in to repair it. His cathedral here uses clustered piers and pointed arches, features that European builders would not embrace for another century or more. The dome fell long ago, and now the sky does its work — light drops through the opening and moves slowly across the stone floor.

In 1064 the Seljuk Turks took the city after a siege, and Ani began its long habit of changing hands. From this era survives the Menüçehr Mosque, often called the earliest Seljuk mosque in Anatolia, perched at the very lip of the gorge. Climb carefully to its arcaded window openings — the view straight down to the river is the best in Ani, and the stonework mixes Armenian and Islamic craft in a way that summarizes the whole city.

For color, find the Church of Saint Gregory built by the merchant Tigran Honents in 1215. It stands low on the slope toward the gorge, and its interior still glows with frescoes — rare for Armenian churches, which usually kept their walls plain. Scenes from the life of Christ and of Saint Gregory cover the walls in reds and blues that have survived eight hundred winters on an open plateau.

Then there is the Church of the Redeemer — or half of it. The building stood whole for nine hundred years until a storm in the 1950s brought down one entire side, slicing the structure like a cake. What remains is a cross-section: you stand outside and see inside, apse, cornices, and dome-ring hanging in the air. Engineers have braced it, and it has become, almost by accident, the most photographed ruin in Ani.

The city's decline came in waves. Mongol raids battered it in the thirteenth century. A major earthquake in 1319 threw down towers and emptied streets. Trade routes shifted south and the caravans stopped coming. Ani did not fall in a single dramatic siege — it simply lost its reasons, family by family, until shepherds grazed sheep in the cathedral's shadow.

Its modern history is strange in its own way. The Akhurian river below marks the border with Armenia, and for much of the twentieth century Ani sat inside a closed military zone, visited only with permits and escorts. Those restrictions eased, conservation teams arrived, and in 2016 UNESCO listed Ani as a World Heritage Site. Today you wander freely where cameras were once forbidden — though the border fence across the gorge reminds you the geography is still sensitive.

Practical matters: the site lies about 45 kilometres east of Kars, an easy taxi or tour run across open steppe. Give it three unhurried hours at least — the ruins spread across the whole plateau, and distances deceive. There is almost no shade, so carry water and sun cover in summer; in late afternoon the volcanic stone turns amber and rose, and photographers get their reward. Winters here are severe and beautiful, with snow lying deep against the walls.

End at the gorge edge, near the mosque or the Honents church, and let the place settle. The river talks quietly below. On the far bank, Armenian fields face their old capital across a border neither side can cross here. The wind combs the grass over foundations of streets whose names nobody remembers. Cities are usually louder than the land they stand on... Ani has returned that loan. As you walk back toward the walls, listen — grass, wind, your own steps — the entire orchestra of a city one thousand years old.
