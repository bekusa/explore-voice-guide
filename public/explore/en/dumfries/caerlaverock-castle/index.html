The guide

The story

Listen first. A soft wind comes off the Solway marshes, and somewhere beyond the trees the geese are calling. Water lies flat and dark around the walls. Then the stone comes into view. It is a deep red, warm even on a grey day, rising straight out of the moat. Take a moment here before you cross the bridge. Very few castles anywhere in Britain look quite like this one.

The reason is its shape. Caerlaverock is a triangle. Most medieval castles are built around squares, rectangles or rough circles, but here three curtain walls meet at three corners. At the northern point, a great twin-towered gatehouse faces you. Round towers once guarded the other two corners. Seen from the air, the castle looks almost like a shield laid on the water, and that comparison suits a place built for war.

The Maxwell family built this castle in the thirteenth century. They were a powerful family in this corner of southwest Scotland, and they held these lands for generations. Here is a detail many visitors miss. This is not the first Maxwell castle on the site. A short walk to the south, archaeologists have found the remains of an earlier castle. It stood on ground too close to the marsh. The damp, shifting earth gave it poor foundations, so the family moved to firmer ground and started again.

That choice of location tells you a lot about life on the border. England is only a short distance across the Solway Firth. In clear weather, you can see the Cumbrian hills from the shore nearby. For centuries, armies, raiders and messengers crossed this landscape. A castle here controlled the route into southwest Scotland by land and by sea. It was a gate, and the people who held it were never far from conflict.

The most famous moment in the castle's story came in the summer of 1300. King Edward the First of England marched north with a large army. Edward was the king who tried to conquer Scotland, and later he earned the name Hammer of the Scots. His army surrounded Caerlaverock, brought up siege engines and attacked the walls. Inside, the defenders numbered only around sixty men.

Those sixty men held out for a short time against a royal army. In the end they surrendered. Some accounts say several of the garrison were hanged afterwards, while others say they were spared, so the full truth is not certain. What is certain is this. The siege was remembered in an unusual way, and that record still exists today.

A writer who travelled with the English army composed a long poem in Anglo-Norman French, the language of the English court at the time. It is known as the Siege of Caerlaverock, or the Roll of Caerlaverock. The poem describes the castle and the attack. But its real treasure is the heraldry. It names many of the knights in Edward's army and describes the colours and designs on their banners and shields. Historians still use it today as a guide to medieval coats of arms.

So as you stand at the moat, imagine the scene from that poem. A long line of tents across the flat ground. Bright banners in red, blue, gold and silver. The sound of hammers as carpenters built war machines. And above the gate, a small group of defenders watching it all. On site you may see full-sized reconstructions of medieval siege engines. They help you understand the scale of what the garrison faced.

The castle changed hands several times during the long wars between Scotland and England. It was damaged, repaired, and rebuilt more than once. When you walk around the walls, look closely at the masonry. The stone shows different phases of work. Some sections are rougher and older. Others are neater and later. Each change in the stone marks a moment when someone decided this place was worth saving again.

Now cross into the courtyard, and prepare for the second surprise. Behind those hard defensive walls stands something gentle and elegant. In the seventeenth century, Robert Maxwell, the first Earl of Nithsdale, built a new range of rooms here. It is known as the Nithsdale Lodge. Its style is Renaissance, which means it follows the ideas of classical Greek and Roman design that spread across Europe after the Middle Ages.

The contrast is striking. Around you are medieval walls built for arrows and siege engines. In front of you are tall windows, carved stone panels, and triangular pediments, the decorative peaks above each window. Many of these carvings show scenes and figures from classical mythology and heraldry. The earl wanted a comfortable, fashionable home, not just a fortress. For a while, this castle was a place of learning and display.

That comfort did not last long. In 1640, during a period of religious and political conflict in Scotland, the castle was besieged once more. This time the attackers were the Covenanters, a Scottish movement opposed to the religious policies of King Charles the First. The siege lasted around thirteen weeks before the garrison surrendered. Afterwards, part of the castle was deliberately damaged so it could not be used again. The Maxwells never returned to live here, and the castle slowly became the ruin you see today.

The family story continued elsewhere. One later Maxwell, William, the fifth Earl of Nithsdale, became famous in 1716. He was imprisoned in the Tower of London after a failed rising. His wife Winifred helped him escape, reportedly by disguising him in women's clothing. It is one of the boldest escapes in British history, and it belongs to the same family that once held these walls.

Before you leave, walk slowly around the outside of the moat. Notice how the reflection of the red stone moves in the water. Listen for the birds from the nearby wetlands, where thousands of geese arrive in winter. This castle was built for danger, and it survived siege after siege. Today it stands quietly in a flat green landscape, and the loudest sound may be the wind in the reeds.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full moat circuit before entering the courtyard.
Bring waterproof layers for wind off the Solway marshes.
Visit the nearby wetland reserve for winter geese.
Follow the woodland path to the earlier castle site.
Allow time to see the reconstructed siege engines.
