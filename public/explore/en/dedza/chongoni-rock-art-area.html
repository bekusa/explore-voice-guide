The guide

The story

Run your hand along the granite here and it feels warm and rough, like coarse sandpaper left in the sun. Overhead, the rock curves out into a shallow shelter. The air smells of dry grass and woodsmoke from a village somewhere below. Then your eyes adjust to the shade, and you see them. Faint red lines on the stone. Pale white shapes beside them. Someone stood exactly where you are standing and painted this wall.

You are in the Chongoni Rock Art Area, in the hills of central Malawi, south-east of Lilongwe near the town of Dedza. This is upland country. The air is cooler than in the capital, and the slopes are covered in miombo woodland, a type of open dry forest common across south-central Africa. Scattered through these hills are well over one hundred painted rock shelters. Together they hold the densest concentration of rock art in Central Africa.

In 2006, UNESCO added Chongoni to its World Heritage List. That makes it one of only a small number of World Heritage Sites in Malawi. But the international recognition is only part of the story. What makes this place unusual is that the paintings were not made by one people in one period. They were made by different communities, over a very long span of time. And for some families living nearby, these rocks still matter today.

Start with the red paintings. These are generally the oldest layer. Archaeologists link them to hunter-gatherer peoples who lived in this region during the Late Stone Age, long before farming communities arrived. In Malawi they are often called the Batwa, or the Twa. Their art here is mostly geometric. Look for circles, parallel lines, grids, ladder-like patterns, and shapes that look a little like the sun.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. Elsewhere in southern Africa, hunter-gatherer rock art often shows animals and people in detail. At Chongoni, the red art is mainly abstract. Researchers still debate what these patterns meant. Some connect them to rain, to water, or to the spiritual world. The honest answer is that we do not fully know. The people who painted them did not leave an explanation, and their way of life here faded centuries ago.

Now look for the white paintings. These are usually more recent, and they were made by Chewa farming communities, the largest ethnic group in central Malawi. The white pigment often looks thicker and more smudged, as if applied with fingers. Many of these images are linked to two important Chewa institutions. One is chinamwali, the initiation of girls into womanhood. The other is Nyau, a secret society of masked dancers.

Nyau is the source of the Gule Wamkulu, which means "the great dance". Masked performers appear at funerals, initiations, and other important moments, and they represent spirits and ancestors. UNESCO has recognised the Gule Wamkulu as part of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity. In some Chongoni shelters, the white figures seem to show Nyau masks and dance structures. Some look like animals. Some are hard to read at all.

And here is a second surprise. Among the white paintings, there are images that appear to show motor cars. Locals and researchers connect these with Nyau, where large constructed figures, including vehicles, can appear in performances. So this is not frozen ancient art. People kept painting these walls into the twentieth century, and they added the new things they saw around them. Ancient geometry and modern machines share the same granite.

This is why Chongoni is often described as a living landscape. Some Chewa communities still regard certain shelters as places connected to ancestors, to rain, and to ceremony. Rituals linked to these sites have continued in living memory. So please treat the shelters with the same respect you would give a church or a shrine. You may be looking at art, but for some people nearby, you are also standing in a sacred place.

Two of the best-known shelters are Mphunzi and Chentcherere. Mphunzi sits on a hillside not far from the main road, and it is one of the easier sites to reach. Its rock faces carry both red geometric designs and white figures, so you can compare the two traditions side by side. Chentcherere is associated with excavations that have helped archaeologists understand who lived in these hills and when.

As you look, notice how the paintings sit on the rock. The artists did not choose random places. Many images are on smooth, sheltered surfaces, protected from the worst of the rain. Notice too how often the white paint sits on top of the red. That overlap is a quiet record of time. One community came, painted, and left. Another arrived much later and added its own marks to the same wall.

The paintings are fragile. Sun, rain, lichen, smoke, and dust all wear them away slowly. Even the oils from a single human hand can damage pigment that has survived for centuries. Water was sometimes splashed on rock art in the past to make it look brighter, and that also causes harm. So keep your hands off the painted surfaces, and photograph without a flash. The faintness is part of what makes these images real.

Take a moment before you leave the shelter. Step back to the edge of the overhang and look out. Below you, the woodland rolls away in green and brown. You may hear birds, cattle bells, or children calling from a field. The painters saw a version of this same view. Their world was different from yours, but the hills, the light, and the shade of this rock were the same.

As you walk back down the path, look again at the boulders you pass. Some of them may carry faint marks you missed on the way up. A red line. A pale smudge. In these hills, the past does not sit behind glass. It sits on open stone, under open sky, waiting quietly for someone to notice it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Hire a local guide to find the scattered shelters.
Visit in the dry season when hill tracks are passable.
Wear sturdy shoes for rocky, uneven hillside paths.
Bring water; shade and supplies are limited on the hills.
Combine your visit with a stop in nearby Dedza town.
