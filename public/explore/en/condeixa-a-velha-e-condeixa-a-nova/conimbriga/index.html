The guide

The story

Listen to your footsteps for a moment. Gravel crunches, then the sound turns flat and hollow as you step onto ancient paving. Around you, low walls of pale limestone rise only knee-high or waist-high. Between them, tiny coloured stones form patterns that have lain on this hillside for close to two thousand years. Swallows cut across the open sky. Somewhere nearby, water trickles. This is Conímbriga, the ruins of a Roman town in central Portugal, a short drive south of Coimbra.

Before the Romans came, people already lived on this ridge. The name itself tells you so. The ending “briga” comes from a Celtic word that usually points to a fortified height, a hilltop settlement. You can see why the site was chosen. The ground drops away steeply on two sides into river valleys, so the town had natural protection. The Romans did not build on empty land. They took over an Iron Age community and slowly reshaped it in their own image.

That reshaping took place across the first century of our era. Under the emperor Augustus, the town gained a forum, the public square at the heart of every Roman settlement. Later, under the Flavian emperors, Conímbriga received the status of a municipium, a self-governing town with Roman legal rights. The forum was rebuilt on a grander scale, with a temple and a colonnade. Today you can trace its outline on the ground. Close your eyes and picture the traders, the officials, the inscriptions, and the noise of a provincial capital in miniature.

The town sat in Lusitania, one of the Roman provinces that covered much of today’s Portugal and part of western Spain. A major Roman road ran between Olisipo, the city we now call Lisbon, and Bracara Augusta, today’s Braga. Conímbriga lay close to that route. So goods, soldiers, letters and fashions all passed nearby. That connection helps explain the wealth you will see in the houses. These were not frontier huts. They were the homes of families who wanted to live like Romans.

And water made that life possible. A Roman town needed a steady supply for fountains, baths and kitchens. Conímbriga had an aqueduct that carried water from a spring a few kilometres away. Part of the channel ended in a tower near the town, which helped distribute the flow. Keep that aqueduct in mind as you walk. Almost everything that impresses visitors here, from public baths to private fountains, depended on that quiet, constant flow of water arriving from the hills.

The most famous house on the site is the House of the Fountains. A modern roof now shelters it, so the light inside is softer and cooler. Look down at the floors. The mosaics are made from thousands of small cubes of stone in reds, ochres, greys, blacks and creams. You will find geometric borders, plants, animals and hunting scenes. In the gardens, channels and basins hold small jets of water. These jets are turned on for visitors, and their sound gives you a sense of how the house felt when people lived here.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The House of the Fountains lies partly outside the later town wall. In fact, the wall cuts straight through the area where the house stands. That tells you something important. When the wall went up, the people of Conímbriga were willing to sacrifice part of their own neighbourhood. Fine homes were abandoned or torn down. Security had become more important than comfort.

That wall is worth a close look. It dates from late in the Roman period, when the empire faced crisis and raids became a real fear. The builders worked quickly. They reused stones from older buildings, including carved blocks and pieces of public monuments. Walk along it and you may notice how mixed the masonry is. Some stones are neatly cut. Others look rough and hurried. The wall also made the protected town much smaller than before. It is a record, in stone, of a community that felt the ground shifting beneath it.

Inside the walls stands another large residence, often called the House of Cantaber. It is one of the biggest private houses known in the Roman west, with private baths, courtyards and many rooms. A popular story is attached to it. Legend has it that during one of the attacks on the town, the wife and children of a man named Cantaber were captured. The tale is moving, and it gave the house its name. But treat it as a story passed down, rather than as a detailed record of what happened in these rooms.

What we know with more certainty is that the fifth century brought real violence. After Roman authority weakened, a Germanic people called the Suevi established a kingdom in the northwest of the Iberian Peninsula. Written sources describe Suevic attacks on Conímbriga in the 460s. Over time, the town declined. Its bishop eventually moved to a nearby town on the river Mondego, called Aeminium. And here is the second surprise. Aeminium gradually took on the name of the older town. Over the centuries, that name became Coimbra.

So the city of Coimbra, with its famous university, carries the name of the ruins under your feet. Conímbriga itself was never completely forgotten, but it slowly emptied. Farmland and soil covered much of it. Archaeologists began serious excavations around the end of the nineteenth century, and the work expanded greatly during the twentieth century. Even now, only part of the ancient town has been uncovered. Much of Conímbriga still lies beneath the olive trees and fields around you.

Before or after the ruins, spend time in the site museum. It displays objects found during the excavations, things that rarely survive in the open air. You may see pottery, glass, coins, jewellery, tools, statues and inscriptions. These small objects bring the houses back to life. A hairpin, a lamp or a cooking pot tells you about ordinary mornings, not just grand architecture. Seeing the objects first can make the empty rooms outside easier to imagine.

As you walk, pay attention to the scale of daily life. Look for doorways, drains, steps, and the bases of columns. Notice how the streets slope, and how water was guided away. In the public baths, look for the raised floors and spaces underneath. Hot air once moved through those gaps to warm the rooms above. These practical details are often more revealing than the big monuments. They show a town that was planned, maintained and lived in.

Now pause for a final moment. Look across the low walls to the green valleys beyond. The view has changed less than the town. Roman families once looked toward these same slopes from their courtyards. As you leave, listen again to the gravel under your shoes, and to the small sound of water in the fountains. Conímbriga is not a frozen city. It is a hillside where people built, defended, abandoned and remembered. And the name of this place still travels on, in the city just to the north.
