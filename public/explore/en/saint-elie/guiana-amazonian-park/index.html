The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look at anything, just listen. A deep, rolling roar moves through the trees. It sounds like wind, or a far-off storm, but it is neither. It is howler monkeys, calling across the canopy to claim their territory. Under that sound, there are insects, dripping leaves, and the slow movement of water somewhere below. The air is warm and heavy. It smells of wet earth and rotting wood. This is the Guiana Amazonian Park, and you are standing at the edge of one of the largest intact forests on Earth.

Let us start with scale, because scale is the first surprise here. This park is the largest national park in France. In fact, it is the largest national park in the whole European Union. That sounds strange in the middle of the Amazon, but French Guiana is not a colony or a separate country. It is a full part of France, an overseas region on the north coast of South America. So this rainforest is, legally, as French as the vineyards of Burgundy. The euro is the currency here, even among the jaguars.

The park was created in 2007. It covers millions of hectares of forest in the southern half of French Guiana, far from the coast where most people live. There are almost no roads into this interior. People travel by small plane, by long wooden canoe on the rivers, or on foot along forest trails. On the southern side, the park meets the border with Brazil. Across that line lies another vast protected area, Tumucumaque National Park. Together, they form one of the biggest blocks of protected tropical forest in the world.

Now, about Saint-Élie. This is one of the smallest and most remote communities in French Guiana. It grew up during the gold rush of the late nineteenth century, when prospectors pushed deep into the forest in search of gold in the riverbeds. Gold is still the story here. Mining shaped the settlement, and mining continues in the region today. So as you look around, keep in mind that this landscape carries two histories at once: wild forest, and more than a century of people digging for metal.

That tension sits at the centre of the park's work. Illegal gold mining is one of the biggest threats to this forest. Small, hidden mining camps clear patches of trees and churn rivers into brown mud. Many illegal miners use mercury to separate gold from sediment. That mercury enters the water and the fish. And fish are a key food for the people who live along these rivers. So protecting the park is not only about animals and trees. It is also about the health of the families downstream.

And people do live here. This is the second thing many visitors do not expect. The Guiana Amazonian Park was designed with inhabited areas, not as an empty wilderness. Indigenous peoples, including the Wayana, the Teko, and the Wayampi, have lived along these rivers for generations. There are also Maroon communities, such as the Aluku. Maroons are descendants of enslaved Africans who escaped the coastal plantations and built free societies deep in the forest. Their knowledge of this land is old and detailed.

Because of these communities, the park has a special structure. There is a core zone, where protection is strongest. Around it, there is a wider zone where towns and villages agree to cooperate with the park. The idea is to protect nature and also support local ways of life — hunting, fishing, farming small forest gardens, and passing down traditional skills. It is a compromise, and it is not always simple. But it treats the forest as a home, not only as a museum.

Look up now, toward the canopy. The trees here can rise very high, and their crowns form a nearly closed roof. Down at ground level, the light is soft and green, even at midday. Many trees stand on wide buttress roots. These are tall, flat fins of wood that spread out from the base of the trunk, like the walls of a tent. In thin tropical soil, roots stay shallow, and the buttresses help the giant trees stay upright.

The animal life here is enormous, but it is also shy. Jaguars live in this forest, though you are very unlikely to see one. Giant otters hunt in the rivers in family groups. The harpy eagle, one of the most powerful birds of prey in the world, nests in the tallest trees and hunts monkeys and sloths. Most of the time, you will not see these animals. You will hear them, or find traces — a track in the mud, a feather, a scratched trunk.

Smaller creatures are easier to spot, if you move slowly. Watch the leaf litter for tiny poison dart frogs. Their bright colours are a warning to predators. Look for columns of leafcutter ants carrying green fragments across the trail. In some areas, you might glimpse the Guianan cock-of-the-rock. The male is a bright orange bird with a curved crest over its beak. It looks almost unreal against the dark green forest.

Another feature of this region is the inselberg. The word means island mountain. These are bare domes of granite that rise above the forest like the backs of whales. On their rocky tops, the plants are completely different from the forest below. They have to survive sun, wind, and very little soil. Inselbergs give rare open views across the canopy. From the top, the forest looks like a green ocean, stretching without a break to the horizon.

If you explore here, go with care and with local guidance. The forest is easy to enter and very easy to get lost in. Every direction looks the same once you leave the trail. Rain can arrive suddenly and heavily, and rivers can rise fast. Carry water, wear long sleeves against insects, and respect any rules about where you may go. Some areas near villages and mining zones need permission to enter, and that rule protects both you and the communities.

Before you move on, stand still for one more minute. Notice the layers of sound around you — the high insects, the birds in the middle, the low call of the howlers far away. Notice how the light shifts as clouds pass over the canopy. Somewhere near you, a river is carrying water toward the Atlantic. This forest was here long before the gold seekers came to Saint-Élie. With care, it will still be here long after you walk away.

Before you go

Practical tips

Hire a local guide before leaving any marked trail.
Wear long sleeves and strong insect repellent.
Carry plenty of water and pack for sudden rain.
Check entry permission rules for villages and protected zones.
Walk slowly and quietly to spot more wildlife.
