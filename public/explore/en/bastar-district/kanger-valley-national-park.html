The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the tall trees, the air is cooler than on the open road. Dry leaves crack under your shoes. Somewhere above you, a bird calls, then another answers. Light comes down through the canopy in narrow shafts and lands on the forest floor in small, moving patches. This is Kanger Valley National Park, in the Bastar district of Chhattisgarh, in central India. It is a place of forest, river, limestone and caves.

The park takes its name from the Kanger River, which runs through the valley from end to end. The river is the spine of this place. It cuts a long, narrow corridor through the hills, and the forest grows thick along both sides. The protected area was declared a national park in 1982. It sits a short drive from Jagdalpur, the main town of Bastar. Yet once you are under the trees, the town feels very far away.

Look at the trees around you. Much of this forest is sal, a tall hardwood with broad leaves that is common across central and eastern India. You will also see teak, and dense stands of bamboo that creak when the wind moves them. In the dry months, the forest floor becomes a carpet of brown leaves. After the monsoon, everything turns green almost overnight. The same path can feel like two different places, depending on the season you arrive.

Now think about what lies under your feet. Much of this valley rests on limestone. Limestone is a soft rock that slowly dissolves in rainwater. Over a very long time, water seeps through cracks, widens them, and carves hollow spaces deep underground. That is why Kanger Valley is known for its caves. The forest you see is only the top layer. Below it, there is a second landscape, dark and quiet, that most visitors never imagine when they first arrive.

The best known of these is Kotumsar Cave. Its entrance is modest — a narrow opening in the rock. Inside, the passages open into chambers filled with stalactites and stalagmites. Stalactites hang down from the ceiling like stone icicles. Stalagmites rise up from the floor to meet them. Both form from mineral-rich water, drop by drop, over thousands of years. In the beam of a lamp, the wet stone shines in shades of cream, grey and pale gold.

Here is something many visitors miss. The deep parts of Kotumsar are in total darkness, and some animals have adapted to life without light. Researchers have studied small fish in the cave pools that are blind or nearly blind. Cave crickets and other creatures also live here. Over many generations, eyes matter less in a place with no sun. If you see something pale moving in the water, watch quietly. You are looking at a life shaped entirely by the dark.

Kotumsar is not the only cave. Kailash Cave and Dandak Cave also lie within the park, each with its own chambers and rock formations. They are less visited, and access can depend on the season and on local arrangements. Inside any of these caves, the temperature stays steady and the air feels damp and still. Sound behaves differently too. A single voice can echo off the walls, and a drip of water can seem loud in the silence.

Back above ground, the water of the valley makes itself heard in another way. Tirathgarh Falls is one of the most visited places in the park. Here, the water drops over a series of wide rock steps, breaking into white streams and spray. In the monsoon and the months after, the falls run at their strongest. In the dry season, the flow is gentler, and the shape of the rock terraces becomes easier to see. Either way, the sound fills the air long before you reach the edge.

Now, a second surprise. Keep your ears open for a bird that seems to talk. The hill myna is a glossy black bird with a bright yellow patch of skin near its head and an orange beak. It is the state bird of Chhattisgarh, and Bastar is closely linked with it. Hill mynas are famous for their wide range of calls, whistles and imitations. You may hear one long before you see it, high in the canopy.

The forest holds much more wildlife, though most of it stays out of sight. Many mammals here are shy and move at dawn, at dusk, or at night. You are more likely to notice signs than animals — a track in soft mud, a scratched tree trunk, a rustle off the path. Butterflies are easier to spot, especially in the green months. Stand still in a clearing for a few minutes, and the forest often becomes louder and busier around you.

This valley is not empty wilderness. Bastar is home to Adivasi communities, the term for India's Indigenous peoples. Gond and other groups have lived with these forests for generations. They gather leaves, fruits and forest produce, and many hold deep knowledge of plants and seasons. Some of the local guides who lead visitors into the caves come from nearby villages. If you walk with one, ask questions. The forest looks different through the eyes of someone who grew up beside it.

A little practical sense goes a long way here. The caves are dark, wet and sometimes slippery, so good shoes matter more than good clothes. Paths in the forest can be uneven, with roots and loose stones. The heat in the dry season can be strong in open areas, even when the forest feels cool. Carry water, move at an easy pace, and treat the caves gently. The rock formations grow so slowly that a careless touch can mark them for centuries.

Before you leave, pause once more where you stand. Notice the light through the leaves, and how it has shifted since you arrived. Listen for the river, for the birds, for the wind moving through the bamboo. Remember that under this green floor, in the dark, water is still dripping and stone is still slowly growing. As you walk back toward the road, the forest will go on as it has for a very long time — quietly, on both levels at once.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for wet cave floors.
Bring a torch, even when guides carry lamps.
Hire a local guide for entry into the caves.
Visit after the monsoon to see the falls in full flow.
Carry water for the hot, open stretches of trail.
