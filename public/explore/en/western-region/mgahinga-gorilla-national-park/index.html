The guide

The story

Listen first. A breeze moves through the bamboo, and the tall green stems knock softly against each other, like wooden chimes. The air is cool and damp, even near the equator. Underfoot, the soil is dark, almost black, and crumbly with old volcanic ash. Above the tree line, three great cones rise from the mist. You are standing at the foot of the Virunga volcanoes, in the far southwestern corner of Uganda, close to where three countries meet.

This is Mgahinga Gorilla National Park, in Kisoro District. It is the smallest national park in Uganda, covering only about thirty-four square kilometres. But size can mislead. This small park is one piece of a much larger forest system. It joins Volcanoes National Park in Rwanda and Virunga National Park in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Together they form one connected home for wildlife that pays no attention to borders.

Now look at the three volcanoes. Each one has a name and a character. The tallest is Muhavura, a smooth, steep cone that rises above four thousand metres. Its name is usually translated as the guide, or the one who shows the way. Local tradition says travellers once used its peak to find their direction across the hills. On a clear day, you can see why. Its outline is easy to pick out from far away.

The middle volcano is Gahinga, lower and rounder than its neighbours. The park takes its name from this mountain. In the local language, a gahinga is a pile of stones. Farmers in this region clear volcanic rocks from their fields and stack them into heaps. As you drove or walked here, you may have passed these stone piles at the edges of the gardens. The mountain, people say, looks like one of them.

The third volcano is Sabinyo, and it is the oldest of the three. Its ridge is broken and jagged, worn down over a very long time. Its name is often translated as old man's teeth, and once you hear that, it is hard to see anything else. Here is a detail most visitors miss. The summit of Sabinyo is the point where Uganda, Rwanda, and the Congo meet. Climbers at the top can stand in three countries at once.

All three volcanoes are considered extinct. They no longer erupt. But their past is all around you. The rich volcanic soil is the reason this land is so fertile. Look down the slopes, away from the park boundary, and you will see a patchwork of small farms climbing almost to the forest edge. Potatoes, beans, and sorghum grow in tight terraces. The contrast is sharp. On one side, cultivated fields. On the other, an unbroken wall of forest.

That forest is the reason the park exists. Mgahinga was declared a national park in 1991, to protect the mountain gorilla. These great apes live only in two places on Earth. One is these Virunga volcanoes. The other is Bwindi, a forest to the north. Mountain gorillas have thick, dark coats, much longer than those of gorillas in the lowlands. That heavy fur keeps them warm in the cold, wet air of the high slopes.

The gorillas here live in family groups, led by a mature male called a silverback. He is named for the band of grey hair across his back. In Mgahinga, visitors track a habituated family known as the Nyakagezi group. Habituated means the gorillas have slowly grown used to calm human company over years of careful work by rangers. Trackers follow signs from the day before, such as broken stems, flattened nests, and fresh droppings, to find where the family has moved.

The park has a short motto that is easy to remember. Where gold meets silver. The silver is the silverback gorilla. The gold is the golden monkey, a rare primate with a bright orange-gold back and a dark face. Golden monkeys live in the bamboo zone, and they move fast. You will often hear them before you see them. Listen for rustling high in the stems, and sharp calls passing from one group to another.

People have a long story here too. The Batwa, a forest people, lived in these mountains for many generations. They hunted, gathered honey, and knew which plants could heal. When the park was created, they were moved out of the forest, and that loss is still felt today. The Batwa Trail now lets visitors walk with Batwa guides. They share songs, show how fire was made, and explain how their families once lived among the trees.

One stop on that trail is Garama Cave, a long lava tube in the side of the mountain. Lava tubes form when the outer layer of a lava flow cools and hardens, while hot lava keeps running inside. When the flow drains away, a tunnel remains. According to Batwa tradition, the cave once served as a refuge and a meeting place for their king. Inside, it is dark and cool, and voices echo off the rock.

The forest itself changes as you climb. Near the bottom, there is mixed woodland and farmland at the edge. Higher up comes the bamboo, dense and green. Higher still, the plants become stranger. Giant lobelias and tree-like groundsels grow on the upper slopes, plants shaped by thin air and cold nights. At the very top of Muhavura, a small crater lake rests in the summit. It is a long, steep climb, but a quiet reward waits there.

So pause for a moment before you move on. Look again at the three cones, the guide, the stone pile, and the old man's teeth. Notice how the cloud settles and lifts across their shoulders. Listen to the bamboo, and to the birds calling from the forest edge. Somewhere up in that green, a gorilla family is feeding, unaware of the border lines on our maps. As you walk away, watch for the stone piles in the fields. They carry the name of this place.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a gorilla tracking permit well before your visit.
Bring a waterproof jacket for sudden mountain rain.
Wear sturdy boots for muddy, steep volcanic trails.
Carry gardening gloves for gripping nettles and branches.
Join the Batwa Trail to hear local forest traditions.
