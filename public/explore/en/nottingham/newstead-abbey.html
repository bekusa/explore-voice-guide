The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches underfoot, a peacock calls somewhere across the lawns, and water moves quietly at the edge of the lake. Then you look up. In front of you stands a tall Gothic wall of pale, weathered stone. It has a great empty window at its centre, open to the sky, and pointed arches marching along its face. This is the ruined west front of a medieval church. Behind it and beside it, a country house is built right into the old monastery. Welcome to Newstead Abbey, a short drive north of Nottingham, on the edge of what was once Sherwood Forest.

The story begins in the twelfth century. King Henry the Second founded a priory here for Augustinian canons, a community of priests who lived together under a shared rule of prayer and work. According to a long-held tradition, the king did this partly as an act of penance after the murder of Thomas Becket, the Archbishop of Canterbury, in 1170. Historians treat that link with some caution, but the royal foundation itself is firm. For more than three centuries, canons sang their daily services here. They farmed the land, fished the ponds, and kept the rhythm of the church year deep in the forest.

Look closely at that west front. It dates from the thirteenth century, and its proportions are still elegant after so much loss. High up, near the top of the gable, there is a small niche. Inside it sits a carved figure of the Virgin Mary holding the Christ child. Here is the surprise. Statues like this were often smashed during the Reformation, the sixteenth-century break with the Catholic Church. This one survived, high above the reach of hammers. It is worth a long look, because it has watched over this lawn for roughly seven hundred years.

That Reformation brought the monastic life to an end. In 1539, under Henry the Eighth, the priory was dissolved, along with hundreds of religious houses across England. The next year, the king granted the estate to Sir John Byron, a local landowner. He did something many new owners did at the time. He did not tear the buildings down. Instead, he turned the cloisters, the chapter house, and the canons' living quarters into a private home. The church itself was mostly left to fall, and only its west front remained standing. That is why the house and the ruin sit side by side today.

The Byron family held Newstead for nearly three hundred years. One of them became known as the Wicked Lord. He was William, the fifth Baron Byron, and in 1765 he killed his cousin and neighbour, William Chaworth, in a sword fight in a London tavern. He was tried by his fellow lords and escaped serious punishment. But his reputation never recovered. In later years he withdrew to Newstead, and the estate fell into debt and neglect. He cut down valuable timber and let the house decay around him.

He also had a taste for spectacle. The Wicked Lord built small castle-like forts beside the lake. He sailed boats on the water and staged mock naval battles, with cannons firing across the surface. Some of those little forts can still be seen today. Stand by the shore and imagine the smoke drifting over this quiet water. Local stories added darker colour to his memory, and some of those tales are probably exaggerated. Still, by the time he died in 1798, the great house was in poor condition.

That year, the estate passed to a ten-year-old boy, his great-nephew. His name was George Gordon Byron, and he would become Lord Byron, one of the most famous poets in the English language. Byron came to Newstead as a child and later lived here at different times as a young man. He loved the place deeply, even in its damaged state. He wrote about the ruined abbey in his poetry, and its broken arches and dark corridors suited the brooding romantic image he would carry across Europe.

Byron was not a quiet tenant. He and his friends from Cambridge sometimes dressed in monks' robes and held lively parties here. One famous object comes from this time. A gardener dug up a skull in the grounds, probably from an old burial of the priory. Byron had it mounted in silver and polished into a drinking cup. He even wrote a short poem about it. It is a strange, slightly shocking detail, and it tells you a lot about the young poet's love of drama.

Now walk out into the gardens and find a large stone monument. It marks the grave of Boatswain, Byron's beloved Newfoundland dog. Boatswain died in 1808, after catching rabies. Byron was devastated. He wrote an epitaph praising the dog's loyalty and honesty, comparing him favourably with most human beings. Here is the second surprise. Byron once intended to be buried in the same vault beside his dog. In the end that never happened. The poet died in Greece in 1824 and was buried in the family vault at the church in Hucknall, a few miles away.

Byron could not afford to keep Newstead. In 1818 he sold it to Thomas Wildman, an old friend from his school days at Harrow. Wildman had made money and spent a great deal of it restoring the house. Much of the building you see now, with its battlements and Gothic-style rooms, reflects that nineteenth-century work. Later owners, the Webb family, continued to care for the estate and its gardens. Over time the Byron connection drew a steady stream of admirers who came simply to walk where the poet had walked.

One more curious tale belongs to the lake. In the eighteenth century, a large brass lectern in the shape of an eagle was pulled from the water. The canons had probably hidden it there during the dissolution, hoping to save it. When it was opened, old documents were found inside. The lectern later went to Southwell Minster, a cathedral in Nottinghamshire, where it still stands. And, of course, there are ghost stories. Legend has it that a hooded figure called the Black Friar haunts the house. Byron claimed to have seen him. Treat that one as folklore.

In the twentieth century, the estate was given to the city of Nottingham, and it has been cared for as a public place ever since. Inside, rooms hold Byron's belongings, portraits, and manuscripts. Outside, the grounds are a series of different worlds. There is a formal garden with a long pond, a fernery, a walled garden, and a small Japanese garden created in the early twentieth century, with stepping stones and little bridges. Peacocks wander freely, and they are not shy about crossing your path.

Before you leave, come back to the west front. Notice how the light falls through the empty window, framing sky where stained glass once glowed. Listen again for the gravel, the birds, and the water. Nine centuries of people have stood on this ground: canons at prayer, a disgraced lord firing cannons on the lake, a young poet raising a silver cup. Now it is your turn. As you walk away, glance back once more at that small stone Virgin in her niche. She is still there, keeping her watch.
