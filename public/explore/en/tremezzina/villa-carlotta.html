The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches under your shoes, water drips into a stone basin, and from somewhere below comes the low slap of the lake against the shore. The air carries a faint sweetness... citrus leaves, damp earth, and in spring the heavy scent of flowering shrubs. In front of you, terraces and staircases climb toward a pale villa. Behind you, across the water, the rooftops of Bellagio catch the light. This is Villa Carlotta, on the western shore of Lake Como, at Tremezzo.

Take a moment to look at how the place is built. The villa does not sit alone on the hillside. It is part of a single design that runs from the water's edge upward. Ramps, balustrades and fountains lead the eye step by step toward the house. This is the Italian garden tradition — geometry, symmetry and stone, with nature arranged rather than left wild. From the lake, the whole composition reads like a stage set, and that was entirely the point.

The story begins at the end of the seventeenth century. The villa was built for the Marquis Giorgio Clerici, a member of a wealthy Milanese family. Families like his built summer houses on Lake Como to escape the heat of the city. The lake offered cool breezes, fresh water and, above all, a view. The house you see today kept much of its original shape, though later owners changed its interiors and greatly expanded the grounds.

The next great chapter belongs to a very different kind of man. In the early nineteenth century the villa passed to Gian Battista Sommariva. He was a lawyer and politician who rose to power in the Napoleonic period, when French rule reshaped northern Italy. Sommariva became extremely rich, and he spent much of that fortune on art. He wanted his lakeside house to show the world his taste. He turned it into something close to a private museum of the newest art of his age.

That art was neoclassical. In simple terms, neoclassical artists looked back to ancient Greece and Rome for calm, ideal beauty — smooth marble, balanced forms and noble subjects. Sommariva collected works connected to the most admired sculptor of the time, Antonio Canova. Inside the villa you will find a famous version of Canova's Cupid and Psyche, the two lovers caught in a tender embrace. This marble was carved by Adamo Tadolini, a student of Canova, after his master's design.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look up along the walls of the main hall. A long marble frieze runs around the room, showing a triumphal procession. It is connected to the Danish sculptor Bertel Thorvaldsen and his celebrated design for the Triumph of Alexander the Great. The procession of soldiers, horses and figures moves slowly around the space. Many people walk straight past it, looking only at the free-standing statues. Take a minute to follow it with your eyes.

Not every treasure here is marble. The villa also holds a painting by Francesco Hayez, one of the leading Italian painters of the Romantic era. It shows the last kiss of Romeo and Juliet. Romanticism was the movement that followed neoclassicism, and it favoured strong emotion over calm order. Standing between the cool statues and this charged painting, you can feel art changing its mood within a single generation. It is a small lesson in taste, told in one building.

Now, the name. Why is it Villa Carlotta? In the middle of the nineteenth century the estate was bought by Princess Marianne of Nassau, wife of a Prussian prince. She gave it as a wedding gift to her daughter, Charlotte, when Charlotte married the heir to the German duchy of Saxe-Meiningen. Carlotta is simply the Italian form of Charlotte. Her story has a sad ending. She died young, only a few years after the wedding. Yet her name has stayed on the house ever since.

The German family left a lasting mark — not so much on the walls as on the land. They developed the grounds into the botanical garden that surrounds you today. The slopes behind and beside the villa were planted with species from many parts of the world. The mild climate of the lake made this possible. Mountains to the north block the coldest winds, and the deep water holds warmth. Plants that would struggle elsewhere in the Alps can grow here.

If you are here in spring, you will understand why many visitors come for the garden first and the art second. Azaleas and rhododendrons cover the hillsides in reds, pinks, purples and whites. Some of the shrubs are old and very large, forming tunnels of colour over the paths. Other seasons bring their own rewards — camellias earlier in the year, deep green shade in summer, and warm tones in autumn. The garden changes noticeably from month to month.

Wander a little further and the character of the garden shifts. There is a valley of ferns, where the air turns cool and damp and the light goes green. There is a bamboo garden, where tall stems knock softly against each other in the breeze. There are rock gardens and quiet corners with views of the water. Paths wind up and down the slope, so wear comfortable shoes. The contrast between the formal terraces and these wilder sections is part of the design.

In the twentieth century the estate passed out of private hands. Today it is cared for by a foundation that maintains both the house and the garden for the public. That continuity matters. A garden is never finished. Trees age, storms knock down branches, and new plants must be raised to replace old ones. What you see today is the work of generations of gardeners, many of whose names were never recorded.

Before you leave, walk back down to the lower terrace and turn toward the lake. Watch the ferries cross the water toward Bellagio and Menaggio. Notice how the villa frames the view, and how the view frames the villa. A marquis, a Napoleonic politician and a German princess all stood near this spot and looked at the same mountains. Listen once more to the water in the fountains... and to the lake beyond them, still moving softly against the stone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in spring to see the azaleas and rhododendrons in bloom.
Wear comfortable shoes for the steep, winding garden paths.
Arrive by ferry to see the villa's full lakeside composition.
Tour the interiors first, then spend unhurried time outdoors.
Come early in the day for quieter garden paths.
