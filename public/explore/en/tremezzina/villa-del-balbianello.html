The guide

The story

Listen first. Water laps against stone just below the path, slow and steady. A boat engine fades somewhere across the lake. Then the gravel crunches under your feet as you climb. Around you, the green is close and deep, with laurel, clipped hedges, and the cool shade of old trees. You are standing on a narrow wooded point that pushes out into Lake Como. This is Villa del Balbianello.

The point is called Lavedo. It sits on the western shore of the lake, in the municipality of Tremezzina, near the village of Lenno. Look at the shape of the land. Water lies on almost every side, so the villa sits apart from the rest of the shore. For centuries, that separation shaped what people built here. The first people to settle this point were religious, not aristocrats.

Before the villa, a Franciscan monastery stood here. The monks came for quiet, and the lake gave them plenty of it. The monastery is long gone, but part of it survived. Look for the two church towers near the water. They belonged to the old monastery church. The later owners did not tear them down. They kept the towers and built the new villa around them. So the oldest part of what you see is sacred, not grand.

The villa itself took shape in the late eighteenth century. Its builder was Cardinal Angelo Maria Durini, a churchman, diplomat, and lover of literature. He wanted a retreat where he could read, write, and receive learned friends. He did not choose a flat, easy site. He chose a steep, rocky point that most visitors could reach only by boat. Even today, many people still arrive the way his guests did, across the water.

As you walk up, notice how the garden works. There is very little flat ground here. The gardeners terraced the slope and followed the rock, so paths turn and climb in tight curves. Lawns roll down toward the lake in soft green steps. The plants are trimmed with care. Hedges, balls of box, and sculpted shapes keep the eye moving. Nothing here is wild, yet the garden never feels stiff.

Now look up at the trees near the top. These are plane trees, and they have an unusual shape. Gardeners prune them so the branches spread out flat and rise in parallel lines, a little like the arms of a candlestick. It is a slow, patient technique. Each tree takes years of careful cutting to hold its form. Many visitors walk straight past them. Once you see the pattern, it is hard to stop noticing it.

At the highest point stands the loggia. A loggia is an open gallery with arches on one side, a room that is half indoors and half outdoors. This one sits right on the ridge of the promontory. From one side you look south down the lake. From the other side you look north. A climbing fig covers much of the stone with small, dense leaves. The loggia holds a library and a map room. Cardinal Durini's world of books and conversation lived here.

After the cardinal, the villa passed through several hands. In the nineteenth century, members of the Arconati Visconti family owned it. This was the era when Italy was struggling toward unity, a period Italians call the Risorgimento. The villa became a meeting place for writers and liberal thinkers. Picture quiet conversations on these terraces about books, politics, and the future of a country that did not yet exist as one nation.

Then comes the twist in the story. The last private owner was not a cardinal or a count. He was an explorer. Guido Monzino was a Milanese businessman who spent his life on expeditions to the coldest places on earth. He travelled to Greenland and the Arctic. In 1973 he led an Italian expedition to Mount Everest. He bought the villa in the 1970s and filled its rooms with the record of those journeys.

Inside, Monzino's collections make a surprising contrast with the elegant rooms. You will find objects linked to his expeditions, along with photographs and mementos from the polar regions and the Himalaya. There are also fine furnishings, art, and objects he collected over many years. A villa built for a scholar of books became the home of a man of ice and rope. Visits to the house interior are usually guided, so the stories come room by room.

Monzino died in 1988. He left the villa to the FAI, the Fondo Ambiente Italiano, a national trust that protects Italian heritage. That gift is why the gates are open to you today. He also chose to stay here forever. Monzino is buried on the grounds of the villa he loved. As you walk, remember that this place is a garden, a museum, and a resting place all at once.

The villa has a second life on screen. Its terraces and loggia have appeared in major films. In Star Wars, Episode Two, the lakeside scenes of a romantic retreat were filmed here. The 2006 James Bond film Casino Royale also used the villa for scenes of recovery and quiet. Film crews came for the same reason the cardinal did. The light on the water, the old stone, and the steep green slopes look like nowhere else.

Before you leave, find a spot near the water or under the loggia arches. Look out across the lake. Close by, you can see the small wooded island of Comacina, the only island in Lake Como. Mountains rise steeply behind the far shore, and their colour changes with the hour. Notice how the sound here stays soft. There is the water, the wind in the leaves, and voices kept low by habit.

As you walk back down, slow your pace on the curving paths. Look once more at the old monastery towers, the shaped plane trees, and the fig leaves on the loggia walls. Each one belongs to a different chapter of this point of land. There were monks, then a cardinal, then patriots, then an explorer. Now there is you, on the same gravel, listening to the same water against the stone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive by boat from Lenno for the classic lakeside approach.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, curving garden paths.
Book the guided interior tour separately if you want the rooms.
Visit early in the day to enjoy the loggia in quiet.
Bring a light layer for the cooler breeze on the point.
