The guide

The story

Listen first. On a calm day, the loudest sound here is the wind moving through old trees and over broken stone. Underfoot, the grass is soft. Above you, grey limestone walls rise in uneven lines, some full and solid, some bitten away by time. The light in Haapsalu has a pale, sea-washed quality, because the Baltic is never far. You can feel it in the air, cool and a little salty. Welcome to Haapsalu Castle, once the seat of a powerful medieval bishop on the western coast of Estonia.

To understand these walls, imagine this coast in the thirteenth century. German crusaders and the church had pushed into the lands of the Estonians, and new territories were divided among knights and bishops. One of these territories was the Bishopric of Ösel-Wiek, which covered the islands and the western mainland. Its bishops needed a fortified home, a place to pray, to rule, and to defend. They chose this low peninsula, with water on several sides. Building began in the medieval period and continued for generations, as the castle grew and changed.

This was not simply a fortress with a chapel inside. It was a fortress built around a church. The bishop was both a religious leader and a territorial lord, and the architecture shows that double role. Thick defensive walls ring a large enclosure. At its heart stands the cathedral, which is part of the fortification itself. Its walls are heavy and plain, built more like a stronghold than a delicate house of prayer. Here, faith and force stood side by side.

If you step inside the cathedral, notice the space. There are no rows of columns dividing the interior into aisles. Instead, there is one wide, open nave under a vaulted ceiling. It is often described as the largest single-nave church in the Baltic states. Standing in the middle, you can sense how the builders wanted the room to feel — open, cool, and quiet, with the sound of footsteps carrying from one end to the other. The stone holds the chill even in summer.

Now for the story that almost every visitor in Haapsalu hears. Legend has it that a young woman once fell in love with a canon of the cathedral. Women were forbidden inside the castle, so she disguised herself as a choirboy to stay near him. For a time, the trick worked. But she was discovered. According to the tale, the punishment was terrible — she was walled up alive inside the baptistery. Her lover, the story says, was thrown into a dungeon.

This is folklore, not documented history, but locals tell it with real affection. They say that on bright moonlit nights in August, the pale figure of a woman appears in a window of the baptistery. This is the White Lady of Haapsalu. People gather in the castle grounds in late summer, looking up at the wall, waiting for the moonlight to fall in just the right way. Whether you see a ghost or simply the shape of light on glass, the moment can feel strangely still.

The legend has become part of the town's identity. In August, Haapsalu holds a festival named after the White Lady, with performances and events linked to the castle. Even outside that season, you will find her image on postcards and in shop windows around town. It is a good example of how a place keeps its memory. The documented past is carried in stone. The emotional past is carried in stories, repeated from one generation to the next.

The castle's real history is turbulent enough without ghosts. In the sixteenth century, the Livonian War swept across this region. The old order of bishops and knights collapsed. The bishopric passed out of the hands of its last bishops, and the land came under foreign crowns — first Danish, then Swedish rule in the following decades. The seat of power in the region eventually shifted elsewhere. Without its bishops, the castle slowly lost its central purpose.

Later wars brought more damage. By the eighteenth century, much of the fortress was in ruins. Parts of the walls were taken apart, and stone was carried away for other buildings. That is why so much of what you see today is broken outline rather than complete structure. Look closely at the edges of the walls. You can see where stone was removed, where gaps open into empty space, and where newer repairs meet the older masonry. The ruin itself is a record of those centuries.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Across the grounds, the castle was once divided into different zones, with an inner core of buildings and wider outer areas. Today, much of that space is a quiet park. Families walk dogs here, children run across the lawns, and couples sit on benches against ancient walls. The bishop's forbidding enclosure has become one of the gentlest public spaces in town. The change in use tells you a lot about how this town lives with its past.

If you have the energy, look for the tall watchtower. Climbing it is one of the best ways to understand the castle's position. From the top, you can see how close the sea is, and how the low land spreads out around the town. You can follow the line of the walls and picture the full shape of the fortress. Inside the castle, there is also a museum, where you can learn more about medieval life, weapons, and the people who lived here.

When you leave the castle grounds, take a short walk through the surrounding streets. Haapsalu grew into a seaside resort in the nineteenth century, famous for its healing mud baths. Many of the wooden houses around the old town come from that later era. They are painted in soft colours, with carved details around windows and porches. The contrast is striking — heavy medieval stone at the centre, and light wooden architecture around it. Together they show two very different chapters of the same small town.

Before you go, pause for a moment where you stand. Look at the texture of the limestone, the patches of moss, the way the light changes on the walls as clouds pass. Listen again to the wind in the trees. Somewhere above you is the baptistery window of the legend, quiet in the daylight. As you walk away, notice how the castle slowly disappears behind roofs and branches, and how the sound of the sea returns. The stone stays here, as it has for centuries.
