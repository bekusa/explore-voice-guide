The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Water drips somewhere in the cedar shade. A stream runs over stones beside the path. Leaves move high above you. Here and there, a dark opening in the hillside breathes out cool, damp air. This valley feels like quiet countryside. Yet for centuries, these green hills held one of the most productive silver mines in the world, and its metal travelled as far as Europe and China.

You are standing in Iwami Ginzan, in the hills above the coast of Shimane Prefecture, within the city of Ōda. In 2007, UNESCO added the site to its World Heritage list. The listing covers more than the mine itself. It is called the Iwami Ginzan Silver Mine and its Cultural Landscape, so it includes the mining town, the old transport roads, and the harbours that shipped the silver out to sea.

The story of the mine as a large enterprise usually begins in the 1520s. According to the traditional account, a merchant from Hakata, a trading port on the island of Kyushu, saw a glint of silver from his ship off this coast. His name was Kamiya Jutei. He organised miners to dig into the mountain. Some details of that sighting may belong more to legend than to record, but the merchant's role in opening the mine is widely accepted.

Here is the first surprise. The real breakthrough was not finding the ore. It was refining it. In the 1530s, a smelting technique called haifuki arrived here from the Korean Peninsula. In this process, workers melt silver ore with lead, then remove the lead to leave nearly pure silver behind. Before this method, much of the ore travelled away for processing. Afterwards, silver could be refined right here, in these narrow valleys, and output increased sharply.

This matters on a world scale. In the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, Japan became one of the largest silver producers on Earth. Historians often estimate that Japan supplied a large share of the world's silver in that period, and Iwami was a major source. Portuguese traders knew this region. Some European maps from that era even mark the area of Iwami by name. Silver from these hills helped pay for trade with China, where silver was the basis of the economy.

Around 1600, after the wars that unified Japan, the Tokugawa shogunate took direct control of the mine. The government in Edo, the city now called Tokyo, appointed magistrates to manage it. Their headquarters was the magistrate's office in the town of Ōmori, at the foot of the valley. Today you can still visit the restored office complex. It shows how seriously the shogunate treated this source of wealth.

Now look at the hillsides around you. Those small dark openings are mabu, the Japanese word for mine tunnels. Researchers have recorded hundreds of them across the mountain. Many are just big enough for one person to crawl inside. Miners worked them with hand tools and lamps. The work was hard and often dangerous. Dust and smoke damaged their lungs, and many miners did not live long lives.

One tunnel, the Ryugenji Mabu, is open for visitors to walk through. Inside, the air is cool all year. The walls are rough and uneven, and you can still see the marks of chisels in the rock. A narrow side shaft follows the line of a silver vein. Another larger tunnel, the Okubo Mabu, is usually entered only on a guided tour, so plan ahead if you want to see it.

Here is the second surprise. Iwami Ginzan is famous today partly because of what you cannot see. Many mines leave bare, poisoned land behind them. Here, the forest is thick and green. The mining relied on wood for fuel, but the forests were managed and allowed to grow back. UNESCO highlighted this balance between industry and nature. When the mine finally closed in the early twentieth century, the trees slowly covered the old workings again.

Before you leave the valley, find the temple of Rakan-ji, near the road between the mine area and the town. Across from it, small caves are cut into the cliff. Inside sit hundreds of carved stone figures called rakan. These are disciples of the Buddha who reached enlightenment. They were carved in the eighteenth century, and tradition says they honour the miners who died. Each face has a different expression. Locals say that if you look carefully, you may find a face that reminds you of someone you have lost.

Walk down into Ōmori, and the town opens up along one long street. Wooden houses line both sides, with dark tiled roofs and lattice windows. Some belonged to samurai officials, others to merchants. The Kumagai residence, a large merchant house, is open to visitors and shows how a wealthy family lived here. Today the street holds cafés, small shops, and craft studios. People still live in these houses, so the town is not a museum. It is a working community.

The silver did not stay here. Workers carried it along mountain roads to the coast, to small harbours such as Tomogaura and Okidomari. From there, ships took it away. The hot spring town of Yunotsu, also part of the World Heritage site, grew up near one of these ports. If you have time, the old roads and the quiet harbours complete the picture. They show that the mine was only one part of a much larger system.

Now, as you stand here, listen again. The stream, the leaves, perhaps a bicycle bell on the path to Ōmori. It is hard to imagine the noise and smoke this valley once held. Look at the moss on the stone walls, the dark tunnel mouths, the cedars growing over old work sites. As you walk away, notice how gently the forest has closed over everything. The silver is gone, but the hills have kept the story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Rent a bicycle in Ōmori for the uphill valley path.
Book ahead for the guided Okubo Mabu tunnel tour.
Bring a light jacket for the cool tunnel air.
Wear sturdy shoes for damp, uneven forest paths.
Add Yunotsu and the old harbours if time allows.
