The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days the loudest sound here is the wind, moving through dry grass and around the bases of columns that have stood in these hills for nearly two thousand years. The stone under your feet is pale and worn smooth. In places you can still see the grooves left by cart wheels. Around you the land falls away into deep green valleys, and the air feels thinner and cooler than on the coast. Welcome to Djémila.

The name is Arabic, and it means "the beautiful one". The Romans had another name for this town: Cuicul. It was founded at the end of the first century as a colony for retired Roman soldiers. These veterans received land in return for their service. In exchange, they helped to hold this mountainous corner of North Africa for Rome. The site sat in the old region of Numidia, the kingdom that existed here before the Romans took control.

Now look at the shape of the ground. The town sits on a narrow spur of land between two stream valleys. This was not an easy place to build. The Roman planners usually liked flat ground and a neat grid of streets. Here the rock forced them to adapt. The town stretches out long and thin, following the ridge, and the streets bend to fit the hill. That is the first surprise of Djémila. It is a Roman city shaped by a mountain rather than by a ruler and a plan.

The oldest part of town lies at the northern end, around the first forum. A forum was the heart of a Roman town — a paved public square for business, law, and religion. Here you would find the Capitol temple, the council house, and a market. Citizens met, argued, traded, and listened to official announcements. Try to imagine the square filled with voices, with stalls and statues and painted walls, instead of the silence you hear today.

Near the old forum stands one of the most charming buildings in the town: a market built by a family named Cosinius, whose generosity is recorded in inscriptions. It is a small courtyard surrounded by little shops. In the stone counters you can still find carved measures, used to check that traders gave honest quantities. These ordinary details matter. They show a town where people bought meat and grain, where someone worried about being cheated, and where a wealthy family wanted to be remembered for paying for the building.

As Cuicul grew wealthier, it outgrew its first walls. In the second and third centuries the town spread south, down the ridge. A new forum appeared there, larger and more open. This is where the town showed its loyalty to the imperial family. One of the emperors of that age, Septimius Severus, was himself born in North Africa, in what is now Libya. Local towns took real pride in that connection, and Djémila shows it clearly.

On the edge of the new forum rises a tall temple dedicated to the Severan family. It stands on a high platform reached by a broad staircase, with columns still standing at the front. From the steps you look down across the whole square. Nearby stands a triumphal arch dedicated to the emperor Caracalla, the son of Severus. Its single opening frames the sky and the hills beyond. An arch like this had no military use. It was a monument of loyalty — a way for a provincial town to say, in stone, that it belonged to the empire.

Walk a little further and the land drops into a natural bowl. Here the builders set the theatre, cut partly into the slope outside the old walls. Its stone seats curve around the stage. From the top rows you can see out over the valley, so the audience watched both the play and the mountains at the same time. On a quiet day, try speaking from the stage area. The shape of the seating still carries a voice up the slope.

Life in a Roman town also meant bathing. Djémila had large public baths, and their remains are still easy to read. Look for the low brick pillars in some rooms. They held up the floor, and hot air from furnaces flowed underneath to warm it. The baths were not only for washing. People came to exercise, to gossip, to close deals, and to spend the afternoon. They were as much a social club as a place to get clean.

Now comes the second surprise. At the southern end of the site lies a Christian quarter, built when the town had become Christian, in late antiquity. There are two basilicas — large church halls with long aisles — and a baptistery, where new believers were baptised. The baptistery is round, and it is roofed again today to protect it. Inside, a basin is set into a floor of mosaic. Standing there, you can see how a town of pagan temples changed its faith while keeping its Roman stone, its streets, and its craftsmen.

Those craftsmen left their finest work in mosaics. Many of the best pieces are now kept in the small museum on the site. Thousands of tiny cubes of coloured stone make up scenes of gods, hunts, animals, and the sea. Some of them once covered the floors of rich houses here. Take your time with them. The colours are often softer and warmer in person than in photographs, and you can see how carefully each line was laid by hand.

One reason Djémila looks the way it does is its location. Many Roman towns along the coast were built over by later cities. Their stones were reused and their plans disappeared under new streets. This mountain town was left alone after its decline. As a result, the layout survives with unusual clarity. In 1982 it was listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site, recognised as one of the best preserved Roman towns in the mountains of North Africa.

Before you leave, find a high point and look back along the ridge. Follow the line of the main street from the old forum to the arch, then on to the churches. That one walk covers several centuries of change. Notice the colour of the stone as the light shifts, from grey in shadow to honey in the sun. Listen again to the wind in the grass. The town is quiet now, but its streets still lead exactly where they always did — and you have just walked them.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone paving and slopes.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is scarce.
Visit early morning or late afternoon for softer light.
Save time for the on-site mosaic museum.
Climb the theatre's top rows for valley views.
