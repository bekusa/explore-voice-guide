The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the trees, the city noise drops away. You hear crows, the dry rustle of leaves underfoot, and somewhere beyond the palms, a horn from a boat on the river. The air feels heavier here, a little cooler, and it smells of damp earth and bark. You are standing in one of the oldest botanic gardens in Asia, on the western bank of the Hooghly, just across the water from Kolkata.

This place began in 1787. An East India Company army officer named Colonel Robert Kyd proposed a garden here at Shibpur. His idea was practical, not poetic. The Company wanted to find plants of commercial value. That meant spices, timber, and crops that might grow in Bengal and make money. So the garden started as an experiment in trade, with soil and seed as its tools.

Within a few years, the garden found the man who shaped its scientific life. William Roxburgh, a Scottish surgeon and botanist, became its superintendent in the 1790s. Many people call him the father of Indian botany. He documented hundreds of plant species across the subcontinent. He also commissioned Indian artists to paint them in fine detail. Those paintings became a lasting record, and they show how much local skill went into the science of that era.

Now for the reason most visitors come. Somewhere in these grounds stands the Great Banyan. From a distance, it does not look like one tree. It looks like a small forest. A banyan is a type of fig. It drops roots from its branches, and when those roots reach the ground, they thicken into new trunks. Over centuries, this single tree has spread outward in every direction.

Here is the first surprise. The original main trunk is gone. Two strong cyclones in the 1860s damaged the tree badly. Later, the central trunk became infected with fungus, and in 1925 it was removed. Yet the tree did not die. It kept living through its thousands of aerial roots. What you see today is a living circle with an empty heart, a tree that survived by becoming many.

Walk slowly around its edge. Gardeners guide the hanging roots down into the soil, sometimes along supports, so the canopy can keep growing. A path rings the tree, and a low barrier keeps feet off the roots. Look up into the shade. Birds move through the branches, and the light comes down in small, shifting patches. Standing here, it is hard to say where one trunk ends and another begins.

The second surprise is about tea. In the early nineteenth century, this garden took part in early trials of tea cultivation for British India. Plants and seeds passed through these grounds as officials tested what might grow in the hills of the northeast. Those experiments were one thread in a much larger story. That story ended with India becoming one of the great tea-growing countries of the world.

The garden carries a second name today. In 2009, it was renamed in honour of Jagadish Chandra Bose. Bose was a Bengali scientist who worked in physics and in plant biology. He built sensitive instruments to measure how plants respond to touch, heat, and other changes. He argued that plants react to the world in ways people had overlooked. Naming this garden after him links the place to Indian science, not only to colonial trade.

The grounds are large, so pace yourself. Wide avenues run between lawns and groves, and a network of artificial lakes winds through the garden. Different sections hold palms, bamboos, cacti, and trees from many continents. Some trees carry small labels with their scientific names. Even if you do not know the plants, the variety of bark is worth your attention. Some trunks are smooth and pale. Others are deeply cracked, rough, almost like stone.

Near the water, watch the surface of the ponds. In some seasons, giant water lilies spread their round leaves across the water. These leaves have upturned rims, like huge green trays. The plant comes from the Amazon region of South America. It is a reminder of how far this collection reaches. A seed or a cutting from another continent can end up floating quietly in a pond in Bengal.

The garden is not only a park. It is also a working scientific site. The Botanical Survey of India manages it, and the grounds include a large herbarium. A herbarium is a library of dried, pressed plant specimens, kept for study. Researchers use collections like this to identify species and track how plants change over time. So while families picnic on the lawns, quiet scientific work continues in the background.

For many people in Howrah and Kolkata, this is simply a place to breathe. On holidays and weekends, you will see families on blankets, students with books, and couples walking slowly under the trees. Winter mornings bring the softest light and the most comfortable air. In the heat of summer, the shade of the old trees becomes the real attraction. The pace here is slow, and that is part of the point.

Before you leave, stop for a moment wherever you are. Notice the ground under your shoes, the shape of the branches above you, the sound of the river traffic beyond the trees. This garden began as a plan to profit from plants. Over more than two centuries, it became something gentler. As you walk back toward the gate, look again at the roots hanging from the branches, still slowly finding their way down to the earth.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early on winter mornings for cool air and soft light.
Wear comfortable shoes; the grounds are large and spread out.
Head to the Great Banyan first before weekend crowds build.
Carry water and a hat for walking in the summer heat.
Consider the river ferry from Kolkata for a scenic approach.
