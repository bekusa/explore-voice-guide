The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere beyond the walls, the Danube is moving — wide, brown, and patient. Under your feet the ground is soft with grass, and in front of you rises a long line of stone and brick, banded in pale and rust-coloured layers. The towers stand close together, like teeth along a jaw. On a windy day you can hear the gusts catch on the battlements. Welcome to Smederevo Fortress, one of the largest medieval lowland fortresses in Europe.

Most castles in this part of the world sit on hilltops. This one does not. It sits almost at the level of the water, at the point where the small Jezava river meets the Danube. The builders used the rivers as a moat on two sides and dug a ditch along the third. The result is a rough triangle on the map. It is enormous, flat, and very exposed — a fortress that relied on the height of its walls rather than the height of the land.

The man behind it was Despot Đurađ Branković, the ruler of Serbia in the first half of the fifteenth century. A despot, in this context, was a high Byzantine title for a ruling prince — not an insult. Belgrade had passed to Hungary, and Serbia needed a new capital. Đurađ chose this riverbank. Work began in the late 1420s, and the core of the fortress rose remarkably fast, within just a few years.

That speed came at a price, and here the story moves from records into folklore. Đurađ's wife was Irene Kantakouzene, a woman from a Byzantine noble family. Serbians call her Jerina. Legend has it that she drove the peasants to exhaustion to finish the walls, and folk songs gave her a grim nickname — Cursed Jerina. Historians treat this as a popular tale, not documented fact. But the legend has stayed with the stones for centuries.

Now look at the walls themselves. Notice how the layers change — rows of rough stone, then thin courses of red brick, then stone again. This banded style comes from Byzantine building traditions, the architecture of the Eastern Roman Empire centred on Constantinople. Here and there you may spot stones that look out of place — carved blocks, smoother faces, odd shapes. Builders in a hurry often reused older material, and some of these pieces are thought to come from earlier ruins in the region.

The fortress has two main parts. The smaller enclosure, near the river corner, is called the Small Town. This was the heart of power — the despot's residence, his hall, his private world. It had its own walls and its own moat, so it could hold out even if the larger area fell. Around it spreads the much bigger enclosure, the Large Town, where soldiers, craftsmen, and townspeople once lived inside the defences.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. On one of the towers of the Small Town, there is an inscription laid in brick, set into the masonry itself. It names Đurađ as the builder and carries a date in the old Byzantine calendar, which counted years from the creation of the world. In our calendar, it points to around 1430. So the wall is, in a sense, signed by the man who raised it.

The towers deserve a slow walk. There are roughly two dozen of them along the walls, square and massive, spaced close enough that defenders on one could cover the next. Some still stand almost to full height. Others are broken off, their insides open to the sky. Look up at the tops and you can still make out the shapes of crenellations — the gaps through which archers once fired.

The capital did not stay Serbian for long. The Ottoman Empire pressed north through the fifteenth century, and in 1459 Smederevo surrendered to Ottoman forces. Historians often mark this moment as the end of the medieval Serbian state. For a fortress built to save a kingdom, that is a heavy ending. But the walls themselves survived, and their story continued under new masters.

The Ottomans adapted the fortress for a new age of warfare — the age of cannons. They added an outer line of defences with lower, thicker towers, shaped to absorb and return artillery fire. As you move around the site, watch for the difference. The tall square towers belong to the original Serbian plan. The lower, rounder or many-sided works belong to the later layer. Two empires and two styles of war sit side by side.

The fortress also carries a much more recent scar. In June 1941, during the Second World War, a huge ammunition store inside the walls exploded. The blast killed many people and damaged large parts of the town. Some of the fortress walls suffered too. When you see gaps, breaks, and restored sections, remember that not every wound here is medieval. Some come from the twentieth century.

Today the place feels open and calm. Locals cut across the grass, walk dogs, and sit along the riverbank. Families come for picnics, and the wide inner spaces sometimes host concerts and festivals, especially in the warmer months and around the grape harvest, which matters a great deal in this wine-growing area. The fortress is less a sealed monument than a park with very old walls around it.

Before you leave, walk toward the river side and turn back. Let your eye follow the line of towers as it runs away from you along the water. Notice the colours — grey stone, faded red brick, green moss in the joints. Listen again for the Danube, the same river that once carried boats, soldiers, and news of war to this shore. Six centuries later, it is still moving past these walls, as patient as ever.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full wall circuit to see both building phases.
Visit near sunset for warm light on the brickwork.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven grass and broken paving.
Check local listings for concerts held inside the walls.
Bring water, since the open grounds offer little shade.
