The guide

The story

Under the arches of a Spanish cloister, a wall of dark grey stone curves in one smooth, unbroken sweep. The blocks are small, tight, and perfectly level. Outside, traffic passes on one of Cusco's busiest avenues. In here, the room stays cool and quiet. This wall was already old when the church above it was young.

You are in Qorikancha, once the most important temple of the Inca empire. The name means golden enclosure in Quechua. This was the house of Inti, the sun god, and the religious heart of a state that ruled millions of people along the Andes. Pilgrims came here from every corner of the empire, and conquered lords sent their finest offerings.

Spanish chroniclers who saw the temple before its destruction struggled to describe it. They wrote of walls lined with sheets of gold, and of a garden where corn stalks, llamas, and shepherds were modeled in gold and silver at life size. Gold, to the Inca, was not money. It was the sweat of the sun, reserved for the divine.

That treasure vanished quickly. In 1533, the captured emperor Atahualpa offered rooms filled with gold and silver in exchange for his freedom. By the standard account, the ransom filled one room with gold and two with silver. Much of Qorikancha's gold was stripped, carried north, and melted into bars. Atahualpa was executed anyway. Within a few years, Spanish rule reached Cusco itself.

The conquerors gave the temple to the Dominican order. On top of the Inca walls, the friars raised the church and convent of Santo Domingo, using the temple's own upper walls as a quarry. The message was deliberate. The new faith would stand, quite literally, on the old one. What they could not easily do was tear the Inca foundations down.

Those foundations refused to fall. A great earthquake in 1650 wrecked much of colonial Cusco, and another in 1950 brought down large parts of the church here. Each time, the Inca masonry underneath survived almost untouched. The 1950 quake exposed Inca chambers that the convent had hidden for centuries, and restorers chose to leave them visible.

Walk the inner courtyard and you can enter several of those chambers. Tradition and early accounts assign them to the moon, the stars, thunder, and the rainbow, each once served by its own attendants. The stones here are laid in even courses, like brickwork, a style reserved for the holiest buildings. The trapezoid niches are cut so precisely that their shadows fall in straight, even lines.

The finest piece of engineering is the curved wall you met at the start, which follows the street outside in a gentle arc. Curved walls are rare in Inca building, and this one is considered among the best stonework in Peru. It has survived every earthquake since the fifteenth century.

Qorikancha was also the center of the empire's sacred map. From this temple, about forty invisible lines called ceques radiated outward across the landscape, linking hundreds of shrines, springs, and stones around Cusco. Priests and local families maintained them in a fixed order through the year. The temple you are standing in was, in effect, kilometer zero of the Inca world.

The sun itself was tracked from here. At the June solstice, the season of Inti Raymi, the temple hosted the empire's greatest festival. Chroniclers describe the Inca ruler drinking a toast to the sun in this precinct, surrounded by images of his ancestors. Mummies of former rulers were kept in the temple and brought out for ceremonies.

The colonial floors above hold their own art. The cloister carries paintings of the Cusco School, in which Andean artists learned European technique and quietly kept their own symbols, their own plants and birds, inside Christian scenes. Look at the gold leaf laid thickly on canvas, an echo of what these walls once wore.

Outside, along Avenida El Sol, a terraced garden drops below street level. It stands roughly where the golden garden once stood, and today it grows real corn and native plants instead of metal ones. From the avenue below, you can see the full curve of the great wall riding above the terraces, one of the best free views in Cusco.

Two buildings share one body here, and neither has let go. The church needs the temple to hold it up. The temple wears the church like a roof. Stand where grey stone meets white plaster, and rest your eyes on that line. Five centuries fit inside a single seam.
