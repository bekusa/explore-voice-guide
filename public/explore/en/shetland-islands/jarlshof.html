The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the sea in long, steady pushes. Waves break on the shingle just below the walls. Somewhere overhead, a gull calls and is gone. Under your feet the grass is short and springy, cropped low by weather and sheep. In front of you, low stone walls curl and overlap like the lines of a fingerprint. This is Jarlshof, at the southern tip of Shetland's Mainland. People lived on this small piece of ground, again and again, for more than four thousand years.

The first surprise is how this place was found. For centuries, most of it lay hidden under sand and turf. Then, in the late nineteenth century, violent storms tore away part of the shoreline. The sea uncovered old stonework that nobody alive had seen before. Later excavations through the twentieth century showed what lay beneath. It was not one settlement but many, built one on top of another. Stone age, bronze age, iron age, Viking, medieval, and early modern... all on the same few acres.

The name is the second surprise. Jarlshof sounds ancient and Norse. It means something like the earl's house. But no Viking ever used it. The name comes from Sir Walter Scott, the Scottish novelist. He visited Shetland and later set part of his novel The Pirate here, published in 1822. He took the ruined house on the site and gave it this romantic name. The name stayed. So the oldest site in this part of Shetland carries a name from a nineteenth-century book.

Why did so many people choose this spot? Look around and the answer becomes clear. There is fresh water close by. There is shelter from the worst of the Atlantic weather. The bay in front gives a landing place for boats. The sea gives fish, seals, and shellfish. The land behind gives grazing and small fields. For a farming and fishing people, it was a good place. And when one group left or faded, another found the same advantages.

The oldest houses are the smallest and the hardest to read. They sit low to the ground, near the edge of the site. These are the homes of early farmers from the late Stone Age and the Bronze Age. Their walls are thick and rounded, with small rooms set into them. Archaeologists found evidence of metalworking here too. Clay moulds for casting bronze tools and weapons turned up in one building. Imagine the heat of a small furnace in this wind, and the smell of hot metal.

Next, look toward the sea. You will see the remains of a broch, a tall, round, dry-stone tower of the Iron Age. Brochs are found mainly in northern and western Scotland. Nobody is fully sure what they were for. They may have been defended homes, or symbols of power, or both. Here, the sea has already taken part of it. Half of the broch has gone over the edge. What stays is a curved section of wall, cut open like a cross-section in a textbook.

Near the broch are the wheelhouses, and these are many visitors' favourite part. A wheelhouse is a round stone house with walls that run inward from the outside, like the spokes of a wheel. Each spoke divided the space into small bays around a central hearth. Some of these structures still stand to a good height. You can step inside and feel how enclosed they are. The stone holds the quiet. The wind becomes a sound outside rather than a force on your face.

Then the Vikings arrived. Norse settlers came to Shetland around the ninth century, and they built here too. Their houses are different again. Instead of circles, you see long rectangles. These are Norse longhouses, with people at one end and animals often at the other. The settlement grew over generations, with new buildings and outbuildings added. Shetland stayed under Norse rule for centuries, and only passed to the Scottish crown in the fifteenth century. Many local place names still come from the Norse language.

After the Norse came a medieval farmstead, smaller and plainer. It is easy to walk past, but it matters. It shows that ordinary farming life carried on here, quietly, through the Middle Ages. No famous names, no great events. Just families working the land, mending walls, and watching the weather. Much of what you see at Jarlshof is like this. It is a record of daily life, not of kings and battles. That is part of what makes it feel so close.

The tallest ruin on the site is the last big addition. This is the Old House of Sumburgh, a stone laird's house from the late sixteenth century. A laird was a Scottish landowner. The house is linked with the Stewart earls of Orkney, who held power over these islands at that time. It was the ruin of this house that caught Walter Scott's imagination. Today it is a roofless shell. Its gable ends rise above the older walls and give the whole site a clear landmark.

The Old House is also the best place to understand the layout. If access allows, find a raised spot and look down. From above, the pattern becomes readable. Circles for the older homes. Long rectangles for the Norse. Straight, squared walls for the later farm and the laird's house. Each age built in its own shape. Together, they form a kind of map of Shetland's past, laid out on the grass in grey stone and green turf.

The site sits close to Sumburgh Airport, and that contrast is worth noticing. Planes rise and land not far from houses that are thousands of years old. Not far away, Sumburgh Head stands high above the sea, with its lighthouse and its seabird cliffs. In the right season, puffins nest there. The modern visitor arrives the same way many older visitors did, from across the water. Only the boats have changed. The sea, the wind, and the light are much the same.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the shore. Look at the line where the broch wall meets empty air. That gap is the sea, still working on this place, just as it did when the storms first uncovered it. Feel the wind on your face, and listen to the waves on the stones below. People stood here, in this same weather, for forty centuries. As you walk back past the low, curving walls, you are simply the latest visitor in a very long line.
