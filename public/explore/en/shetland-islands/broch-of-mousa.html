The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves across short grass. Waves break on rock somewhere below you. And in front of you stands a round tower of grey stone, slightly waisted like a cooling tower, with no mortar holding a single stone in place. Put your palm against the wall. The stones are cold, rough and flat, laid one on top of another by hand. People did this here roughly two thousand years ago. The tower has stood through every Shetland winter since.

You are on Mousa, a small uninhabited island off the east coast of the Shetland mainland. The structure in front of you is a broch. That word describes a type of round drystone tower found only in Scotland, mostly in the far north and west. Hundreds of brochs once stood across the Highlands and islands. Most are now low rings of rubble. This one is different. It is the best-preserved broch anywhere, and it is generally described as the tallest still standing.

Archaeologists date brochs to the Iron Age, roughly the last few centuries before Christ and the early centuries after. Mousa is usually placed in that window, about two thousand years old. That makes it older than most of the castles of Europe by more than a thousand years. Nobody wrote down who built it. The builders left no names, no inscriptions and no records. What they left is the building itself, and it tells us a great deal.

Look at the shape. The wall is thick at the base and leans gently inward as it rises, then flares very slightly near the top. The tower stands around thirteen metres high. Its walls are built entirely without mortar, a technique called drystone, where weight and careful placement do all the work. The local stone splits into flat slabs, and that is a big reason this tower survived. The slabs lock together. Gravity keeps them there.

Now for the first surprise. The wall is not one wall. It is two. An inner wall and an outer wall rise side by side, with a narrow gap between them. Horizontal slabs tie the two together at intervals, creating a series of galleries stacked one above the other. Inside that gap, a stone staircase winds upward around the tower. This hollow design made the structure lighter and more stable. It also let people climb to the top from inside the wall itself.

Walk to the entrance. It is a low, narrow passage, and most adults must duck to pass through. Feel how the light changes as you move inside. The walls close in around you, the wind drops, and the sound of the sea grows muffled. A narrow entrance like this was easy to defend and easy to keep warm. Whether brochs were mainly forts, homes, status symbols or all three is still debated by archaeologists today.

Step into the interior and look up. You are standing in a circular space open to the sky. The room is surprisingly small compared with the thickness of the walls around it. Along the inner face you can see openings, small rectangular gaps arranged in vertical lines. These let light and air into the galleries inside the wall. Low stone features on the floor come from later phases, when people reused and adapted the space long after the original builders.

If access is open when you visit, you can climb the staircase inside the wall. It is steep, dark and tight. The steps are uneven stone, worn by many feet. Take it slowly and use your hands. At the top, you reach a narrow walkway with the open interior on one side and the island on the other. From there you look across Mousa Sound toward the Shetland mainland. It is one of the few places where you can stand on an Iron Age wall this high.

The tower appears in written history much later, through Norse sagas. Shetland was settled by Norse people from Scandinavia, and their stories mention Mousa by name. According to one saga, a young couple eloping from Norway were shipwrecked here and sheltered in the broch over a winter. Another saga tells of a later episode in the twelfth century, when a man ran off with a noblewoman and held out in the tower while her son, an earl, besieged it.

Those saga accounts are a second surprise. They suggest the broch was already ancient when the Norse arrived, yet still strong enough to shelter people and resist attack. In the siege story, the attackers reportedly found the tower too difficult to take by force, and the matter ended in negotiation. The sagas mix history with storytelling, so treat the details with care. But they show that this building mattered to people more than a thousand years after its construction.

The third surprise is alive. Small seabirds called storm petrels nest inside the gaps in the drystone walls. They are tiny, dark birds, about the size of a sparrow, that spend their days far out at sea. On summer nights they return to the broch in large numbers, fluttering around the tower in the half-light. You may hear them before you see them, a soft churring sound coming from inside the stones. Mousa is protected as a nature reserve partly because of them.

The island itself deserves attention. There are no houses, no roads and no shops. You walk on grass, heather and peat, past old field walls and the ruins of abandoned farms. Seals often haul out on the rocks and beaches, especially along the sheltered shores. Arctic terns and skuas nest on the open ground in summer, and they defend their nests with real determination. Keep to the paths, watch where you step, and give the birds space.

Think about the people who built this tower. They had no metal cranes, no cement and no written plans that survive. They chose a spot by the shore, gathered flat stones, and raised them course by course into a shape that still works today. They did it on a small island at the edge of the North Atlantic, in wind and rain. Other brochs fell. This one held, partly through skill and partly through good stone.

Before you leave, stand back from the tower and look at it once more. Notice how the grey stone changes colour as clouds pass over the sun. Notice the lichen, orange and pale green, spreading slowly across the outer wall. Listen again for the wind and the water. As you walk back toward the landing, turn around at least once. The tower will look smaller with distance, but it will still be standing, as it has for about two thousand years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the seasonal boat from Sandwick well in advance.
Join a summer night trip to see storm petrels.
Wear sturdy waterproof boots for wet, uneven island paths.
Bring a small torch for the dark internal staircase.
Keep away from nesting terns and skuas in summer.
