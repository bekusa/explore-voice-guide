The guide

The story

Listen first. Waves break against black rock on every side, and the sound never quite stops. The boat from Malvan rocks gently as it nears the island. Salt hangs in the air, mixed with the faint smell of diesel and drying fish from the shore behind you. Ahead, a long line of dark stone walls rises straight out of the Arabian Sea. You are approaching Sindhudurg Fort, and the sea is doing exactly what it was chosen to do: keeping you at a distance.

The name itself tells the story. In Marathi, "Sindhu" refers to the sea, and "durg" means fort. So this is, quite simply, the sea fort. It stands on a small rocky island called Kurte, a short boat ride from the town of Malvan, on the Konkan coast of Maharashtra. The whole district now carries the fort's name. That tells you how much this place matters to the people who live along this shore.

The man behind it was Shivaji, the seventeenth-century Maratha ruler who is known across India as Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj. The Marathas were a regional power from western India, and Shivaji built them into a kingdom. Work on this fort began in 1664 and took around three years. Shivaji understood something many inland rulers ignored. Whoever controlled the coast controlled trade, supplies, and the safety of the land behind it.

And the coast was crowded with rivals. Portuguese, English, and Dutch traders were all active along these waters. To the north, the Siddis of Janjira held their own island fortress and raided the shoreline. Shivaji wanted a navy, and a navy needs a base it can defend. This island, ringed by reefs and shallow rocks, was a natural choice. Large enemy ships could not easily get close without risking their hulls.

As your boat pulls in, look at the walls. They are built from large blocks of dark stone, fitted closely together. Popular accounts say that molten lead was poured into the foundations to bind the stones against the sea. You will hear this from almost every boatman and guide here. Whatever the exact quantities, the result is clear. More than three and a half centuries later, these walls still stand in the surf.

Now comes the first surprise. Try to find the main entrance from the water. It is not easy. The gate is set behind overlapping sections of wall, so from the sea it stays out of sight until you are almost upon it. An attacker could sail along the fort and never see the door. Only up close does the gap appear, turning at an angle that would slow any rush of soldiers.

Step inside, and the mood changes. The roar of the waves drops away, and you hear birds, voices, and wind moving through trees. The fort encloses a surprisingly large area. Dozens of bastions curve out from the walls, each one shaped to give defenders a wide view along the outside face. Walk up onto the ramparts if you can. From there, the full shape of the island and the coastline becomes clear.

Here is the second surprise. Inside a fort surrounded entirely by salt water, there are wells of fresh, drinkable water. Locals call three of them by playful names that translate as the milk well, the curd well, and the sugar well. For a garrison under siege, fresh water meant survival. How it gathers here, on a small island of rock in the open sea, still makes visitors stop and wonder.

Look out also for a small shrine near the walls. Here, preserved in hardened lime, are marks that local tradition says are the handprint and footprint of Shivaji himself. People bend down to touch them, and many leave flowers. Whether you take the story as history or devotion, watching people pause here shows you how close this ruler still feels to the community, centuries after his lifetime.

Deeper inside stands the Shivrajeshwar Temple. It is said to have been built by Rajaram, one of Shivaji's sons, and it holds an image of Shivaji. Temples dedicated to Shivaji as a figure of worship are very rare, and many people describe this one as unique. The image shows him seated, without the usual crown. Visitors often move more quietly here. Take off your shoes, and step in slowly.

Something else surprises many people: this is not an empty ruin. A small number of families still live inside the fort walls. You may see houses, washing lines, a few shops, and children walking between the old stones. They are part of the fort's life today. Please treat their homes with respect, keep your voice low near the houses, and ask before taking photographs of people.

Once you are back on the mainland, the area around Malvan is worth your time. The coast here has long sandy beaches, and nearby Tarkarli is known for clear water and snorkelling trips. Malvan itself is famous for its seafood cooking, often rich with coconut and red chilli. Small eateries near the jetty serve fish fresh from the morning boats. It is the same sea that once protected this fort.

Before you leave the island, find a quiet spot on the walls and look back toward the shore. Watch the fishing boats come and go, just as supply boats once did for Shivaji's sailors. Feel the warm stone under your hand, rough with salt. When your boat pulls away, turn around once more. Notice how quickly that hidden gate disappears again into the long dark line of the wall.

Practical tips

Reach the fort by boat from Malvan jetty.
Check sea conditions; boats may stop in rough weather.
Carry water, a hat, and sunscreen for exposed walls.
Wear easy shoes; remove them at the temple.
Pair the visit with Malvani seafood near the jetty.
