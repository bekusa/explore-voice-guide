The guide

The story

Listen first. The boat engine drops to a low hum, and water slaps softly against the hull. Ahead of you, low on the surface of Lake Pichola, a palace of pale stone and white marble seems to float. On a calm morning its reflection is almost perfect. On a breezy afternoon the image breaks into ripples of cream and gold. This is Jag Mandir, the island palace of Udaipur, and the best way to meet it is exactly as you are doing now: slowly, from the water.

Before we land, look around the lake itself. Lake Pichola is an artificial lake, created centuries ago and later enlarged by the rulers of Mewar, the kingdom that made Udaipur its capital. The hills rise on every side. Along the eastern shore, the long cream facade of the City Palace stretches above the ghats, the stone steps that lead down to the water. Every royal building here faces the lake. Water was not decoration in this dry region. It was life, power, and pleasure all at once.

Jag Mandir belongs to the Sisodia dynasty, the ruling family of Mewar. The island palace grew in stages over the seventeenth century. Work began under Maharana Amar Singh the First, continued under his son Karan Singh the Second, and was completed under Maharana Jagat Singh the First. The palace carries his name. Jag Mandir is often translated as the Lake Garden Palace, and you will soon see why. Gardens and courtyards fill much of the island.

Now, as the boat draws near the jetty, watch the waterline. A row of stone elephants stands along the edge of the island, trunks lowered, as if they are guarding the entrance. They are carved in a pale stone and set at regular intervals. From a distance they look almost alive, as though a royal procession has paused at the shore. Many visitors photograph the palace and miss them completely. Take a moment. They are some of the most charming details in all of Udaipur.

Here is the first surprise. This quiet pleasure island once sheltered a rebel prince from the Mughal empire. In the 1620s, Prince Khurram rose up against his father, the emperor Jahangir. The rulers of Mewar gave the young prince refuge here, on the lake. Years later, that same prince took the throne in Delhi and Agra under a new name: Shah Jahan. He is the emperor who later built the Taj Mahal.

That connection has grown into one of Udaipur's favourite stories. Locals say that the domes and inlaid marble of Jag Mandir stayed in the young prince's memory, and that they helped inspire the Taj Mahal. It is a lovely idea, and you will hear it often. But it is a legend, not documented history. What we can say is simpler and still remarkable: the future builder of the Taj once lived on this small island, looking at these same hills.

The building most closely linked to the prince is the Gul Mahal, a domed pavilion near the heart of the island. It was built for him during his stay. Look at its shape. The dome and the slender lines show a blend of Rajput and Mughal taste. Rajput refers to the warrior clans who ruled much of this region. Mughal refers to the empire based in northern India. Here, the two styles meet in a single structure, which tells you something about the politics of the time.

Inside and around the pavilions, look closely at the stone surfaces. In places, the marble carries inlay work, where small pieces of coloured stone are set into the white surface to form patterns. The craft takes great patience. Each piece is cut by hand to fit its neighbour. Run your eyes along the borders and the arches. The patterns are precise, but the stone also shows the marks of age, repair, and centuries of monsoon rain.

Now walk into the gardens. Paths run between beds of plants and shaded corners. Courtyards open up between the pavilions, and the sound of the lake comes through every gap in the walls. In the heat of a Rajasthan summer, a garden on water was the ultimate luxury. Cool air moved across the lake. Royal families came here to rest, to celebrate, and to escape the noise of the court on the shore.

Here is the second surprise. In 1857, during the great uprising against British rule in India, the island played a protective role once again. The Maharana of the time gave shelter on Jag Mandir to a group of European families. He reportedly had the boats on the lake taken away, so that no one could reach the island. The same water that made Jag Mandir a pleasure garden also made it a fortress.

Today the island has a new life. Parts of the palace are used for dining, private events, and weddings, and the courtyards are often prepared for celebrations. If you visit in the evening, you may see lamps and decorations along the terraces. The atmosphere changes completely after sunset. The stone turns warm and amber, the lake goes dark, and the lights of the city begin to appear along the shore.

Before you leave, find a spot near the edge of the island and look back toward the city. This is a view most people in Udaipur never see. The City Palace rises in layers above the water. To one side, the other island palace, Jag Niwas, sits white and low on the lake. Behind it all, the Aravalli hills form a ring of soft brown and green. From here, you understand how the whole city was designed around this water.

So take one more breath of lake air. Notice the stone under your feet, smoothed by centuries of footsteps. Listen to the water against the steps, and to the boats crossing toward the ghats. When your boat pulls away, turn around once more. Watch the stone elephants grow smaller at the waterline, and the palace settle back into its reflection, as it has done every evening for nearly four hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the boat from the ghats near the City Palace.
Sit on the boat's open side for clear water views.
Bring sunglasses; glare off the lake is strong at midday.
Stay near sunset to watch the city lights appear.
Check ahead, since private events sometimes close areas.
