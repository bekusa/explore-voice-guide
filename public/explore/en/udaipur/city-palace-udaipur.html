The guide

The story

Start with the stone under your feet. It is cool, worn smooth by centuries of bare soles and slippered ones. Above you, cream-coloured walls climb in layer after layer of balconies, domes and latticed windows. Somewhere below, out of sight for now, lies the still water of Lake Pichola. The air carries a faint smell of dust and marigold. You are standing at the edge of a palace that took roughly four hundred years to finish... and in a sense, it is still not finished.

This is the City Palace of Udaipur. It is not one building but many, joined together over generations. Each ruler added a wing, a courtyard or a tower. The result rises along a ridge on the eastern shore of the lake. From the water, it looks like a single white cliff. Up close, it feels more like a small town, with its own lanes, stairways and hidden corners.

The story begins in the sixteenth century. The rulers here were the Maharanas of Mewar, from the Sisodia clan of Rajputs, a warrior caste of western India. Their old capital was the hill fortress of Chittorgarh, to the east. Under growing pressure from the Mughal Empire, Maharana Udai Singh the Second looked for a safer seat. Tradition says he chose this spot beside the lake, protected by the Aravalli hills. The city that grew around the palace still carries his name.

Legend adds a softer detail. Local tellers say a holy man meditating on this ridge advised Udai Singh to build here, promising that the site would be well protected. Whether or not that meeting happened, the choice made sense. The hills form a natural wall. The lake gives water and a defensive moat. And the ridge lifts the palace high enough to watch every approach.

As you move through the gates, notice how they are built. The main entrance, the Badi Pol, or Great Gate, leads toward the Tripolia, a gate with three arches. Gates like these were not only grand. They slowed down visitors, and in older times, attackers. The passages turn. The ground rises. Even today, you can feel the palace controlling your pace, making you walk the way its builders wanted you to walk.

Look closely at the architecture and you will see two traditions meeting. The strong walls, the high towers and the defensive layout are Rajput. But the delicate arches, the inlaid patterns and the carved screens show Mughal influence. This mix is interesting, because the Mewar rulers were famous for resisting Mughal power. They fought the empire, yet they still borrowed its sense of beauty. Politics and taste do not always follow the same road.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Parts of the palace are built directly on the natural rock of the ridge. Because of this, a garden called the Amar Vilas sits high up in the complex, at what feels like rooftop level. Trees grow there, and there is a pool and arcades around it. You climb stairs for what seems like several floors, and then you step out into a garden. It is a quiet surprise in a building of stone.

Now look for the sun. The face of the sun appears again and again across the palace, on walls, panels and doorways. It is the emblem of the Mewar dynasty, which traces its descent from the sun. The rulers also saw themselves as servants of the god Shiva, worshipped locally as Eklingji. In this view, the Maharana did not own the kingdom. He looked after it on behalf of the god.

One of the most loved spaces is the Mor Chowk, the Peacock Courtyard. Its walls hold peacocks made from small pieces of coloured glass, set in mosaic. The peacock is the national bird of India, and here the feathers shine in deep blues and greens. Stand still for a moment and move your head slowly. The glass catches the light differently from each angle, so the birds seem to change as you watch them.

Nearby, rooms decorated with mirror work show another taste of the court. Small pieces of mirror cover walls and ceilings. In the days before electric light, a single lamp in such a room could fill the whole space with points of light. Elsewhere, look for painted miniatures, coloured glass windows and narrow, low doorways. Those small doors were not a mistake. They helped with defence, and they kept the inner rooms cool.

The palace also holds one of its strangest stories. In the late nineteenth century, Maharana Sajjan Singh ordered a large collection of crystal furniture from a glassmaker in Birmingham, England. Chairs, tables, even beds were made of cut crystal. According to the widely told account, the ruler died before the shipment arrived. For many years, the crates stayed sealed. Today the collection is displayed in the Fateh Prakash wing, in what is known as the Crystal Gallery.

The City Palace is not only a museum. The descendants of the Mewar royal family still live in part of the complex. Other wings now serve as heritage hotels. A large section is open as a museum, run by a family trust. So as you walk, you are moving through a living place, not a frozen one. Ceremonies and festivals still take place here, and the courtyards still fill with people on special days.

Before you leave, find a window or balcony that faces the water. Out on Lake Pichola, you can see white palaces that seem to float. One is the Lake Palace, built as a royal summer retreat and now a hotel. Another is the island palace of Jag Mandir. In the evening, their reflections stretch across the lake. Boats move slowly between them, and the hills behind turn grey and then blue.

Take one more look around you. The cool stone, the carved windows, the voices of other visitors echoing in the courtyards. Four centuries of builders shaped this ridge, one wing at a time. As you walk back down through the gates, notice how the palace hands you back to the city. The lanes outside are narrower and louder. But if you turn around, the white walls are still there, watching over the water.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early to enjoy the courtyards before tour groups arrive.
Wear comfortable shoes for many steep, uneven stone stairs.
Save the lake-facing balconies for late afternoon light.
Book the Crystal Gallery separately, as it needs its own ticket.
Combine your visit with an evening boat ride on Lake Pichola.
