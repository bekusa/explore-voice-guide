The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves through rough grass and heather, and somewhere below, water touches a stony shore. The air here smells of peat, wet rock, and pine. Glenveagh sits a short drive inland from Gaoth Dobhair, the Irish-speaking district on the Atlantic edge of County Donegal. As you leave the coast behind, the land rises into the Derryveagh Mountains. The valley opens around a long, dark lake, and at its far shore, a granite castle stands among the trees.

The lake is Lough Veagh, and the glen takes its name from the same Irish root. The valley is long and narrow, shaped by ice during the last glaciation. Steep slopes rise on both sides. Much of the ground is open bog and moorland, with patches of old oak woodland on the lower slopes. Today this landscape forms Glenveagh National Park, one of the largest protected areas in Ireland. It covers a wide sweep of mountain, lake, and bog.

The story of the castle begins with a man named John George Adair. He was a land speculator from County Laois, in the Irish midlands. In the middle of the nineteenth century, he bought up several estates in this part of Donegal. He was drawn, it is said, by the wild scenery and the sport of hunting. But his arrival brought one of the most painful episodes in the history of this region.

In 1861, after a dispute with his tenants, Adair carried out the Derryveagh evictions. Families across the surrounding townlands lost their homes in a matter of days. Many of the houses were pulled down so that no one could return. Some of those evicted later emigrated, with a number of them settling in Australia. Local memory of these events is still strong. As you walk through this quiet valley, it is worth remembering that the emptiness of the land is partly a human story.

The castle came later. Adair began building it in the late 1860s, and work finished in the early 1870s. It is a castellated mansion, built in a romantic style that borrows from Scottish baronial houses. Look at the square tower, the battlements, and the thick walls of local granite. It looks like a medieval fortress. In fact, it was a comfortable Victorian home, designed for shooting parties and long dinners rather than war.

Adair's wife was Cornelia, an American from a wealthy New York family. After her husband died in the 1880s, she spent much time here and shaped the estate's early gardens. She is also linked with the red deer of Glenveagh. A large herd still lives in the park today. In autumn, during the rut, the stags call across the valley. The sound is deep and rough, and on a still evening it carries a long way over the water.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The gardens are a surprise in this landscape. Outside the castle grounds, the hills are bare, wet, and exposed to Atlantic weather. Inside, sheltered by trees and by the shape of the glen, plants from much warmer places grow well. You will find tree ferns, rhododendrons, and exotic shrubs from South America, Asia, and the Himalayas. The mild, damp climate of the Irish west coast makes this possible.

In the twentieth century, the estate passed through new hands. In 1929, an American art historian, Arthur Kingsley Porter of Harvard University, bought Glenveagh. He was a scholar of medieval sculpture and a lover of Irish culture. His story ends in mystery. In 1933, he disappeared while visiting Inishbofin, a small island off this same Donegal coast. He was never found, and no one knows for certain what happened.

The next owner left the deepest mark on the gardens. Henry McIlhenny, an art collector from Philadelphia, bought the estate in 1937. He was a lively host, and over the following decades he welcomed artists, actors, and writers to the castle. He also spent years developing the gardens, planting carefully and adding formal features. McIlhenny sold the wider estate lands to the Irish state in the 1970s. He later gave the castle and gardens as well. The national park opened to the public in the 1980s.

When you walk the gardens, look for the different spaces. There is a walled garden with vegetables, fruit, and flowers arranged in neat beds. There is an Italian-style terrace with statues and clipped hedges. And there is a path known as the Belgian Walk. According to the popular local account, it was built by Belgian soldiers who stayed at Glenveagh while recovering during the First World War. It is a small, human detail in a very large landscape.

Here is a second surprise. Glenveagh is part of a conservation story about the golden eagle. These great birds once lived across Ireland, but they disappeared from the country in the early twentieth century. From the early 2000s, a reintroduction project brought young eagles from Scotland and released them in this park. Sightings are not guaranteed, and the birds range widely. But if you see a very large dark bird gliding high above the ridges, keep watching.

Most visitors arrive at the visitor centre near the head of the lake. From there, a road follows the shore to the castle. You can walk it, cycle it, or take the shuttle bus. The walk is flat and gentle, with the lake on one side and the hills on the other. Weather here changes fast. A bright morning can turn to mist and rain within an hour, and then clear again. Good boots and a waterproof jacket make a real difference.

Before you leave, stand for a moment by the water. Look along the length of the lake, toward the mountains at its far end. Notice how the colours shift with the light, from silver to grey to deep brown. Listen for the wind, for running streams, perhaps for a stag. This valley holds both beauty and loss. As you walk back toward Gaoth Dobhair and the sea, you carry a little of both with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a waterproof jacket; Donegal weather changes very fast.
Walk the lakeshore road one way, take the shuttle back.
Wear sturdy boots if you plan to leave the paths.
Visit in autumn evenings to hear the red deer rut.
Bring binoculars for scanning the ridges for eagles.
