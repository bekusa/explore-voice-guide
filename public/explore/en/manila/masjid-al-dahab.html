The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look up at the dome, notice the sound of Quiapo around you. Jeepney engines idle and cough. Vendors call out prices in Tagalog, and sometimes in Maranao, a language from the southern island of Mindanao. There is the smell of grilled meat, of spices, of diesel and warm concrete. Then, above the low rooftops and tangled electric wires, a rounded dome catches the light. This is Masjid Al-Dahab, the Golden Mosque of Manila.

The name tells you what to expect. Al-Dahab means gold in Arabic, and the dome was painted gold to match. Many visitors arrive expecting a quiet monument on an open square. Instead, they find a working mosque pressed into a dense neighbourhood. Shops, stalls and homes crowd close to its walls. That closeness is part of the story. The mosque is not set apart from daily life here. It sits in the middle of it, and daily life flows around it.

The mosque dates from the mid-1970s. The story most often told about its origins involves a visit that never happened. The building is widely reported to have been prepared for a planned trip by the Libyan leader Muammar Gaddafi. At that time, Libya was involved in peace talks between the Philippine government and Muslim rebel groups from Mindanao. The visit was cancelled. The mosque, however, remained, and the community made it their own.

That detail surprises many people. A building raised partly for a diplomatic moment became something more lasting. Accounts commonly link its construction to the circle of Imelda Marcos, the First Lady at the time, who was known for large building projects across Manila. Whatever its political beginnings, the mosque soon became the spiritual centre for Muslims in the capital. Today it is often described as the largest mosque in Metro Manila.

To understand why a mosque stands here, it helps to know the wider picture. The Philippines is mostly Catholic, but Islam arrived in the southern islands centuries before Spanish colonisers came in the sixteenth century. Muslim communities in Mindanao and the Sulu archipelago kept their faith through long periods of conflict. In the twentieth century, many families moved north to Manila for work and safety. Quiapo became one of the places where they settled.

So the streets around you carry the life of that migration. Many residents are Maranao, a people whose homeland is around Lake Lanao in Mindanao. Others come from different Muslim groups of the south. You may see women in bright headscarves, men in white caps, and children running between stalls. Signs advertise halal food. This is often called the Muslim Quarter of Quiapo, and it feels quite different from the streets only a few blocks away.

And those streets are very close. Quiapo is also home to Quiapo Church, the Minor Basilica of the Black Nazarene, one of the most important Catholic shrines in the country. Millions of devotees gather there each January for a huge procession. Here is a second surprise for many visitors. A short walk connects a major Catholic shrine and Manila's largest mosque. Two faiths share one crowded district, and their worshippers pass each other in the same markets.

Now look at the building itself. The design is simple and clear. A central dome rises over the prayer hall, and a minaret stands nearby. A minaret is the tower from which the call to prayer is traditionally given. The walls are plain compared with the dome. Over the decades, the gold paint has faded, chipped and been renewed. Depending on when you visit, the dome may look bright and fresh, or softened and weathered by tropical rain.

That weathering is worth noticing. Manila is hot, humid and heavy with traffic fumes. Monsoon rains arrive for months each year. Every building here takes damage, and this one shows it honestly. Stains run down some surfaces. Repairs sit beside older work. The mosque does not look like a museum piece, and it is not meant to. It looks like a building that people use every single day.

If you go inside, the mood changes at once. Outside there is noise and heat. Inside there is a wide, carpeted hall, and the sound of the street drops away. The prayer hall faces the qibla, the direction of Mecca, toward which Muslims pray. You will see a niche in the wall that marks this direction. Worshippers line up in rows on the carpet. The space is open, without pews, so that everyone stands and kneels together.

A few customs will help you feel welcome. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. Women are usually asked to cover their hair. Shoes come off before you step onto the carpet. Avoid visiting during the five daily prayers, and especially during the Friday midday prayer, when the hall fills with worshippers. Ask before you photograph people. A polite greeting and a quiet manner go a long way here.

Before you leave the area, spend some time in the surrounding streets. The food stalls are one of the best reasons to linger. Look for Maranao dishes made with turmeric and a spice paste called palapa, which gives food a deep yellow colour and a gentle heat. Look for grilled skewers and sweet snacks. Eating here is a good way to meet the community, because conversations often start over a shared table.

So take one more look at the dome before you go. Notice how it sits among the wires, the water tanks and the patched rooftops of Quiapo. A building made for a visitor who never came now serves thousands of people who did. As you walk away, listen again. The jeepneys, the vendors, perhaps the call to prayer rising above the street. All of it belongs to this place, and now, for a moment, so do you.
