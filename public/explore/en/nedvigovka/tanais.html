The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes off the river flats, moving through tall steppe grass. Beneath your feet, the ground is pale, chalky earth, broken here and there by low walls of rough stone. Below the bank, a quiet branch of the Don delta slides past, edged with reeds. It seems an unlikely place for a city. Yet more than two thousand years ago, this was the farthest northeastern outpost of the Greek world.

You are standing in Tanais, an ancient trading city on the lower Don, near the village of Nedvigovka. The city took its name from the river itself. Tanais was the Greek name for the Don. For the ancient Greeks, this river was more than water. Many geographers treated it as the border between two continents. On one bank lay Europe. On the other lay Asia. This city sat right on that imagined line.

Greek settlers founded Tanais around the third century before the common era. They came from the Bosporan Kingdom, a Greek state centred on the Kerch Strait, the narrow channel that joins the Sea of Azov to the Black Sea. The Bosporan kings controlled a network of colonies around these waters. Tanais was their most distant link. It sat at the point where river traffic from the interior met boats coming up from the sea.

Why build here? The answer is trade. The ancient geographer Strabo described Tanais as a common market for nomads from Asia and Europe, and for Greek merchants arriving by sea. Nomadic peoples of the steppe, including Sarmatians, brought livestock, hides, and enslaved captives. Greek traders brought wine, olive oil, fine pottery, and metalwork. Fish from the delta was salted and shipped south. This bank became a meeting point for very different worlds.

That mix of peoples shaped the city itself. Inscriptions found here mention officials called Hellenarchs — leaders of the Greek community. Their title suggests that Greeks and non-Greeks lived side by side, perhaps with separate leaders for each group. Names on the stones are a blend of Greek and Iranian forms. So Tanais was never a pure copy of a Greek city. It was a frontier town, where languages, gods, and customs mixed every day.

Life here was not always peaceful. Near the end of the first century before the common era, the city rebelled against Bosporan rule. The Bosporan king Polemon the First answered with force, and ancient sources say he destroyed Tanais. For a time, the site lay damaged. But the trade routes did not disappear, and neither did the reasons to live here. In the first centuries of the common era, the city was rebuilt and grew again.

This second life is the city you mostly see today. Look at the stone foundations around you. They mark houses, streets, and storage rooms packed tightly inside defensive walls. Beyond the walls, a deep ditch once protected the city from attack. The builders used local limestone, laid without much decoration. These were practical buildings for a working port. Archaeologists have found cellars, hearths, and workshops — the ordinary rooms of merchants and craftsmen.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Among the most common finds at Tanais are fragments of amphorae. An amphora is a tall clay jar with two handles and a pointed base, used for carrying wine and oil across the ancient Mediterranean. Thousands of broken pieces have come out of this ground. Each one is a small record of a voyage. Clay from distant workshops ended its journey here, at the edge of the steppe.

The end came in the third century of the common era. Gothic raiders and other groups moved through the region, and Tanais was destroyed by fire. Layers of burnt material in the soil still show that disaster. Later, people returned and lived here on a smaller scale, but the old city never recovered its role. Over the centuries, earth and grass covered the ruins, and the river channel slowly changed its course.

A second surprise: the medieval trading town of Tana, run by Italian merchants from Venice and Genoa, was not on this spot. That later town stood near the modern city of Azov, upstream. The shared name confused scholars for a long time. Systematic excavation here began in the nineteenth century, and serious work continued through the twentieth. Today the site is an archaeological museum-reserve, and digging still goes on most summers.

As you walk, notice how the museum mixes ruins with reconstruction. Some areas show the original stone foundations, exposed in open trenches. Elsewhere, you may find rebuilt structures that give a sense of how houses once looked. Exhibition spaces hold finds from the city and the burial grounds around it. Look especially for the carved stone figures from the steppe. Their worn faces and simple forms come from a different tradition than the Greek inscriptions nearby.

Take a moment at the edge of the bank. From here, you can see why the location mattered. The land drops toward the water, and the delta spreads out in channels and reed beds. Boats could reach this point from the sea, while caravans could arrive overland from the east. The view has changed, of course. The water has shifted, and the busy harbour is gone. But the shape of the land still explains the choice.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute and listen again. The grass moves. A bird calls from the reeds. Somewhere a car passes on the village road. Under all of it lie the foundations of a city where Greek merchants once bargained with steppe riders, on the line between Europe and Asia. As you walk back, look down at the pale soil. Somewhere in it, there may still be a fragment of clay from a ship that came a very long way.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and open excavation trenches.
Bring a hat and water; the steppe offers little shade.
Walk to the riverbank edge to understand the city's location.
Visit in summer to possibly see active archaeological digging.
Use insect repellent near the reeds in warm months.
