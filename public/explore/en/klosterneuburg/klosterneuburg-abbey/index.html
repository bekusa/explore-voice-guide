The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, a bell marks the hour, and the sound drifts out over the rooftops toward the Danube. The air here often carries a faint smell of damp stone, and in autumn, of pressed grapes. Under your feet, old cobbles slope gently uphill toward a cluster of pale walls, copper-green domes and two sharp Gothic spires. This is Klosterneuburg Abbey, and it has stood on this rise above the river for roughly nine hundred years.

The abbey belongs to the Augustinian Canons Regular. These are priests who live together under a shared rule but also serve parishes in the outside world. That double life explains a lot about this place. It is a working religious house, but it is also a landowner, a winemaker, a museum and a living part of the town around it. The canons have kept that role, with interruptions, since the early twelfth century.

The founder was Leopold the Third, a margrave from the Babenberg family. A margrave was a noble who governed a border territory, and the Babenbergs ruled the lands that became Austria before the Habsburgs. Leopold made his residence here and founded the abbey in the early 1100s. The main church was consecrated in the 1130s. Centuries later, in 1485, the Church declared Leopold a saint. Today he is the patron saint of Lower Austria and of Vienna.

There is a much-loved story about how the site was chosen — and it is a legend, not documented history. According to the tale, a gust of wind carried away the veil of Leopold's wife, Agnes. Years later, while out hunting, Leopold found the veil hanging untouched on an elder bush. He took it as a sign and built the abbey on that spot. Locals still tell the story, and you will find the veil in the abbey's art and imagery.

Now look up at the skyline. You may notice that two of the domes carry something unusual on top — crowns. One represents the imperial crown of the Holy Roman Empire. The other represents the Austrian archducal hat, the symbol of Austria's rulers. They are not decoration chosen at random. They point to a grand imperial dream from the eighteenth century, and to a plan that was never finished.

In the 1730s, Emperor Charles the Sixth decided to turn Klosterneuburg into a combined monastery and imperial palace. His model was the Escorial near Madrid, the vast royal monastery of the Spanish kings. The design called for many courtyards and a forest of domes, each crowned with a different crown of the Habsburg lands. Then Charles died in 1740, and building slowed and stopped. What you see is only a part of that vision. The rest exists on paper.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The finished Baroque wing looks enormous, yet it is only a fragment. Inside, you can walk through the imperial rooms that were prepared for the court. There are wide staircases, painted ceilings and a large marble hall built for ceremony. The rooms feel almost too grand for a monastery, and that is exactly the point. They were meant for an emperor, not for canons.

The church itself shows layers from many centuries. Its core is Romanesque, the heavy, round-arched style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Inside, the decoration is largely Baroque, with gilding, marble and painted surfaces. The two pointed towers outside look medieval, but they were rebuilt in a Gothic style during a restoration in the late nineteenth century. So the church you see is partly twelfth century, partly eighteenth and partly Victorian-era reimagining.

The abbey's greatest treasure is small enough to stand in front of, and it is easy to underestimate. It is the Verdun Altar, made by the goldsmith Nicholas of Verdun and completed in 1181. It consists of dozens of gilded and enamelled panels. They show scenes from the Bible in deep blues and gold. The figures have flowing robes and real movement, which was striking for that period. Many art historians count it among the finest medieval enamel works in Europe.

The altar was not always an altar. Nicholas originally made the panels to decorate a pulpit. In the fourteenth century, after a fire damaged the church, the panels were rearranged into the folding altar form you see today. It now sits in the chapel dedicated to Saint Leopold, close to his remains. The idea is quite moving — the founder resting beside the most precious object his foundation ever acquired.

Now for the second surprise, and it is a cheerful one. Klosterneuburg is also one of the oldest wine estates in Austria. The canons have tended vineyards on these slopes for centuries. In the cellars there is a giant wooden barrel from the early eighteenth century. Every year around Saint Leopold's feast day, in mid-November, visitors line up to slide down its smooth curved side. Locals call this barrel sliding, and it is said to bring good luck.

The treasury holds further reminders of the abbey's link to Austrian power. Among its objects is the Austrian archducal hat from the early seventeenth century. This is the same symbol you saw on top of the dome. There is also a large painted family tree of the Babenbergs from the late fifteenth century. It shows the dynasty in small portrait scenes and helped celebrate Leopold after his canonisation.

As you walk the courtyards, notice how the abbey sits between two worlds. On one side, the town of Klosterneuburg with its shops, cafés and parish life. On the other side, the Danube and the green hills of the Vienna Woods. Vienna is only a short ride away, but up here the pace is slower. You hear footsteps on stone, voices from the wine tavern and, often, choir practice through an open door.

Before you leave, take one last look at those crowned domes against the sky. They mark an empire that wanted more than it could build. Below them, the older church still holds its Romanesque core, and the canons still pray and make wine. As you walk back down the slope, listen again for the bell. It has called people up this hill for nine centuries — and today, it called you.
