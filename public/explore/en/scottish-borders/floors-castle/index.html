The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere below the lawn, the River Tweed runs wide and shallow over its stones, a low, steady sound under the calls of rooks in the old trees. The air here smells of cut grass and damp earth. Ahead of you, a long pale front of stone stretches out, crowded along its roofline with turrets, pinnacles and chimneys. It looks less like a fortress and more like a castle from a storybook. That impression is not an accident.

Welcome to Floors Castle, on the edge of the market town of Kelso, in the Scottish Borders. This is the family home of the Dukes of Roxburghe. It is often described as the largest inhabited castle in Scotland. That word, inhabited, matters. This is not a ruin or a museum emptied of life. A family still lives here, and the rooms you may walk through are part of a working house and estate.

Here is the first surprise. Despite its name, Floors was never built to withstand a siege. It began in the 1720s as a grand country house for the first Duke of Roxburghe. The architect was William Adam, one of the leading Scottish designers of his day, and father of the more famous Robert Adam. His original house was handsome, symmetrical and fairly plain. There were no fairy-tale turrets at all.

The castle you see now is largely the work of a later century. In the 1830s and 1840s, the architect William Henry Playfair remodelled the building for the sixth Duke. Playfair is better known for the classical buildings of Edinburgh. Here, he did something quite different. He added the towers, the lead-capped turrets, the battlements and the forest of pinnacles. He turned a sober Georgian house into a romantic Victorian vision of a Scottish castle.

Why would anyone do that? In the nineteenth century, Scotland was being reimagined. Writers such as Sir Walter Scott, who lived not far from here, filled readers' minds with Border ballads, knights and old feuds. Great families wanted homes that looked as if they belonged to that history. So Floors became a kind of theatre in stone. It tells a story about the past, even though most of what you see was built in a time of railways and steam.

Now turn your eyes towards the river. The Borders were not always a peaceful landscape of parkland and grazing sheep. For centuries, this was contested ground between the kingdoms of Scotland and England. Armies crossed these hills again and again. Across the Tweed, on a ridge near where the Teviot river joins it, stood Roxburgh Castle, once one of the most important fortresses in Scotland. Today only scattered fragments of its walls survive.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a dark one. In 1460, King James the Second of Scotland was besieging Roxburgh Castle, which was then held by the English. The king was fond of artillery, the newest weapon of his age. According to the chroniclers, one of his cannons burst as it was fired, and the king was killed. In the grounds of Floors, a holly tree is traditionally said to mark the spot where he died. Treat that exact location as local tradition rather than proven fact.

Inside the house, the character changes again. In the early twentieth century, the eighth Duke married May Goelet, an American heiress from New York. She was one of many wealthy American women who married into British aristocratic families in that era. She brought with her a remarkable collection of tapestries and fine French furniture. Much of the feeling of the state rooms today comes from her taste. If the house is open during your visit, look closely at how those woven scenes fill entire walls.

The collections also include paintings, porcelain and objects gathered by the family over generations. What makes a house like this interesting is the layering. A Georgian plan sits under Victorian decoration. American wealth sits beside centuries of Scottish lineage. Each generation added something and rarely threw much away. Walk slowly. Notice the light from the tall windows, the creak of floorboards, the smell of old wood and polish. These small details are part of the house too.

Floors has had a few moments in the wider spotlight. The castle appeared in the 1984 film Greystoke, about the legend of Tarzan, where it played the role of the family estate in England. It was also here, in 1986, that Prince Andrew proposed to Sarah Ferguson, according to widely reported accounts at the time. For a house so far from London, it has had a surprising number of brushes with fame.

The estate around you is as important as the building. Look out across the parkland, and you will see the gentle shape of the Tweed valley. Beyond it, on a clear day, the Cheviot Hills rise to the south, near the line of the border with England. Behind the castle there is a walled garden, a traditional feature of Scottish estates. Its high walls protected fruit, vegetables and flowers from the cold winds of the Borders.

The Tweed itself is famous among anglers, especially for salmon. On a quiet morning, you may see a lone figure standing in the water, casting a long line across the current. Kelso, just a short walk away, grew up around this river crossing. In the town you can find the ruins of Kelso Abbey and a broad cobbled square. The castle and the town have shaped each other for a very long time.

Before you leave, stand back from the castle and take it in once more. Count the turrets if you can. Follow the roofline from one end to the other, and notice how each tower is slightly different from the next. Then look down at the river, still running over its stones, the same river that carried the sound of cannon in 1460. The house changed many times. The water did not. Take that quiet sound with you as you walk on.
