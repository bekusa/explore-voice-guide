The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, the gravel paths of the park crunch under passing feet. Ducks call across the pond, and the plane trees move gently overhead. Then you turn towards a pale stone building with tall columns, set back among the green. The city traffic fades here. This is the Museo de Bellas Artes de Bilbao, the Fine Arts Museum of Bilbao. For more than a century it has quietly gathered the art of Spain, Europe, and the Basque Country under one roof.

Many visitors come to Bilbao for the titanium curves of the Guggenheim, a short walk away along the river. Fewer make it to this museum, and that is their loss. The Guggenheim shows art from recent decades. This building tells a much longer story. It runs from medieval panels to contemporary sculpture. In a single afternoon you can move through roughly eight centuries of painting and carving, without the crowds you find at larger capitals.

The museum was born from local ambition. In 1908, a group of Bilbao citizens founded a fine arts museum for their growing industrial city. Bilbao was rich then, from iron ore, shipyards, and banking. Its leading families wanted culture to match their wealth. A separate museum of modern art followed in the 1920s. For years the two collections lived apart. Then, in 1945, they were joined together and moved into the building in front of you.

Look at that older wing first. It is a calm, classical design, with a symmetrical front and a sober stone surface. Classical here means a style that borrows from ancient Greek and Roman temples, with columns and balanced proportions. It was built in the years around the Second World War, a hard period in Spain. The design feels restrained and serious. It was meant to say that art deserved a dignified home, even in difficult times.

Now look to the side, and the mood changes. In 1970, the museum added a new wing in a completely different style. Instead of stone columns, you see clean lines, glass, and metal. This is mid-century modernism, an approach that rejected decoration in favour of simple shapes and open space. The two wings stand side by side like two generations of the same family. One is formal and traditional. The other is lighter and more open to the park.

And the story of the building has not finished. In recent years, the museum began a major expansion, led by the studio of the British architect Norman Foster, working together with a Basque architect. The project adds new space and a light-filled pavilion above the existing structure. Depending on when you visit, you may see finished new galleries or signs of ongoing work. Either way, the museum is changing again, as it did in 1945 and in 1970.

Step inside, and begin with the older masters. The collection holds Flemish, Italian, and Spanish paintings from the late Middle Ages onwards. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. A museum of this size, in a regional city, holds works by El Greco, Zurbarán, and Goya. These are names you might expect only in Madrid. Goya, the great Spanish painter of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, appears here as a sharp and honest portrait painter.

From the Flemish world, look for the quiet religious panels, with their tiny landscapes painted behind the figures. Flemish artists, from the region that is now Belgium, loved minute detail. Lean in, and you may find a distant castle, a winding road, or a small bird hidden in the background. These details were painted with very fine brushes, often in oil glazes that still glow. Take your time with them. They reward a slow, close look.

Moving forward in time, the galleries open towards the modern age. Here is a second surprise. The museum holds a painting by Paul Gauguin of women washing clothes in Arles, in the south of France. It also holds a tender work by the American painter Mary Cassatt, showing a woman with a child in her arms. Further on, you meet the twentieth century, including a painting by the British artist Francis Bacon, with his twisted, unsettling figures.

But the heart of this museum is Basque. The Basque Country is a region in northern Spain and southwest France, with its own ancient language and strong traditions. Its artists fill many rooms here. Look for Ignacio Zuloaga, whose dark, dramatic portraits show Spanish types and landscapes. Look for painters of the early twentieth century who showed fishermen, farmers, and village life. Through their eyes, you see the region changing from rural valleys to an industrial city.

The Basque story continues into sculpture. Two great names dominate the twentieth century here: Eduardo Chillida and Jorge Oteiza. Chillida worked with iron, wood, and stone, creating heavy forms that seem to hold empty space inside them. Oteiza explored emptiness too, with geometric pieces that open and close like puzzles. Their work feels connected to the iron and shipbuilding history of Bilbao itself. Notice how the metal is treated with respect, almost like a living material.

When your eyes grow tired, step back outside into the park. It is named after Doña Casilda Iturrizar, a nineteenth-century benefactor who gave generously to the city. Locals come here to walk dogs, push prams, and sit by the pond. The museum and the park belong together. Many people pass from the galleries straight onto a bench under the trees. It is a good place to let the paintings settle in your mind before moving on.

So, before you leave, pause where you are. Look once more at the two faces of the building, the stone and the glass. Think of the merchants who founded this collection more than a century ago, and the city that kept adding to it. As you walk back towards the river, you carry a little of these rooms with you. The colours of Gauguin, the iron of Chillida, the quiet of the park. Bilbao is more than one famous museum.
