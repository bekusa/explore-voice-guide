The guide

The story

The path is steep here, and the air grows thinner with every step. Loose stones shift under your boots. Below you, the forests of Arasbaran roll away in dark green waves. Often a layer of cloud drifts through the valleys beneath the summit, so the walls ahead seem to float on white mist. Then the ruins come into view: weathered stone and brick sitting on a ridge of reddish rock, guarded on almost every side by sheer drops.

This is Babak Fort, a mountaintop stronghold above the town of Kaleybar in East Azerbaijan Province, in northwest Iran. Locals often call it Qal'eh Babak. Medieval Arabic and Persian writers knew a fortress in this region by the name Badhdh, and many historians link that name to these ruins. For the people of this region, the fort is more than an old building. It is tied to one man, and to one of the longest revolts of the early Islamic world.

That man was Babak Khorramdin. In the early ninth century, he led an uprising against the Abbasid Caliphate, the Arab dynasty that ruled a vast empire from Baghdad and later from Samarra. His followers belonged to a movement called the Khorramdin. Their beliefs mixed older Iranian religious ideas with resistance to foreign rule and heavy taxation. Many of them wore red clothing, and some chroniclers called them simply the red-clad ones.

The revolt began in the 810s and lasted roughly two decades. That is a remarkable span. The caliphs sent army after army into these mountains, and Babak defeated many of them. Look around you and you can understand why. The terrain did much of the fighting. Narrow valleys, thick forests and cliffs made it very hard for a large army to move, supply itself or surround a position like this one.

Now take a moment to study the site itself. The fort sits on a high summit, more than two thousand metres above sea level. Deep gorges fall away around it. In many places, the walls rise straight from the edge of the rock, so the builders used the mountain as part of the defence. Where the slope was gentler, they added stone walls and towers. Much of what you see is ruined, but the outline of rooms, passages and defensive lines is still clear.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The main approach does not open into a wide gate. Instead, the final stretch narrows into a tight passage between rocks. In places, only one person can pass at a time. For a defender, this was ideal. A small group could hold the entrance against a much larger force. As you walk through it, imagine soldiers in armour trying to push up this same route under fire from above.

Another surprise concerns the age of the walls. The fort carries Babak's name, but scholars do not fully agree on who built it first. Some researchers believe the site was fortified before Babak's time, possibly in the Sasanian era, when a Persian dynasty ruled Iran before the Arab conquests. Others link most of the remains to later periods. So the stones under your hand may be older than the hero whose name they carry... or they may be younger. The question is still open.

The end of the story is a sad one. In the 830s, the caliph al-Mu'tasim gave command of the war to a capable general named Afshin. Afshin did not rush. He built forts, secured supply lines and slowly tightened the circle around the mountains. Eventually Babak's stronghold fell, around 837. Babak escaped, but an Armenian prince, Sahl ibn Sunbat, handed him over to the Abbasids. Babak was taken to Samarra and executed there.

History did not end with his death. Over the centuries, Babak became a symbol of resistance to foreign rule. Different communities in the region claim him as their own. Iranians see him as a defender of Iranian identity, and many Azerbaijani Turkic speakers also honour him as a local hero. Each summer, groups of people climb to the fort to remember him. On those days, the quiet summit can fill with visitors, songs and speeches.

On most days, though, the fort is a place of wind and silence. Listen for a moment. You may hear the call of a bird of prey circling over the gorge, or the bells of sheep grazing on the lower slopes. The walls hold the heat of the sun in the afternoon, and the stone feels warm under your palm. At sunrise and sunset, the reddish rock turns a deep copper colour.

The forests below belong to the Arasbaran region, which is protected as a biosphere reserve. These hills are home to oak, hornbeam and wild fruit trees, and the area is known for its wildlife and birds. In spring, wildflowers cover the open slopes. In autumn, the trees turn yellow and rust. The view changes with every season, so the climb never looks the same twice.

The town of Kaleybar lies below, at the foot of the mountain. It is a small place, and many walkers start the hike from its edge. The climb is long and steep, with stone steps on some sections and rough ground on others. Most people stop several times to catch their breath. That is not a weakness. It is the best way to look back and see how far the land opens up behind you.

Before you leave, find a quiet spot near the walls and look out across the valleys. Follow the line of a ridge with your eyes, then the dark fold of a gorge. This is the same landscape that Babak's fighters watched for signs of an approaching army. As you start the walk down, notice how the fort slowly disappears behind the rocks... and how, from below, it is almost impossible to imagine anyone could ever have taken it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the climb early to avoid midday heat.
Wear sturdy hiking shoes for loose stones and steps.
Carry enough water for a long steep ascent.
Bring a warm layer; summit wind is often cold.
Expect large crowds during summer commemorative gatherings.
