The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Above the path, leaves shift in the canopy, and somewhere a bird calls in short, hollow notes. The air is warm and damp, heavy with the smell of wet earth and leaf litter. Underfoot, the trail is soft and uneven, threaded with roots. Then, through a gap in the trees, a wall of pale limestone appears. Its stones are stacked in steep, neat steps. You are standing in the southern lowlands of Quintana Roo, at the Maya city now called Chacchoben.

That name is worth a pause, because it is not ancient. Nobody today knows what the original builders called this place. The name comes from a nearby modern village, and it is usually translated as "place of red corn" in the Yucatec Maya language. Maize was the centre of Maya life and belief. Many Maya stories describe the first people as made from corn. So even a borrowed name carries an old idea. It links this ruined city to the fields that once fed it.

Here is the first surprise. For centuries, this whole complex lay under forest, and much of it still does. The low, rounded hills you see around the clearings are not natural. Many of them are unexcavated buildings, covered by soil and roots. The site came to the attention of archaeologists in the early 1970s. At that time, local families were farming the land around the mounds. Excavation and restoration came much later, and the site opened to visitors around the start of this century.

Archaeologists believe people lived in this area for a very long time — from roughly the first millennium before the common era. The city reached its greatest size during the Classic period, the great age of Maya cities, which ran from roughly the third to the tenth century. In that era, the southern lowlands were full of rival kingdoms. Chacchoben was not one of the giants like Tikal or Calakmul. It was a regional centre, important to the farms and villages around it.

Walk on toward the large raised platform at the heart of the site. Archaeologists call it the Gran Basamento, which simply means the great base. It is an enormous artificial terrace, built up by hand with earth and stone. On top of it stand several temples and buildings. Think about the labour involved. There were no metal tools, no wheeled carts, and no pack animals. Every stone came here on human backs, organised by people with real power.

The tallest building on the platform is known as Temple One. Look at its shape — steep, stepped terraces rising to a small structure on the top. That top room was the sacred space. The stairway led priests and rulers up toward it, in full view of the crowd below. Visitors cannot climb the temples here today. That rule protects the fragile stone, and it also keeps the original intent intact. These buildings were made to be looked up at.

Now look closely at the surfaces of the walls. In some sheltered places, you may see patches of smooth, pale plaster. This is stucco, a lime-based coating. The Maya covered their buildings with it, and then they painted it. Traces of red pigment survive here in places. This is the second surprise for many visitors. These pyramids were not grey. In their prime they were bright and smooth, and the red colour would have stood out sharply against the green forest.

Another group of buildings is called Las Vasijas, meaning the vessels. The name comes from pottery found during excavation. Ceramics are one of the main tools archaeologists use to date a site. Changes in shape, decoration, and clay help them build a timeline. Broken pieces of pots can tell them when people lived here, who they traded with, and how long the city lasted. At Chacchoben, much of the story comes from these small, everyday objects.

Pause and take in the trees around you. This is tropical forest, and it shaped life here in every way. Look for tall palms with long, arching fronds, and broad hardwood trees with tangled roots. Some sapodilla trees in this region carry old diagonal scars on their bark. These marks come from chicle tappers, who cut the trees to collect sap for chewing gum during the twentieth century. If you see such scars, you are looking at a more recent chapter of the forest's history.

Keep your ears open as you walk. Monkeys live in the forests of this region, and visitors sometimes hear or see them in the canopy. Howler monkeys make a deep, rough roar that carries a long way. The first time you hear it, it can be unsettling. Birds, lizards, and insects are everywhere too. The ancient city was never separate from this forest. Its people farmed clearings, gathered wood, and hunted in the same green world you are walking through now.

Zoom out for a moment, and picture the map. Chacchoben sits inland, not far from Lake Bacalar, a long freshwater lagoon known for its bands of blue water. The Caribbean coast lies further east. Water and trade routes mattered enormously to the Maya. Rivers, lagoons, and coastal paths connected inland cities to the sea. Goods like salt, shells, obsidian, and jade moved along these networks. A centre like this one likely sat on routes that linked several worlds together.

Today, many visitors arrive from the Costa Maya cruise port on the coast, so the site can get busy in waves. Between those waves, the place becomes quiet again. That quiet is worth seeking out. Stand at the edge of a clearing and let the group noise fade. Then the forest sounds return, one by one. In those moments, it is easier to imagine the plaza filled with people, smoke from cooking fires, and voices speaking Maya.

Before you leave, look once more at the mounds still wrapped in green. Somewhere under that soil are rooms, stairways, and perhaps painted walls that no one has seen in over a thousand years. Feel the warm air on your skin, and listen to the leaves moving overhead. As you walk back along the trail, notice the roots underfoot and the light falling through the canopy. The forest kept this city for centuries. It is still keeping much of it now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring insect repellent for the humid forest trails.
Wear closed shoes for uneven paths and exposed roots.
Carry plenty of water; shade is limited in the clearings.
Arrive between cruise groups for a quieter visit.
Stay behind ropes; climbing the temples is not allowed.
