The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The jungle around you hums with insects, and somewhere a bird calls from the canopy. Under your feet the ground feels solid, pale and a little rough, with limestone showing through thin red soil. That solid feeling is misleading. Below you, in total darkness, water is moving slowly through a vast network of flooded passages. You are standing on the roof of one of the longest underwater cave systems ever mapped on Earth.

This is Sistema Ox Bel Ha. It lies in the Mexican state of Quintana Roo, on the eastern side of the Yucatán Peninsula, in the area south of Tulum. On the surface you see forest, scrub and the occasional round pool of clear water. Underground, divers have surveyed passages that run for hundreds of kilometres. They twist, branch and reconnect beneath the jungle like the roots of a giant tree.

The name comes from Yucatec Maya, the language that people still speak in villages across this peninsula. Ox means three. Bel means path. Ha means water. Together the name is usually translated as three paths of water. It is a modern name, given by the explorers who mapped the system. Even so, it fits a landscape where water has always shaped human life.

Here is the first surprise. The Yucatán Peninsula has almost no rivers on its surface. Rain does not run off into streams. It soaks straight down through the porous limestone and collects underground. For thousands of years, the only reliable fresh water here came from below. People reached it through cenotes. A cenote is a natural sinkhole, formed where the roof of a cave has collapsed and opened the water to the sky.

The word cenote comes from the Maya word dzonot. For the ancient Maya, these openings were more than wells. Many communities saw them as sacred places and entrances to Xibalba, the Maya underworld, a realm of gods and the dead. Archaeologists have recovered offerings, pottery and human remains from cenotes across the peninsula. So when you look into one of these dark pools, you are looking at a place that people treated with deep respect for centuries.

Now the second surprise, and it changes how you see these caves. Many of these passages were not formed underwater. During the last ice age, huge amounts of water were locked in ice sheets, and the sea level was far lower than today. These caves stood dry and full of air. Water dripped from the ceilings for thousands of years. Slowly it built stalactites, stalagmites and columns of stone. Then the ice melted, the sea rose, and the caves flooded.

That is why divers in Ox Bel Ha swim past formations that could only have grown in open air. Delicate stone curtains hang from ceilings. Columns join floor to roof. Each one is a record of a drier world. Today these formations sit perfectly still in water so clear that a diver's light can travel far down a passage. Many divers say the water feels less like water and more like air.

There is a third strange feature, called the halocline. Fresh rainwater is lighter than seawater, so it floats on top. Closer to the coast, salt water from the Caribbean pushes inland beneath it. Where the two layers meet, the water shimmers and blurs like hot air above a road. A diver passing through it sees the cave walls ripple and distort. The salt layer is also slightly warmer. Divers often feel the change in temperature on their skin.

Exploration of Ox Bel Ha began in earnest in the late 1990s. Small teams of cave divers worked their way through it, one passage at a time. This is slow, careful work. Divers lay a thin guideline as they go, so they can always find the way back. They measure distance, depth and direction by hand. Every new section of the map comes from hours underwater, often far from any opening to the surface.

It is important to be clear about one thing. Exploring these deep passages is only for trained cave divers with specialist certification and equipment. Cave diving is a different discipline from open water diving. There is no direct route to the surface, and visibility can vanish in seconds if sediment is disturbed. For most visitors, the experience of Ox Bel Ha comes through the cenotes. You can swim or snorkel in the open water, in daylight, and look down into the blue.

And that experience is remarkable in its own way. Stand at the edge of a cenote and notice the light. Sunbeams fall through the opening and cut through the water in long bright columns. Roots from the trees above hang down toward the surface. Small fish move in the shallows. The water is often cool, especially after the heat of the jungle path. Notice how quiet it is. Sound seems to sink into the stone.

This water connects to much more than the caves. The flow moves slowly through the limestone toward the coast, and eventually it reaches the mangroves and the Caribbean Sea. This makes the system fragile. Whatever enters the ground here can travel through the aquifer, the underground store of water that people across the region depend on. Development along this coast has grown fast in recent decades, and scientists and local groups watch the health of this water closely.

That is why many cenotes ask swimmers to shower before entering. They also ask visitors to avoid regular sunscreen and insect spray, which can leave a film on the water. These small rules are part of what keeps the water clear. When you follow them, you are helping to protect a system that took many thousands of years to form. It cannot simply be replaced.

Before you leave, take one more look at the ground beneath your feet. The pale stone, the thin soil and the roots that push down into the cracks all feel ordinary. Somewhere below, in darkness, the water continues its slow journey toward the sea. Three paths of water... and many more beyond them. As you walk back through the trees, listen again to the jungle. It is only the top layer of this place.
