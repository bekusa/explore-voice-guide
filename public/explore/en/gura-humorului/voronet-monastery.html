The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere in the courtyard, a nun may be striking a long wooden plank with a mallet, a steady knocking rhythm that rolls across the grass. Around you, the air smells of cut hay and pine from the hills. And in front of you stands a small stone church covered from roof to foundation in painted saints, angels and sinners. Much of it is painted in one deep, unusual shade of blue.

Welcome to Voroneț Monastery, in the village of Voroneț, just outside the town of Gura Humorului. This is Bukovina, a region in the northeast of Romania. Historically, it was part of the medieval principality of Moldavia. The church here belongs to a small group of painted churches in the area. UNESCO lists them together as World Heritage sites. Many people who know them well consider Voroneț the finest of the group.

The story of this place begins with a ruler and a hermit. The ruler was Stephen the Great, Prince of Moldavia in the late fifteenth century. He spent much of his reign fighting the Ottoman Empire. The hermit was a monk named Daniel. He lived a life of prayer in these forested valleys, and the Orthodox Church later recognised him as a saint.

Locals tell a story about the two men. According to the popular tale, Stephen came to Daniel after a hard defeat, unsure whether to continue the war. The hermit told him not to give up. He also told him to build a church if he won. Whether the conversation happened exactly like this, no one can say for certain. But Stephen did go on fighting, and he did build this church. Daniel's tomb is still kept inside it today.

The church was built in 1488, and it went up very quickly. Records say the work took less than four months, from late spring to early autumn. It is dedicated to Saint George, the soldier saint who killed the dragon. That was a fitting choice for a warrior prince. Look at the shape of the building. It is long and narrow, with steep roofs and a small tower. The walls are thick stone, and the windows are few and small.

Now for the first surprise. When Stephen built the church, the outside walls were not painted. The famous exterior frescoes came about sixty years later, in 1547. The man behind them was Grigore Roșca, a Metropolitan, or senior bishop, of Moldavia. He also added an extra porch at the western end. That flat western wall gave the painters a large, open surface. They used it for their most ambitious scene.

Walk around to that western wall. This is the Last Judgment, and it covers almost the entire facade. At the top, angels roll up the sky like a scroll, and the signs of the zodiac are painted on it. That is the end of time itself. Below sits Christ in judgment. On one side, the righteous move toward paradise. On the other side, a red river of fire flows down and carries the damned away.

Take a closer look at the crowds waiting to be judged. The painters dressed the different groups in costumes from their own world. You can find figures in turbans and other foreign clothing, often identified as Turks and Tatars, the enemies of Moldavia at the time. Near the bottom, the dead rise from their graves. Even wild animals return the parts of people they once ate. This is a strange, almost humorous detail, and many visitors walk past without seeing it.

And then there is the blue. People call it Voroneț blue, and the colour is famous across Romania and beyond. It is a deep, cool blue that still looks rich after nearly five centuries of snow, rain and summer sun. No one knows for sure exactly how the painters mixed it. Researchers have suggested different minerals, and the debate continues. Painters still talk about this blue the way perfumers talk about a lost recipe.

Why paint the outside of a church at all? One common explanation is practical. Village churches were small, and on feast days many worshippers had to stand outside. Few people at the time could read. So the walls became a book in pictures. The paintings taught Bible stories, the lives of the saints and the shape of Orthodox faith. A farmer standing in the grass could follow them from beginning to end.

Move to the south wall, which usually gets the warmest light. Here you will see the Tree of Jesse, a family tree of Christ. It grows across the wall as winding branches, with figures of ancestors and prophets among them. Now compare it with the north wall. The north side faces the harshest weather, and its paintings are much more faded. You can still pick out scenes from Genesis there, including Adam and Eve. The difference between the two walls shows how much the climate has worn the work.

The monastery has not always been full of life. After Bukovina came under Austrian Habsburg rule in the late eighteenth century, the authorities closed it, and for about two hundred years it stood without a community. The church survived as a parish church, and its paintings survived with it. In 1991, after the end of communist rule in Romania, the monastery opened again. Today a community of nuns lives here. They look after the grounds, sing the services and keep the old rhythm of prayer going.

That is the sound you may have heard when you arrived. It is called the toaca, a wooden plank that is beaten by hand to call the community to prayer. Please remember that this is a working convent, not only a monument. Speak softly inside, dress modestly, and follow any signs about photography. The interior is darker and more crowded with images than the outside. Let your eyes adjust for a minute before you look up.

Before you leave, stand back on the grass and look at the whole church at once. Notice how the deep roof overhangs protect the upper paintings. Notice how the colours change as clouds move over the hills. Five hundred years ago, people stood exactly here and read these walls like a book. As you walk back toward the gate, look over your shoulder one more time... and see if that blue looks a little different now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the west wall for the Last Judgment.
Visit in morning light to see the colours clearly.
Bring a shoulder covering for modest dress inside the church.
Keep voices low because nuns live and pray here.
Bring binoculars to study the upper painted scenes.
