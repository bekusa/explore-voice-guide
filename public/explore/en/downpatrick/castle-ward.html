The guide

The story

Listen first. On a still day the loudest sound here is often the water of Strangford Lough, moving against the shore below the house. Add the call of wading birds, the crunch of gravel underfoot, and the wind in old trees. The air smells of salt and cut grass. Before any story begins, this landscape of soft green slopes and grey-blue water sets the mood. Castle Ward sits on the southern shore of the lough, a short drive from Downpatrick in County Down.

The estate is cared for today by the National Trust, the British conservation charity that looks after historic houses and landscapes. It covers a large sweep of farmland, woodland, and shoreline. Most visitors come for the eighteenth-century mansion. Many stay for the walking paths, the old farmyard, and the views across the water. Take your time. This place rewards slow attention, and the details that matter most are easy to miss if you rush.

The Ward family arrived here long before the mansion. In the late sixteenth century they acquired land on this shore and built a tower house. A tower house is a compact stone fortified home, common across Ireland at that time. It was tall, narrow, and practical — built for defence as much as comfort. That earlier building still stands in the farmyard, close to the stables and the old working buildings. It is a good reminder that this estate grew in layers, one generation after another.

The great house came much later, in the 1760s. It was built for Bernard Ward, who later became the first Viscount Bangor, and his wife, Lady Anne. They were wealthy, fashionable, and confident. They wanted a home that showed their taste to every visitor. There was just one problem — they could not agree on what that taste was. And the result of their disagreement is the reason people still travel here today.

Walk around the outside of the house and you will see it for yourself. One side is classical. It follows the Palladian style, a calm, balanced design inspired by the buildings of ancient Rome and the Italian architect Andrea Palladio. Look for the columns, the triangular pediment, and the strict symmetry. This was the style Bernard preferred. It speaks of order, reason, and good breeding, which were the ideals of the polished gentleman of his age.

Now walk to the other side. Here the house changes completely. The windows have pointed arches. The roofline carries battlements and pinnacles, like a medieval castle or church. This is the Gothic style, which Lady Anne favoured. In the eighteenth century, Gothic was a playful, romantic fashion, full of mystery and imagination. It is the same building, with the same stone and the same roof — yet it seems to belong to two different worlds.

The division continues inside. Rooms on the classical side follow classical decoration. Rooms on the Gothic side have arches, tracery, and fanciful ceilings. The most famous is Lady Anne's boudoir, her private sitting room. Its ceiling is covered in rounded plaster forms that curve down from above. Visitors have compared it to many things, and the comparisons are often cheeky. If you take a guided tour, ask your guide about it. Most guides enjoy that question.

Here is the part most visitors do not expect. The story of a split house matches the story of a split marriage. Bernard and Anne did eventually separate, and Anne left the estate. Whether the argument over architecture was a cause or simply a sign of deeper differences, no one can say for certain. Locals and guides still tell the tale with a smile. But it is worth remembering that a real family lived through this, behind those two very different faces.

From the house, follow the path toward the long, calm stretch of water called Temple Water. It is an artificial lake, made to improve the view and to give the gardens a sense of quiet grandeur. On the slope above it stands a small classical temple, a decorative garden building of the kind wealthy owners loved. On a still morning, the water holds a clear reflection of the trees. It is one of the best places on the estate to pause and simply look.

Back in the farmyard, the estate shows its working side. Here you will find old stables, a laundry, and buildings once used for milling and estate work. These were the places where servants and labourers kept the great house running. The grand rooms tell you how the family wanted to be seen. The farmyard tells you how the estate actually functioned. Notice the rough stone, the heavy doors, and the worn thresholds — the marks of daily use over many years.

This farmyard also gives Castle Ward a second life. Parts of the television series Game of Thrones were filmed on the estate. The farmyard and tower house became part of Winterfell, the northern home of the Stark family in the story. Fans come from many countries to stand where the cameras stood. Some arrive in costume. You may hear visitors comparing camera angles from memory. The tower house was built for real defence, and centuries later it now appears in fiction.

If you have time, walk down toward the shore. The paths lead through woodland and along the edge of Strangford Lough. The lough is a large sea inlet, almost enclosed by land, and it is an important home for birds and marine life. In colder months you may see flocks of geese and wading birds on the mudflats. Further along the shore, look for another old stone tower standing alone near the water, a sign of the older, fortified history of this coast.

Castle Ward is a house of contrasts. It has a classical face and a Gothic face, grand rooms and working yards, real history and screen fiction. It is easy to treat the divided house as a simple joke. But it also shows something lasting. Two people with different ideas built one home, and they left both ideas standing in the same stone.

As you leave, look back once more at whichever side faces you. Ask yourself which half you would have chosen. Then listen again — to the lough, to the birds, to the gravel under your feet. Those sounds were here before the Wards arrived, and they will continue long after today's visitors have gone home.
