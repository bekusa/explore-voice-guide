The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you will hear the river. The Jia Bhoroli runs fast and clear over a bed of smooth grey stones, and its sound fills the air at the edge of Nameri. Under your feet, the ground is a mix of sand, pebbles, and fallen leaves. The air smells of wet earth and green wood. Somewhere above you, a bird calls once, then again. This is the northern edge of Assam, where the plains end and the mountains begin.

You are standing in Nameri National Park, in the Sonitpur district of Assam, in northeast India. The park sits in the foothills of the Eastern Himalaya. Look north and the land starts to rise, fold after fold, toward Arunachal Pradesh. The forest you see does not stop at the state border. Nameri joins Pakke Tiger Reserve on the Arunachal side, and together they form one large, continuous block of wild country. Animals move between the two freely. The line on the map means nothing to them.

The river is the heart of this place. In Arunachal Pradesh it is called the Kameng. When it comes down from the hills into Assam, people call it the Jia Bhoroli. The river forms the western edge of the park, and for many visitors it is also the front door. To enter Nameri, you usually cross the water first, often in a small boat. That short crossing changes the mood. Behind you are tea gardens and villages. Ahead of you is forest.

Nameri was protected in stages. It began as a reserved forest, later became a wildlife sanctuary, and was declared a national park in the late 1990s. Today it is also part of the Nameri Tiger Reserve. That status may surprise you, because tigers here are rarely seen. Like many tiger reserves, Nameri protects whole habitats, not one animal. The forest, the river, the grasslands at its edges — all of it is held together under one shield.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. In most Indian national parks, you see the forest from a jeep. In Nameri, visitors have long been able to walk. Guided treks follow forest trails, with a forest guard who usually carries a rifle for safety. The rifle is a reminder, not a threat. This forest has wild elephants, and elephants have the right of way. On foot, you notice everything — a footprint in the mud, a broken branch, the sudden silence of birds.

The forest itself is layered. Much of Nameri is semi-evergreen and moist deciduous woodland. In simple terms, some trees keep their leaves all year, while others drop them in the dry season. Tall trunks rise straight up, and climbers wrap around them. Below, there is cane, bamboo, and thick undergrowth. Along the riverbanks, the forest opens into strips of grass and gravel. These open edges are often the best places to see wildlife, because animals come down to drink.

Now, the birds. Nameri is one of the most important bird areas in Assam, and serious birdwatchers come here from around the world. Hundreds of species have been recorded in and around the park. The most famous is the white-winged wood duck. It is a large, shy forest duck, and it is endangered. It lives in quiet forest pools, not on open lakes, which makes it very hard to find. If you see one, you have seen something special.

Look up, too. Great hornbills live in these forests. They are large birds with a heavy yellow and black bill topped by a curved casque — a hollow helmet on top of the beak. You may hear them before you see them. Their wingbeats are loud, a deep whooshing sound, almost like a slow engine passing overhead. Along the river, watch for kingfishers, cormorants, and the small, quick river birds that hop between the stones.

The river holds its own treasure. The Jia Bhoroli is known for the golden mahseer, a large, powerful fish of Himalayan rivers. Anglers once came here specifically for it, and the river became known for catch-and-release fishing, where the fish is returned to the water alive. The mahseer needs clean, fast, well-oxygenated water to survive. So when you look at this clear river, you are looking at a sign of health. A polluted river would not hold these fish.

The Jia Bhoroli also draws people for a gentler adventure. Rafting trips float downstream along the edge of the park. These are mostly calm stretches, with a few easy rapids. From the raft, the forest looks different. You sit low on the water, and the trees seem taller. Birds cross the river in front of you. On a quiet day, the only sounds are the paddles, the water, and the wind moving through the canopy.

The land around the park tells another story. This part of Sonitpur is tea country. Neat rows of tea bushes run right up to the forest edges, and villages sit between the gardens and the trees. Life here involves a careful balance. Elephants sometimes leave the forest to raid crops, and farmers must protect their fields. When you visit, remember that people live with this wildness every day. The forest is a neighbour, not just a view.

Light changes everything in Nameri. Early morning is often misty, especially in the cooler months. The far bank of the river may disappear in white haze, then slowly return as the sun rises. Birds are most active at this hour. By midday, the forest grows quiet and the heat settles in. Late afternoon brings soft, golden light on the water, and animals begin to move again. Each hour offers a different park.

So take a moment before you leave. Listen to the river once more. It has been running over these stones long before anyone drew a park boundary, and it will keep running after you go. Look at the line where the forest meets the water. Watch for movement in the grass. As you walk away, notice how the sound of the Jia Bhoroli fades slowly behind you — and how the quiet of the forest stays with you a little longer.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive at dawn for the best birdwatching and misty river views.
Book guided forest walks in advance with park authorities.
Wear muted colours and closed shoes for forest trails.
Bring binoculars; many birds stay high in the canopy.
Keep quiet and follow the guard if elephants appear.
