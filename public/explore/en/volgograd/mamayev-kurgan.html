The guide

The story

Feel the steps under your feet first. Broad, pale granite, worn smooth in the centre by millions of shoes. The wind comes up from the Volga and pushes against your coat. Somewhere ahead, half hidden by the slope, a figure rises into the sky with her arm thrown back and a sword held high. You hear the shuffle of other visitors, and sometimes something stranger: the sound of gunfire and a voice reading war bulletins. You are climbing Mamayev Kurgan, the hill that decided the fate of this city.

The word kurgan means a burial mound. It comes from the Turkic languages of the steppe peoples who once lived along this river. The first half of the name is less certain. A popular tradition links it to Mamai, a commander of the Golden Horde, the Mongol state that ruled these lands in the fourteenth century. Historians are cautious about that story. What is certain is the shape of the ground. The hill rises a little over one hundred metres above the river and looks down over the whole city.

That view is the reason so many people died here. In 1942 and early 1943, this city carried the name Stalingrad. German and Soviet armies fought for it street by street during the Battle of Stalingrad. Whoever held this hill could watch the river crossings and fire on them. Control of the slope changed hands again and again. Soldiers fought over trenches, shell craters, and single water towers. For months, the hill was one of the most dangerous places in the city.

Here is the first detail most visitors miss. After the battle, the ground itself was full of metal. Surveys after the war described hundreds of shell and bullet fragments in every square metre of soil. Locals say that the following spring, the hill stayed black while the rest of the steppe turned green. Whether every part of that story is exact or not, the soil under these lawns is not ordinary earth. It is mixed with the remains of the battle, and with the remains of the people who fought it.

The memorial you are walking through opened in 1967. Its chief sculptor was Yevgeny Vuchetich, who worked with the engineer Nikolai Nikitin. They did not design a single monument. They designed a route — a slow climb from the foot of the hill to the summit, with each stage telling part of the story. It is often said that there are two hundred steps from the base to the top, one for each day of the battle. Count them as you go, if you like.

As you move upward, you reach a long avenue lined with poplars, then a square with a pool of still water. Rising from the water is a huge, muscular soldier, bare-chested, gripping a sub-machine gun. This figure stands for the order the defenders were given — to hold the line and not retreat. Beyond him, stairs climb between tall walls carved to look like ruins. The concrete is cracked, scarred, and covered with slogans and faces of soldiers.

This is where you may hear the sounds. Recordings play among the ruined walls: the noise of battle, songs from the war years, and the voice of a radio announcer reading reports from the front. Many older Russians recognise that voice instantly. It belongs to Yuri Levitan, the man who read the most important Soviet war announcements on the radio. Pause here for a moment. The design wants you to feel crowded and uneasy before you reach the calm spaces ahead.

Next comes the Square of Heroes, with another long pool and a row of sculptures on each side. Each group shows a moment of courage — a wounded soldier still fighting, a nurse carrying a man to safety, a sailor throwing a grenade. They are larger than life and very direct. Vuchetich worked in a style called Socialist Realism, the official Soviet art style, which aimed to show heroes clearly and without doubt. You do not need to share its politics to feel the weight of the scenes.

Then the path enters a round building set into the hill. This is the Hall of Military Glory. Inside, the light is dim and golden. In the centre, a giant stone hand rises from the floor, holding a torch with an eternal flame. The walls carry the names of soldiers who died defending the city. An honour guard stands by the flame and changes with careful, slow steps. Quiet music plays — often Robert Schumann's piece called Träumerei, which means dreaming. A German composer, chosen for a Soviet memorial. That choice surprises many visitors.

A ramp spirals up and out of the hall, back into the wind. You arrive at the Square of Sorrow. Here a mother kneels over the body of her dead son, his face covered. After so much shouting stone and raised weapons, this figure is silent. It is perhaps the most personal moment on the whole hill. Many families stop here and leave flowers. Look at the hands of the mother. Watch how the sculptor made her grief feel heavier than all the battle scenes below.

And then you see her fully: The Motherland Calls. From the base of the statue to the tip of the raised sword, she measures about eighty-five metres. When the memorial opened, she was described as the tallest statue in the world. Here is the second surprise. She is often said to stand on her foundation by her own weight, without being fixed to it. The original sword was later replaced, because the wind made the blade move and vibrate. Engineers have worked on her stability for decades.

She is also part of a larger story. Many people read this statue as the middle figure of three Soviet monuments. In Magnitogorsk, in the Ural Mountains, a worker hands a sword to a soldier — the factories arming the front. Here, the Motherland raises that sword and calls her people to fight. In Treptow Park in Berlin, a soldier lowers the sword over a rescued child at the end of the war. The sword moves from the factory, to this hill, to the fallen capital of the enemy.

The hill is also a cemetery. Thousands of defenders were buried here. Marshal Vasily Chuikov, who commanded the army that held the city, asked to be buried on Mamayev Kurgan, and his grave lies here. The famous sniper Vasily Zaitsev was also reburied on the hill. Nearby, a small Orthodox church with golden domes was added in more recent years. It stands near the old Soviet monuments. Together they show how different generations have chosen to remember the same ground.

Before you go down, turn around. Look out past the statue's shoulder toward the Volga, wide and grey or blue depending on the light. Below you, the city stretches along the river for kilometres, rebuilt after it was almost completely destroyed. Children run on the lawns. Couples take photographs on the steps. The wind is still blowing. As you walk back down the long stairs, listen to your own footsteps on the granite — and remember that every step was laid for someone who did not walk away from this hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start at the foot and climb the full route in order.
Bring a windproof layer, the summit is exposed to river winds.
Speak softly inside the Hall of Military Glory.
Visit early morning for quieter steps and softer light.
Allow time to walk down slowly and look back at the river.
