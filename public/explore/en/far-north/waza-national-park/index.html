The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here the loudest sound is often the wind, moving through dry grass the colour of straw. Then a bird calls, sharp and metallic, from an acacia tree. The ground underfoot is hard, cracked clay, pale and dusty in the dry months. The light is wide and flat, and the horizon seems very far away. Welcome to Waza National Park, in the Far North Region of Cameroon. This is the Sahel, the long belt of dry savanna that sits between the Sahara Desert and the wetter lands to the south.

The park lies in the narrow northern tip of Cameroon. Nigeria is to the west, and Chad is to the east, just across the Logone River. The main road from Maroua, the regional capital, runs north past the park toward Kousseri and the Chadian border. Waza is large, roughly 1,700 square kilometres of open country. That is bigger than many cities. Yet from the ground it rarely feels enclosed. There are no mountains to frame the view, only grass, scattered trees and an enormous sky.

The protection of this land began in the colonial era. Waza was first set aside as a hunting reserve in the 1930s, when northern Cameroon was under French administration. In 1968, after independence, it became a national park. Later, in 1979, UNESCO recognised Waza as a biosphere reserve. That status is meant to show how people and wildlife can share a landscape. It matters here, because people have lived, farmed, fished and herded cattle around this land for centuries.

The park has two main faces. In the south and west, the ground is sandier, with acacia trees and other thorny bush. In the east, the land drops into a vast floodplain. Local people call these seasonal grasslands the yaérés. During the rainy season, water from the Logone River system spreads slowly across this flat country. When the water pulls back, it leaves fresh grass behind. That grass feeds antelope, cattle and birds through the long dry months.

Here is something many visitors do not know. The floodplain changed sharply in the late twentieth century. In the late 1970s, a dam and rice irrigation scheme at Maga, south of the park, held back much of the floodwater. Less water reached the yaérés. Grasslands dried out, and wildlife and local fishing communities both suffered. Later projects reopened some old water channels to bring part of the flood back. Ecologists still study Waza as an example of how one engineering decision can reshape a whole ecosystem.

Now, the animals. Waza has long been known for its savanna wildlife. Elephants move through the park, often in large family groups, especially near water in the dry season. Giraffes live here too. They belong to a northern population, sometimes called Kordofan giraffes, with pale, irregular patches. You may also see antelope such as kob, roan antelope and topi. Spotted hyenas and lions have also lived here, though lions are now rare and difficult to find.

The birds are a reason to come on their own. When there is water, the pools fill with life. Pelicans, storks, herons and cranes gather to feed. Ostriches sometimes walk across the open plain, tall and slow, like dark shapes in the heat haze. Small, colourful birds move through the bushes, and birds of prey circle above. If you carry binoculars, you will use them constantly. Even a short stop at a pool can show you dozens of species.

The secret of Waza is water. During the dry season, there is very little of it. Animals must gather at the remaining waterholes, which local guides call mares. These pools act like natural meeting points. Park managers have also dug or maintained some of them to help wildlife survive the driest weeks. So a quiet visit often means sitting near one pool and waiting. Elephants arrive, then antelope, then birds — each taking its turn at the edge of the water.

Patience changes the experience here. Waza is not a place where animals appear on schedule. You drive slowly on sandy tracks. You stop. You switch off the engine. Then you listen. A branch cracks somewhere in the bush. Dust rises on the horizon. A guide points to a shape you had not noticed at all. In open savanna like this, animals see you long before you see them. Moving quietly, and keeping a respectful distance, gives you the best chance.

Look also at the land itself. Near the village of Waza, by the park entrance, large rocky hills rise suddenly from the flat plain. Geologists call these isolated hills inselbergs, a German word meaning island mountains. They are old granite rock, worn smooth by wind and heat. From their slopes, the savanna spreads out in every direction. For generations, travellers on this road have used these rocks as landmarks. They are among the few high points in the whole region.

The human story around the park matters too. Many communities live nearby, including Kotoko fishing people along the rivers, Arab Choa herders and Fulani, or Fulbe, cattle keepers. Their lives depend on the same grass and water as the wildlife. Herders move cattle with the seasons. Fishers follow the flood. This is one reason the park is difficult to manage. Protecting animals and supporting human livelihoods are not separate problems here. They are the same problem, seen from two sides.

There is also a difficult recent chapter. Since the mid 2010s, the wider Lake Chad region has suffered serious insecurity linked to armed groups. Tourism to Waza fell sharply, and the park has faced poaching pressure and limited funding. Many governments still advise against travel to parts of the Far North. If you are here, you are among a small number of visitors. Follow the guidance of local authorities, rangers and your guide closely. Their knowledge of current conditions is more reliable than any guidebook.

Before you leave, stop for a moment. Look at the grass around you, and the line where it meets the sky. Notice the tracks in the dust — perhaps an antelope, perhaps a bird, perhaps the wide, round print of an elephant. Feel the heat in the air, and listen for the next bird call. This landscape changes with every season, rising with the flood and drying again in the sun. Whatever you saw today, you saw one moment in that long cycle.

Practical tips

Carry plenty of water, sun protection and a hat.
