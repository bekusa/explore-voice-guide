The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the colour of these walls. It is not quite red and not quite orange. It is a warm, dusty shade somewhere between brick and autumn leaves. On a grey Petersburg afternoon it seems to hold its own light. Around you, the city moves in pale yellows and soft greens. This building does not match any of them. That was the point.

You are looking at Saint Michael's Castle. Many locals still call it the Engineers' Castle, and you will hear both names. It was built at the very end of the eighteenth century for one man, Emperor Paul the First. He was the son of Catherine the Great, and he did not trust the palace his mother had lived in. He wanted something new. He wanted a fortress.

Think about that word for a second. By the 1790s, Saint Petersburg was a capital of ballrooms, canals and grand façades. Nobody needed a castle here. But Paul feared plots against him, and those fears were not imaginary. Russian rulers had been removed by palace coups before. His own father had been overthrown and died soon after. So Paul built himself a home that could close its doors to the world.

The castle was surrounded by water. On two sides, the Moika and Fontanka rivers already ran past the site. Builders dug canals to complete the ring, and drawbridges connected the island to the city. Most of those moats were filled in during the nineteenth century. Part of the water line has been restored, and as you walk around, you can still trace where the old defences ran.

There is a personal layer to this place as well. The castle stands where an earlier wooden summer palace once stood, built for Empress Elizabeth. Paul was born in that older palace. He chose to build his fortress on the spot of his own birth. Several architects shaped the design, and Vincenzo Brenna, an Italian who worked for the imperial court, led the construction. Work moved very fast, at enormous cost.

Now here is something many visitors miss. Walk slowly around the building, and you will find that no two façades are the same. One side faces the city with formal columns and a grand entrance. Another side is calmer and almost private. A third looks out towards the gardens. Inside, the plan wraps around an octagonal courtyard, an eight-sided space hidden from the street. It feels less like one palace and more like several buildings joined together.

About that unusual colour. Locals love a story here, so treat it as legend, not history. According to one popular tale, Paul picked the shade from a glove worn by a lady at court whom he admired. It is a charming idea, and it is repeated often. There is no firm proof behind it. What is certain is that the colour sets the castle apart from almost everything else in the city.

The name has its own story too. The castle is dedicated to the Archangel Michael, the warrior angel of Christian tradition. Paul had a deep interest in chivalry and knightly orders, and the dedication suited his view of himself as a protector. Look for the tall, thin spire rising above the roofline. It marks the palace church, which was part of the plan from the start, not an addition.

And now the darkest chapter. Paul moved into his new fortress early in 1801. He lived here only a few weeks. In March of that year, a group of officers and courtiers entered his rooms at night. The emperor was killed inside these walls. The moats, the drawbridges and the guards had not saved him. The danger, in the end, came from people he already trusted.

After his death, the imperial family left the castle, and it stood without a clear purpose for some time. Then, in the 1820s, it became the home of the Main Engineering School, which trained military engineers. That is where the second name comes from. One of its most famous students was the writer Fyodor Dostoevsky, who studied here as a young man, long before he wrote his great novels about this city.

Of course, a building with a murdered emperor attracts ghost stories. Students, guards and staff have passed them down for generations. Some say a figure walks the corridors at night. Others claim to hear footsteps where no one is walking. These are folk tales, and nobody can prove them. But they show how strongly this one night in 1801 has stayed in the memory of the city.

Today, the castle belongs to the State Russian Museum. Inside, you can see restored interiors and changing exhibitions, many of them focused on portraits and on the history of the building itself. Before you go in, look at the square in front of the southern entrance. A bronze equestrian statue of Peter the Great stands there. Paul placed it in front of his new home, with a short inscription that reads, in Russian, To great-grandfather, from great-grandson.

One more small surprise. Walk towards the bridge over the Fontanka near the castle and look down at the stone embankment wall. Somewhere on a little ledge, you may spot a tiny bronze bird. Locals call him Chizhik-Pyzhik, the hero of an old children's song about the students of a law school that once stood nearby. People try to land a coin on his ledge for luck. Most coins end up in the river.

Before you leave, turn back once more and look at the whole building. The warm walls. The thin spire. The line of water at its feet. It was built to keep danger out, and it could not do that for even one season. Notice the people walking past it now, students, families, dog walkers on the embankment. The fortress has become part of an ordinary city afternoon, and that may be the quietest ending of all.
