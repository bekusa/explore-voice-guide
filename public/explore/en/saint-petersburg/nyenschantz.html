The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Water is moving on two sides of you. The smaller Okhta River slides in from the east and meets the wide, grey Neva. Gulls work the current. Traffic hums across the bridge. Underfoot the ground feels ordinary, flat and unremarkable. Yet this patch of riverbank is one of the most layered places in Saint Petersburg. Long before the city existed, people chose this exact meeting of rivers. And they kept choosing it, again and again, for thousands of years.

The name you are hearing is Nyenschantz. It is a Swedish word, and it points to a Swedish fortress. In the early seventeenth century, Russia was weakened by civil war and foreign invasion, a period Russians call the Time of Troubles. Sweden pushed into this region along the Neva. In 1611, Swedish forces built a fort right here, on the point between the two rivers. It was a simple idea. Whoever held this corner could watch every boat that moved between the lakes inland and the Baltic Sea.

After a peace treaty in 1617, this land was formally Swedish. A town grew up beside the fort. The Swedes called it Nyen, and it became a real trading place. Merchants came from Sweden, Finland, the German lands and Russia. Ships carried timber, grain, furs and tar. Think about that for a moment. Nearly a century before Saint Petersburg, there was already a working port town on this river, with streets, houses, a church and a market. Most visitors to the city never hear its name.

Over the seventeenth century, the Swedes rebuilt and strengthened the fortress. Earth ramparts and bastions rose around it. Bastions are pointed, arrow-shaped corners on a wall, designed so defenders could fire along the walls at attackers. This was the military fashion of the age across Europe. Here, at the edge of the Swedish empire, it guarded a modest garrison and a quiet trading town. For decades, life went on. Then a young Russian tsar arrived with very different plans for this river.

That tsar was Peter the Great. In 1700 Russia went to war with Sweden in a long struggle known as the Great Northern War. Peter wanted a port on the Baltic. The mouth of the Neva was the prize. In the spring of 1703, Russian troops came down the river and besieged Nyenschantz. The garrison was small and the Russian force was large. After a short bombardment, the Swedish defenders surrendered. The fortress that had watched this river for almost a century changed hands in a matter of days.

Here is the first surprise. Peter did not keep this fortress as his main base. He looked at the site and decided it was in the wrong place. It sat too far up the river, and it was hard to defend from the sea. So he went downstream to a small island in the Neva. Within days of the capture, work began there on a new fortress. That fortress became the Peter and Paul Fortress. Around it, Saint Petersburg was born. In a real sense, the city began with a decision made right here.

The captured Swedish fort was given a new name for a short time, and Russian soldiers used it. But its importance faded fast. The ramparts were later taken apart. The town of Nyen disappeared, and its people scattered or moved into the new city. Over the following centuries, this riverbank became industrial. Shipyards and workshops covered the ground. Generations of workers walked over the buried walls without knowing what lay beneath their feet. The fortress lived on mostly in old maps and in history books.

Now the second surprise, and it goes even deeper. Nyenschantz was not the first fortress on this spot. Around 1300, Swedes built an earlier castle here called Landskrona, which means crown of the land. It stood for only a very short time. Forces from the Russian city of Novgorod attacked and destroyed it almost at once. For centuries, Landskrona was known mainly from medieval chronicles. Historians argued about exactly where it had stood. The answer came from the soil itself.

In the 2000s, a giant skyscraper was planned for this site. The proposal caused strong public protest in Saint Petersburg. Many residents felt a tower here would change the historic skyline of the city. Before any building work could begin, archaeologists excavated the ground. What they found was remarkable. They uncovered remains of the Swedish fortress, traces of the medieval castle, and much older material. In the end, the tower project moved to another part of the city, on the shore of the Gulf of Finland.

The oldest finds here go back far beyond any castle. Archaeologists found evidence of Stone Age settlement, with tools and pottery left by people who fished and hunted on this riverbank thousands of years ago. So the layers stack up like the pages of a book. First, early fishing camps. Then a medieval castle. Then a Swedish fortress and a trading town. Then shipyards. And now the modern city around you. Every group saw the same advantage in this meeting of two rivers.

As you look around today, you will not see tall walls or towers. That can feel disappointing at first. But the real shape of this place is in the landscape. Notice how the land forms a point between the Okhta and the Neva. That point is the reason for everything that happened here. Look across the wide river to the far bank. The pale blue and white form of Smolny Cathedral stands there. It is part of the city that grew because this fortress fell.

Take a moment to think about the people who lived in Nyen. They were traders, soldiers, craftsmen and their families. They spoke Swedish, Finnish, German and Russian. They heard church bells and the creak of ships at the wooden quays. Their town vanished so completely that few people in the modern city know it existed. Yet the river they knew is the same river in front of you now. It carries the same cold water toward the same sea.

Before you walk on, look once more at the place where the two rivers join. Watch the current turn and mix. Listen to the traffic on the bridge and the wind over the water. You are standing at a kind of doorway. Behind you lies a buried Swedish town and a lost medieval castle. In front of you stretches a great Russian city. As you leave, notice how quickly the ordinary streets take over. History here is not on display. It is under your feet.

Before you go

Practical tips

Stand at the river point to see both waters meet.
Visit the Peter and Paul Fortress afterwards to complete the story.
Bring a windproof layer; the open riverbank gets cold.
Come in clear weather for long views across the Neva.
