The guide

The story

Stand on the corner where Nevsky Prospekt meets the Fontanka River, and let the traffic noise wash past you for a moment. Across the water, bronze horses rear against the sky on the Anichkov Bridge. And right beside you, a wall of deep red plaster rises up, crowded with white columns, carved figures and heavy window frames. The colour is warm, almost glowing on a grey northern day. This is the Beloselsky-Belozersky Palace. It is one of the last great private palaces built on Nevsky Prospekt.

Look up first, before anything else. The facade is not calm or flat. It pushes forward and pulls back in layers. Columns stand in pairs. Window frames carry curling ornaments. Above the ground floor, muscular stone figures appear to hold the building up on their shoulders. These are Atlantes, male figures used as supports, and they share the work with female figures called caryatids. The whole effect is busy, theatrical and very deliberate. Someone wanted this house to be noticed from the bridge.

That someone was a noble family with a very long name. The Beloselsky-Belozersky princes were an old Russian aristocratic line. In the 1840s, the family commissioned a new palace on this prominent corner. The architect was Andrei Stackenschneider, a favourite of the imperial court in the middle of the nineteenth century. He was a master of grand interiors and confident facades. Here, he was asked to create something that would stand out on the most fashionable street in the capital.

Here is the first surprise. This palace looks older than it is. Many visitors assume it dates from the eighteenth century, the age of Empress Elizabeth and her architect Rastrelli. That is exactly what Stackenschneider intended. He borrowed the language of Russian Baroque, the flowing, decorated style of the seventeen-hundreds. The red walls and white details recall the Stroganov Palace, which Rastrelli built further along this same avenue. So this is a revival, a nineteenth-century tribute to an earlier golden age.

Why would an architect look backwards? By the 1840s, much of Saint Petersburg had been rebuilt in strict classical style. Long, pale, orderly facades lined the main streets. Fashion was beginning to change. Wealthy clients wanted variety, richness and a sense of personal display. Stackenschneider gave them that. In a way, this palace marks a turning point in the city's taste. It shows a move away from discipline and towards decoration, a change you can trace in many later buildings along Nevsky.

Now turn around and look at the location itself. The palace sits at one of the most visible spots in the city centre. The Fontanka River once marked the edge of the early city. Over time, it became a line of noble estates and elegant embankments. The Anichkov Bridge, with its four bronze horse-taming groups, sits only a few steps away. Anyone crossing the river in a carriage would have seen this red facade straight ahead. Few addresses in the capital could promise that kind of audience.

Later in the nineteenth century, the palace changed hands. It was acquired by the imperial family. Grand Duke Sergei Alexandrovich, a son of Emperor Alexander the Second, made it his residence. For a time, the building became known as the Sergievsky Palace, after him. His wife was Grand Duchess Elizabeth Feodorovna, a German-born princess. She was the elder sister of the last Russian empress. Her later life took a very different path, and the Orthodox Church now honours her as a saint.

That link gives the building a quiet second surprise. The couple's life in these rooms belonged to the final decades of imperial Russia. The Grand Duke was later assassinated in Moscow in the early twentieth century. Elizabeth Feodorovna then turned away from court life and devoted herself to charity and religious work. When you look at these lavish windows, it is worth remembering how much changed for the people inside them. The facade stayed the same. The world around it did not.

After the revolution of 1917, palaces like this one lost their private owners. The building passed to the new Soviet state. For many decades, it served official and administrative purposes, including use by a local Communist Party committee. Grand rooms became offices and meeting halls. This was a common fate for aristocratic homes across the city. Some interiors suffered. Others survived surprisingly well, protected by the simple fact that the building stayed in continuous use.

Today, the palace has found a new role as a cultural venue. Concerts and events take place in its historic halls. The best known space is the Mirror Hall, a formal room of gilded decoration and tall mirrors. Chamber music performed there carries a particular atmosphere. The sound bounces softly off old surfaces, and the candle-like lighting catches the gold. If a concert is on during your visit, it is one of the easiest ways to see the interiors properly.

Step back now, perhaps to the edge of the bridge, and look at the whole composition again. Notice how the corner of the building is treated. It faces two directions at once, the river and the avenue. The architect had to make both sides feel important. Look too at how the white details stand out against the red ground. In soft evening light, the plaster turns almost crimson. On a bright winter morning, with snow on the sills, the contrast becomes sharp and graphic.

Listen for a moment as well. Nevsky Prospekt never really sleeps. Buses hiss, tourists call to each other, and in summer, small boats pass along the Fontanka below. Guides on those boats often point up at this facade as they slide under the bridge. From the water, the palace looks even taller. The stone figures seem to lean outwards over the river. If you have time later, a boat trip gives you a view that most pedestrians never see.

So before you walk on, take one last look at those carved figures holding up the upper floors. They have carried that weight through princely balls, imperial dinners, Soviet meetings and modern concerts. Behind them, the rooms have changed owners and purpose many times. The red wall in front of you is the same one that carriage passengers saw from the bridge long ago. As you cross the river or head down Nevsky, glance back once. The palace looks different from every angle, and it is worth one more look.
