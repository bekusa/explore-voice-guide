The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Behind you, traffic hums along one of the long avenues of southern Saint Petersburg. In front of you, a small building rises in stripes of soft pink and white, like a cake or a toy fortress. The air here feels quieter. The pointed spires catch the light, and the narrow windows look more like a medieval chapel in England than a Russian church. This is the Chesme Church, and it is one of the strangest and most charming buildings in the city.

Its full name is the Church of Saint John the Baptist at the Chesme Palace. Most people simply call it the Chesme Church. The name comes from a sea battle, not from this place. In 1770, during a war between the Russian Empire and the Ottoman Empire, a Russian fleet destroyed the Ottoman navy in the Bay of Chesma, off the western coast of what is now Turkey. It was one of the largest naval victories in Russian history, and Empress Catherine the Great wanted it remembered.

Catherine ordered a small palace and this church built on the road between the capital and her summer residence at Tsarskoye Selo. In those days, this spot stood outside the city, among fields and marshy ground. The palace served as a resting stop on the journey south. The church was built in the late 1770s and consecrated in 1780. The architect was Yury Felten, a court architect who also worked on the granite embankments of the Neva and on parts of the Hermitage.

There is a story connected to the dedication. According to tradition, news of the victory at Chesma reached the empress on the feast day of Saint John the Baptist. That is said to be why the church carries his name. The detail may be partly legend, but the church was indeed consecrated on that feast day, at midsummer. So when you stand here, you are looking at a building that joins a naval victory, a saint, and a royal road into one memorial.

Now look closely at the style. Russian Orthodox churches usually have onion domes, thick walls, and rounded arches. This church has none of that. Instead, you see pointed Gothic arches, slender pinnacles, and tiny turrets with notched tops, like the battlements of a castle. This is called Gothic Revival — a fashion in eighteenth century Europe for copying, and playing with, the look of medieval cathedrals. In Russia it was rare, especially for a working church. Here is the first surprise: this was one of the earliest Gothic Revival buildings in the whole country.

Walk slowly around the building and notice its shape. The plan is not a simple rectangle. It is built from four rounded lobes around a central core, a little like a four-leaf clover. Each side curves outward. Above, five small domes rise, one in the centre and four around it, which is a quiet nod to Orthodox tradition. So the church mixes two worlds. The outline follows Russian custom, but the dress is borrowed from Gothic Europe.

The colours matter too. The vertical white bands run from the base to the roofline. They make the walls look taller than they are and give the whole building a light, almost weightless feeling. On a grey Petersburg day, the pink stands out warmly against the sky. On a bright winter morning, with snow on the ground, the white lines almost disappear into the light. Many visitors say the church looks different every time they come back.

Across the way stands the Chesme Palace itself, also designed by Felten. It was built in a triangular shape with round towers at the corners, again in a playful castle style. Catherine filled it with portraits of European rulers and with a famous dinner service made in England, decorated with views of the British countryside and a small green frog emblem. The palace later changed purpose many times. Today it belongs to a university, so you usually see it only from the outside.

In the nineteenth century, the palace became an almshouse for retired soldiers and veterans. Next to it, a military cemetery grew up, where many of those veterans were buried. Later, soldiers from other wars were laid to rest there too. If you walk a short way from the church, you can find this cemetery. It is a quiet, shaded place, and it adds a serious note to a building that looks, at first glance, so cheerful.

The twentieth century was hard on the church. After the revolution, the Soviet authorities closed it in the 1930s, as they closed many churches across the country. For years it served practical purposes, including use as a storage space. The interior was stripped. Later, in the Soviet period, the building housed a museum dedicated to the Battle of Chesma. So, in a way, it kept its memory of the sea victory even while it was not a place of worship.

In the early 1990s, the church returned to the Orthodox Church, and services began again. Restoration work followed over the years. Here is the second surprise for many visitors: inside, the space feels much smaller and more intimate than the outside suggests. Icons, candles, and the soft voices of worshippers fill the room. It is a working parish, not a museum, so people come here to pray, light candles, and mark the feasts of the church year.

If you go inside, move slowly and quietly. Women usually cover their heads, and visitors dress modestly, with covered shoulders. During services, it is polite to stand at the back and watch. You may smell beeswax and incense, and hear a small choir. Even if you do not share the faith, the contrast is striking: a European Gothic shell around an Orthodox heart, with prayers in Church Slavonic under pointed windows.

Before you leave, step back across the small square and look at the church one more time. Notice how the pinnacles point upward like needles, how the pink and white stripes repeat around every curve. Listen again to the traffic on the avenue, and remember that this was once an empty road through fields to the summer palace. As you walk away, look back once more over your shoulder. The little church stays bright among the city blocks, still holding the memory of a battle far away at sea.
