The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. The walls in front of you are a soft sky blue, edged with brilliant white columns. On a grey Petersburg day, the colour seems to glow against the clouds. Gilded domes cluster above the facade like a crown, and the Neva River moves slowly just beyond. Listen... the traffic of the city fades here. What remains is wind, the call of gulls, and the crunch of gravel underfoot.

This is the Smolny Convent, and its central church is the Resurrection Cathedral. The ensemble rises at a wide bend of the Neva, on the eastern edge of the historic centre. It looks like a wedding cake, or a palace that wandered into a monastery. That impression is not an accident. The man who designed it also designed the Winter Palace — and he brought the same theatrical style here.

The name has a surprisingly humble origin. In the early eighteenth century, when Peter the Great was building his new capital and his navy, this riverbank held a tar yard. The Russian word for tar is smola. Ships needed tar to seal their wooden hulls, and this site produced it. So one of the most elegant buildings in the city carries the name of a smoky, sticky industrial yard.

Later, the area became a residence for Peter's daughter, Elizabeth. She spent part of her youth here, away from the centre of court life. When Elizabeth became empress in 1741, she did not forget this place. She decided to build a convent on the site. Her plan was personal and unusual. According to the traditional account, she intended to retire here herself one day, and to end her life as a nun.

For the design, Elizabeth turned to her favourite architect, Bartolomeo Francesco Rastrelli. He was an Italian, born in Paris, and he worked for most of his life in Russia. Rastrelli shaped the look of Elizabeth's reign — rich, curving, decorated, full of movement. Construction here began in 1748. Rastrelli blended the grand European Baroque style with old Russian traditions, and that mix is what you see in front of you.

Look carefully at the domes. A traditional Russian church often has five domes — one large central dome and four smaller ones around it. Rastrelli kept that old pattern. But he set the four small domes very close to the main drum, almost touching it. The result is a tight, upward cluster that feels Russian and European at the same time. Baroque, by the way, is the dramatic style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, full of curves and contrasts of light and shadow.

Now look at the buildings that surround the cathedral. These low wings held the nuns' cells. They form an enclosed square, with the cathedral at its heart. At the corners stand four smaller churches, each with its own dome. From above, the whole ensemble forms the shape of a cross. The contrast is deliberate. The plain, repeating rows of the cells make the central church look even taller and richer.

Here is something many visitors never learn. Rastrelli planned a huge bell tower in front of the main gate. It was meant to be taller than any building in the city, rising high above the river. That tower was never built. A large wooden model of the full design was made, and it still survives in a Saint Petersburg museum. Imagine the open space in front of you filled with an enormous tiered tower... the skyline would look completely different.

Why did the work stop? Money and war. During the Seven Years' War, a European conflict in the mid eighteenth century, Russia's treasury was stretched thin. Funds for Smolny slowed and then dried up. Elizabeth died in 1761, and she never took her vows here. Her successor, Catherine the Great, preferred the calmer, more classical style that was becoming fashionable. Rastrelli's dramatic Baroque fell out of favour, and the cathedral stood unfinished for decades.

Catherine found another use for the convent buildings. In 1764, she founded a school here for girls from noble families. It became known as the Smolny Institute for Noble Maidens, and it was the first state educational institution for women in Russia. Later, a separate classical building was constructed nearby for the school. That building has its own famous chapter. In 1917, it served as the headquarters of the Bolsheviks during the October Revolution.

The cathedral itself was finally completed in the 1830s, under the architect Vasily Stasov. He respected Rastrelli's exterior, but he finished the interior in a cooler, classical manner. That is the second surprise of this place. If you step inside, you will not find gold and colour everywhere. You will find bright white walls, tall arches, and a sense of calm open space. The outside belongs to one century... the inside to another.

During the Soviet period, the church was closed for worship, like many others across the country. In later decades the cathedral was used as a concert and exhibition hall, and its acoustics suit music well. Today it operates as a museum and still hosts concerts. Visitors can also climb a long staircase to a high observation level. From there, the Neva, the bridges, and the rooftops of the city spread out below you.

Before you leave, walk slowly back toward the gate and turn around once more. Notice how the white columns catch the light, and how the blue changes as the clouds pass. You are looking at an empress's private dream, interrupted by war and changing taste, and finished by other hands. Listen again to the wind off the river. Then take one last look at the empty space where the great bell tower was meant to stand.
