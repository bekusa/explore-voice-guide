The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the clock tower, the square hums with buses and car tyres. Inside, the sound changes. Rolling suitcases click over stone, a departure announcement echoes, and somewhere a train exhales. Moskovsky railway station sits at the eastern end of Nevsky Prospekt, the city's great avenue. For more than a century and a half, this is where journeys to Moscow have begun and ended.

Stand back and look at the facade. It is long, low, and calm, painted in warm tones with white trim. Two storeys of arched windows run along the front. In the centre, a tower with a large clock rises above the roofline. The style takes its cues from the Italian Renaissance, the period of palazzos in Florence and Rome. It feels more like a grand townhouse than a machine for trains, and that was deliberate.

The architect was Konstantin Thon, one of the most important builders of the reign of Tsar Nicholas the First. Thon also designed the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow. The station opened in 1851, together with the new railway between Russia's two great cities. That railway was a huge undertaking for its time. It linked the imperial capital on the Baltic coast with the old heart of the country, hundreds of kilometres to the southeast.

Here is the first surprise. This building has a twin. At the other end of the line, in Moscow, Thon designed a station that looks almost the same. Today that one is called Leningradsky station. So a traveller in the nineteenth century left one clock tower and, many hours later, stepped out under another. The two buildings work like bookends. They frame the journey, and they still face each other across the country.

The station has not always carried this name. It first honoured Tsar Nicholas the First and was known as the Nikolaevsky station. After the revolution, the old imperial names fell away, and in the Soviet years the station became Moskovsky, which simply means Moscow station. The name points to the destination, not to a ruler. That small change says a lot about how the twentieth century rewrote the map of this city.

There is a well-known story about the railway itself. Legend has it that the Tsar drew the route on a map with a ruler, in one straight line. The story says his finger was in the way, so the pencil made a small bump around it. Historians explain the real curve differently, as an engineering choice about gradients. But locals still enjoy the tale. And the line really is famously straight for most of its length.

Now step inside. The main hall is bright and high, with pale walls and a strong sense of order. In the central hall, look for the bust of Peter the Great. Peter founded Saint Petersburg in the early eighteenth century, on marshland at the mouth of the Neva River. It feels right that he greets travellers here. In Soviet times, the place of honour belonged to a different figure. The bust you see today arrived after the Soviet era ended.

Walk on toward the platforms and the building changes character. The front is a nineteenth-century palace, but the back is a working station. Over the decades, the complex has grown and been modernised many times. Glass, steel, and electronic boards meet the older halls. Watch the people around you. Business travellers with small bags, families with heavy cases, soldiers, students, grandmothers with parcels of food. The whole country passes through this space.

Here is the second surprise, and it is a sound. The overnight train called the Red Arrow has run between this city and Moscow since the Soviet period. It leaves late in the evening, close to midnight. As it pulls away, music plays over the platform: the Hymn to the Great City, by the composer Reinhold Glière. For many people, this melody is the sound of Saint Petersburg itself. If you are here at the right hour, stay and listen.

Today most daytime travellers take the high-speed Sapsan. The name means peregrine falcon, the fastest bird in the world. These sleek trains cover the route in a few hours, a trip that once took most of a day. Thon's building has seen wood-fired locomotives, steam, electric trains, and now high-speed rail. The clock above the entrance has kept time for all of them. The architecture stayed, while the technology around it changed again and again.

Now look back out at the square in front of you. This is Uprising Square, named for the street fighting of the February Revolution in 1917, when crowds and soldiers clashed here. For decades, a heavy bronze statue of Tsar Alexander the Third stood in the centre. It was later moved away and now stands elsewhere in the city. In its place rises a tall granite obelisk. It honours Leningrad, the Soviet name of this city, as a Hero City for its suffering during the Second World War.

This corner of the city is one of its busiest crossroads. Two metro stations serve the area, and Nevsky Prospekt runs straight from here toward the river and the Admiralty spire. If you walk west along the avenue, you follow the same route that arriving travellers took in carriages long ago. Shops, cafes, and old apartment buildings line the way. The station acts like a front door. Many visitors see this facade first, before any palace or cathedral.

It is easy to rush past a railway station. Most people here are trying to catch a train, not admire a cornice. But this building is one of the oldest major stations still in use in the country. It belongs to the first great age of the railway, when engineers and architects wanted stations to look as noble as palaces. Give it a few quiet minutes. Notice the rhythm of the windows and the steady face of the clock.

Before you go, turn once more toward the tower. Watch the hands move, and listen to the square around you: trams, voices, footsteps, the next announcement from inside. Somewhere behind those walls, a train is getting ready for Moscow, as trains have done here since the middle of the nineteenth century. When you walk away down Nevsky Prospekt, you carry on the same journey that millions began right here.

Key facts

Designed by architect Konstantin Thon for Tsar Nicholas the First.
Opened in 1851 with the Saint Petersburg to Moscow railway.
Its near-identical twin is Leningradsky station in Moscow.
Originally named Nikolaevsky station after Tsar Nicholas the First.
The Red Arrow departs to Glière's Hymn to the Great City.
Look closer

What to look for

The central clock tower above the main entrance.
Rows of arched Renaissance-style windows across two storeys.
The bust of Peter the Great in the main hall.
The granite Hero City obelisk in the square outside.
Sleek white-and-red Sapsan trains waiting at the platforms.
Before you go

Practical tips

Come near midnight to hear the Red Arrow departure music.
View the facade from the obelisk side of Uprising Square.
Use Ploshchad Vosstaniya metro station for the easiest access.
Book Sapsan seats ahead, especially on Friday and Sunday.
Walk west along Nevsky Prospekt after your visit.
