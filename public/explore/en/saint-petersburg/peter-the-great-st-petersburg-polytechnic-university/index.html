The guide

The story

Start with the sound of your own footsteps. Here, in the northern part of Saint Petersburg, the city grows quieter. Old trees line the paths, and their branches move above long pale facades. In autumn, the leaves collect against stone steps. In winter, snow softens every edge. This feels more like a country estate than a technical university, and that feeling was planned from the very beginning.

You are standing on the campus of Peter the Great Saint Petersburg Polytechnic University. It is one of the oldest and largest technical universities in Russia. The institution was created at the very end of the nineteenth century, and its first students arrived in the early 1900s. Back then, it was called the Saint Petersburg Polytechnic Institute. Over the following century, the name changed several times, as the city itself changed names and political eras came and went.

The driving force behind the institute was Sergei Witte, the Russian Minister of Finance at the time. Witte believed that Russia's economy needed engineers, economists, and shipbuilders trained at home, not abroad. Railways were spreading across the empire. Factories were growing in the capital. A modern state needed modern specialists. The polytechnic was his answer — a school that would teach both technical subjects and economics under one roof.

Now look at the setting. In the late nineteenth century, this area sat well outside the dense centre of the capital. The planners chose open land on purpose. They wanted space for laboratories, workshops, and housing for professors. They also wanted students to live and study away from the noise and distractions of the city streets. The result is a campus laid out among parkland, with buildings spread wide rather than stacked tight.

The main building is the heart of the ensemble. Its facade stretches a long way, and the eye follows a steady rhythm of tall windows. The style draws on classical architecture — the tradition of ancient Greece and Rome, with columns, symmetry, and calm proportions. Notice how the design avoids heavy decoration. The effect is dignified but practical, which suits a place built to train people who measure, calculate, and build.

Inside the main building, there is a ceremonial hall known as the White Hall. For generations, it has hosted academic ceremonies, lectures, and concerts. If the building is open to visitors when you arrive, it is worth asking whether you may look in. Even from the corridors, you can sense the scale of the interior. The ceilings are high. The light falls in long bars across the floor.

Here is something many visitors miss. Some of the most important physicists of the twentieth century passed through this campus. Abram Ioffe, often called the father of Soviet physics, taught here and helped shape its physics teaching. Among the young people who studied in these buildings was Pyotr Kapitsa. He later received the Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on very low temperatures. Nikolay Semyonov, a future Nobel laureate in chemistry, also has close links with the polytechnic.

Think about that for a moment. The paths under your feet were walked by students who would later change how the world understood matter and energy. In the early twentieth century, Saint Petersburg was a centre of scientific thought, and this campus was one of its workshops. The ideas that grew here went far beyond these walls, into laboratories, power stations, and textbooks around the world.

The twentieth century was not gentle with this place. The institute lived through revolution, civil war, and the long siege of the city during the Second World War. During those years of hunger and cold, the city suffered enormous losses, and the university community suffered with it. Today, memorials on and near the campus honour the students and staff who died. If you see flowers at a plaque, they are often left by people with a personal connection.

As you walk the grounds, look for the other buildings scattered among the trees. Some held chemistry and physics laboratories. Others were built as homes for professors and their families, so that teachers could live close to their work. Look also for a tall brick water tower on the campus. It was built to supply water to the institute in its early years, and it still stands as one of the most recognisable shapes here.

Today, the university is a working place, not a museum. Thousands of students study engineering, physics, computer science, economics, and many other subjects. You will hear many languages on these paths, because the university welcomes students from across the world. Respect the rhythm of the day. Lectures are running. People are hurrying between buildings with laptops and coffee. You are a guest in someone else's everyday routine.

The neighbourhood around the campus has its own character. The nearby metro station takes its name from the university, which tells you how closely the area is tied to it. Wide avenues, green courtyards, and Soviet-era apartment blocks surround the older academic core. Students fill the cafes and bookshops nearby. Walk a little beyond the gates, and you will see how a university can shape an entire district over more than a century.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the park. Listen to the wind in the branches, the crunch of gravel, a door closing somewhere in the main building. The founders wanted a calm place where people could think clearly and work hard. More than a hundred years later, that idea is still visible here — in the long facade, the open lawns, and the students who walk past you toward their next lecture.
