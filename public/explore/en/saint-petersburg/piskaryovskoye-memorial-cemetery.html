The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere ahead, quiet orchestral music drifts across the grounds from hidden speakers. Beneath it, you can hear gravel under your shoes and wind moving through the birch trees. The air feels open and flat here, at the northeastern edge of Saint Petersburg. Low green mounds stretch away in long, even rows. They look almost like garden beds. They are not. Each one is a mass grave, and you are standing at the largest burial site of the Siege of Leningrad.

The cemetery takes its name from Piskaryovka, an old village that once stood on this outskirt of the city. A small cemetery existed here before the Second World War. Then, in the autumn of 1941, German and Finnish forces closed a ring around Leningrad, as the city was then called. The blockade lasted roughly 872 days, until January 1944. During that time, this quiet ground became the place where the city buried its dead.

To understand these rows, it helps to picture that first winter. The siege cut the city off from food, fuel, and electricity. Water pipes froze. Trams stopped in the snow. Bread rations fell to their lowest point in late 1941. Many residents then received only 125 grams a day, a small slice made partly with fillers. People collapsed on the streets on their way to work. Hunger and cold, not bombs, caused most of the deaths.

By the winter of 1942, families could no longer bury their own relatives. The ground was frozen hard, and the living were too weak to dig. Bodies were often wrapped in sheets and pulled through the streets on children's sledges. City workers collected them and brought them here by truck. Trenches were dug, sometimes with explosives, and filled. This is the grim reason the mounds around you are so long and so uniform.

No one knows the exact count of people buried here. Estimates run to several hundred thousand, most of them civilians, alongside soldiers who defended the city. Many were never identified at all. Walk to the nearest mound and look at its low granite marker. You will see a year carved into the stone, such as 1942. You will also see one of two symbols. A five-pointed star marks the graves of soldiers. A hammer and sickle marks the graves of civilians.

That simple code is the first detail many visitors miss. There are no individual names on these mounds. Instead, each marker tells you only when people died and whether they wore a uniform. Count the stones with 1942 as you walk. You may notice how many there are. That year, the first full winter of the siege, was the deadliest. The calendar itself became a kind of headstone.

The cemetery you see today took shape after the war. It opened as a formal memorial on 9 May 1960, the anniversary of the Soviet victory in Europe. The architects were Alexander Vasiliev and Evgeny Levinson. Their design is deliberately restrained. There are no towers and no triumphal arches. The ground stays low and open, so the scale of the burials does the talking. Only one figure rises above the landscape.

She stands at the far end of the long central path. This bronze statue is called Mother Motherland, a female figure who represents the nation grieving for its children. She was created by the sculptors Vera Isaeva and Robert Taurit. In her hands she holds a garland of oak leaves, a traditional symbol of honour and endurance. She does not look triumphant. Her head is lowered slightly, as if she is laying the garland on the graves below.

Behind her runs a long granite wall carved with reliefs. The panels show the people of the siege: workers, soldiers, mothers, and the ordinary residents of the city. In the centre, words are cut deep into the stone. They were written by Olga Berggolts, a poet who stayed in the city through the blockade. Her voice reached residents on the radio during those years. The best-known line on the wall says: No one is forgotten, and nothing is forgotten.

That phrase is the second surprise for many visitors. It spread far beyond this cemetery. It became one of the most repeated lines of war remembrance across the former Soviet Union. You may hear it at memorials in many other cities. But it was written for this wall, for these people, by someone who lived through the same winters they did.

Now turn back toward the entrance. Between the two low pavilions near the gate, an eternal flame burns on a stone platform. This is where the central path begins. Inside the pavilions, small exhibitions display photographs and documents from the siege. One of the most moving items is a copy of the diary of Tanya Savicheva. She was a schoolgirl who recorded, in a few brief lines, the deaths of her family members one after another. The original diary is kept elsewhere in the city.

The cemetery is still a living place of memory. On anniversaries of the siege and on Victory Day, people come with red carnations and lay them on the marble steps and the mounds. Older visitors sometimes stand quietly for a long time. Many families in Saint Petersburg have a relative who may lie somewhere here, under one of these anonymous stones. For them, this is not a monument. It is a grave they can visit.

Before you leave, stop for a moment on the central path. Look back along the rows of mounds toward the bronze figure and the grey wall. Notice how the trees soften the edges. Notice how quiet the people around you are. The music will keep playing as you walk to the gate. And when you step back onto the city streets, you may see the tram lines and bakeries a little differently.

Practical tips

Walk the full central path to the statue and back.
Bring flowers if you wish to leave a tribute.
Keep voices low; many visitors come to mourn relatives.
Visit the entrance pavilions for siege photographs and documents.
Allow extra travel time; the site is far from the centre.
