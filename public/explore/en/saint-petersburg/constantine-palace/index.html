The guide

The story

Listen first. Out past the palace terrace, the Gulf of Finland moves against a low, flat shore. Gulls call over the water. Closer by, gravel crunches under your shoes, and long straight canals run toward the sea like lines drawn with a ruler. The air here smells of salt and cut grass. This is Strelna, on the southern shore of the gulf, west of Saint Petersburg. In front of you stands the Constantine Palace, pale and long, set on a raised terrace above the water.

This place begins with Peter the Great. In the early eighteenth century, Peter was building a new capital on the marshes of the Neva. He also wanted a country residence by the sea. It had to be grand enough to impress the courts of Europe. The model in his mind was Versailles, the vast palace of the French kings outside Paris. Strelna was one of the first sites he chose for that dream. Work on a stone palace here began around 1720.

Here is the first surprise. Strelna was meant to be the great fountain residence — the one we now know as Peterhof. Peter wanted fountains that would rival anything in France. But the land here is flat. Engineers struggled to bring water with enough natural pressure to feed powerful fountains. Peterhof, a short distance to the west, had a better slope and better water sources. So Peter moved the fountain project there, and Strelna slowly fell into second place.

The palace did not stop growing, though. Several architects worked on it across the eighteenth century. The Italian architect Niccolò Michetti prepared early designs. Later, Bartolomeo Rastrelli, the architect behind many of Saint Petersburg's Baroque palaces, worked here under Empress Elizabeth, Peter's daughter. Baroque, in simple terms, is a style of drama and movement — curves, ornament, and grand effects. Yet the building you see today is calmer and more restrained than Rastrelli's work in the city.

That calmer look comes from a later chapter. At the end of the eighteenth century, Emperor Paul the First gave the estate to his son, Grand Duke Konstantin Pavlovich. The palace takes its name from him. In the early nineteenth century, a fire damaged the building badly. The Russian architect Andrei Voronikhin led the rebuilding. He gave the interiors and parts of the exterior a cleaner, classical character, closer to the taste of his own time.

For most of the nineteenth century, the palace stayed with Konstantin's line of the imperial family. Grand dukes and their households spent summers here. They walked the same terrace where you may be standing now. They looked down the same canals toward the sea. Life at Strelna was quieter than at the great imperial residences. It was a family estate more than a stage for state ceremony, and that difference shaped its atmosphere.

Then came the twentieth century, and the palace's fortunes turned. After the revolution of 1917, the imperial family lost its estates. The building took on new roles in the Soviet years. It served various institutions, including schools, and its royal past was pushed aside. The rooms that once held grand ducal furniture filled with desks and practical equipment. Many original interiors and objects were lost or scattered during these decades.

The heaviest blow came in the Second World War. Strelna lay near the front line during the long siege of Leningrad, as Saint Petersburg was then called. Fighting and occupation left the palace a shell. Roofs, floors, and interiors were destroyed. For decades after the war, the building stood damaged and neglected. Older visitors from the region still remember it as a ruin by the sea, with empty windows facing the gulf.

Here is the second surprise. Almost everything polished that you see today is very recent. Around the turn of the twenty-first century, a large restoration project began. It was timed for the 300th anniversary of Saint Petersburg in 2003. Builders restored the palace, the terraces, the canals, and the park. Craftspeople recreated lost interiors from old drawings, photographs, and descriptions. In a sense, you are looking at both an eighteenth-century idea and a twenty-first-century reconstruction.

Today the palace has an official role. It is a state complex used for high-level meetings and government events. In 2006 it hosted the summit of the G8, a group of major world economies. Because of this role, visits work differently than at a typical museum. Access is usually through organised guided tours, and parts of the complex may close during official events. It helps to plan ahead and bring identification.

Now look at the shape of the park around you. The layout is formal and geometric, in the spirit of Peter's age. The main canal runs from the palace straight toward the gulf. Smaller canals cross it and divide the land into islands and sections. From the terrace, the lines lead your eye out to the water and the horizon. On a clear day, you can sometimes see the outline of the opposite shore across the gulf.

Take a moment with the building itself. The long façade stretches left and right, calm and symmetrical. The terrace below forms a strong base, with arched openings set into it. At the top of the palace rises a belvedere, a small raised structure for looking out over the view. The colours are soft, and the light off the water changes them through the day. In the morning the walls look cool. Toward evening they warm slightly.

Before you go, stand still for one more minute. Look down the canal toward the sea, the way Peter imagined it three centuries ago. He wanted fountains here, and he never got them. What he left instead is this long, quiet line of water running out to the gulf. As you walk away, listen again for the gulls, and notice how flat the land is beneath your feet. That flat ground is the reason Strelna became a palace of water lines instead of fountains.

Practical tips

Check for closures during official government events.
