The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Beneath the linden trees, the gravel crunches softly underfoot. Somewhere close by, water moves against stone, where the Fontanka river meets the wide grey Neva. The air here often smells of damp leaves and river mist. In front of you stands a small yellow building with white trim and a steep roof. It looks almost too modest to belong to an emperor. That modesty is exactly the point, and it is where our story begins.

This is the Summer Palace of Peter the Great. It is one of the oldest surviving buildings in Saint Petersburg. Work began around 1710 and was finished by about 1714, only a decade after Peter founded the city on marshy ground near the Baltic Sea. At that time much of Saint Petersburg was still timber, mud and ambition. This house was among the first serious buildings made of stone and brick. It was a statement that the new capital was here to stay.

The architect was Domenico Trezzini, a builder from the Italian-speaking part of Switzerland. He shaped much of early Saint Petersburg, including the Peter and Paul Cathedral across the river. Here he worked in a style often called Petrine Baroque. Think of it as Russian taste filtered through Dutch and northern European models. Peter loved the Netherlands. He had worked in Dutch shipyards as a young man, and he wanted his city to feel practical, orderly and close to the sea.

Look at the proportions of the house. It has only two storeys and a simple rectangular plan. There are no grand domes, no towering columns, no gold leaf on the facade. Instead, the walls carry clean lines and tall windows that let in the pale northern light. Between the two floors runs a band of carved relief panels. Their designs are linked to the German sculptor Andreas Schlüter, and they show allegorical scenes connected to Russia's long war with Sweden for access to the Baltic.

Now lift your eyes to the roof. Above the house, a weathervane turns in the wind. It shows Saint George, the warrior saint, slaying a dragon. Here is a detail many visitors miss. Inside the palace, a dial on the wall was linked to this weathervane. Peter could check the wind direction without stepping outside. For a ruler obsessed with ships, sailing and the navy, the wind was not small talk. It was daily information.

The position of the house also tells you about Peter. The palace sits right at the corner where two waterways meet. In Peter's time, a small harbour was cut beside the building, so boats could pull up almost to the door. Peter often travelled by water rather than by road. In those early years, the river was the main street of the city. Arriving by boat at his own front step was simply how the tsar came home.

Inside, the layout was split between husband and wife. Peter's rooms were on the ground floor. His second wife, Catherine, who later ruled as Empress Catherine the First, had her rooms on the floor above. The rooms are not vast halls. They are human in scale, with low ceilings, wooden floors and tiled stoves for the cold months. Walking through them, you get a sense of a working household, not a stage set for ceremony.

One room deserves special attention: the kitchen. It is lined with blue and white Dutch-style tiles that gleam in the window light. Here is the second surprise. The kitchen had running water. A pipe system brought water into the house, which was an advanced comfort for the early eighteenth century in this young city. A stone sink sat ready for use. For a man who liked to see how things worked, this was a small engineering pride.

Peter also kept a workshop in the house, which says a lot about him. He was a ruler who used his hands. He turned wood on a lathe, studied shipbuilding, and liked tools as much as titles. Today the rooms hold furniture, objects and decorations from the period, arranged to suggest how the palace looked in his lifetime. Not every item belonged to Peter himself, but together they show the taste and texture of his era.

The palace was only one part of a larger vision. All around you lies the Summer Garden, laid out at the same time in a formal, geometric style. Peter wanted avenues, clipped hedges, marble statues and fountains, much like the great gardens of western Europe. The fountains were so central that they gave the nearby river its name. The word Fontanka comes from the fountains that once drew water from it. A whole river is named after a garden.

As you walk the paths, notice the white marble figures standing between the trees. They show gods, heroes and allegories of virtues and seasons. Peter brought many sculptures from Italy, and in his day they were a lesson as much as decoration. Most Russians had never seen classical statues before. Strolling here, guests met the myths of ancient Greece and Rome for the first time. Today many originals are protected indoors, and careful copies stand in the garden.

The garden has survived floods, storms and long changes in fashion. Over the centuries, parts of the original design were lost, and fountains vanished. In recent decades, a large restoration worked to bring back the spirit of Peter's layout, with fountains, trellises and pavilions. Along the Neva side, you will also see a tall iron railing, added later in the eighteenth century. Its elegant dark bars and gilded details are now part of the city's postcard view.

Today the Summer Palace is cared for as part of the Russian Museum. Visitors move through it slowly, often in small groups, because the rooms are compact. That closeness is part of the experience. You stand a few steps from a tiled stove that warmed the tsar's family, or a window where Catherine looked out at the river. The scale makes the history feel near. Peter becomes less a statue and more a person who lived here.

Before you move on, pause once more outside. Look at the yellow walls against the green of the trees. Glance up at Saint George turning on the roof, still reading the wind after three centuries. Then listen again — to the gravel under your feet, to the water at the corner of the garden. This small house watched a muddy settlement grow into a great city. And it still stands here, quietly, at the edge of the river.
