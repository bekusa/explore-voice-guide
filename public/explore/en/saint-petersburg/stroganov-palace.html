The guide

The story

Stand for a moment where Nevsky Prospekt meets the Moika River. Traffic hums behind you. Water moves slowly under the bridge, dark and cold, with a faint smell of stone and river. And in front of you, a long facade in warm, rosy terracotta rises above the pavement, broken by white columns and deep windows. This is the Stroganov Palace, one of the oldest buildings still standing on the city's main street.

The palace was finished in the 1750s, when Saint Petersburg was still a young city. Its architect was Bartolomeo Francesco Rastrelli, an Italian-born designer who shaped the look of imperial Russia in the middle of the eighteenth century. Rastrelli also designed the Winter Palace, a short walk from here. If you know that green and white giant on the river, you may notice a family resemblance. The same rhythm of columns. The same love of light and movement on a wall.

Rastrelli worked in the Baroque style. In simple terms, Baroque is an architecture of drama. It uses curves, rich ornament, and strong shadows to make a building feel alive. Look at how the facade does not sit flat. Columns step forward, window frames carry carved heads and scrolls, and the roofline lifts above the central entrance. On a sunny afternoon, the shadows move across these details, and the whole wall seems to change shape as the hours pass.

Now, who were the Stroganovs? This is the first surprise. Long before they owned a palace on Nevsky, they were merchants and industrialists in the far north and east of Russia. They made their fortune in salt, furs, and land in the Urals. In the sixteenth century, the family helped fund the Cossack expeditions that pushed Russian power into Siberia. So the money behind this elegant city house came, in part, from the opening of an enormous frontier thousands of kilometres away.

By the eighteenth century, the family had climbed from merchants into the high aristocracy. The palace was built for Baron Sergei Stroganov, a member of the court. Look up above the main entrance on the Nevsky side. There you will find the family coat of arms. The Stroganov arms include sables, the small dark animals whose fur once built the family fortune. It is a quiet reminder, carved in stone, of where all this wealth began.

Later in the century, the palace passed to Count Alexander Stroganov, one of the great collectors of his age. He filled the rooms with paintings, sculpture, minerals, coins, and books. He also served for many years as president of the Imperial Academy of Arts. Under him, the house became something close to a private museum, a place where artists and scholars came to look, talk, and learn. Many of the interiors were redesigned in the newer, calmer Classical style of the time.

That redesign brings us to the second surprise. Much of the new interior work was done by Andrei Voronikhin, an architect who was born a serf on the Stroganov estates. A serf, in old Russia, was a peasant bound to the land and to its owner, with very few rights. Voronikhin's talent was noticed early, and the family supported his education. Locals have long repeated a story that he was the count's own illegitimate son. That remains a rumour, not a proven fact.

Whatever the truth of his birth, Voronikhin went on to design one of the largest churches in the city. Turn and look across Nevsky Prospekt, a little way along the street. The great curved colonnade of Kazan Cathedral is his work. So from this one corner, you can see both the house of the family that once owned him and the cathedral that made his name. Few street corners in Europe hold a story quite like that.

Inside, the palace mixes these two eras. Some rooms keep the swirling, gilded spirit of Rastrelli. The grand hall, with its tall windows and painted ceiling, is the clearest example of his style. Other rooms show the cooler lines of the later Classical rebuild. Walking through, you move from one century's taste into another's, often with only a doorway between them. Restorers have spent many years bringing these rooms back after long periods of neglect and heavy use.

The twentieth century was hard on the house. After the revolution of 1917, the palace and its collections were taken by the state. For a time, it served as a museum of aristocratic life. Then the collection was broken up. Many works went to the Hermitage, and others were sold abroad in the 1920s and 1930s, when the Soviet government needed foreign money. Today, objects from the Stroganov collection hang in museums far from this riverbank.

The building itself was later used as offices and institutions, and its rooms were divided and altered. In the late 1980s, the palace was given to the State Russian Museum, which now runs it as one of its branches. Since then, restoration has moved room by room. Exhibitions inside change over time, so what you see may differ from one visit to the next. That slow return is part of the palace's story too.

And here is one more small detail that surprises many visitors. The dish known around the world as beef Stroganoff carries this family's name. Exactly which Stroganov it honours, and who first cooked it, is still debated by food historians. Many popular tales place it in a nineteenth-century Stroganov household. Treat those stories as kitchen legend. But it is a pleasant thought that a name carved above this door also appears on menus on many continents.

Before you go, step back toward the bridge and look at the whole building once more. Notice how the corner facade turns to face both the river and the avenue, as if it greets two streets at once. Watch the light on the pink walls and the white stone. Listen to the trams, the voices, the water under the bridge. The street around it has changed many times. The palace still holds its place on the corner, as it has since the city was young.
