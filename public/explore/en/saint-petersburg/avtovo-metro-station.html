The guide

The story

Listen first. The rumble of an approaching train rolls along the platform, and the light changes with it. Above you, chandeliers hang between rows of columns that glitter as if covered in frost. Reach out, if the crowd allows, and you will feel something cool and smooth under your fingers. It is not ice, and it is not crystal from a palace. It is glass, shaped and set by hand, inside a working metro station that thousands of commuters pass through without looking up.

This is Avtovo, in the southwest of Saint Petersburg. It opened in 1955 as part of the city's first metro line, which was then called the Leningrad Metro. At first, it was the end of the line. Trains from the city centre stopped here and turned back. Today, the line runs further south, but many people still consider Avtovo the most ornate station on the whole network. It is easy to see why. The platform looks more like a ceremonial hall than a place to wait for a train.

To understand the decoration, you need to know where you are standing. During the Second World War, the German army besieged Leningrad for about nine hundred days. The front line came very close to this southwestern edge of the city. Avtovo was not a quiet suburb in those years. It was near the fighting. So when the architects designed this station a decade later, they chose a clear theme — the defence of Leningrad and the victory that followed. Almost every ornament here connects to that idea.

The lead architect was Yevgeny Levinson, a respected Leningrad designer of the Soviet period. His team worked in the style that historians often call Stalinist architecture. That style used classical forms — columns, arches, wreaths, heavy cornices — to make public buildings feel grand and permanent. In the metro, the goal went further. Ordinary workers on their daily commute were meant to pass through spaces as rich as a palace. Avtovo is one of the clearest examples of that ambition.

Now look closely at the columns. Many of them are faced with cast glass panels. The glass is ribbed and patterned, so it catches the light from the chandeliers and breaks it into small sparkles. The effect is cold and bright, like crystal. Here is the detail that most visitors miss. Not every column is glass. If you walk the platform slowly, you will notice that some columns are clad in marble instead. The full glass design was not completed, and the stone columns fill the gaps.

Why glass at all? The answer is partly practical and partly symbolic. After the war, Soviet industry wanted to show what it could produce. Glass on this scale was a technical statement. It also gave the hall a feeling of lightness, which matters underground. Many visitors say the columns look like they glow from inside. They do not — the light comes from the chandeliers. But the illusion is part of the design, and it works best when you stand still and let your eyes adjust.

Raise your eyes to the top of the columns. The capitals — the decorated crowns where each column meets the ceiling — carry military symbols. Look for laurel wreaths, which in classical tradition mark victory and honour. You may also pick out details such as banners and weapons worked into the ornament. These are not random flourishes. Each one repeats the station's message. A wreath on a column in Saint Petersburg says the same thing as a wreath on a Roman monument: this city fought, and this city endured.

At the far end of the platform, a large mosaic fills the wall. It shows a mother holding a child, surrounded by symbols of peace and plenty. After so much military imagery along the columns, this ending feels deliberate. The journey through the hall moves from war to peace. Walk toward it and watch how the wall grows larger between the rows of columns. Stand in front of it for a moment. Many visitors find this the quietest spot in the station, even when trains are arriving behind them.

Here is a second surprise. Most Saint Petersburg stations are very deep. The city sits on wet, soft ground near the sea, so engineers had to dig far down to find stable layers. Some escalators here take several minutes to ride. Avtovo is different. It is a shallow station, close to the surface. That is why the hall feels so open, and why the descent from the street is short. In a city famous for its endless escalators, this one gives you a rare, almost gentle entrance.

When you return to the street, look back at the entrance building. It is a round pavilion with columns, standing on the square like a small classical temple. Inside, the decoration continues the same theme of victory. Outside, the station sits among broad avenues and the large apartment blocks of the post-war years. This part of the city was rebuilt after the siege. The pavilion and the housing around it come from the same period of recovery.

The name Avtovo is much older than the metro. It belonged to a village that stood here long before the city spread this far. Where does the name come from? Nobody can say for certain. Locals like to tell a story about Peter the Great, the tsar who founded Saint Petersburg. According to one popular tale, a peasant named Avtonom helped people during a terrible flood, and the village took his name. It is a charming legend, but it remains folklore rather than documented history.

A practical note while you are here. This is a working station, not a museum. Trains arrive often, people move fast, and the platform can fill quickly at rush hour. Stand near a column rather than at the platform edge. Keep to the side when a crowd gets off a train. If you want photographs, take them calmly and without blocking anyone. Local commuters are used to visitors here, and a little patience makes the experience better for everyone.

Before you leave, take one last look down the length of the hall. Notice how the chandeliers line up in two straight rows. Notice the difference between the glittering glass columns and the plain marble ones. Listen to the next train coming in, and watch the light shiver across the glass as it arrives. Then think about the people who rode here in 1955, only ten years after the siege. For them, this bright hall was more than decoration. It was a promise that the city had come back.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit outside rush hour for a calmer platform.
Stand beside a column, never near the platform edge.
Walk the full platform toward the end mosaic.
Photograph quietly without blocking commuters' paths.
Step outside afterwards to see the round entrance pavilion.
