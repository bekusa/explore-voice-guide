The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Underfoot, gravel crunches softly. Somewhere close, water moves against granite. The air on this island feels cooler than in the city centre, and it smells of wet leaves and river. Ahead of you, pale walls and white columns rise above a sweep of lawn. You are standing on Yelagin Island, in the delta where the Neva River splits into channels before it meets the sea. This is Yelagin Palace, an imperial summer house built for one woman's quiet retreat.

The island takes its name from Ivan Yelagin, an eighteenth-century courtier. He served at the court of Catherine the Great, and for a time he directed the imperial theatres. Yelagin was a man of the arts, a writer, and a well-known figure among the Freemasons of his day. He owned this island and built a country house here. Little of his original house survives today. His name, however, stayed with the island long after he was gone.

According to one popular tale, Yelagin's island house once hosted a famous guest. Count Cagliostro, the Italian adventurer and self-styled magician, visited Saint Petersburg in the late eighteenth century. Locals say he performed strange experiments here, and some even claim he hid treasure in the cellars. Treat this as legend, not history. No treasure has ever been found. Still, the story tells you something real. This island has always felt a little apart from the city, a place where rumours could grow.

The palace you see now belongs to the early nineteenth century. In 1817, Emperor Alexander the First bought the island for his mother, the Dowager Empress Maria Feodorovna. She was the widow of Emperor Paul the First, and she loved gardens and country life. Her son wanted to give her a summer home close to the capital, but quiet and green. So the old estate was rebuilt from the ground up, with a new house, new service buildings, and a reshaped landscape.

The architect was Carlo Rossi. If you have walked through central Saint Petersburg, you have already seen his work. Rossi designed the curving General Staff Building on Palace Square and the street now named after him. Yelagin Palace was one of his first major commissions. Here he worked on a smaller, more personal scale. He did not design only the building. Rossi also designed furniture and many interior details, so the rooms and the objects inside them belonged to one idea.

Look at the facade facing the water. A broad columned portico stands at the top of a wide staircase, and stone lions guard the steps. This style is called Neoclassical — an architecture that borrows the columns, proportions, and calm symmetry of ancient Greece and Rome. On the opposite side, the building changes character. There, a rounded bay with columns curves outward toward the park. One house, two faces: formal toward the river, softer toward the garden.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The palace is only one part of Rossi's ensemble. Walk around the island and you will find other buildings from the same project. There is a kitchen block, shaped in a gentle curve. There is an orangery, where the household grew citrus trees and exotic plants through the long northern winter. There are stables, and small pavilions near the water. Together they formed a working estate, not just a pretty facade. The servants, cooks, and gardeners all lived and worked within sight of the house.

Inside, the centre of the palace is an oval hall under a dome. Light falls from above, and the room feels taller than the building looks from outside. Around it lie smaller rooms, each with its own colour and character. You will see painted ceilings, artificial marble, gilded details, and carved doors. Artificial marble, by the way, is a polished plaster made to imitate real stone. In a damp northern climate, it was practical, and in skilled hands it looks convincing.

Now for a second surprise. Much of what you see inside is a careful reconstruction. During the Second World War, Leningrad, as the city was then called, lived through a long siege. The palace burned during the war, and its interiors were largely destroyed. Only the outer walls remained standing. After the war, restorers worked for many years to bring the rooms back. They used old drawings, photographs, and surviving fragments. Today the palace operates as a museum of decorative arts and historic interiors.

After the empress died, the island remained in the hands of the imperial family. For much of the nineteenth century it served as a summer residence for later members of the court. The house was never meant to impress foreign ambassadors. It was a place to rest, walk, row on the ponds, and take tea. That private, domestic feeling is still here. The rooms are elegant, but they are not enormous. You can imagine actual people living in them.

In the twentieth century, the island took on a new role. In the Soviet period it became a public park of culture and leisure, open to ordinary city residents. Families came here to walk, skate, and picnic. That tradition continues. On weekends you will hear children on bicycles, runners on the paths, and the creak of oars on the ponds. The palace sits quietly at the centre of it all, a former imperial retreat that now belongs to everyone.

Before you leave, consider walking to the western tip of the island. Locals call this point the Strelka, which means the spit or arrow. A granite embankment faces open water toward the Gulf of Finland. In the evening, people gather here to watch the sun go down over the water. In early summer, during the White Nights, the sky stays light very late. It is one of the few places in the city where the horizon feels truly wide.

Now turn back toward the palace. Notice how the white columns catch the light, and how the trees frame the building from every angle. Rossi planned these views. The paths still guide your eye toward the house, just as they did two centuries ago. As you walk away, listen again for the gravel, the water, and the wind in the leaves. These are the same sounds an empress heard when she came here to rest.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk to the Strelka spit for sunset over open water.
Come on a weekday morning for quieter park paths.
Wear comfortable shoes; the island is best explored on foot.
Allow extra time to find Rossi's orangery and kitchen block.
Visit during the White Nights for very late evening light.
