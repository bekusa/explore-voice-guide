The guide

The story

Stand on the corner where Nevsky Prospekt meets the Griboyedov Canal, and listen for a moment. Tram bells, the scrape of shoes on granite, water slapping against the stone embankment below. Now lift your eyes. Above the traffic, a slim tower climbs toward a glass globe, and two bronze figures stand guard at its base. This is Singer House. Many people in the city simply call it the House of Books. Either way, it is one of the most photographed corners of Saint Petersburg.

The name comes from an American company. Singer made sewing machines, and in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries those machines spread across the world. Russia became one of the company's biggest markets. Families saved for a Singer the way people later saved for a car. The company needed a grand headquarters for its Russian business, and it wanted that headquarters on the most famous street in the empire. Construction ran from 1902 to 1904, and the result still stands in front of you.

The architect was Pavel Suzor, a respected Saint Petersburg designer. He worked in a style that Russians call Style Moderne. Elsewhere in Europe it is known as Art Nouveau, a movement that loved flowing lines, plant shapes and new materials like iron and glass. Look at the window frames and the ironwork. Nothing here is perfectly square or severe. The building seems to bend gently, like something grown rather than built.

Here is the first surprise. Singer reportedly hoped for something much taller — a true skyscraper, in the spirit of the company's tower in New York. But Saint Petersburg had a strict rule. Private buildings in the historic centre were not supposed to rise above the cornice of the Winter Palace, the tsar's residence. The city's skyline was meant to stay low and even. So how do you make a short building feel tall? Suzor's answer is the tower you see at the corner.

The tower pulls your eye upward. Its narrow shape and vertical lines give the impression of height without breaking the rule. At the very top sits the glass globe. It is a simple, confident symbol — a company that sold its product across the whole world. At night the globe is lit from inside, and it glows above the prospekt like a small moon. Many visitors only notice it after dark.

Now look just below the globe. Two female figures in bronze hold up the crown of the tower, and an eagle spreads its wings between them. They were made by Amandus Adamson, a sculptor from Estonia. People often call these figures valkyries. In Norse mythology, valkyries are warrior maidens who choose the fallen in battle. Here they are not fighting anyone. They seem to stand watch over the street, a little wild, a little theatrical.

The second surprise is hidden inside the walls. Behind the granite and decoration, the building rests on a metal frame. This was modern engineering for its time in Russia, closer to the methods used for American office towers. A metal frame allowed bigger windows and lighter walls. It also meant more daylight for the people working inside. Walk along the façade and notice how much of it is glass. For a building of the early twentieth century, that is a bold choice.

The ground floor and lower levels are faced with polished stone. Run your eyes over it, and you may see small differences in tone where repairs were made over the decades. Bronze details frame the entrances. The upper floors are lighter and more decorative. This layering is deliberate — heavy and solid near the street, airy and ornamental near the sky. It is a quiet lesson in how architects guide your gaze.

After the revolution of 1917, Singer's Russian business came to an end. The building found a new life. During the Soviet period it became home to publishers and, above all, to a large bookshop, the House of Books. For generations of local readers, this was the place to hunt for a new novel, a dictionary or a map. Many residents of the city still remember coming here as children. That memory is part of why the building feels so loved.

The bookshop is still here today, after a major restoration in the early twenty-first century. You can walk in freely and browse. The interior keeps much of its old character, with wide staircases and tall windows. Upstairs there is a café with windows facing the street. From there, the view across Nevsky Prospekt is one of the best in the city. In more recent years, the upper floors have also housed offices of a large Russian technology company.

Turn around now and look across the avenue. The great curved colonnade opposite belongs to Kazan Cathedral, built in the early nineteenth century. Its long arms of stone columns seem to reach toward you. The two buildings make an unusual pair. One is a church modelled on classical Rome. The other is a commercial tower inspired by modern America. Between them runs the main street of the city, full of people going about their day.

The canal beside you adds another layer. Look down the Griboyedov Canal, and you will see the coloured domes of the Church of the Savior on Spilled Blood in the distance. Small boats pass under the bridge here, and guides on deck often point up at the globe. From the water, the tower looks even taller. If you have time later, a canal trip gives you a completely different angle on this corner.

Before you move on, take one last look up. The glass globe, the bronze figures, the long windows catching the light. A company that sold sewing machines built this to show its success, and the city turned it into a home for books. Stand here a moment longer. Feel the stone under your feet and the steady flow of people around you. Then, as you walk away down Nevsky, glance back once — the globe will still be there, watching over the corner.

Practical tips

Come back after dark to see the globe lit up.
Visit the upstairs café for views across Nevsky Prospekt.
Cross to Kazan Cathedral's colonnade for the full façade.
Take a canal boat to see the tower from water.
Browse the bookshop for local maps and postcards.
