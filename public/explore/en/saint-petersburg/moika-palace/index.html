The guide

The story

Listen first to the water. The Moika River moves slowly here, dark and narrow between its granite walls. A tour boat passes, and its wake slaps softly against the stone. Above the embankment rises a long, pale-yellow façade with white columns. It looks calm and orderly. It does not look like a place where one of the strangest nights in Russian history took place... but it is.

This is the Moika Palace, which most people in Saint Petersburg call the Yusupov Palace. For about a century it was home to the Yusupovs, one of the wealthiest noble families in the Russian Empire. Their fortune came from land, estates and industry across the country. The family was rich enough that they did not just decorate a house. They shaped a small world inside it, room by room, over several generations.

The Yusupovs were not the first owners. The palace began life in the second half of the eighteenth century, during the great age of building that followed the founding of the city. Its early design is linked to the French architect Jean-Baptiste Vallin de la Mothe, who worked on several major buildings in Saint Petersburg. The Yusupov family acquired the property in 1830. After that, they rebuilt and expanded it to match their taste and their wealth.

Stand back from the façade for a moment. Notice the portico — the row of columns that frames the entrance like a temple front. This is the language of neoclassicism, a style that copied the architecture of ancient Greece and Rome. In this city, that style meant order, power and education. The front is fairly restrained. That restraint is deliberate. The real display was saved for the inside, for invited guests.

And inside, the mood changes completely. The state rooms run one into the next, each with its own character. There are halls with gilded plasterwork, rooms lined with silk, and floors of patterned parquet — wooden floors set in geometric designs. Crystal chandeliers hang above. Many visitors expect a museum of paintings. Instead, they find something closer to a lived-in stage set, where every surface was planned to impress.

Here is the first surprise. Hidden within the palace is a private theatre. It is small, almost like a jewel box, with tiers of boxes in red and gold curving around a tiny stage. The family used it for performances for their friends and guests. Standing in it, you feel how close the audience sat to the performers. It is one of the most intimate theatres in the city, and many people walk past the palace without knowing it exists.

The Yusupovs were also collectors. Over the generations they gathered paintings, sculpture, porcelain and other works of art. Much of the original collection was dispersed after the Russian Revolution of 1917. Some pieces went to state museums. Others were lost or sold. So when you walk through these rooms today, you are seeing restored interiors and carefully chosen objects, not the full private treasure house that once filled them.

Now we come to the story that draws so many people to this embankment. In December 1916, during the First World War, Grigori Rasputin was killed in this palace. Rasputin was a Siberian peasant and self-proclaimed holy man. He had gained great influence over the family of Tsar Nicholas the Second, mainly because the Empress believed he could help her son, who suffered from haemophilia, a disease that stops blood from clotting.

Many aristocrats saw Rasputin as a danger to the monarchy. A small group of conspirators decided to act. They included Prince Felix Yusupov, the heir of the family, together with Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich, a cousin of the Tsar, and the politician Vladimir Purishkevich. They invited Rasputin to the palace late at night. He was taken to a room in the lower level of the building, beneath the main living quarters.

What happened next has become legend, and it is important to separate story from record. According to Felix Yusupov's own later account, Rasputin was offered cakes and wine laced with poison, but seemed unaffected. Then he was shot. Popular tales say he rose again, fled into the courtyard, and was finally thrown into a frozen river. Historians still debate many of these details. What is certain is that Rasputin died, and his body was later found in the icy waters of the Neva.

Today, part of the lower floor tells this story with an exhibition and wax figures, set in rooms arranged to evoke that night. It is a strange contrast. Upstairs, the gilded ballrooms speak of celebration. Downstairs, the low ceilings and thick walls feel enclosed and quiet. The killing did not save the old order. Within months, revolution swept the Tsar from power, and the Yusupovs left Russia for exile in Europe.

Here is the second surprise. After the revolution, the palace was not simply turned into a museum. From the 1920s it was given to the city's teachers and educators, and it still serves as a cultural and educational centre. Conferences, concerts and meetings take place inside these grand rooms. So the building is not frozen in one moment. It continues to be used, as it was in the family's time, for gatherings of people.

As you leave, walk a little way along the embankment. Look back at the yellow façade reflected in the slow water of the Moika. Notice the iron railings, the small bridges crossing the river, the sound of footsteps on the granite. It is an ordinary city scene now. But behind those calm columns sit a jewel-box theatre, rooms of gold and silk, and the memory of one long winter night that changed how Russia remembers its last empire.
