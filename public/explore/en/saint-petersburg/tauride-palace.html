The guide

The story

Stand on Shpalernaya Street for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along the embankment side of the city, but here the sound softens. In front of you, a low, wide building stretches out in pale yellow and white. There is no gold leaf on the façade. There are no carved saints along the roofline. Just columns, a shallow dome, and long, calm walls. For a palace in Saint Petersburg, it is surprisingly quiet.

This is the Tauride Palace. It was built in the 1780s for one man — Prince Grigory Potemkin. He was the closest companion and most powerful statesman of Empress Catherine the Great. Many historians believe the two were secretly married. Whatever the truth of that, Potemkin was the empire's great builder, soldier and organiser in the south. This palace was his home in the capital.

The name comes from the south. Taurida is the old Greek name for Crimea. Potemkin led the annexation of Crimea by the Russian Empire in 1783. As a reward, Catherine gave him a new title — Prince of Taurida. So when people later called this building the Tauride Palace, they were naming it after his greatest triumph. The name is a reminder of a conquest far from these cold northern streets.

The architect was Ivan Starov, one of the first Russian-born architects to work at this level. He designed the palace in the neoclassical style. That means it follows the calm, ordered rules of ancient Greek and Roman buildings. Look at the central portico in front of you. Six columns hold up a simple triangular pediment. Above it rises a flat green dome. The side wings reach out like two open arms.

Here is the first surprise. In its time, this restraint was radical. The palaces of the earlier part of the century were Baroque — heavy with curves, statues and gold, like the Winter Palace across town. Starov chose the opposite. Contemporaries were struck by how plain the outside looked. The drama was saved for the inside, where the visitor walked from a modest entrance into enormous spaces full of light.

The heart of the palace is the Great Hall, often called the Catherine Hall. It is a very long room lined with two rows of white columns. In Potemkin's day, it was one of the largest halls in Europe. Beyond it lay a winter garden under glass. Guests could leave the frozen city and walk among tropical plants, statues and the sound of water. In a Russian January, that must have felt almost unreal.

In the spring of 1791, Potemkin gave the most famous party of his life here. The occasion was a victory in the war against the Ottoman Empire, including the capture of the fortress of Izmail. Catherine came as the guest of honour. Thousands of candles lit the halls. The poet Gavrila Derzhavin wrote verses for the evening and later described the splendour in detail. It was a celebration on a scale the capital had rarely seen.

And here is the second surprise. That glittering night was close to the end. Potemkin left for the south soon afterwards. A few months later, he fell ill and died on the road in the steppe, far from this palace. The building he had filled with music passed back to the crown. Catherine used it as a residence in her final years, sometimes spending the warm months here.

Then the palace fell on hard times. Catherine's son, Paul the First, disliked Potemkin and much of his mother's world. When he came to the throne, he handed the palace over to a cavalry regiment, the Horse Guards. The Great Hall was used as a riding school and stables. Valuable interiors were stripped and taken elsewhere. For a few years, horses stood where empresses had danced. Later rulers restored the building, but much of the original decoration was lost.

In the early twentieth century, the palace found a completely new role. In 1906, after the revolution of the previous year, Russia created its first elected parliament — the State Duma. The Duma met here, in the old halls of Potemkin. The building where a favourite of the empress once entertained the court now heard speeches about land, taxes and the limits of the tsar's power.

In 1917, this building stood at the centre of the revolution. After the fall of the monarchy, both the Duma's committee and the Petrograd Soviet — the council of workers and soldiers — gathered here. Crowds of soldiers and civilians filled the halls and corridors. That spring, Lenin presented his famous April Theses at a meeting in this palace. In January 1918, the Constituent Assembly, elected to decide Russia's future, met here — and was closed by the Bolsheviks after a single session.

Today the palace still serves as a place of assemblies. It houses the Interparliamentary Assembly of the Commonwealth of Independent States, an organisation of several former Soviet countries. Because it is a working institution, it is not an ordinary museum. Visits inside usually happen through organised tours. Even from the street, though, you can feel the long story layered into these walls.

Behind the palace lies the Tauride Garden. It began as Potemkin's private landscape park, with ponds, small bridges and winding paths. Today it is a public park where local families walk, children play and older people sit on benches in the sun. In winter, the ponds freeze white. In summer, the trees close over the paths. It is one of the gentlest places in this part of the city.

So take one more look at the façade. Those six columns have seen a prince's ball, a regiment's horses, a young parliament and a revolution. Notice how calm the building still looks — as if none of it disturbed its proportions. As you walk away, perhaps toward the garden, listen again for the quiet. It is the same quiet Starov designed, more than two centuries ago.
