The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes. It crunches softly with every step, a steady rhythm that has followed visitors along these paths for nearly three centuries. Ahead, through the trees, a long wall of colour comes into view. It is sky blue, broken by white columns and flashes of gold. The air smells of cut grass and damp leaves, and somewhere a crow is calling over the water. Welcome to Tsarskoye Selo, the summer world of the Russian emperors.

The name is usually translated as the Tsar's Village. It sounds simple, almost humble. But many historians think the name began somewhere else entirely. Before the Russians came, this land belonged to Finnic-speaking peoples, and an old manor here carried a name linked to a Finnish word for an island or a raised piece of ground. Over time, Russian ears turned that sound into something royal. So the village did not begin as the Tsar's. The Tsar's name was folded into it later.

The story of the estate begins with Peter the Great. Soon after he founded Saint Petersburg, he gave this land to his wife, the future Empress Catherine the First. She built a modest stone house here. Their daughter, Empress Elizabeth, had much larger dreams. In the middle of the eighteenth century, she called on the Italian-born architect Bartolomeo Rastrelli. He took the earlier buildings and stretched them into the palace you see now — named the Catherine Palace in honour of Elizabeth's mother.

Look along the main facade. It runs for more than three hundred metres, so long that it is hard to see the whole of it at once. Rastrelli worked in the Baroque style, a style that loves movement, curves, and decoration. Notice the rhythm of the columns, the deep window frames, and the carved male figures who seem to hold up the upper floors on their shoulders. In Elizabeth's day, much of this sculpture was covered in real gold leaf. Visitors arriving by carriage would have seen the whole building shine.

Inside, the palace becomes a sequence of rooms, one opening into the next, in a long straight line. This is called an enfilade. Standing at one end, you can look through door after door, all the way down the building. The most famous of the halls is the Great Hall. It is filled with tall windows on both sides, and between them are mirrors and gilded carving. On a bright day, the light comes in from two directions at once, and the room seems to have no solid walls at all.

And then there is the Amber Room. Its story starts in Prussia. In the early eighteenth century, the Prussian king gave panels of carved amber to Peter the Great as a diplomatic gift. Later, the panels were brought here and expanded to fill a whole room. Amber is fossilised tree resin, warm in colour, ranging from pale honey to deep brown. Lit by candles, the room glowed like the inside of a lantern.

Here is the part many visitors do not expect. The room you can see today is not the original. During the Second World War, German forces occupied the palace and took the amber panels away. After the war, they were never found. Their fate is still one of the great unsolved mysteries of the twentieth century. Russian craftsmen spent many years studying old photographs and recreating the room by hand. The reconstruction was completed in 2003, in time for Saint Petersburg's three hundredth anniversary.

That wartime story touches the whole estate. When the occupation ended, much of the palace was a burnt shell. The roofs were gone, and many interiors were destroyed. What you walk through now is the result of decades of patient restoration, and parts of the work continue. Keep this in mind as you look at a gilded ceiling or a painted wall. In many cases, you are seeing the skill of post-war restorers, working from fragments, drawings, and memory.

Step outside again into Catherine Park. It holds two very different ideas of a garden. Near the palace, the paths are straight and formal, in the French manner, with clipped hedges and neat lines. Further away, the park softens into the English landscape style, with winding paths, open lawns, and a large pond. Catherine the Great, who ruled later in the eighteenth century, preferred this more natural look. Around the water, you will find small pavilions, bridges, and a tall column rising from the pond, built to celebrate a Russian naval victory over the Ottoman fleet.

Catherine the Great also left her mark in stone close to the palace. She invited the Scottish architect Charles Cameron, who designed a long open gallery with slim columns. It sits above the gardens like a viewing terrace. Catherine liked to walk there, and she placed bronze busts of ancient thinkers and poets along it. Here is another surprise. Cameron came to Russia with deep knowledge of ancient Roman baths, and that interest shaped the calm, classical style of his work here.

A short walk away stands a quieter building, the Alexander Palace. Catherine the Great had it built for her grandson, the future Emperor Alexander the First. Much later, it became the main home of the last emperor, Nicholas the Second, and his family. They lived here more privately than in the city. In 1917, after the revolution, the family was held here before being sent away into exile in Siberia. They never came back. The calm park around the palace makes that history feel even heavier.

The estate also shaped Russian literature. Beside the Catherine Palace is the Lyceum, a school for the sons of noble families. Its most famous student was Alexander Pushkin, the poet many Russians see as the founder of their modern literary language. He studied here as a teenager, and he wrote about these parks again and again. In 1937, a century after his death, the town was renamed Pushkin. Look for the bronze statue of a seated girl with a broken jug in the park. Pushkin wrote a short poem about her.

One more fact few visitors know. The very first public railway in Russia was built to connect this town with Saint Petersburg. It opened in the 1830s. For the first time, people could travel here quickly, not only by carriage. This changed the town. It was no longer only a world for emperors and courtiers. It also became a place where ordinary city families could come for a summer day among the trees.

So pause here for a moment. Look at the blue walls, the white columns, the gold catching the light. Listen again to the gravel, the birds, the low voices of other visitors in many languages. Empresses walked these same paths, and so did a young poet, and so did a family who did not know their time was ending. As you walk on, notice the water in the pond. It moves slowly and holds the reflection of the trees — just as it has for a very long time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book palace entry in advance; Amber Room visits are timed.
Arrive early to beat tour groups inside the palace.
Wear comfortable shoes for long walks across the parks.
Allow a full day to see both palaces and gardens.
Bring a light jacket; the parks feel cooler near water.
