The guide

The story

Stand on the pavement of Kronverksky Prospekt and look up. Above the grey northern stone of the city, a dome of deep turquoise and cobalt rises into the sky. On a cloudy day the tiles hold a cool, glassy light. When the sun breaks through, they flash a blue that seems to belong to another climate. Trams rattle past, and the Neva wind carries the smell of river water. Then, for a moment, you could be standing in Central Asia.

This is the Cathedral Mosque of Saint Petersburg, the city's main Muslim house of prayer. It sits on the Petrograd Side, a short walk from Gorkovskaya metro station and close to Alexander Park. Across the water stands the Peter and Paul Fortress, where the city itself began. That closeness matters. The mosque was not placed on the edge of town. It was built near the historic heart of the imperial capital, in full view of anyone who crossed the bridges.

To understand why, picture Saint Petersburg in the early twentieth century. The Russian Empire stretched across many Muslim lands — the Volga region, the Caucasus, Crimea, and Central Asia. Thousands of Muslims lived and worked in the capital. There were Tatar merchants, soldiers, students, and officials. For years they gathered to pray in private rooms and borrowed spaces. Community leaders pushed for a proper mosque, one that would show their place within the empire.

Permission came, and construction began around 1910. The project gathered support from across the empire. A major donor was the Emir of Bukhara, the ruler of a Central Asian state that was then a protectorate of Russia. Ordinary believers also gave money. The building opened for prayer in 1913. That was the same year the Romanov dynasty celebrated three hundred years on the throne. The timing carried a clear message of loyalty to the crown.

The design is credited mainly to the architect Nikolai Vasilyev, working with the engineer Stepan Krichinsky. They looked far to the south and east for inspiration. The model was the Gur-e-Amir in Samarkand. That is the fifteenth-century mausoleum of Timur, the Central Asian conqueror also known as Tamerlane. Look at the ribbed dome above you. Its swelling, melon-like shape is a direct echo of Samarkand. Here is the first surprise — a Baltic city holds a careful tribute to a tomb in Uzbekistan.

Now look closer at the walls. The base is clad in grey granite, rough and heavy. This is the same kind of stone you see along the embankments of the Neva. Above it, the colour changes. Glazed ceramic tiles cover the dome, the minarets, and the great entrance portal. The pattern mixes several shades of blue with white and touches of other colours. The effect is a meeting of two worlds: northern stone below, Central Asian colour above.

Two minarets stand beside the main building. They are tall, slender towers, and they rise even higher than the dome. In many Muslim cities, a minaret is where the call to prayer begins. Here, in the middle of a Russian city, they also work as landmarks. From the bridges and from the fortress walls, the two towers help you find the mosque at once. They give the whole building a strong vertical line against the flat northern skyline.

The main entrance faces the street. Above the doorway, the portal is framed by tilework and Arabic calligraphy. The inscriptions are verses from the Quran, the holy book of Islam. Even if you cannot read the script, notice how the letters become part of the decoration. In Islamic art, writing is often the most respected form of ornament. The words are not added to the building. They are woven into its surface.

The twentieth century brought hard years. After the revolution, the Soviet state worked against religion of every kind. Churches, synagogues, and mosques across the country were closed. This mosque was shut around 1940. During the Second World War, when the city lived through the long siege, the building was used for storage. The blue dome stayed in place above a city that was starving. Many buildings did not survive those years. This one did.

The second surprise concerns how the mosque reopened. According to a widely told story, the visit of Indonesia's first president, Sukarno, played a part. Sukarno came to Leningrad, as the city was then called, in the 1950s. The story says he asked to visit the mosque and was troubled to find it closed. Soon after, in 1956, the building was returned to the Muslim community. The details vary between tellings, but locals still repeat the tale with pride.

Later decades brought restoration. Craftsmen worked to repair the tiles and the dome, which had suffered from damp, frost, and neglect. Replacing glazed ceramic in a northern climate is not simple. Water gets behind the tiles, freezes, and pushes them loose. If you look carefully, you may notice small differences in tone between older and newer pieces. Each one marks a moment when someone climbed up and fixed the surface by hand.

Today the mosque is a working place of worship, not a museum. On Fridays, men gather for the main weekly prayer. On the great Muslim festivals, the crowd can grow so large that worshippers spread their prayer mats on the pavement and into the street outside. If you visit as a guest, dress modestly. Women usually cover their hair, and everyone covers shoulders and legs. Shoes come off before you step onto the carpets inside. Quiet behaviour is expected.

Before you leave, step back across the street and take in the whole view once more. Notice how the granite meets the blue tiles. Notice the two minarets against the pale northern sky. Behind you, the trams keep running, and the city carries on with its day. As you walk toward the river, look back one last time. For over a century, this blue dome has quietly told passers-by that this northern capital has always belonged to many peoples.
