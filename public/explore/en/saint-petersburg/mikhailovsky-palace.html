The guide

The story

Stand at the edge of Arts Square and listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under someone's shoes. A tram bell sounds faintly from Nevsky Prospekt, just a short walk behind you. Through the trees, a long yellow and white facade rises behind a black iron fence. That is the Mikhailovsky Palace. Today it is the main building of the State Russian Museum. It is one of the calmest and most carefully composed views in the whole city.

First, a small warning that saves many visitors from confusion. Saint Petersburg has two buildings with almost the same name. The Mikhailovsky Castle is a different building. It is a moody, fortress-like structure a few minutes away, where Emperor Paul the First was murdered in 1801. The building in front of you is the Mikhailovsky Palace. It is lighter, younger, and was built for a much quieter purpose. Both names come from the same family, but they tell very different stories.

The palace was built for Grand Duke Mikhail Pavlovich. He was the youngest son of Paul the First, and a younger brother of two emperors, Alexander the First and Nicholas the First. Construction ran through the early 1820s, and the palace was finished in 1825. That same year brought the Decembrist uprising, a failed revolt by army officers against the new tsar. So the grand duke moved into his new home in a very tense year for the Russian court.

The architect was Carlo Rossi. He was an Italian-born architect who grew up in Saint Petersburg and shaped much of its centre. Rossi did not think in single buildings. He thought in whole ensembles. Here he designed the palace, the square in front of it, and the new streets that lead toward it. Look straight back toward Nevsky Prospekt. The short street in that direction lines up with the centre of the palace. That alignment is deliberate. Rossi wanted you to arrive and see the building framed perfectly.

Now look at the facade itself. In the centre stands a tall portico of Corinthian columns. These are columns topped with carved leaves, a style taken from ancient Greece and Rome. Above them runs a long frieze of sculpted figures. The yellow walls and white details are typical of the city's Neoclassical style. This style looked back to the calm order of classical antiquity. Wide stone steps lead up to the entrance, and two stone lions guard them from either side.

Don't overlook the fence. The cast-iron railing and its gates are part of Rossi's design, not a later addition. The fence closes off a front courtyard, called a cour d'honneur, or courtyard of honour. In the past, carriages rolled through these gates to drop guests at the steps. Today, visitors walk the same path. Notice how the black iron makes the pale facade look even brighter behind it.

After the grand duke died, the palace passed to his widow, Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna. She was born a German princess and became one of the most intelligent figures at the Russian court. Her salon in this palace drew writers, scientists, statesmen, and musicians. She supported the pianist and composer Anton Rubinstein. Through that support, she helped build the foundations for professional music education in Russia, including the Saint Petersburg Conservatory. Some people at court even discussed the freeing of the serfs in her rooms.

The palace changed its role at the end of the nineteenth century. The state purchased it, and it was rebuilt inside to become a museum. The new institution opened in 1898. It was named the Russian Museum of Emperor Alexander the Third, after the tsar who had wanted a national museum of Russian art. The idea was simple but new. Russia would have one great building devoted only to its own artists. Many rooms were reworked for this purpose.

Not everything was lost in that conversion. Inside, the White Hall keeps much of its original decoration from Rossi's time. It has gilded carving, painted panels, and elegant furniture. It gives a rare picture of how the grand ducal family actually lived. The main staircase is also worth a slow climb. Rossi designed it to impress guests, and it still does. Columns rise around you, and the light falls from above as you step up toward the galleries.

The collection inside is enormous, and it covers Russian art from medieval icons to the twentieth century. A few works stop almost everyone. Karl Bryullov's painting The Last Day of Pompeii shows the city's destruction on a huge canvas. Ivan Aivazovsky's The Ninth Wave shows a small group of survivors clinging to a mast in a glowing storm. Ilya Repin's painting of a ceremonial session of the State Council is so large that it fills an entire wall.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The museum is far bigger than this single palace. In the early twentieth century, a new wing was added along the canal side. It is known as the Benois Wing, after its architect, Leon Benois. Over time, the museum also took over other historic buildings nearby, including the Mikhailovsky Castle you heard about earlier. So the two buildings with confusing names now belong to the same museum.

Behind the palace lies the Mikhailovsky Garden. It is a green, quiet park that runs down toward the water. It is part of the same ensemble and has its own pavilion by Rossi near the river. On a warm day, local families walk here, and students sit on the benches with books. In front of the palace, in the middle of Arts Square, stands a bronze statue of the poet Alexander Pushkin. He raises one arm, as if he is reciting a poem to the palace.

The name Arts Square is earned. Around you are several theatres and concert halls, including the home of the Saint Petersburg Philharmonic and the Mikhailovsky Theatre. In the evening, people in coats and scarves cross the square toward performances. Music, painting, and poetry all meet in this one small space. It is a quiet continuation of Elena Pavlovna's salon, two centuries later, spread across a whole neighbourhood.

Before you go, look once more at the palace from the square. Follow the line of the columns, then the long white cornice, then the trees moving above the fence. Rossi planned this view for carriages and candlelight. Today it holds trams, phones, and tourists with maps. And yet the building still feels calm and balanced. As you walk away toward Nevsky Prospekt, turn around once. The palace will still be framed at the end of the street, exactly as its architect intended.

Good to know

Key facts

Designed by architect Carlo Rossi, completed in 1825.
Built for Grand Duke Mikhail Pavlovich, son of Paul I.
Opened as the Russian Museum in 1898.
Not the same building as Mikhailovsky Castle.
Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna hosted a famous salon here.

What to look for

Corinthian columns of the central portico.
Rossi's original cast-iron fence and gates.
Gilded original decoration inside the White Hall.
Pushkin statue raising his arm toward the palace.
Before you go

Practical tips

Start at Arts Square for the full framed view.
Pick a few key rooms; the collection is enormous.
Walk through Mikhailovsky Garden behind the palace afterwards.
Check whether Benois Wing exhibitions require a separate ticket.
Approach from Nevsky Prospekt to see Rossi's planned alignment.
