The guide

The story

Listen first. A dry wind moves through the grass. Somewhere above, a peacock gives its sharp, rising call. Underfoot, the track is pale dust and broken stone, and the trees around you are low, grey-green, and twisted. This is Sariska, in the Aravalli Hills of Rajasthan. It is not a deep, dark jungle. It is open, sun-washed country, where every movement in the scrub makes you hold your breath.

The Aravallis are among the oldest mountain ranges on Earth. Over an enormous span of time, wind and rain have worn them down into these rounded ridges and narrow valleys. Look at the rock along the roadside. It is folded and cracked, layered like old paper. These hills form a long spine across Rajasthan, and they shape everything here: where the water collects, where the grass grows, and where the animals move.

The forest itself is mostly dry deciduous woodland. That means many trees drop their leaves in the hot months to save water. The most common tree here is the dhok, a small, tough tree with thin, drooping branches. In the dry season, the hills can look almost bare and silver. After the monsoon rains, the same slopes turn green within weeks. If you come back in another season, you may hardly recognise the place.

For a long time, this land belonged to the rulers of Alwar, the princely state just to the north. The forest was their hunting ground. Royal parties came here for tiger and other game. That history may feel strange in a place now built around protection. But this is a common story in India. Many of today's reserves began as private royal hunting preserves, and the hunting is part of why the forests survived at all.

One trace of that royal era still stands near the entrance. Sariska Palace was built by a ruler of Alwar as a grand hunting lodge, with domes and arched verandas in a European-influenced style. Today it operates as a hotel. From the road, its pale walls look surprising against the rough hills. It is a reminder that people once came to this forest with rifles and servants, not cameras and binoculars.

After Indian independence, the protection of the forest grew step by step. Sariska became a wildlife sanctuary in 1955. In 1978 it joined Project Tiger, the national programme launched to save India's tigers from disappearing. The core idea was simple. Protect large areas of habitat, control hunting, and give the big cats room to breed. For years, Sariska was counted as one of the places where this effort was working.

Then came a shock. In the early 2000s, reports showed that the tigers of Sariska had vanished. Poachers had killed them, one by one, while official records still listed tigers in the forest. In 2005, the news became a national scandal. A tiger reserve with no tigers. It forced India to look hard at how its reserves were watched and managed, and it changed conservation policy across the country.

What happened next is the surprise most visitors do not know. In 2008, Sariska became the site of India's first tiger relocation. Tigers were moved here from Ranthambore, another reserve in Rajasthan, and the first animals were brought in by helicopter. Over the following years, more tigers arrived, and cubs were born in these hills. So if you see a tiger here today, you may be looking at the descendant of a cat that once flew.

The tiger is the name on the gate, but it is rarely the animal you meet first. Watch the open grass for spotted deer, called chital, and for the larger, darker sambar. Blue-grey nilgai, a big Asian antelope, often stand quietly in the clearings. Leopards also live here, and they are very hard to see. Rhesus macaques and grey langur monkeys gather near water and temples. And peafowl are everywhere, walking across the track as if they own it.

Sariska also holds human history inside the forest. On a hilltop stands Kankwari Fort, a stone fortification with long views over the plateau below. A popular tradition says the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb held his brother Dara Shikoh prisoner here during their struggle for the throne. Historians debate the details, so treat it as a local story. Still, standing near those walls, you understand why someone might choose this lonely place for a prison.

There is faith here too. Pandupol, deep in the reserve, is home to a Hanuman temple and a natural rock arch. Legend has it that the Pandava brothers, heroes of the Indian epic the Mahabharata, sheltered in these hills, and that the strong brother Bhima broke open the rock. On certain festival days, pilgrims come in large numbers. Elsewhere, near Neelkanth, ruined temples with carved stone show that people built and worshipped here many centuries ago.

That human presence is part of Sariska's challenge. Villages lie inside and around the reserve, and herders still graze cattle and goats. A busy road has also run through the forest. Some villages have been resettled outside, a slow and sometimes painful process for the families involved. The reserve is not a sealed wilderness. It is a shared landscape, where people, livestock, temples, and tigers all depend on the same water and the same hills.

So as your vehicle rolls on, keep your eyes low and your voice quiet. Watch for fresh pugmarks, the paw prints of big cats, pressed into the soft dust. Listen for alarm calls. A sharp bark from a sambar, or a sudden chatter from the langurs, often means a predator is near. Your guide will read these signs. Sometimes the forest gives you nothing at all, and that is honest too.

Before you leave, take one slow look around. Notice the colour of the hills in this light, the dust settling behind you, the stillness of the dhok trees. Somewhere out there, a tiger may be resting in the shade, watching the track. You may never see it. But it is here because people chose, after losing everything, to try again. Carry that with you as you drive back out through the gate.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book a guided safari in advance through official reserve channels.
Choose early morning drives for cooler air and active animals.
Bring binoculars to scan open clearings for deer and nilgai.
Wear dusty-coloured clothing and carry a scarf against dust.
Stay quiet during drives so you can hear alarm calls.
