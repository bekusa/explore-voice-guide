The guide

The story

Listen first. On most days you will hear the sea before anything else. Small waves fold onto the sand a short walk away, and the wind comes down off the mountains behind you. The ground here is flat and pale, scattered with dry grass and stones. Then there is the castle itself: a low, square block of honey-coloured stone, standing alone on the plain between the White Mountains and the Libyan Sea.

This is Frangokastello, on the south coast of Crete, in the region of Sfakia. The name is a local nickname, and it means "the castle of the Franks." For centuries, Greeks used the word "Franks" for Western Europeans in general, especially Catholics from Italy and France. Here, the Franks were the Venetians. Venice ruled Crete for more than four hundred years, and this fortress is one of the things they left behind.

The Venetians built the castle in the fourteenth century. They had a clear reason. Sfakia was the hardest corner of Crete to control. The mountains behind you are steep and full of gorges, and the people who lived in them had a long habit of rebellion. Pirates also raided this coast. A garrison on the plain could watch the shore, guard the landing places, and remind the Sfakians who held power on the island.

The Venetians gave it an official name, after a local saint. But the people of Sfakia never really used it. They called it the castle of the foreigners, and that name has stayed for more than six hundred years. It is a small detail, but it tells you how the locals saw this building. It was never their castle. It was a fort placed here to watch them.

Now look at the shape. The fortress is a simple rectangle, with a square tower at each corner. One tower, at the southwest, is much larger and heavier than the others. The walls are thick and plain, with a line of battlements along the top. There is very little decoration. This was a working garrison post, not a palace, and its builders cared about strength and visibility more than beauty.

There is one exception. Walk to the main gate, on the side facing the sea. Above the entrance, look for a stone panel carved in relief. It carries Venetian heraldry, including the winged lion of Saint Mark, the symbol of the Venetian Republic. The carving is worn by salt and wind, so you may need a moment to find the shapes. Many visitors walk straight under it without looking up.

Here is the first surprise. From the outside, the castle looks complete. But step through the gate, and you will find that the inside is mostly empty. There is no grand hall and no maze of rooms. The walls enclose an open courtyard of earth and stone, with traces of old foundations. Frangokastello is a shell. Its strength was always in the outer walls.

The castle changed hands as the island changed hands. In the seventeenth century, the Ottoman Empire took Crete from Venice, and the fort passed to new rulers. Sfakia stayed restless. In 1770, during a Sfakian uprising, the rebel leader Daskalogiannis surrendered to the Ottomans here, at Frangokastello. He was later executed in Heraklion. Today he is remembered across Crete as a hero of resistance.

The most famous event at this castle came in 1828, during the Greek War of Independence. A commander named Hatzimichalis Dalianis, who came from the mainland region of Epirus, occupied the fort with a few hundred fighters. A much larger Ottoman and Egyptian army marched against them. Local Sfakians reportedly urged Dalianis to retreat into the mountains, where the terrain favoured defenders. He chose to stay and fight on the open plain.

The result was a massacre. Dalianis and most of his men were killed. The castle walls could not protect them against such a large force. The battle took place in the month of May, and it became one of the best-known stories of the war in Crete. When you stand on this flat ground and look at the mountains, you can understand why the locals wanted him to retreat. There is almost nowhere here to hide.

Now for the second surprise, and this one is a legend. Locals say that in late May, around the anniversary of the battle, ghostly figures appear near the castle at dawn. They call them the Drosoulites, which means something like "the dew shadows." According to the tale, they are the spirits of Dalianis and his men, marching silently towards the sea before the sun burns them away. Some people suggest a natural explanation involving morning mist and light. Nobody has proved either version.

Whatever you believe, the story shows how deeply this place sits in Cretan memory. Away from the legend, the castle today is quiet. Fishermen, swimmers, and families come to the beach just below it. The water there is shallow and clear, and the sand is soft. On a calm day, children play in the sea while the old fortress stands behind them. It is a strange and gentle mix of war memory and summer holiday.

Before you leave, walk once around the outside of the walls. Notice how the light changes the colour of the stone, from pale grey at noon to warm gold in the late afternoon. Look up at the mountains to the north, and then out to the open sea to the south. This small castle sits exactly between the two... between the people who lived in those mountains, and the empires that arrived by sea. Take a last look at the worn lion above the gate as you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning or late afternoon to avoid midday heat.
Bring swimwear for the shallow sandy beach below the castle.
Wear a hat; the open plain offers almost no shade.
Walk the full outer circuit before going inside the walls.
Visit at dawn in late May for the Drosoulites legend.
