The guide

The story

Pause for a moment on the pavement. Water lies flat and dark in the canal beside you, and old brick houses lean gently along its banks. Bicycles tick past over the cobbles. Somewhere behind these quiet Dutch façades stands a stone temple from Egypt — real, ancient, and completely out of place. This is the Rijksmuseum van Oudheden, the National Museum of Antiquities of the Netherlands. A quick note on geography first. The museum stands in the old centre of Leiden, on the Rapenburg canal, just across the municipal border from Leiderdorp. If you have come from Leiderdorp, you have crossed into the neighbouring city. That is the right place. This is where the country keeps its oldest stories.

The museum was founded in 1818, and its roots lie in the university next door. Leiden University is the oldest in the Netherlands, and scholars here studied the ancient world long before most people could travel to see it. The museum's first director was Caspar Reuvens. Historians often describe him as the first professor of archaeology anywhere in the world. That title is worth thinking about. When this museum opened, archaeology as a university subject was only just beginning.

Reuvens did not wait for objects to arrive by chance. He bought them. In the 1820s, the museum acquired large collections of Egyptian material that dealers and diplomats had gathered in Egypt. Papyri, statues, coffins and small everyday objects crossed the sea to this canal. That early buying gave Leiden one of the important Egyptian collections in Europe. It also tells a more complicated story, about who took antiquities from where, and why. Museums today discuss those questions more openly.

Now, the temple. As you enter the main hall, you meet the Temple of Taffeh. It is a small sanctuary of pale sandstone, and it once stood on the bank of the Nile in Nubia, in southern Egypt. The temple dates from the Roman period, when Egypt was a province of Rome. You can walk right up to it. Look at the stone surfaces and you will see the wear of many centuries.

Why is an Egyptian temple in a Dutch city? In the 1960s, Egypt built the Aswan High Dam. The new lake behind the dam threatened to flood many ancient monuments in Nubia. An international rescue campaign, led by UNESCO, moved temples to higher ground. The Netherlands helped with that effort. As a gesture of thanks, Egypt gave the Temple of Taffeh to the Dutch. Only a handful of countries received a temple this way.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The temple did not stay a pagan shrine. In later centuries, people in Nubia converted it into a Christian church. So the stones you see carried two faiths, one after the other. A building made for Egyptian gods became a place for Christian prayer. Then, much later, it became a museum object in a cool northern hall. Few buildings have travelled so far, in both distance and meaning.

Beyond the hall, the Egyptian galleries continue the journey. Here you will find painted coffins, mummies, and statues of officials and gods. Take your time with the small things. Amulets, cosmetic jars and tools show daily life, not only royal power. Many visitors rush to the mummies. Slow down at the cases of little objects instead. They often say more about how ordinary people lived, worked and hoped.

The museum does not stop at Egypt. Other galleries cover the ancient Near East and the classical world of Greece and Rome. Marble sculpture, pottery and bronze objects fill these rooms. The light here is often softer and the crowds thinner. It is a good place to rest your eyes and your feet. Notice how the styles change from room to room, as you move between civilisations.

And then comes the second surprise. A large part of the collection comes from the soil of the Netherlands itself. For centuries, the river Rhine marked the northern frontier of the Roman Empire. Roman soldiers built forts and roads along it, close to where you stand now. Archaeologists have found their weapons, coins, pottery and jewellery in Dutch ground. Leiden was not on the edge of the ancient story. It was part of it.

The Dutch archaeology rooms go back even further, to prehistoric farmers and hunters. Some of the most striking finds are stone altars dedicated to a local goddess called Nehalennia. Fishermen and archaeologists recovered them from the coast of Zeeland, in the south-west of the country. Traders crossing the North Sea seem to have thanked her for safe journeys. It is a reminder that people here have always lived with water, and feared it.

The building itself is worth a glance as you move through it. The museum sits inside older canal-side architecture, with modern galleries added inside over the years. Old rooms and new spaces meet behind the historic front. Outside, the Rapenburg is one of the most elegant canals in Leiden, lined with grand houses and tall trees. The university's buildings and the old botanical garden are close by, so the whole street feels like a single campus of learning.

If you have time, pair your visit with a slow walk. Follow the canal, cross a small bridge, and look back at the museum front. It looks modest from outside. Nothing on the street prepares you for the temple inside. That contrast is part of the pleasure. Leiden keeps its treasures quietly, behind brick and glass, in the middle of everyday city life.

So, as you leave, listen again to the bicycles and the soft movement of the canal. Think of the stones in that hall — cut beside the Nile, prayed in by two religions, carried across the sea. Now they rest a short walk from here, in a calm Dutch street. Look at the water once more before you go. It is the same kind of river frontier the Romans once guarded, right here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Head straight to the main hall to see the temple first.
Linger at small everyday objects, not only the mummies.
Save energy for the Dutch archaeology rooms near the end.
Walk along the Rapenburg canal after your visit.
