The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the parapet. The limestone is pale, almost cream, and by midday it holds the sun like a warm loaf of bread. Below you, the sea moves against the rocks with a slow, heavy sound. Above you, swifts cut through the air. And all around, a city of orange roofs sits inside a ring of stone. You are standing on the Walls of Dubrovnik, and for the next stretch of your walk, they are your road.

The circuit runs close to two kilometres around the old town. It is not a flat promenade. The path climbs, drops, turns sharply, and climbs again. Steps are worn smooth in the middle, polished by centuries of feet. Some sections are wide enough for a small crowd. Others narrow to a single file. Take it slowly. The walls were built to be walked by guards on watch, not by visitors in a hurry.

To understand why this ring of stone exists, you need to know about Ragusa. That was the old name of Dubrovnik, and for centuries it was the heart of a small but very wealthy maritime republic. Ragusa had no large army. What it had was trade, diplomacy, and walls. The city survived between larger powers — Venice across the Adriatic, the Ottoman Empire inland — mostly by negotiation. But behind every negotiation stood this stone.

The defences grew in layers over many centuries. Early fortifications existed long before the walls you see now. Most of the present structure took shape between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. During that period, the threat of Ottoman expansion pushed the city to strengthen everything. Towers were added. Walls were thickened. New forts rose at the weak points. What looks like one single design is really a long conversation between builders across generations.

Here is something many visitors miss. The walls are not the same on every side. On the land side, facing the hills, they rise to great height and become very thick — several metres of solid stone. On the sea side, they are often thinner and lower. The reason is simple. Attack from land, with cannon and siege engines, was the bigger danger. The sea, with its cliffs and rocks, already did much of the defensive work.

At the highest point of the circuit stands the Minčeta Tower. It is round, heavy, and crowned with battlements, and it guards the northwest corner of the city. Its design is linked with notable fifteenth-century builders, including the Dalmatian master Juraj Dalmatinac. From the top, the view opens in every direction — the roofs, the harbour, the mountain behind the city, and the open sea. If you climb only one tower, many people choose this one.

Now look outward, across the small bay to the west. On a rock high above the water sits Fort Lovrijenac, separate from the main walls. Over its gate is a Latin inscription. In English, it says: freedom is not sold for all the gold in the world. For a republic that lived by trade, where almost everything had a price, those words carry real weight. Freedom was the one thing the city refused to sell.

Next, look down at the roofs. From up here, you can see something that you cannot see from the streets. The tiles are not all the same colour. Some are faded, soft, and almost pink. Others are bright, new orange. This patchwork is a map of damage. During the siege of 1991 and 1992, in the war that followed the breakup of Yugoslavia, shells struck the old town. Many roofs were later rebuilt with new tiles.

The walls themselves also carry those years, but the repairs were done with care. Restorers worked to match the stone and the building methods. Dubrovnik was already a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 1979, and the damage caused international concern. Today, the restoration is so careful that most visitors walk past the repaired sections without noticing. If you look closely at some walls and houses, you may still find small marks — quiet reminders of a recent war.

The city has known older disasters too. In 1667, a powerful earthquake destroyed much of Dubrovnik and killed many of its people. Churches, palaces, and houses fell. Yet large parts of the walls survived. Much of the old town that you see below you was rebuilt after that earthquake. The long main street, the Stradun, with its smooth, shining limestone, took much of its present, uniform look during that rebuilding.

As you continue along the sea side, the mood changes. The wind is stronger here. The water below is deep blue, sometimes almost green near the rocks. Look for the small doors and openings in the outer walls. Some lead to tiny cafés and bathing spots on the rocks outside. Locals swim here in summer. It is strange and lovely to watch people dive into the sea from the foot of a medieval fortress.

In recent years, the walls have gained a second life on screen. The television series Game of Thrones used Dubrovnik as a filming location for its fictional capital, King's Landing. Fans now walk these ramparts looking for familiar angles. But the real story of this place needs no fiction. A small republic kept its independence for centuries, mostly through intelligence, money, and patience — and with this stone ring around it.

Walk the walls early in the day if you can. The stone is cooler, the light is soft, and the paths are quieter. There is very little shade up here, so water and a hat matter more than you might expect. Wear shoes with good grip, because the old steps can be slippery. And stop often. The best moments here usually come when you stand still and simply look.

Before you go down, pause one more time. Listen to the city below — church bells, voices from the cafés, a door closing somewhere in a narrow lane. Look at the roofs, the old ones and the new ones side by side. When you walk back through the city gate and onto the Stradun, remember that the stone under your feet was laid by people who believed that walls could protect a way of life. Here, for a long time, they did.
