The guide

The story

Leave the wide, polished limestone of the Stradun and turn into a narrow side street. The light drops at once. The walls lean closer, and the paving under your feet is worn smooth by centuries of shoes. The noise of the main street fades behind you. This narrow lane is Žudioska ulica, which simply means Jews' Street. Somewhere above your head, behind an ordinary stone façade, is one of the oldest working synagogues in Europe.

From the street, you could walk past it without noticing. There is no dome and no grand portal. It looks like a tall, narrow townhouse, much like its neighbours. That plainness is part of the story. For much of its life, this community worshipped quietly, inside a city that tolerated it but kept it within careful limits. The prayer hall sits on an upper floor, tucked into the house rather than standing apart from it.

Jewish merchants and doctors were present in Dubrovnik by the late Middle Ages. Back then the city was the independent Republic of Ragusa, a small maritime state that grew rich on trade between the Ottoman lands and Italy. Tradition places the founding of a synagogue here in the fourteenth century. Historians are more cautious about that early date. What is certain is that this building has served Jewish worship for many centuries, and it is often described as the oldest Sephardic synagogue still in use anywhere.

That word, Sephardic, needs a short explanation. Sephardic Jews are the descendants of the Jewish communities of Spain and Portugal. In 1492, Spain expelled every Jew who refused to convert. Portugal followed a few years later. Families scattered across the Mediterranean, carrying their language, their melodies and their sacred books. Some of them reached this harbour. Ragusa valued their trading networks, their languages and their medical skills, and so a Sephardic community took root here.

Tolerance had firm edges, though. In 1546, the Republic confined the Jewish population to this street, creating a ghetto. Gates stood at each end, and they were locked at night. Look along the lane and try to imagine it closed off after dark, with families, workshops and the synagogue all inside. The street is short. The whole community lived within a few dozen steps of the place where it prayed.

Now climb the stairs. The stairwell is steep and narrow, like many in old Dubrovnik houses, so take it slowly. On the way up you pass through the small museum. Here, behind glass, you will see ritual objects that the community has kept for generations. There are Torah scrolls, which are the handwritten scrolls of the five books of Moses. There are also textiles, silver pieces and old documents connected to Jewish life in the city.

One detail surprises many visitors. Among the scrolls, according to community tradition, is a Torah that was carried here by refugees from Spain after the expulsion. Treat that as a cherished family story rather than a laboratory result. But pause in front of the scrolls anyway. Whatever their exact journey, these objects travelled with people who had lost almost everything else, and they chose to save these.

The prayer hall itself is small and intimate. The room feels more like a large family parlour than a cathedral. Lamps hang from the ceiling on long chains. At one end stands the ark, the cabinet that holds the Torah scrolls, framed in carved and gilded baroque woodwork. Baroque, in simple terms, is the ornate style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, full of curves and decoration. In front of it is the bimah, the raised platform where the scroll is read aloud.

Look up and to the side as well. Women traditionally sat apart from men in Orthodox synagogues. Here, they followed the service from behind a screen of wooden latticework. From the women's side, the room would appear in small fragments through the gaps. It is a detail that is easy to miss, and it tells you a great deal about how the room was used and who sat where.

The building has survived more than old age. Dubrovnik sits in an earthquake zone, and powerful tremors have struck the city more than once over the centuries. In 1979, a strong earthquake hit this coast and damaged the synagogue. Then, during the war that followed the breakup of Yugoslavia in the early 1990s, the Old Town came under shelling. The synagogue was hit, and its restoration took years of patient work afterward.

The twentieth century brought a deeper wound. During the Second World War, many of Dubrovnik's Jews were deported or killed, and the community never returned to its former size. Community accounts say that local people helped hide some of the synagogue's treasures during those years. Today the Jewish population of the city is very small. Yet the room is still a place of prayer and not only a museum, and that continuity is the real wonder here.

There is one more thing worth noticing. Out on the main street, the city's great churches and palaces announce themselves loudly. This building does the opposite. It holds its history inside, up a staircase, behind a plain door. In a way, it shows how minority communities survived in old Mediterranean cities. They stayed close to the centre of trade, but they kept their sacred spaces discreet and protected.

When you come back down, stand in the lane for a moment before you rejoin the crowds. Notice how narrow it is, and how the stone walls keep it cool and shaded. Listen to the echo of footsteps. Families once walked this same short stretch from their doors to their prayers, day after day, for generations. Then walk slowly back toward the Stradun. As the street opens and the light returns, glance over your shoulder once. The house will look ordinary again. Now you know what is kept upstairs.
