The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the polished limestone of Luža Square. Under your feet, the paving has been worn so smooth by centuries of footsteps that it shines like wet glass, even on a dry afternoon. Pigeons scatter. Café cups clink nearby. In front of you, a short, wide flight of steps rises to a pale stone facade. This is the Church of Saint Blaise, the small baroque church that sits at the heart of Dubrovnik's civic life.

Notice where you are standing. The long main street of the Old Town, the Stradun, ends right here and opens into this square. Around you stand some of the city's most important buildings: the Sponza Palace, the bell tower with its clock, and the old seat of government a few steps away. The church sits among them like a family member at the table. Here, faith and politics shared one small public room.

To understand why this church matters, you need to meet the man it honours. Saint Blaise was a bishop in the early Christian east, in a town called Sebaste, in what is now Turkey. He was martyred in the early fourth century. Across Europe he is linked with healing the throat. Many churches still hold a special blessing for sore throats on his feast day. In Dubrovnik, though, he became much more than a healer — he became the city's protector.

Locals still tell the story of how this began. According to the legend, sometime in the tenth century a Venetian fleet anchored near the city. Their sailors claimed they had only come for water. That night, the story goes, Saint Blaise appeared to a local priest and warned him that the Venetians planned an attack. The city prepared itself, and the threat passed. It is a tale, not a documented event. But it explains why the saint's face appears almost everywhere you walk here.

And here is the first thing many visitors miss. Once you know his look — a bearded bishop with a tall pointed hat and a staff — you will find him all over Dubrovnik. He stands in a niche above the Pile Gate, where most people enter the Old Town. He appears on the city walls, on fortresses, and once he appeared on the Republic's coins and flags. This church is simply his home address.

The building in front of you is not the first church on this spot. An older church stood here in the Middle Ages. It survived the great earthquake of 1667, which destroyed much of Dubrovnik, though it was damaged. Then, in 1706, a fire swept through it. The city decided to rebuild, and the new church rose in the early eighteenth century, designed by the Venetian architect Marino Gropelli. There is a quiet irony in that. The saint who, in legend, protected the city from Venice now lives in a church designed by a Venetian.

Look up at the facade. Baroque is a style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries that loves movement, depth, and drama in stone. You can see it in the strong columns, the deep shadows between them, and the way the front seems to step forward to greet the square. Right at the top, a statue of Saint Blaise watches over the crowd. The broad steps below are a natural meeting point. On warm evenings, people sit here to rest and watch the light change.

Now step inside, if the doors are open. The space is compact and bright. Your eyes will move quickly to the high altar. There, behind glass and in a gilded setting, stands a silver statue of the saint. This statue is older than the church around it. It dates from the fifteenth century, and it survived the fire of 1706. People in Dubrovnik saw that survival as a sign. The saint had protected his own image, just as he protected his city.

Here comes the second surprise, and it is a lovely one. Look closely at the statue's hands. The saint holds a small model of Dubrovnik. Because the statue was made before 1667, that little model shows the city as it looked before the great earthquake. Towers, walls, and buildings that later fell are preserved there in silver. Historians have studied it as a record of the lost medieval city. You are looking at a portrait of a Dubrovnik that no longer exists.

Around you, the windows add a modern note. The coloured glass was added in the twentieth century, and on a sunny day it throws patches of colour onto the marble and stone. It is a reminder that this is not a museum. It is a working church. People come in to light a candle, to sit quietly, or to say a short prayer on the way to work. Please keep your voice low, and give them space.

Every year on the third of February, this square changes completely. That is the Feast of Saint Blaise, the city's greatest celebration. People from villages around Dubrovnik arrive in traditional dress, carrying old embroidered banners. Relics of the saint are carried through the streets in procession. The festival has been held for more than a thousand years, according to local tradition. In 2009, UNESCO added it to its list of the world's intangible cultural heritage — traditions and practices, rather than buildings.

The church has also lived through hard modern times. During the siege of Dubrovnik in the early 1990s, the Old Town was shelled, and many roofs and buildings around this square were damaged. Careful repair work followed. Today, the city around you looks calm and complete. But the people who sit on these steps include many who remember those months. For them, the saint on the altar is not only history. He is part of how the city survived.

Before you leave, turn around and face the square again. Just in front of the church stands a stone column with the figure of a knight, known as Orlando's Column. For centuries, this was where the Republic announced its decisions and raised its flag. Let your eyes move from the knight, to the clock tower, to the long straight Stradun. As you walk away, look for the bearded bishop in his tall hat. You will see him again — above a gate, on a wall — still keeping watch.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to see Luža Square before tour groups.
Keep voices low inside; this is an active place of worship.
Cover shoulders and knees before entering the church.
Sit on the front steps at dusk to watch the square.
Plan around the third of February for the saint's festival.
