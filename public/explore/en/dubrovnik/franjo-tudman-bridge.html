The guide

The story

Listen first. A low hum runs through the air here. Tyres roll over the deck. Steel cables catch the wind coming in from the sea. Below, the water of a narrow green inlet lies almost still, and gulls drift past at the height of the roadway. Before Dubrovnik shows you a single stone wall, it shows you this white bridge, with its cables fanning out against the hills.

This is the Franjo Tuđman Bridge. For many travellers it is the true gateway to Dubrovnik. If you came by road from the north, from Split or from the airport side of the coast, you probably crossed it without a second thought. It carries the main coastal highway across the mouth of an inlet that locals call Rijeka Dubrovačka, the Dubrovnik River. From up here the old town is still hidden behind the hills, a few kilometres to the southeast.

The bridge is a cable-stayed design. That means the road deck does not hang from great looping cables like a classic suspension bridge. Instead, straight cables run directly from a single tall pylon down to the deck. They spread out like the ribs of a fan. The result looks light, almost delicate, though the structure is more than five hundred metres long. The pylon rises well over a hundred metres, and it is the tallest thing you will see on this stretch of coast.

Now look down at the water. The inlet below is the estuary of the Ombla river. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The Ombla is often described as one of the shortest rivers in the world. It does not trickle down from distant mountains. It bursts out of a spring at the foot of a limestone cliff, only a few kilometres inland, at a village called Komolac. Within moments of leaving the rock, its fresh water meets the salt water of the Adriatic.

This happens because the land here is karst. Karst is limestone country, full of caves and hidden channels. Rain falls on the high plateaus behind Dubrovnik, sinks into the rock, and travels underground for long distances. Then it emerges all at once at the coast. So the short river you see is really the end of a long, invisible journey through the mountains. On a calm day, look for a slight change in colour where cool spring water mixes with the sea.

For centuries this inlet mattered to Dubrovnik. The old Republic of Ragusa, as Dubrovnik was once known, was a wealthy merchant city. Its noble families built summer residences along these sheltered shores. They came here for shade, fresh water, gardens and cooler air. Some of those villas still stand along the banks further inland, half hidden among cypress and pine. The inlet was a place of retreat, a world away from the crowded streets inside the walls.

But for travellers, the inlet was also an obstacle. Before this bridge existed, the coastal road had to turn inland and wrap all the way around the head of the estuary. That meant a slow detour on winding roads, past Komolac and back out again along the far shore. A bridge across the mouth of the inlet was an obvious idea. Turning that idea into steel and concrete took far longer than anyone expected.

Here is the second surprise. Work on the bridge began before the war of the early 1990s. When Croatia declared independence from Yugoslavia, fighting reached this coast. Dubrovnik was besieged, and construction stopped. For years the project stood unfinished. Only after peace returned did the builders resume. The bridge finally opened in 2002. So this calm white structure carries a quieter story of interruption, damage and recovery, like much of the region around you.

The name reflects that era. Franjo Tuđman was the first president of independent Croatia, and he led the country through the war years. Naming the new gateway to Dubrovnik after him was a political statement as much as a civic one. Opinions about his legacy differ, both inside Croatia and abroad. For most visitors today, though, the name simply marks the moment the road reaches the city. It is the last big landmark before Dubrovnik itself.

Turn your attention toward the sea side of the bridge. Just beyond the mouth of the inlet lies Gruž, Dubrovnik's working harbour. This is where ferries leave for the islands, and where large cruise ships often tie up. From the bridge, those ships can look almost toy-sized against the water. Behind Gruž rises the wooded Lapad peninsula. On clear days, the low shapes of the Elaphiti islands sit on the horizon, grey-blue in the haze.

Now look inland, along the length of the inlet. The water narrows between steep green slopes. Boats sit in neat rows at the marina near Komolac. Small houses and gardens climb the hillsides of Mokošica, a residential area where many Dubrovnik families actually live. This is a part of the city that few visitors explore. Life here moves at a local pace, with school runs, market shopping and evening walks by the water.

The bridge changes with the light. In the morning, the sun comes from behind the eastern hills and the cables glow white against shadowed slopes. At midday the whole structure looks flat and bright. Toward evening, the light turns warm and the pylon throws a long shadow across the water. After dark, the lit structure becomes a pale shape above the black inlet, easy to spot from the harbour below.

If you are standing at a viewpoint, take a moment before you move on. Follow one cable with your eyes, from the deck all the way up to the top of the pylon. Then look down at the still water, and inland toward the spring where the river begins. Behind you, the road runs on toward the old walls. Ahead of you, the inlet curves away into quiet hills. Listen once more to the hum of the cables in the wind... and carry that sound with you into the city.

Before you go

Practical tips

Photograph the bridge from the Gruž harbour waterfront.
Come in late afternoon for warm light on the cables.
Take a boat trip inland to see the inlet's villas.
Visit Komolac to see the Ombla spring at the cliff.
Never stop a car on the bridge deck for photos.
