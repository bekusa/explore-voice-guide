The guide

The story

The light here is low and soft. Your eyes need a moment to adjust as you step inside. The air is cool and quiet, and the floor gives back a hush under your shoes. Somewhere ahead, small glowing scenes wait behind glass. This dimness has a purpose. Many of the works around you are drawings on paper, and paper fades in strong light. So the room stays gentle, and that makes you lean closer and look more carefully.

You are standing in the Moomin Museum in Tampere, inside Tampere Hall, the city's big concert and congress centre. It feels a little unexpected at first. Orchestras rehearse and conferences meet in the same building. Yet down here, you will find a small world of round white trolls, wandering philosophers and stormy seas. The museum opened in this building in 2017. Before that, the collection lived in other places in the city under the name Moominvalley.

The story of this museum begins with one woman: Tove Jansson. She was born in Helsinki in 1914 into a family of artists. Her father was a sculptor, and her mother was an illustrator. Jansson belonged to Finland's Swedish-speaking minority, and she wrote her Moomin books in Swedish, not Finnish. She was also a painter, a cartoonist and later a novelist for adults. The Moomins became her most famous creation, but they were only one part of a long and restless creative life.

The first Moomin book appeared in 1945, just after the Second World War. It was a small story about a mother and son searching for a lost father through a dark, flooded landscape. That background matters. Under the cosy surface of Moominvalley, there are floods, comets, long winters and lonely characters. Jansson knew fear and loss. Her stories offer shelter, but they never pretend that the world outside is always safe.

So why are these treasures in Tampere, and not in Helsinki? Here is a surprise that many visitors do not expect. In the 1980s, Tove Jansson donated a large collection of her original Moomin artwork to the Tampere Art Museum. It included many hundreds of drawings and illustrations — the collection is often counted at around two thousand works. Jansson had a warm connection with the city, and the gift turned Tampere into the home of the world's largest Moomin art collection.

As you walk, look at the original illustrations closely. Many are pen and ink drawings. You can see the hand of the artist: fine cross-hatching, quick confident lines, and sometimes small corrections. The printed books make these images look smooth and finished. Here, they look alive and slightly fragile. Because the paper is sensitive to light, the museum rotates the works on display. The drawings you see today may rest in storage on your next visit.

Then there are the tableaux. These are small three-dimensional scenes, built like miniature stage sets behind glass. Jansson created them together with her life partner, the graphic artist Tuulikki Pietilä, and with the help of another collaborator, Pentti Eistola. Tiny figures stand in tiny rooms. Moonlight falls on miniature forests. The scale pulls you in, and you may find yourself bending down to child height to see better. That is exactly the right way to look at them.

The most famous piece is the tall blue Moominhouse. It is a round tower of several storeys, and it is taller than most adults. Its walls open so you can see inside, floor by floor. There are little rooms, furniture, stairs and household objects, all made by hand. Take your time with it. Walk slowly around it, and look into each level. It feels less like a model and more like a real home that someone has just stepped out of.

Notice how the museum is arranged. The rooms do not feel like a classroom. They feel more like a journey through moods: calm, then dark, then bright again. This fits the books well. Moominvalley changes with the seasons. Summer is warm and full of guests. Winter is strange and silent, when most Moomins sleep. Jansson used the seasons to talk about change, about being an outsider, and about finding your own way.

It is worth remembering that the Moomins also reached the world through comics. In the 1950s, Jansson drew a Moomin comic strip for a London newspaper, and it spread to readers in many countries. The work was demanding, with constant deadlines. Later, her brother Lars took over the strip. That success made the characters global, but the drawings here take you back to the beginning, to one person at a desk with a pen.

As you move through the rooms, listen to the sounds around you. You may hear children whispering the names of characters. You may hear adults speaking in many languages, recognising old friends from their own childhood. Little My, Snufkin, the Groke, Moominmamma with her handbag. Each visitor seems to carry a favourite. This is a small museum, but it holds a lot of personal memory for the people who come here.

When you leave, the bright corridors of Tampere Hall wait for you again. Outside, the city has its own character. Tampere grew as an industrial town, with red-brick mills and rapids running between two large lakes. It is a practical, working city. And yet, it keeps this quiet room full of trolls and moonlight. That contrast says something kind about the place.

Before you go, pause for one more moment in the low light. Look again at the small scene closest to you. Notice the care in a tiny chair, a painted window, a lamp no bigger than your fingernail. Someone made this slowly, by hand, for no reason except love of the story. Carry that feeling with you as you step back into the brighter world outside.
