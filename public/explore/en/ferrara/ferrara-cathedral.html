The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look up. The stone in front of you is pale, almost the colour of old bone, and it warms to honey when the sun reaches it. Pigeons shift along the ledges. Bicycle bells ring behind you, because in Ferrara almost everyone cycles. You are standing before the Cathedral of Saint George, the spiritual heart of this city for close to nine hundred years.

The story begins in the twelfth century. Work on the cathedral started in 1135, at a time when Ferrara was growing in wealth and confidence along the waterways of the Po valley. The city dedicated its new church to Saint George, the soldier saint who fights the dragon. He was already Ferrara's patron, and you will meet him again and again as you look around this building.

Look at the facade as a whole, and you may notice that it seems to belong to two different worlds. The lower part is Romanesque, the sturdy style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with round arches and heavy, simple forms. Higher up, the style changes. Pointed arches and open galleries appear, added later in the Gothic period. So this front is really a conversation between two eras, stacked one on top of the other.

Now bring your eyes down to the main doorway. In front of it stands a small porch on columns, and those columns rest on the backs of two stone lions. Under the lions, crouching figures seem to carry the weight on their shoulders. This was a common idea in northern Italy at the time. Strength and patience hold up the entrance to the sacred space, and every visitor walks between them.

Above the door, in the curved panel called the lunette, Saint George sits on horseback and drives his spear into the dragon. This carving is the work of a master sculptor known as Nicholaus, who also worked on churches in Verona and Piacenza. His name appears in an inscription here, which is rare. Most medieval craftsmen are anonymous. Nicholaus wanted to be remembered, and in a way, he has been.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look above the porch, at the gallery on the upper level. There you will find a carved Last Judgment, added in the thirteenth century. Christ sits in the centre. On one side, the saved are led upward. On the other, the damned are pushed toward the mouth of hell. Medieval worshippers did not need to read. The message was carved in stone, right above the door they walked through every Sunday.

Now walk to the right, around the side of the building, toward the long square called Piazza Trento e Trieste. Something surprising waits here. Along the flank of the cathedral, a row of small shops leans directly against the church wall. This is the Loggia of the Merchants. For centuries, traders have sold their goods here, sheltered under arches, with the cathedral at their backs. Faith and commerce sit shoulder to shoulder, and nobody seems to mind.

This same side once had another great entrance, known as the Door of the Months. It was decorated with carvings of the labours of each month of the year, scenes of farming, harvest, and the rhythm of rural life. In the eighteenth century, the door was taken down. Fortunately, many of the carved panels survived, and today they are kept in the Cathedral Museum, a short walk from here, inside a former church across the square.

That museum holds other treasures too. Among them are the painted organ shutters by Cosmè Tura, one of the great painters of Renaissance Ferrara, showing Saint George and an Annunciation. There is also a gentle Madonna by the Tuscan sculptor Jacopo della Quercia. If the cathedral is the stage, the museum keeps the costumes and props that once filled it. It is worth the extra steps if you have time.

Before you leave the south side, look toward the bell tower. It is made of pink and white marble, and it rises in neat, square stages. But look at the very top... it simply stops. The tower was never finished. Its design is often linked to Leon Battista Alberti, the Renaissance architect and thinker. The money and the plans ran out before the final stage, and Ferrara has lived with its incomplete tower ever since.

If you step inside, you may be surprised again. After the medieval exterior, the interior feels very different. It was largely rebuilt in the early eighteenth century in the Baroque style, the grand and decorative fashion of that period. The space is bright, with white surfaces, gilding, and painted altars. In 2012, a series of earthquakes struck this region of Emilia, and the cathedral was damaged. Restoration has been long and careful, so parts of the building may look different on the day you visit.

Step back now into the square in front of the facade. Across from the cathedral stands the old town hall, once the residence of the Este family, the dukes who ruled Ferrara during the Renaissance. Their red brick castle, with its moat, is only a few minutes away. The historic centre of Ferrara is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site, and this square shows why. Church, palace, and market stand in one tight space.

Think about how many lives have passed through this spot. Medieval farmers came to market. Renaissance courtiers walked past in silk. Merchants opened their shutters under the loggia each morning. Today, students cycle through on their way to class, and tourists like you stop and tilt their heads back. The building has changed with each generation, but it has never stopped being used.

Before you move on, take one last look at Saint George above the door, still frozen in the moment of his victory. Notice the lions, worn smooth where countless hands have touched them. Listen to the bicycles, the café cups, the voices in the square. As you walk away, glance back once at that unfinished tower. Ferrara left it incomplete... and somehow, it suits the city perfectly.
