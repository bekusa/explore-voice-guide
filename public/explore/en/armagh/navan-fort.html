The guide

The story

Feel the grass under your feet as the slope rises. It is soft, close-cropped, and often damp. Up here on this low, rounded hill, the wind comes across the fields from every direction. You may hear sheep, a distant car, a crow somewhere in the hedges. And then you reach the top, and the land opens around you. This quiet green circle is Navan Fort. In the old Irish stories it has another name: Emain Macha.

Look first at the shape of the place. A great circular bank runs around the crown of the hill, enclosing a wide, open space. Inside, you can see a large grassy mound. Close by, there are fainter traces of a second, older circle. From a distance it can look like little more than a pleasant hilltop. But this is one of the most important prehistoric sites in Ireland. For centuries, poets and storytellers treated this hill as a royal centre of the ancient province of Ulster.

Here is the first surprise. Walk to the edge and study the earthwork carefully. The ditch is on the inside of the bank, not the outside. Think about what that means. A fort built for defence would put the ditch outside, to stop attackers. Here, the arrangement is reversed. Archaeologists read this as a strong sign that the enclosure was not built to keep enemies out. It was probably built to mark off a special, ceremonial space. So the name "fort" is a little misleading.

The hill sits about two kilometres west of Armagh city. The landscape around it is drumlin country. Drumlins are small, smooth hills shaped by glaciers at the end of the last Ice Age. They rise and fall across this part of Ulster like a field of green eggs. Navan is one of them, but it was chosen and reshaped by people. From the top, you can see why. The hill gives wide views, but it is not dramatic. It feels gathered and calm.

To understand why this place matters, we need to go into the stories. Emain Macha is the setting for much of the Ulster Cycle. This is a body of early Irish tales, written down by medieval monks but drawing on much older tradition. In these stories, the King of Ulster, Conchobar mac Nessa, holds court here. His household includes the warriors of the Red Branch. And the greatest hero of them all is the young Cú Chulainn, the Hound of Culann.

These are legends, not history. No one has proved that Conchobar or Cú Chulainn ever lived. But the tales give the hill its emotional weight. They describe feasts, quarrels, boasting contests, and battles. The most famous story is the Táin, the Cattle Raid of Cooley. In it, the armies of Connacht invade Ulster, and Cú Chulainn defends the province almost alone. When people in Ireland hear the name Emain Macha, these are the images that come to mind.

The name itself carries a legend. Macha was a figure of great power in Irish tradition, linked with sovereignty and with horses. According to one popular tale, a woman named Macha was forced to race against the king's horses while she was heavily pregnant. She won the race, gave birth to twins at the finish line, and cursed the men of Ulster. Locals and scholars often explain Emain Macha as "the twins of Macha". Other explanations exist, but this one has stayed in the popular memory.

Now to the evidence in the ground. Excavations took place here in the 1960s and early 1970s, led by the archaeologist Dudley Waterman. Under the large mound, the team found something remarkable. People had once built a huge circular timber structure here, about forty metres across. It was made of rings of upright posts, with a massive central post standing in the middle. Nothing quite like it was known elsewhere in Ireland at that time.

The second surprise comes from the wood itself. The central post survived as a stump, and scientists dated it using tree rings. The tree was cut down around 95 BC. That gives one of the most precise dates in Irish prehistory. And what happened next is just as strange. The builders filled the structure with stones, set it on fire, and then covered it with earth. The mound you see today is the result. It was not a collapse. It looks like a deliberate act.

Why would anyone build something so large and then destroy it on purpose? Nobody knows for certain. Many archaeologists think it was a ritual, perhaps a kind of offering or a way of sealing the site as sacred. Remember the reversed ditch. Everything here points in the same direction. This hill seems to have been a place of ceremony, perhaps linked to kingship, rather than a crowded royal palace. The stories may preserve a memory of its importance, even if the details are imagined.

One more find connects this quiet hill to a much wider world. The excavations uncovered the skull of a Barbary ape. This small monkey lives in North Africa. It must have been brought here, over enormous distances, as a gift or an exotic treasure. Imagine it for a moment. Someone in prehistoric Ulster owned a creature from the far side of Europe. Navan was not an isolated place. It was part of networks of exchange and prestige.

The hill also has neighbours. Nearby lies a small lake called Loughnashade. In the eighteenth century, people found bronze horns, or trumpets, near its edge. Another nearby site is the King's Stables, an artificial pool. A short distance away is Haughey's Fort, an older hilltop enclosure. Together these places form a ritual landscape around Navan. Close to the hill, the Navan Centre offers exhibitions and reconstructions if you want to see more after your walk.

There is also a link to Armagh itself. Tradition says that when Saint Patrick came to this region, he chose the nearby hill where Armagh now stands for his church. Many writers have suggested that he settled close to Emain Macha on purpose, near an old centre of power. That is an interpretation rather than a proven fact. Still, it is worth noticing that Armagh became the religious capital of Ireland, only a short walk from this ancient hill.

Before you go, stand still near the mound and turn slowly in a full circle. Look at the line of the bank, the soft hollow of the inner ditch, the hedges and fields beyond. The rings of posts are gone. The fire is long cold. The heroes may never have existed at all. But people gathered here, built something enormous, and gave it to the earth. As you walk back down the slope, listen to the wind across the grass. It is the same wind that crossed this hill two thousand years ago.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear waterproof shoes; the grassy slope is often wet.
Visit the Navan Centre after walking the hill.
Walk the full circuit of the bank for context.
Combine your visit with a trip into Armagh city.
Bring a windproof layer; the hilltop is very exposed.
