The guide

The story

Listen first. A low wind moves through casuarina trees. Small waves fold onto grey-gold sand. Somewhere above you, a bird calls from a cliff you can barely see. The air smells of salt, warm rock, and the mud of wetlands. You are standing on the Gulf of Thailand coast, south of the resort town of Hua Hin. In front of you, limestone peaks rise straight out of flat land and sea. Welcome to Khao Sam Roi Yot National Park.

The name is a good place to start. In Thai, it means roughly the mountain of three hundred peaks. Nobody has counted every summit, and the number is poetic rather than exact. But look around and you will understand it. Jagged ridges of grey rock stand one behind another, like broken teeth along the horizon. Some are bare stone. Others carry a thin cover of hardy green scrub, clinging to the cracks.

Locals also tell a different story about the name. According to one popular tale, a large trading ship was wrecked in a storm off this coast long ago. Three hundred people survived, swam ashore, and took shelter among these mountains. It is folklore, not documented history. Still, it is easy to picture. The cliffs drop straight into the sea, and the coves between them feel like natural hiding places from the open water.

These peaks are limestone. Limestone is soft rock, and it forms on the floor of ancient seas from shells and the skeletons of tiny marine creatures. Over very long periods, the land lifted and the rock was exposed. Then rain went to work. Rainwater is slightly acidic, so it slowly dissolves limestone. It carves deep channels, sharp pinnacles, and hollow caves. Geologists call this kind of landscape karst. You see it across Southeast Asia, but rarely this close to a beach.

Here is something many visitors do not know. This was Thailand's first coastal and marine national park, created in the 1960s. Today Thailand has many protected islands and beaches. But this stretch of coast came early. It protects a rare combination in one small area. There are mountains, sandy beaches, mangroves, caves, and wide freshwater marshes, all within a short drive of each other.

The most famous place in the park is Phraya Nakhon Cave. Getting there is part of the experience. Most people take a short boat ride, or walk over a headland, to reach Laem Sala Beach. This is a quiet curve of sand backed by trees. From there, a steep trail climbs the hillside. The path is rocky and uneven in places. It takes some effort, so go slowly, carry water, and rest in the shade along the way.

At the top, the ground opens. You look down into a huge cave whose roof has partly collapsed. The ceiling is gone in two places, and sunlight pours in through the openings. Trees grow on the cave floor, reaching up toward the light. It feels less like a cave and more like a roofless stone hall. The air inside is cooler, and your voice sounds different against the rock.

In the middle of this space stands a small pavilion. It is called Khuha Karuehat. It was built in the late nineteenth century for a visit by King Chulalongkorn, also known as King Rama the Fifth. He is one of the most respected kings in Thai history. The pavilion is a classic Thai design, with a tiered roof and slender columns. Its image appears on postcards across the country, and it is a symbol of Prachuap Khiri Khan Province.

Now for a second surprise. The famous photographs of this pavilion usually show a beam of light falling straight onto it. That beam is real, but it depends on timing. On clear days, the sun enters through the roof openings for a limited period, often in the middle to late part of the morning. If you arrive at the right moment, the dust in the air glows. Look carefully at the cave walls too. Royal inscriptions in the rock mark visits by Thai kings.

The park is not only rock and caves. Inland, you will find wide freshwater marshes. These wetlands are among the most important in Thailand, and they are recognised internationally for their value to birds. Tall reeds and water plants spread across the flat ground between the peaks. Wooden boardwalks lead out over the water in some areas. Walk quietly. You may hear frogs, insects, and the splash of birds lifting off from the reeds.

Birdwatchers come here from many countries. The marshes and mudflats attract herons, egrets, and many migratory birds, especially in the cooler months. On the cliffs, keep watch for dusky langurs. These are leaf-eating monkeys with dark fur and pale rings around their eyes, which make them look like they are wearing spectacles. They move slowly through the treetops and often sit still for long periods. Do not feed them. Feeding harms their health and changes their behaviour.

The land around the park also shows a modern story. As you drive in, you will pass many square ponds and shallow pools. Many of these are shrimp and fish farms. Over recent decades, farming has changed large parts of the original wetland. This is one reason protection here matters. The park sits beside working villages, fishing boats, and farms. Conservation and daily life share the same flat coastal land, and the balance is not always easy.

For a wide view of all this, many visitors climb to viewpoints on the smaller hills near the coast. From above, the pattern becomes clear. You see the blue Gulf, the pale beaches, the green marsh, and the grey peaks rising in rows. In the early morning, mist can sit in the low ground. In the evening, the limestone turns warm and golden. Fishing boats move slowly along the shore below.

Before you leave, stop for a moment wherever you are standing. Touch the rock if it is near you. Feel how rough and sharp it is, shaped grain by grain by rain over a very long time. Listen again to the wind, the birds, and the water. As you walk away, look back once at the ridgeline. Try to count the peaks. You will not reach three hundred... but you will understand why someone once chose that number.

Before you go

Practical tips

Reach Phraya Nakhon Cave via boat or headland walk to Laem Sala.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep, rocky climb to the cave.
Carry plenty of water and rest in shade during the climb.
Arrive mid to late morning for light beams in the cave.
Visit the marsh boardwalks early morning for the best birdwatching.
