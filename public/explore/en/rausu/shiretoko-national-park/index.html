The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. On the Rausu side of Shiretoko, the wind comes straight off the sea. It carries salt, cold air, and sometimes the sharp smell of kelp drying on racks near the harbour. Behind you, dark forest climbs steeply toward volcanic peaks. In front of you, a narrow strait of grey water separates you from land that is very close, but belongs to a different story. This is the edge of Japan, and it feels like it.

The name Shiretoko comes from the language of the Ainu, the Indigenous people of Hokkaido and the islands to the north. It is usually translated as the end of the earth, or the place where the land sticks out. Both meanings fit. The peninsula pushes northeast into the sea like a long finger. Much of it has no road at all. The tip can only be reached by boat, or by experienced hikers on foot.

Japan made this area a national park in 1964. In 2005, UNESCO added Shiretoko to its list of World Natural Heritage sites. That listing was unusual for its time, because it included the surrounding sea as well as the land. The reason is simple. Here, the ocean and the forest are not two separate places. They work as one system, and the story of that system begins with ice.

Every winter, drift ice arrives on this coast. It forms far to the north, in the Sea of Okhotsk, and travels south on the winds and currents. Shiretoko is often described as one of the southernmost places in the Northern Hemisphere where sea ice reaches the shore each year. When the ice comes, the harbour goes quiet. The waves stop. The sea turns white, and the coast can sound strangely muffled, as if someone has covered it with a blanket.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. The ice is not empty. It carries tiny plant life on its underside. When spring sunlight reaches it, this plankton blooms, and the whole food chain wakes up. Small fish feed on the plankton. Larger fish feed on them. Seals, seabirds and whales follow. And in late summer and autumn, salmon swim back up the short, fast rivers of the peninsula to spawn.

That salmon run is where the sea climbs onto the land. Brown bears wait in the rivers to catch the fish. Eagles and foxes take what the bears leave behind. Dead salmon break down along the riverbanks and feed the soil. Scientists have traced nutrients from the ocean into the trees of the forest itself. So the spruce and birch on these slopes are, in a real sense, partly made from the sea.

The bears deserve their own moment. Shiretoko has one of the densest populations of brown bears in Japan. Locals treat them with deep respect, and park staff work hard to keep people and bears apart. You may see warning signs, bear bells on hikers' packs, and food lockers at campsites. If you do see a bear, it will probably be from a boat or across a river. That distance is exactly how it should be.

Now look out across the water. On a clear day, the long, low shape on the horizon is Kunashiri, one of the southern Kuril Islands. Japan claims it, but Russia has administered it since the end of the Second World War. The dispute is still unresolved. From Rausu, the island looks close enough to reach in a short boat ride. For many older residents of this coast, it is a reminder of families and fishing grounds that were lost.

Rausu itself is a fishing town, and it does not pretend to be anything else. The harbour fills with boats, nets, floats and the sound of engines in the early morning. The town is known across Japan for Rausu kombu, a thick, rich kelp used to make dashi, the clear stock behind much of Japanese cooking. If you see long brown ribbons drying in the sun, that is kombu. Chefs in distant cities pay attention to where it comes from.

The sea here is deep, and it drops away quickly from the shore. That depth brings large animals close to land. In the warmer months, boats leave Rausu to look for whales and dolphins, and orcas are seen in these waters too. In winter, the same harbour becomes a base for drift ice cruises. Steller's sea eagles, enormous birds with heavy orange beaks, gather on the ice. White-tailed eagles join them. It is a scene that feels closer to the Arctic than to Tokyo.

The Rausu side also shelters one of the rarest birds in the country. Blakiston's fish owl is among the largest owls in the world. It lives along forested rivers and hunts fish at night. Its numbers fell sharply in the last century, and conservation work continues today. Most visitors will never see one. But it is worth knowing that it is out there, somewhere in the dark valleys behind the town.

Above everything rises Mount Rausu, the highest peak on the peninsula. It is a volcano, and the whole spine of Shiretoko is volcanic. That is why hot springs appear along the coast and in the hills. A mountain road crosses the peninsula at Shiretoko Pass, linking Rausu with Utoro on the western side. Snow closes it for much of the year. When it opens, the drive gives wide views of the peaks and, on good days, of Kunashiri across the strait.

So take one more look around you. Notice the colour of the water, which changes from steel to green as the light moves. Notice the line where the forest meets the shore, with almost nothing between them. Listen for gulls over the harbour, or the creak of a boat against the pier. As you walk away, remember that the tree beside the path and the fish in the market are part of the same cycle. Here, at the end of the earth, everything is connected.

Before you go

Practical tips

Carry a bear bell and follow all ranger guidance.
Book winter drift ice cruises from Rausu harbour ahead.
Bring windproof layers even in summer on the coast.
Check whether Shiretoko Pass road is open before driving.
Start at the Rausu visitor centre for current trail conditions.
