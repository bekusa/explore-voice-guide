The guide

The story

Step out of the Karachi heat and into the cool of the galleries. The traffic noise from the road outside fades. The light inside is low and even, and the air smells faintly of old stone and polished floors. Around you, glass cases hold objects older than almost any building on Earth. Some were shaped by hands that worked clay along the Indus River thousands of years ago. Welcome to the National Museum of Pakistan.

The museum stands in the Burns Garden area of central Karachi, surrounded by trees that offer some shade from the coastal sun. Its modern building opened around 1970. Before that, the national collection had a more elegant home. Soon after independence, in 1950, the museum was established in Frere Hall, a Victorian building a short drive from here. Frere Hall still stands today, and you can visit it separately.

It helps to remember why a museum like this mattered so much. Pakistan became an independent country in 1947. A young nation needed a place to hold its long memory. So this institution collects far more than the story of the modern state. It gathers the whole sweep of human life in this region, from the first cities to the twentieth century. As you walk, the galleries move roughly forward through time.

The Indus Valley Civilization is the reason many visitors come. This was one of the world's earliest urban cultures. It flourished at the same time as ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, which means roughly four and a half thousand years ago. Its people built planned cities with straight streets, brick houses, and covered drains. Two of its greatest cities, Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, lie in what is now Pakistan. Mohenjo-daro is in Sindh, the same province as Karachi.

Now look for a small figure of a bearded man. Archaeologists call him the Priest-King, though nobody knows who he really was. He was carved from soft stone and found at Mohenjo-daro in the 1920s. He wears a band across his forehead and a robe decorated with a pattern of trefoil shapes, like three-leafed clovers. Here is the first surprise. He is far smaller than his fame suggests. Most visitors expect a statue, and they find something you could hold in two hands.

Nearby, the Indus seals deserve a slow look. These are small square stones, often carved with animals. You may see a bull, an elephant, or a single-horned creature that scholars call the unicorn. Above the animals runs a line of symbols. This is the Indus script. And here is a second surprise. Despite a century of effort, nobody has managed to read it. The people who made these seals left us their writing, but not its meaning.

Spend a moment with the pottery and the small toys too. There are painted jars, beads, and little clay animals. Some of these figures may have been playthings for children. It is a quiet, human detail. Thousands of years ago, in a city by the Indus, a child may have pushed a toy across a brick floor. That simple picture often stays with visitors longer than the grand ideas about early civilisation.

From the ancient cities, the story moves to the art of Gandhara. Gandhara was an old region in the north-west of what is now Pakistan. Around two thousand years ago, Buddhism flourished there. Local sculptors carved images of the Buddha in grey stone called schist. Their style mixed South Asian ideas with influences from the Greek and Roman world, which reached the region through trade and older conquest. Look at the folds of the robes. They fall in heavy, realistic waves, much like classical Mediterranean statues.

Many Gandharan panels tell stories from the life of the Buddha. You may see his birth, his time as a wandering teacher, or his final passing. Each panel is like a page from a stone book. The faces are calm, with half-closed eyes. Some figures have lost hands or noses over the centuries. These missing pieces are part of their history. They passed through centuries of weather, ruin, and digging before they reached these cases.

The museum also holds Hindu sculpture, a reminder that this region has been home to many faiths. Then the galleries move into the Islamic period. Here the focus shifts from carved figures to writing and pattern. Islamic art often avoids images of people in religious settings. Instead, artists turned calligraphy, the art of beautiful handwriting, into one of their highest forms. You will see tiles, metalwork, and carved panels covered with flowing lines and repeating geometric shapes.

The Quran gallery is one of the quietest rooms in the building. It holds a large collection of handwritten copies of the Quran, the holy book of Islam. Some are very old. Some are tiny, small enough to carry in a pocket. Others are large and decorated with gold and deep blue colours. Look closely at the ink. Each line was written by hand, with care, over many hours. Visitors often lower their voices here without being asked.

Other galleries cover coins, which trace the many rulers and kingdoms that passed through this land, and the everyday life of the region's people. There are displays of traditional dress, jewellery, and tools from different communities across Pakistan. There is also a section on the Pakistan Movement, the political campaign that led to independence in 1947. Here the objects become photographs and documents. The scale of the story changes, from thousands of years to a few decades.

As you leave, step back into the light and noise of Karachi. Notice the trees of the garden, the rickshaws on the road, the sound of a city of many millions. It is a long way from the brick streets of Mohenjo-daro. And yet the river that fed that ancient city still runs through this province today. The small bearded figure inside has seen all of it. Take one last look back at the building before you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start with the Indus Valley gallery while your energy is fresh.
Visit in the morning to avoid the strongest Karachi heat.
Check photography rules at the entrance before using your camera.
Combine your visit with nearby Frere Hall the same day.
Keep your voice low inside the Quran manuscript gallery.
