The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the stone under your feet. It is pale, almost white, and smooth from centuries of footsteps. Salt hangs in the air. Somewhere behind you, small boats knock against a harbour wall. Ahead, a great limestone church rises from an open square at the very edge of the Adriatic Sea, with nothing between it and the water but light.

First, a quick note on where you are. This cathedral does not stand in Andria itself. It stands in Trani, a coastal town a short drive from Andria, in the same Apulian province that joins Barletta, Andria and Trani together. Many visitors come here from Andria for the day. If you have made that journey, you have done what travellers in this corner of southern Italy have done for a very long time.

The building is dedicated to Saint Nicholas the Pilgrim. He is not the famous Saint Nicholas of Bari, just down the coast. This Nicholas was a young Greek wanderer who arrived in Trani at the end of the eleventh century. Accounts say he walked the streets carrying a cross and repeating one short Greek prayer, Kyrie eleison, which means Lord, have mercy. Many people thought he was mad. He died here young, and soon after, the town claimed him as its saint.

That sainthood gave Trani a reason to build. Work on a new cathedral began around the end of the eleventh century, and it continued for generations. The builders did not clear the ground first. They built on top of an older church, and that older church sits on something older still. So the cathedral you see is really a stack of sacred spaces, one placed above another, like pages pressed into a book.

Look at the colour of the walls. This is local limestone, often called Trani stone, quarried in this region and still exported today. In the morning it looks almost silver. In the late afternoon it turns warm, close to honey. Because the cathedral stands alone on its square, with the sea behind it, the stone takes the full weather of the coast. Wind, salt and sun have softened its edges over many centuries.

The style is called Apulian Romanesque. Romanesque is the solid, round-arched way of building that spread across Europe before the pointed arches of the Gothic age. Here in Apulia, it took on its own character. It mixed Norman ideas from the north with Byzantine and Arab influences that arrived by sea. The result is tall, clean and strong, with very little decoration on the large surfaces, so the small carved details stand out sharply.

Now turn your eyes to the bell tower. It rises at one side of the church, and at its base there is a tall open arch. You can actually look through it, and on some days people walk beneath it. Here is a detail many visitors miss. In the twentieth century, the tower was taken down stone by stone because of structural problems. It was then rebuilt in the same form. What looks medieval is medieval stone, carefully put back together.

The main entrance once held a remarkable pair of bronze doors. They were made in the late twelfth century by a local artist known as Barisano da Trani. Their panels show saints, scenes from the life of Christ, and small figures of archers and warriors. Over time, sea air damaged the metal. The original doors have been restored and are now kept indoors, away from the salt. So look for them inside, not only at the entrance.

Above the doorway, follow the stone frame of the portal. You will see carved animals, plants and strange creatures. Medieval sculptors in this region loved these details. Higher up, look for the round rose window and the narrow arched windows around it. They are small compared to the size of the walls. This was deliberate. Thick walls with small openings kept the building strong and kept the interior cool under the southern sun.

Step inside, and the first thing you may notice is how bare it feels. In later centuries, the cathedral was covered with heavy decoration, as many Italian churches were. During restoration work in the twentieth century, much of that was removed to show the medieval structure again. Today you see tall stone columns, plain walls and a soft, even light. The quiet can feel surprising after the brightness of the square outside.

The second surprise lies underground. Below the main church is a crypt dedicated to Saint Nicholas the Pilgrim, with a forest of slender columns holding low vaults. Below or beside that are the remains of the older church of Santa Maria della Scala. Deeper still, there is an ancient burial chamber linked to Saint Leucius, an early bishop of the region. Walking down feels like moving backwards through the history of this town, one level at a time.

When you come back outside, walk around to the back of the building. The rounded end of the church, called the apse, faces the water. From the sea wall, you can see the cathedral almost as sailors once saw it, a pale shape on the shoreline, guiding boats home. Not far away stands the castle built under the emperor Frederick the Second, the medieval ruler who left his mark across Apulia, including the famous Castel del Monte near Andria.

Before you leave, stand in the middle of the square once more. Notice how the cathedral has no buildings pressed against it. The sky frames it on every side. Listen to the gulls, the voices of families crossing the stone, the slow sound of the sea. Then, as you walk back into the town, look for that same pale limestone in doorways, steps and walls. The cathedral is the largest piece of it, but Trani is made of the same stone.

Before you go

Practical tips

Allow extra travel time from Andria to coastal Trani.
Visit in late afternoon for warm light on the stone.
Ask at the entrance whether the crypt needs a separate ticket.
Cover shoulders and knees before entering the cathedral.
Walk the sea wall behind the apse for the best view.
