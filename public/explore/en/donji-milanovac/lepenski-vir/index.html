The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the wide modern roof, the air is cool and still, and the Danube runs just beyond the glass. On the floor below you lie pale outlines of stone, low and quiet, laid out in shapes like opened fans. There are no walls here, and no towers. There is only the plan of a village, pressed into the ground, facing the water. It is one of the oldest planned settlements in Europe.

You are standing in the Iron Gates, the long gorge where the Danube squeezes between the Carpathian and Balkan mountains. Serbia is under your feet. Romania is on the far bank. The name of the site comes from the river itself. In Serbian, a vir is a whirlpool, and this stretch of water was once known for its eddies and fast currents. The people who lived here chose that moving water as the centre of their world.

So who were they? They were hunters, foragers and, above all, fishermen. They lived here during the Mesolithic, the Middle Stone Age, and into the first contact with farming in the Early Neolithic. That puts their main village roughly eight thousand years ago. This is long before writing, before metal tools, and thousands of years before the pyramids of Egypt. Yet these people built with a clear plan, and they repeated it again and again.

Look closely at one of the house floors. Each one has the same shape — a trapezoid, wide at the front facing the river, narrow at the back facing the hill. The floors were made from a hard limestone plaster, smoothed and fired to a reddish tone. The houses were set in rows on a terrace above the water. Some researchers have suggested that the shape echoes the outline of the mountain on the opposite bank. That is an idea, not a certainty, but stand here and the comparison is easy to see.

In the middle of many floors you will find a long hearth, framed with upright stones. Fire sat at the heart of each home. Around it, the builders placed carved boulders and, sometimes, small altars. And here is something many visitors miss. Some people were buried beneath these floors. The living cooked, slept and worked directly above their dead. Home and memory shared the same few square metres.

The discovery itself is a twentieth century story. In the 1960s, Yugoslavia and Romania planned a large hydroelectric dam on the Danube, the Iron Gates project. Archaeologists surveyed the banks before the river rose. On this terrace they found something no one expected. The excavations were led by the Serbian archaeologist Dragoslav Srejović, and through the second half of the 1960s his team uncovered layer after layer of settlement.

The most famous finds are the sculptures. They are carved from round river boulders of sandstone, and many show faces with wide, staring eyes and a downturned mouth, almost like a fish. Archaeologists often call them fish-human figures. One of the best known has been nicknamed the Foremother, or the Ancestress. Others carry wavy, flowing lines like water or scales. Nobody knows exactly what they meant. They may have been ancestors, spirits of the river, or something we no longer have words for.

Why fish? The answer is in front of you. The Danube here was full of large river fish, and the bones found at the site show that fishing fed this community for generations. The whirlpools that made the water dangerous also gathered fish in rich numbers. For people who lived on this terrace, the river was food, road, danger and probably faith, all at once. The carved faces may be the closest we come to hearing their view of it.

Now for the second surprise. The village you are looking at is not in its original position. When the dam was completed, the water level in the gorge rose sharply. The original terrace would have disappeared underwater. So in the early 1970s the site was cut out, lifted and rebuilt higher up the slope, roughly thirty metres above its first location. The floors, hearths and stones were carefully moved and set down again in the same arrangement.

Think about what that means as you look down at the river. Somewhere below the surface lies the ground where these people actually stood. The water you see today is calmer and wider than the river they knew. The famous whirlpool is gone, flattened by the reservoir. What survives is the plan of their village, carried uphill so it would not be lost.

Today a modern protective structure covers the settlement, with walkways that let you look down onto the floors from above. Next to it, the visitor centre displays copies and original pieces, along with the story of the excavation. Take your time with the sculptures. Their faces change as you move. From one angle they look calm. From another they look almost startled, as if they have just come up out of the water.

When you step back outside, look along the gorge. Steep wooded slopes drop straight into the river. Boats pass slowly on the Danube, and the Romanian hills fill the opposite horizon. This whole area is part of Đerdap National Park, protected for its forests, cliffs and wildlife as well as its archaeology. It is easy to see why people settled on this narrow shelf of land. Water, fish, shelter and stone were all within reach.

Before you leave, return for one last look at a single trapezoid floor. Picture the fire burning in its long hearth, the smell of smoke and river fish, the voices of a family, and the sound of the whirlpool below. Then look out at the quiet water of today. Eight thousand years separate you from them... but you are both looking at the same river, from almost the same hillside.
