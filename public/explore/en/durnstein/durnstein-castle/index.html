The guide

The story

Listen first. Below you, the Danube moves almost without sound, wide and grey-green, sliding past terraces of vines. Up here, the wind carries a little dust and the dry smell of warm rock. Under your shoes the path is uneven: broken stone, roots, and steps worn smooth by many visitors. Around you rise the ruined walls of Dürnstein Castle. They have no roof and no windows, and the sky shows through the empty arches above the river.

The ruin sits on a rocky ridge high above the small town of Dürnstein. The town lies in the Wachau, a stretch of the Danube valley in Lower Austria. The Wachau is known for its vineyards, its apricot orchards and its old river villages. In 2000, UNESCO listed the valley as a World Heritage Site because of its cultural landscape. The castle is one of the clearest reasons why. From almost any point on the river, your eye travels up to these broken walls.

The castle dates from the twelfth century. It was built by the Kuenringer, a powerful noble family who served the dukes of Austria and controlled much of this valley. The choice of site was practical. A fortress on this ridge could watch every boat on the Danube. The river was a major trade route between east and west. Whoever held the heights could protect that traffic, or tax it. Look down at the bend in the river, and you can see the same view the guards had.

Now the story that brings most people up this hill. In late 1192, Richard the Lionheart, King of England, was returning from the Third Crusade. The Crusades were a series of medieval wars for control of the Holy Land. Richard travelled through Austria in disguise, because he had made enemies there. Near Vienna, he was recognised and arrested. The man who ordered his capture was Duke Leopold the Fifth of Austria.

Why did Leopold hate him? During the crusade, at the siege of the city of Acre, the two men quarrelled. According to the chroniclers, Richard insulted the duke, and Leopold's banner was thrown down. A king and a duke do not forget such things easily. Leopold sent his prisoner here, to Dürnstein, under the guard of the Kuenringer. For a period of months, one of the most famous kings in Europe was held somewhere inside these walls.

Later, Leopold handed Richard over to the Holy Roman Emperor, Henry the Sixth. The emperor demanded an enormous ransom. England raised it with heavy taxes, and Richard went free in 1194. Here is a detail that many visitors miss. Historians connect part of that ransom money to building projects in Austria. These include new city walls for Vienna and the founding of the town of Wiener Neustadt. In a way, an English king helped pay for Austrian stone.

There is also a legend, and it is important to call it a legend. Locals and old ballads tell of Blondel, a loyal minstrel who searched for his lost king. According to the tale, Blondel went from castle to castle singing a song that only he and Richard knew. Below one tower, a voice answered him with the next verse. That tower, people say, was here at Dürnstein. No reliable record supports the story. But it is a beautiful one, and it has followed this ruin for centuries.

The castle did not fall in Richard's time. It stood for several more centuries and passed through different owners. In the seventeenth century, during the Thirty Years' War, Swedish troops came into this region. The Thirty Years' War was a long and destructive conflict that divided much of central Europe. The castle was badly damaged in that period, and it was never rebuilt. After that, people from the area took stone from it for other uses. Slowly, it became the ruin you see today.

Take a moment to look at the walls themselves. The builders used local rock, set in rough, irregular layers with thick mortar. In places, the walls follow the shape of the cliff, as if they grew out of it. You can see where doorways once stood, and the openings where wooden beams held floors. Nothing here is decorated. This was a working fortress, built for defence. Its beauty today comes from the setting and the age of the stone, not from ornament.

Walk to the edge, carefully, and look down at the town. One building stands out: a church tower painted pale blue and white. It belongs to Dürnstein Abbey, a former monastery rebuilt in the Baroque style. Baroque is a rich and dramatic style of art and architecture from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The tower is perhaps the best-known landmark of the whole Wachau. From up here, it looks almost like a toy, with red roofs and narrow lanes gathered around it.

Notice the shape of the land around you. The hills drop to the river in steep steps, and the steps are vineyards held up by dry stone walls. Generations of winemakers built and repaired these terraces by hand. The Wachau is known for white wines, especially Grüner Veltliner and Riesling. In spring, the apricot trees flower across the valley. In late summer, the fruit is sold in the town below, fresh or as jam and schnapps. This castle once guarded that wealth.

The climb itself is part of the visit. The path rises from the town through rock and trees, with several viewpoints on the way. It is steep in places, so take your time. Many people stop halfway, out of breath, and find the view already worth the effort. At the top, there are rocks to sit on and space to rest. Bring water on warm days, and wear shoes with good grip. After rain, the smooth stone can be slippery.

Stay a little longer before you leave. Watch a boat pass slowly on the river, and follow its wake around the bend. Listen to the wind in the empty windows and the voices drifting up from the lanes below. Somewhere in these walls, a captured king once waited, and looked out at this same water. When you start down the path, turn once more toward the ridge. The ruin will stay above you, pale against the sky, as it has for eight hundred years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for the rocky climb.
Bring water on warm days; the path is steep.
Climb early morning to avoid crowds from river cruises.
Avoid the upper path after rain when stone gets slippery.
Taste local Grüner Veltliner in the town after descending.
