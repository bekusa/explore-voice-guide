The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves fold onto the sand with a soft hiss, then slide back over the grains. The sand itself is unusual. It is golden, coarse, and warm under your feet, and if you pick up a handful you will see tiny crystals catch the light. Behind you, the bush rises steeply in shades of green. Somewhere in the canopy, a bird calls with a sound like a bell. Welcome to Abel Tasman National Park, at the top of New Zealand's South Island.

That golden colour has a simple explanation. Much of the coastline here is made of granite, a hard, speckled rock that formed deep underground a very long time ago. Over many thousands of years, wind, rain and sea have broken it down. The pieces wash into the bays as this honey-coloured sand. So when you walk these beaches, you are walking on the slow ruins of mountains. Look at the headlands and you will see the same rock, rounded and weathered, often with pale streaks and cracks.

Here is the first surprise. This is New Zealand's smallest national park. People often expect the opposite, because the coastline feels so generous. But the park is compact, a strip of hills, forest and coast pressed between Tasman Bay and Golden Bay. Its size is part of its character. You can move from a quiet forest stream to an open beach in minutes, and many of the best places are reachable in a single day on foot or by boat.

Long before any European ship arrived, this coast was home to Māori. For centuries, people fished these waters, gathered shellfish, grew food in sheltered places and travelled the coast by canoe. Traces of that life remain across the area, including old settlement and garden sites. Many places here carry Māori names as well as English ones. The Māori name for the wider Golden Bay region is Mohua. It is worth remembering that the European story is only the most recent chapter.

That European chapter began in December 1642. The Dutch explorer Abel Tasman, sailing for the Dutch East India Company, anchored his ships in Golden Bay, just north of where you are. He was the first European known to reach New Zealand. The meeting went badly. Local Māori of the Ngāti Tūmatakōkiri people paddled out in canoes, and in the clash that followed, four of Tasman's crew were killed. Tasman sailed away without ever setting foot on land.

Tasman gave the bay a grim name — Murderers Bay. Later generations renamed it Golden Bay, after gold was found in the region in the nineteenth century. Today historians see that first encounter differently. The Māori response may have been a defence of their territory and their food grounds against unknown strangers. It is a reminder that the same event can carry two very different meanings, depending on which side of the water you stood.

Now the second surprise. The park was created in 1942, exactly three hundred years after Tasman's visit. The anniversary was deliberate. A key campaigner was Perrine Moncrieff, a naturalist and writer who lived in the Nelson area. She worked hard to protect this coast at a time when much of New Zealand's forest was being cleared. In the middle of a world war, the idea of setting land aside for nature and for the public was a quietly bold act.

The forest around you is not untouched. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, much of this land was logged, burned for farming, or quarried for granite. Some settlers even tried to grow crops on the thin soils, with little success. What you see now is largely regenerating bush. Kānuka and mānuka, two small native trees with fine leaves, came back first. Taller forest is slowly returning behind them. In a sense, the whole park is still recovering, and you are seeing it mid-recovery.

Through this landscape runs the Abel Tasman Coast Track, one of New Zealand's Great Walks — a small set of premier multi-day tracks managed by the Department of Conservation. The track follows the coast over headlands and down to beaches, crossing streams on swing bridges. Some walkers take several days, staying in huts or campsites. Others walk just one section and return by water taxi. The track is well formed, but the hills between bays are steeper than they look from the beach.

The sea controls this place more than any timetable. The tides here are large, and at some estuaries the track crosses open sand that is only safe near low tide. At high water, those same flats become wide, shining channels. Awaroa Inlet is the best-known example. Walkers plan their days around the tide charts, and it is part of the rhythm of the park. Wait an hour, and a whole landscape can appear or disappear in front of you.

Awaroa holds another story. In 2016, a private stretch of beach there came up for sale. Two New Zealanders started an online crowdfunding campaign to buy it for the public. Tens of thousands of people donated, and with help from other contributors, the beach was purchased and added to the park. For many New Zealanders it became a symbol of shared ownership. The sand under your feet in some places belongs, quite literally, to ordinary people who chipped in.

Out on the water, the park continues. Tonga Island Marine Reserve protects the sea along part of the coast, and New Zealand fur seals often rest on the rocks there. Sea kayaks are a popular way to explore, gliding past granite islands and into lagoons no walker can reach. Near the southern edge of the park sits Split Apple Rock, a round granite boulder cut cleanly in two. Its Māori name is Tokangawhā. The shape comes from natural weathering along a crack.

Before you move on, take a moment where you stand. Notice the colour of the water. In the shallows it is clear and pale green, then deeper blue further out. Listen again for the birds — perhaps the bell-like bellbird, or a tūī with its mix of clicks and notes. Feel the coarse sand shift underfoot. Every grain here was once part of a granite cliff. And as you walk away, the tide will keep moving, quietly redrawing the shore behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check tide times before crossing estuaries like Awaroa Inlet.
Combine a one-way walk with a water taxi return.
Bring sandfly repellent for beaches and campsites.
Book Great Walk huts and campsites well in advance.
Carry plenty of water for the steep climbs between bays.
