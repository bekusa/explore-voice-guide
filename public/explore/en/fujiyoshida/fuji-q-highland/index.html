The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere above you, steel wheels rattle along a track. Then comes a long, rising scream from dozens of voices at once, and it fades into the wind. Behind all of it, if the clouds allow, a pale cone fills the sky. You are standing at the foot of Mount Fuji, in one of the few places in Japan where people come to the sacred mountain to be thrown through the air.

Welcome to Fuji-Q Highland, in the city of Fujiyoshida, Yamanashi Prefecture. This amusement park sits on the northern side of Fuji, close to the Fuji Five Lakes area. Most visitors know the mountain from paintings and postcards. Here it becomes the backdrop to loops, drops, and spinning seats. That contrast is the heart of the place. On one side is quiet, snowy Fuji. On the other is the clatter of a lift hill and a crowd waiting for the next drop.

The park opened in the late 1960s under the name Fujikyu Highland. It belongs to the Fujikyu group, a company that began with railways and buses around the mountain. That history matters. Transport companies across Japan built leisure sites at the end of their lines to give people a reason to travel. The station right next to the gates still carries the park's name. Many visitors arrive by train and walk straight from the platform to the entrance.

Now look up at the long, tall coaster that runs along the edge of the park. This is Fujiyama, which opened in 1996. When it first ran, it was among the tallest and fastest roller coasters in the world, and the park still calls it the king of coasters. The ride is named after the mountain, and the view from the top of its first climb is the reason many people queue. For a few seconds, riders see Fuji almost face to face before the drop.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Fujiyama is not mainly about loops. It has no upside-down sections at all. Its thrill comes from height, speed, and long sweeping hills. That makes it an old-school design, built in the style of classic coasters but on a giant scale. Watch a train come down the first drop, and notice how long the fall lasts. Then notice how quickly the cars climb again for the next hill.

Fuji-Q has built its name on chasing records. Over the years the park has introduced rides that aimed to be the fastest, the steepest, or the strangest. One of these is Eejanaika, a so-called fourth-dimension coaster. On this kind of ride, the seats rotate on their own axis as the train moves. Riders flip forward and backward while the track twists around them. The name comes from a Japanese phrase that means something close to "isn't it great?" or "why not?" It is a playful, cheeky name for a very intense ride.

Then there is Takabisha, which opened in 2011. Its signature moment is a drop steeper than vertical. Riders climb straight up, pause at the top facing the sky, and then fall at an angle that tucks back underneath them. Before that moment, the train passes through a dark section inside a building. So the ride begins in shadow and ends in open air. If you stand near its tall hill, you can watch the train hang there for a breath before it goes over.

Not every attraction here is about speed. Fuji-Q is also known for a very long walk-through haunted attraction set in an abandoned hospital. Japanese horror has its own style. It often relies on silence, flickering light, and slow approaching figures rather than sudden noise. Many people treat this walk-through as a test of courage with their friends. Some groups walk out early. Locals joke that the screams from inside can be louder than the ones from the coasters.

For younger children, the park has gentler areas, including a zone themed on Thomas the Tank Engine. Here the rides are slower, the colours are brighter, and the music is softer. It is a useful reminder that this is a family place as well as a thrill park. Parents often split up, with one adult on a big coaster and the other in a calm area. Later they meet again near the central paths.

Now think about the setting. The park sits on high ground at the base of a volcano, so the weather changes fast. Morning can be clear and cold, with Fuji sharp against blue sky. By midday, clouds often wrap the summit. Wind is a real factor here. Strong gusts can stop the tall rides for safety, sometimes for hours. That is not bad luck. It is simply life at the foot of a mountain more than 3,700 metres high.

The ground under your feet also tells a story. Much of this region is built on old lava flows and volcanic soil from Fuji's past eruptions. Nearby, the Aokigahara forest grows on one of those flows, and the Fuji Five Lakes formed partly because lava blocked old valleys. So this amusement park is not on a random plain. It sits on land shaped by the same mountain that fills the view from the coaster tops.

Fujiyoshida itself has a much older role. For centuries, it was a starting point for pilgrims who climbed Fuji as a religious act. A large Shinto shrine in the city still marks the traditional start of one climbing route. So within a short distance, you have two kinds of journeys toward the mountain. One is slow, on foot, and spiritual. The other is fast and loud, and it lasts about two minutes. Both end with people staring at the same summit.

As you move through the park, pay attention to the small things. Notice how the queues often face the mountain, so people waiting can look at Fuji. Notice the mix of visitors, from school groups in matching uniforms to couples and international travellers. And notice the sounds. There is the grinding chain of a lift hill, a burst of screaming, a pause, and then laughter as a train returns to the station.

Before you walk on, stop somewhere with a clear line of sight to the mountain. Let the noise of the rides drop into the background. Look at the shape of Fuji above the steel tracks. It was here long before the park, and it will be here long after the newest coaster. For a moment, the two sit side by side, the ancient volcano and the modern thrill machine, both drawing people to this same patch of ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive by train at the station beside the park gates.
Come early on clear mornings for the best Fuji views.
Check the app or signs for wind-related ride closures.
Bring warm layers since mountain weather changes quickly.
Some attractions require a separate ticket or reservation.
