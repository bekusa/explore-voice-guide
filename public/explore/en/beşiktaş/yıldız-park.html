The guide

The story

The path beneath your feet is packed earth, softened by fallen plane tree leaves. Above you, the canopy filters the light into shifting green-gold patches, and somewhere in the branches, a hoopoe calls — a soft, rhythmic hoop-hoop-hoop that carries through the still air. This is Yıldız Park, eighty hectares of forested hillside rising above the Bosphorus, and it feels less like a city park than a fragment of wilderness that somehow survived Istanbul's relentless sprawl.

The land has been imperial property since the sixteenth century, when Ottoman sultans claimed this slope as hunting grounds. For three hundred years, it remained closed to ordinary people — a private forest where the court rode out to chase deer and boar. In the late eighteen-hundreds, Sultan Abdülhamid the Second transformed the grounds into a vast palace complex, building pavilions and kiosks scattered through the trees. He was a reclusive, cautious ruler, and he preferred this wooded retreat to the exposed grandeur of Dolmabahçe Palace down by the water. He lived here for thirty years, rarely leaving the grounds, governing an empire in decline from behind these high walls.

After the fall of the sultanate in nineteen twenty-two, the new republic opened the gates. Yıldız became a public park — one of the first in the young Turkish state. The pavilions were turned into museums and cafés, the hunting paths became walking trails, and Istanbulites began climbing the hill on weekends to escape the noise of the city below. Today, the park remains much as it was a century ago: steep, shaded, surprisingly quiet given that Beşiktaş and Ortaköy lie just minutes away.

The terrain is hilly and uneven, which gives the park its character. You will not find flat lawns or symmetrical flowerbeds here. Instead, the paths wind upward through groves of oak, chestnut, linden, and plane trees, some of them easily two hundred years old. In spring, the understory fills with wildflowers — anemones, crocuses, violets — and in autumn, the leaf-fall is thick enough that your footsteps make no sound. The air smells of damp earth and resin, and on warm days, the scent of pine sap hangs heavy in the shade.

Scattered through the park are the remnants of Abdülhamid's building program. The most famous is the Şale Pavilion, a sprawling timber structure with carved eaves and latticed balconies, built to host visiting dignitaries. It sits near the park's upper edge, overlooking the Bosphorus, and you can tour its interior — rooms filled with Hereke carpets, crystal chandeliers, and mother-of-pearl inlay. But there are smaller pavilions too, some converted into teahouses, others left empty and gently decaying. You will pass them as you walk: pale stucco walls behind iron gates, shutters half-open, ivy climbing the columns.

The park attracts a particular crowd. You will see families picnicking under the trees, elderly men playing backgammon on stone benches, students reading in the shade, and runners tackling the steep switchbacks. There are fewer tourists here than in Gülhane or Emirgan — Yıldız is too far from the historic peninsula, too steep, too unmanicured. That is part of its appeal. It feels like a place Istanbulites come to be left alone, to sit with a book or a thermos of tea and let the city noise fade into the background hum of leaves and birdsong.

The views from the upper trails are worth the climb. On clear days, you can see across the Bosphorus to the Asian shore, the water a flat blue-grey stitched with the wakes of ferries and tankers. Below you, the red roofs of Beşiktaş cascade down toward the waterfront, and beyond them, the domes and minarets of the old city rise in silhouette. The light changes constantly — soft and diffuse in the morning, sharp and golden in late afternoon, turning the strait into a sheet of hammered bronze.

There is a small lake near the centre of the park, man-made and edged with willows. Ducks paddle in slow circles, and in summer, the surface is dotted with water lilies. A wooden footbridge crosses one end, and locals feed the fish from its railings — carp, mostly, fat and sluggish, rising to the surface in slow, gulping masses. It is a quiet spot, sheltered from the wind, and the benches around the lake are often occupied by people who have brought bread and fruit and settled in for the afternoon.

Yıldız is not a park you visit for monuments or spectacle. It is a park you visit to walk slowly, to notice the texture of bark and the play of light through leaves, to sit on a bench and listen to the hoopoes and the distant clatter of the city below. When you leave, descending back toward the traffic and the crowds, the forest closes behind you as if it were never disturbed — a green pocket of stillness in a city that rarely stops moving.

Practical tips

Climb to the upper trails for Bosphorus views
