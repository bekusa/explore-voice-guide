The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through the beech trees with a dry, steady hiss. Somewhere close, water laps against stones dark with kelp. The air smells of wet moss, peat, and salt. Underfoot, the trail is soft and springy, layered with fallen leaves that never quite dry. This is the edge of the inhabited world, and it does not feel empty. It feels busy and alive, only quieter than most places you have ever stood.

You are in Tierra del Fuego National Park, a short drive west of Ushuaia, on Argentina's side of the great island at the bottom of South America. The park was created in 1960. It protects a strip of land where the Andes come down to meet the sea. Mountains, lakes, peat bogs, forest and coastline sit here side by side. To the west, the park runs right up to the border with Chile. To the south, the waters of the Beagle Channel mark its edge.

The name of the whole region comes from fire. When Ferdinand Magellan's ships sailed through these waters in 1520, the crews saw smoke and flames along the shore. These were the fires of the people who lived here. Europeans called it the land of smoke, and later the land of fire. The name stuck. Those fires were not a sign of danger. They were warmth, cooking, and signals between families in a cold, wet climate.

Those families belonged to the Yámana people, also called the Yagán. For thousands of years they lived along these coasts. They travelled mostly by canoe, moving between bays to fish and gather shellfish. Here is a detail many visitors walk past without noticing. Along parts of the shore, you can see low grassy mounds. Some of these are shell middens, which are heaps of discarded mussel and limpet shells left by generations of meals. They are archaeological sites, so please look, but leave them untouched.

Now turn your attention to the trees. Almost all the forest here is southern beech. Three kinds grow in the park. Lenga is the tall one that turns gold and red in autumn. Ñire is smaller and often grows in wetter, colder ground. Coihue keeps its leaves all year, so it stays green when the others go bare. Notice how many trunks lean the same way. Years of strong westerly wind have shaped them, like flags that have frozen in place.

Look closely at the branches, too. You may see round, orange or pale yellow growths stuck to the bark. These are a fungus that grows on the beech. Locals call it pan de indio, which means Indian bread. It is said the Yámana ate it. You may also see pale green threads hanging from the branches. That is a lichen, and it grows best where the air is clean and damp.

Here comes the first surprise. Some of the strangest landscapes in this park were made by an animal that does not belong here. In 1946, a small group of North American beavers was brought from Canada. The plan was to start a fur industry. The fur business failed, but the beavers stayed. With no natural predators, they spread across the island. Today you can see their dams, their ponds, and whole stands of grey, drowned trees standing in still water.

Those drowned forests look peaceful, even beautiful. But they are a serious problem. The southern beech did not evolve alongside beavers, and it does not grow back the way North American trees do. When you see a quiet pond with a lodge of sticks in the middle, you are looking at an ecological puzzle. Argentina and Chile have worked together on projects to control the beaver population and restore damaged areas.

As you walk, keep your eyes on the shoreline and the forest edge. Upland geese graze in open meadows. The males are white, and the females are brown with barred feathers. On rocky coast you may see kelp geese, which follow the same pattern. In the woods, listen for a loud, steady knocking. That could be the Magellanic woodpecker, a large black bird. The male has a bright red head. If you hear it, stand still and wait.

One of the best-known spots in the park is Lapataia Bay. Here, the long road that runs the length of Argentina, National Route 3, comes to an end. A wooden sign marks the spot. It lists the distance to Buenos Aires, and the much greater distance to Alaska. Many travellers think of this as the southern end of the Pan-American Highway. People queue for photographs here. It is worth taking a minute to look beyond the sign, out to the calm water and the mountains behind it.

Here is the second surprise. Some visitors arrive in the park by train. The Southern Fuegian Railway, often called the End of the World Train, runs a short route into the park. Its history is darker than the cheerful carriages suggest. In the early twentieth century, a railway here carried prisoners from the Ushuaia jail into the forest to cut timber. Some of the stumps you see along the line are left from that work.

The Beagle Channel is named after the British ship HMS Beagle. That ship carried the young Charles Darwin through these waters in the 1830s. Darwin wrote about the cold, the rain, and the people he met here. Today the channel looks very still on a calm day. Then the weather turns, the wind picks up, and the water goes grey and choppy in minutes. Be ready for four seasons in a single afternoon.

Before you leave, find a quiet place on a trail or by the water. Take a slow breath. Notice how the light changes as clouds move fast over the peaks. Notice the cool damp on your skin and the softness of the ground. Behind you are the road, the town, and the rest of the continent. In front of you, there is water, forest, and very little else. As you walk away, listen once more for the wind in the beech trees. It will be there long after you go.

Before you go

Practical tips

Pack a waterproof layer even on sunny mornings.
Wear boots that can handle muddy, spongy trails.
Bring binoculars for geese and woodpeckers.
Arrive early to photograph the Lapataia sign quietly.
Stay on marked trails to protect fragile peat bogs.
