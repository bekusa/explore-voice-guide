The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the wind moves across bare limestone, and the city noise of Mosul is gone. The rock under your feet is pale and dry, warm in the sun and cool in the shade. Below you, the Nineveh Plains spread out in long bands of field and village. Above you, the stone walls of a monastery hold onto the side of a mountain. Christians have prayed on this slope for about sixteen hundred years. Let's walk in slowly.

This is Mor Mattai Monastery, also called Deir Mar Mattai. It sits on Mount Alfaf, a ridge rising out of the plains northeast of Mosul. The word Mor is Syriac for saint or lord. Syriac is a form of Aramaic, the language family spoken across this region in the time of Jesus. The monastery belongs to the Syriac Orthodox Church. It is widely counted among the oldest Christian monasteries still in use anywhere in the world.

The story of the founding comes to us through church tradition. In the fourth century, a hermit named Mattai is said to have fled persecution in the Roman world. Many accounts link his flight to the reign of the emperor Julian, who tried to turn the empire back to the old gods. Mattai crossed into Persian territory and settled on this mountain. He lived in a cave, prayed, and gathered followers. The community that grew around him became this monastery.

There is a famous legend attached to these first years, and locals still tell it with feeling. According to the tale, a local king had a son named Behnam and a daughter named Sarah. Sarah fell gravely ill, and the hermit on the mountain healed her. Brother and sister then became Christians. Their angry father had them both killed. Later, the story says, the grieving king repented and helped to build this monastery. Historians treat this as legend, not record. But it shaped the faith of the whole region.

Now look up at the cliff above the buildings. You may see small dark openings in the rock. These are caves, and monks once used them as cells for prayer and solitude. In the early centuries, monastic life here meant silence, fasting, and long hours alone with the mountain. Some of the hermits lived high on the slope, far from the main community. When you see how steep the rock is, you can understand why they chose it. The place is hard to reach, and that was the point.

Here is something many visitors miss. Much of what you see today is not ancient stone. The monastery has been attacked, damaged, and rebuilt many times over the centuries. Large parts of the present buildings date from rebuilding in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. So the walls are younger than the community inside them. What has lasted is not the masonry but the continuity. Monks have kept returning to this ridge, again and again, for well over a millennium.

Step inside the church, and let your eyes adjust to the lower light. The air is cooler here, and it often carries the faint smell of incense and candle wax. Tradition holds that the remains of Saint Mattai rest within the monastery. Pilgrims come to pray at his tomb, and many light candles or touch the stone as they pass. Watch how local visitors move through this space. Their gestures are quiet and practised, passed down through many generations of families from the plains below.

A second surprise rests here too. The great medieval scholar Bar Hebraeus is buried at this monastery. He lived in the thirteenth century and was a senior bishop of the Syriac Orthodox Church. He wrote on history, philosophy, medicine, grammar, and theology, in both Syriac and Arabic. His histories remain important sources for the Middle East in the age of the Mongols. Few people outside the region know his name. Yet in his time, he was one of the most learned men in Asia.

That love of learning is part of this monastery's identity. For centuries, Mor Mattai kept a library of Syriac manuscripts. Monks copied texts by hand, preserving prayers, chronicles, and commentaries. Some of these manuscripts were lost in raids and wars, and others were scattered to collections elsewhere. Still, the monastery is remembered as a centre of Syriac scholarship. When you hear the liturgy sung here, you are hearing a language that has been prayed in this region for close to two thousand years.

The recent past is part of the story as well. In 2014, the Islamic State group seized Mosul and much of the Nineveh Plains. Christian towns below this mountain were emptied, and many families fled. The front line came close to the monastery, but it was not captured. The years that followed were very hard for the Christians of this region. Today, people have slowly returned to some of the villages you can see. The monastery remains a place of prayer and a symbol of survival.

Walk out to the terrace and face the plains. On a clear day, the view runs a long way toward the south and west. You are looking across land that has seen Assyrian kings, Persian rulers, Arab caliphs, Mongol armies, and Ottoman governors. Those empires came and went. The villages below have been home to Assyrian and Syriac Christians, as well as Kurds, Arabs, Yazidis, and others. From up here, the distances look gentle, but the history is dense.

Notice the sounds around you as you stand on the terrace. You may hear a bell, a door, or a monk crossing the courtyard. On feast days, the monastery fills with families, picnics, and singing, and the mood becomes warm and noisy. On ordinary days, it is very quiet. Either way, this is a working religious house, not a museum. Speak softly in the church, dress modestly, and ask before photographing people or the interior of the sanctuary.

Before you leave, take one more look at the cliff and the caves above you. Picture a single man with almost nothing, choosing this hard rock as his home. Everything around you grew from that choice. As you walk back down toward the plains, feel the slope under your feet and the wind on your face. Turn around once more at the bend in the road. The walls will look small against the mountain... and still they hold on.
