The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere ahead, a chain clicks steadily as a coaster train climbs. Then comes a rush of wheels, and a wave of screams that rises and fades. The air smells of fried dough, sun cream, and a faint salt breeze from the Adriatic, only a few kilometres to the east. Underfoot, the paths are warm. This is Mirabilandia, an amusement park in flat green country just south of Ravenna, and it sounds nothing like the quiet city it belongs to.

That contrast is worth holding on to. Ravenna is famous for its early Christian and Byzantine monuments. Byzantine means the eastern Roman Empire, which ruled from Constantinople. Inside the city's churches, gold mosaics glow in dim light, and people speak in whispers. Out here, the colours are painted plastic and steel, and nobody whispers at all. Both places share one idea, though. They were built to make ordinary people look up and feel a little amazed.

The park sits near the village of Savio, between Ravenna and the seaside towns of Cervia and Milano Marittima. Look at the land around you. It is low and level, shaped over centuries by rivers, marshes, and drainage work. Not far away stands the Pineta di Classe, an old pine forest near the coast. That flatness matters here. With no hills to hide them, the tallest rides become landmarks you can see from the roads around the park.

Mirabilandia opened in the early 1990s. Over the following decades, it grew into one of the largest amusement parks in Italy. It changed owners along the way. Today it belongs to Parques Reunidos, a Spanish company that runs leisure parks in several countries. Each new owner added rides, shows, and themed areas. So the park you walk through is not one single design. It is a collection of additions, made over more than thirty years.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Mirabilandia is part of a long local tradition. The Romagna coast, the stretch of shoreline around Rimini, Riccione, and Cervia, has welcomed holiday crowds for well over a century. Beach clubs, dance halls, and family fairs grew up along the sand. A large theme park is simply the modern version of that seaside fun. Many families visit the park and the beach in the same holiday.

Now look up toward the skyline of the park. The big roller coasters are the stars here, and each one gives a different kind of thrill. Some trains hang below the track, so your feet swing free in the air. Others launch forward at speed from a standing start, with no slow climb at all. Watch the riders' faces as a train returns to the station. The best part of any coaster is often that shaky laugh at the end.

One of the best-known rides is iSpeed, a launched coaster. Instead of a chain lift, it pushes the train forward with a sudden, powerful start. You can often hear it before you see it. Listen for a sharp rising hum, then a roar as the train races out of the station. Then comes a short silence, high above the ground. Riders who wait in line often feel more nervous during that silence than during the ride.

Another favourite is Katun, an inverted coaster. On this kind of ride, the track runs above you, and your seat hangs beneath it. Your legs dangle, and the ground passes directly under your shoes. The ride takes its name from the Maya calendar, the counting system of the ancient Maya people of Central America. That theme appears across this part of the park, in carved stone shapes and jungle colours.

Here is a second surprise. Water plays a big part in the park's design. Some rides end with a deep splash, and the people watching from the path often get wetter than the riders. Look for the crowds standing at the edge of these rides on hot days. They are waiting for the spray on purpose. In the Adriatic summer, a soaking is a gift, and many people return to stand there again.

The giant Ferris wheel, the Eurowheel, offers a gentler kind of view. Its slow turn lifts you far above the park. From the top, on a clear day, you can see the pattern of the land. Fields and canals stretch toward the coast, and pine trees mark the shoreline. Look toward the northwest, too. Ravenna lies in that direction, and on a clear evening its outline can sometimes appear on the horizon.

Not every corner here is built for big thrills. There are areas for small children, with slow trains, small cars, and rides at a gentle speed. Live shows run through the day, often with stunts, music, and characters. For many families, these quieter places are where the best memories form. A grandparent and a small child on a little carousel can share a moment as large as any coaster ride.

As the afternoon goes on, notice how the place changes. In the morning, the paths are fresh and fast, and people hurry toward their favourite rides. By late afternoon, the pace slows down. People sit on benches with ice cream, and children fall asleep on their parents' shoulders. The light turns golden over the flat land, and the steel tracks shine. The screams continue, but they now seem farther away.

Before you leave, stop for one moment. Look at the tallest track against the sky, and listen to the chain lift clicking again. Somewhere, a new group of riders is climbing toward that first drop. Then think of Ravenna, only a short drive away, where gold mosaics have shone for fifteen centuries. As you walk back toward the gate, notice the salt in the breeze. That same sea air reaches both places, the old city and this loud, happy park.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early and head straight to the biggest coasters.
Bring a change of clothes for the splash rides.
Ride the Eurowheel near sunset for golden light.
Pair your visit with Ravenna's mosaics on another day.
Carry sun protection, as shade can be limited.
