The guide

The story

Step inside, and the sound of the street drops away almost at once. The air is cool and still. Your footsteps soften on the floor, and the light dims to protect what lies ahead. Somewhere in these rooms is a small bronze figure that fits in the palm of a hand. Someone carried it across a cold sea more than a thousand years ago. That is where this story begins.

You are standing in the National Museum of Iceland, on Suðurgata, a short walk from the University of Iceland and the quiet water of the Tjörnin pond. The collection began in 1863 as a modest gathering of antiquities. At first it was little more than a few objects that people feared would be lost or sold abroad. Over the next century and a half, it grew into the main record of how this island became a nation.

The building itself carries a piece of that story. Iceland became a fully independent republic in 1944, after a long period under Danish rule. Building a proper home for the national collection was seen as part of that new beginning. The museum moved into this building around 1950. So the walls around you were raised by a young country that wanted to show itself, and the world, where it came from.

The heart of the museum is the permanent exhibition, called The Making of a Nation. It runs in roughly chronological order. It starts with the first settlers in the ninth century and ends close to the present day. The best way to experience it is simply to follow the path and let the centuries pass in sequence. Each room covers a new era, and the objects become more familiar as you move forward.

Those first settlers came mainly from Norway. But the story is more mixed than the old sagas suggest. Many settlers brought people from Ireland and the Scottish islands with them, often as enslaved workers or as wives. Modern genetic studies support this Gaelic thread in the population. As you look at the early finds, keep that in mind. This was never one single people arriving on an empty island. It was a mix of cultures, and they had to learn to survive together.

Look closely at the grave goods from the pagan period. You will see swords, brooches, beads, and tools. People were buried with the things they would need, or the things that showed their status. The oval brooches worn by women are especially striking. They pinned dresses at the shoulders and were often decorated with fine patterns. These are not treasures made for display. They were everyday objects, and they wore down with use.

Now, that small bronze figure. It was found in the north of Iceland, near Akureyri. It shows a seated man holding something in both hands, and it is often identified as the god Thor. Some scholars think the object he holds is his hammer. Others think it may be something else, perhaps a Christian cross. Nobody knows for certain. That uncertainty is part of what makes it so interesting. It may come from a time when the old gods and the new faith existed side by side.

That shift is one of the great turning points here. Around the year 1000, Iceland adopted Christianity at the Althing, the national assembly that met in the open air. According to the saga tradition, the decision was made to avoid civil war, and some pagan practices were quietly allowed for a while. As you walk on, the objects change. Crosses, chalices, and church furnishings slowly replace the swords and brooches of the earlier rooms.

One of the most remarkable pieces in the museum is a carved wooden church door from Valþjófsstaður, in the east of the country. It dates to around 1200. The carving shows a knight on horseback who rescues a lion from a dragon. In the final scene, the grateful lion follows the knight, and then lies on his grave. Stand in front of it for a minute. Wood rarely survives this long. Here you can still follow the story frame by frame.

The middle rooms cover the long centuries that followed, and they are often the most moving. Iceland was poor and remote. It was shaped by harsh winters, volcanic eruptions, famine, and foreign trade monopolies. Look at the carved household objects. You will see bed boards, boxes, and spoons for eating porridge, often cut with patterns and lines of verse. People who had very little still made beautiful things for their own homes. The craft was a way of holding on.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look for the drinking horns, carved with tiny, crowded scenes. Some show biblical stories. Others show ornament so dense that you need to lean in close to follow it. These horns were prized possessions. Their carvers worked through the dark months, by the light of oil lamps and fires. Every line you see was cut by hand, slowly, over many evenings.

As the path moves into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the pace speeds up. You will find national costumes, fishing gear, and the tools of a society that moved from turf farmhouses to concrete towns within a few generations. The independence movement appears here too, with its symbols and its leaders. The final rooms bring you into modern Iceland, with ordinary objects that some older visitors may remember from their own childhoods.

The museum also hosts temporary exhibitions, often with a strong focus on photography. Iceland has a deep archive of images that show farms, harbours, and faces from the past century and more. If one of these shows is on during your visit, give it time. A single photograph of a fishing village can explain the land as clearly as any sword or brooch.

When you step back outside, take a moment on the steps. Notice the wind, the wide pale sky, and the low roofs of the neighbourhood around you. Everything you just saw was made by people who lived under that same sky. Their lives depended on boats and on the weather. Their stories survived the long winters. As you walk toward the pond or back into town, you carry a little of that long story with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Follow the permanent exhibition in order for the clearest story.
Allow at least two hours for the main exhibition.
Check the temporary photography exhibitions on the ground floor.
Combine your visit with a walk around nearby Tjörnin pond.
Bring a light layer, because the galleries stay cool.
