The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind comes off the harbour and moves up the hill, carrying the smell of salt and cold stone. Under your feet the pavement climbs gently, and above the low rooftops a pale grey tower rises. It has no spire. Its top is cut flat, as if someone stopped building and decided it was finished. That flat-topped tower is the first thing to notice here. It is also the first clue to the story of this church.

You are standing at Landakotskirkja, the Catholic Cathedral of Christ the King. It sits on Landakot hill, in the older western part of Reykjavík. The church was consecrated in 1929. For a time it was the largest church in Iceland. That may surprise you. Most of the country is Lutheran, and has been for nearly five hundred years. So why does a Catholic cathedral stand on one of the city's highest points?

To understand that, go back to the sixteenth century. Iceland was Catholic for centuries after its conversion around the year 1000. Then the Protestant Reformation arrived from Denmark, which ruled Iceland at the time. The last Catholic bishop of Iceland, Jón Arason, resisted the change. In 1550 he was executed, together with two of his sons. After that, the Catholic Church almost disappeared from the island for about three hundred years.

Catholic priests began to return in the nineteenth century. They came in small numbers, mostly from mainland Europe. They settled here, on this quiet hill above the harbour. The name Landakot was already attached to the land. Slowly, a small community grew around it. There was a modest wooden chapel first. Nuns and priests opened a school. Later, sisters from a religious order founded a hospital nearby. The hill became the centre of Catholic life in Iceland.

By the 1920s the community wanted a proper cathedral. The design went to Guðjón Samúelsson, who was the State Architect of Iceland. His name may sound familiar. He also designed Hallgrímskirkja, the tall Lutheran church you can see across the city, on the eastern hill. Here is a detail many visitors miss. The same architect shaped both of Reykjavík's great hilltop churches. One is Catholic, one is Lutheran. They face each other across the town.

Look at the style of the building. It is Neo-Gothic. That means it borrows the forms of medieval Gothic churches — pointed arches, tall narrow windows, a strong vertical line. But the material is not carved stone. It is concrete. In Iceland, good building timber was scarce and stone was hard to work. Concrete became the material of the twentieth century here. So this cathedral uses a medieval language, written in a modern material.

Now return to that flat tower. Many European cathedrals end in a pointed spire. This one does not. The square, level top gives the church a calm, solid outline against the sky. Some people think it looks unfinished. Others find it suits the landscape — the flat-topped mountains, the strong wind, the plain northern light. Stand back on the street and compare it with the houses around it. The tower seems to belong to the island, not to Paris or Cologne.

Walk closer and look at the surface of the walls. The concrete has a soft grey tone. In rain it turns darker, almost the colour of wet basalt. In clear weather it looks pale, nearly white. The windows are set deep into the walls. The lines are clean and simple. There is very little carved decoration on the outside. The building relies on shape and proportion rather than ornament.

If the doors are open, step inside. The change is immediate. Outside there is wind and traffic. Inside there is quiet, and the air feels still. The pointed arches continue overhead, leading your eye forward to the altar. Light comes in through the tall windows and falls in long strips across the floor. Take a moment before you look at any single detail. Simply notice how the space slows you down.

The people who pray here are an interesting part of the story. The Catholic community in Iceland is small, but it has grown in recent decades. Many members came from abroad — from Poland, the Philippines, and many other countries. On a given day you may hear Mass in Icelandic, English or Polish. This is a cathedral, so it is the seat of the Catholic bishop of Reykjavík. His diocese covers the whole of Iceland. One church, on one hill, serves an entire country.

Here is a second surprise. Iceland received a papal visit in 1989, when Pope John Paul II travelled through the Nordic countries. For a small community whose church had nearly vanished after 1550, it was a remarkable moment. Think about that long arc. Four centuries ago, Catholic worship was forbidden here. Today a Catholic cathedral stands openly on a hill in the capital, and its bells are part of the sound of the neighbourhood.

When you leave the church, take time with the streets around it. This western district is one of the older parts of Reykjavík. Many houses are clad in corrugated iron, painted red, green, yellow or white. That metal cladding was a practical choice. It protected wooden houses from wind and rain. Gardens are small and neat. The school beside the church is still in use, so you may hear children's voices at certain times of day.

From the hill, walk down toward the old harbour or toward the small lake in the centre of town. Both are close. As you go, turn around once or twice. The flat grey tower will stay in view above the roofs. It works almost like a landmark for this side of the city. Sailors and residents have seen that outline for close to a century now.

Before you go, stand still for a moment. Feel the wind again on your face. Look up at the plain grey tower, the deep windows, the pointed doorway. This is a building made from concrete, faith and patience. It stands where a few returning priests once built a small wooden chapel. As you walk away down the hill, listen for the bells — and remember how long this hill waited to hear them.
