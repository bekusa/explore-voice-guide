The guide

The story

Start with your feet. The courtyard stones are dark and worn smooth, and they hold the warmth of the day long after the sun has moved on. Around you, pale columns stand against walls of black volcanic basalt. Pigeons lift off the rooftops in sudden bursts, and then the courtyard goes quiet again. Somewhere nearby, water runs from a fountain. You are standing inside the Great Mosque of Diyarbakır, known locally as Ulu Cami, the heart of the old walled city.

This mosque is often counted among the oldest in Anatolia, the great peninsula that makes up most of modern Turkey. Its main form dates to the late eleventh century, when the Great Seljuks controlled the region. The Seljuks were a Turkic dynasty from Central Asia. In just a few generations, they built an empire that stretched from Persia to the Mediterranean. An Islamic building stood here before them, and an older tradition places an earlier church on this same ground.

That church story deserves care. Local tradition says the site once held a large Christian church, and that part of it became a mosque after the Arab conquest in the seventh century. Many visitors hear this as settled fact. The details are harder to confirm, and historians still discuss how much of the early structure survived. What you can see with your own eyes is simpler. This building sits on very old foundations, in a city where people have prayed in many languages.

Now look at the courtyard facades. These are the walls that surprise most first-time visitors. They rise in two levels of arches, and many of the columns carry carved capitals in a classical Roman style. Some show curling acanthus leaves, the spiky plant motif that ancient builders loved. These pieces are older than the mosque. Builders reused them from earlier Roman and Byzantine buildings in the city. Architects call this kind of reused material spolia.

Why reuse old stone? Partly it was practical. Carved columns took enormous labour, and an ancient city like this one had plenty lying around. But reuse could also carry meaning. Placing old imperial stones into a new house of prayer showed continuity and power at the same time. So when you scan the arcade, notice that the columns do not always match. Heights vary. Decoration varies. Each one has its own past.

The prayer hall sits on the south side of the courtyard, facing Mecca. Its plan is long and wide, with rows of arches running side by side. A taller central section cuts across them toward the prayer niche. Many scholars compare this layout to the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, the great early mosque of Syria. That link makes sense. Diyarbakır sat on old routes between Anatolia, Syria and Mesopotamia, and ideas travelled along those roads as easily as goods.

Look above the arches and along the walls for bands of writing. Some of the inscriptions use Kufic script, an early angular form of Arabic lettering. It is made of straight strokes and sharp corners, almost like carved geometry. These bands often record who built or repaired a part of the mosque. Even if you cannot read them, notice how the letters work as ornament. They frame doors and run along the stone like a ribbon.

Here is a second surprise. The minaret is square, not round or pencil-thin like the minarets of later Ottoman mosques. Its blocky, solid shape belongs to an older tradition, one that runs back to early Syrian mosques. Some writers suggest it echoes an earlier bell tower, but that remains an idea rather than a proven fact. Step back toward the middle of the courtyard and compare its shape with the domes and slim towers elsewhere on the city skyline.

The courtyard is also a working space, not a museum. Men wash at the ablution fountains before prayer. Older residents sit in the shade of the arcades and talk quietly. Children cross the stones on their way somewhere else. Locals often say the mosque has long served followers of two different schools of Islamic law, each with its own area for prayer. Watch for a moment and you will see how naturally people move through it.

There is a local tale worth hearing too. In the courtyard you may find a simple stone sundial. Some people connect it to al-Jazari, a famous medieval engineer. He worked in this region for the Artuqids, a local dynasty that followed the Seljuks, and he wrote a celebrated book of mechanical devices. Whether he actually made this sundial is not certain. Treat it as a good story with a real figure behind it.

The Artuqids left more lasting marks nearby. Next to the mosque stands a madrasa, a religious school, built in their era around the turn of the thirteenth century. It shows how this courtyard grew into a wider complex for learning as well as prayer. Over the following centuries, other rulers repaired and added to the buildings. The result is not one clean design from one moment. It is layer upon layer, and you can feel that as you walk around.

The mosque sits inside Sur, the historic district enclosed by Diyarbakır's great city walls. Those walls are also built of dark basalt, and they run for several kilometres around the old town. The same black stone gives the whole district its sober colour. Outside the gate of the mosque, narrow streets fill with the smell of fresh bread, grilled meat and strong tea. Shopkeepers call out, and the sound of traffic drifts in from the main roads.

If you have time, walk out toward the walls and the Tigris River valley below the city. From there, you can see how this place grew where it did. There was water, there was fertile land, and there was a rise that was easy to defend. The Great Mosque stood at the centre of all that, at the meeting point of the city's main streets. In many ways, it still marks the centre today.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute in the courtyard. Let your eye move slowly from the black walls to the pale columns and back again. Listen for the water, the pigeons, the low voices under the arches. Notice how the light changes on the stone as clouds pass. Then walk out through the gate, and watch how the old city closes around you again.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly and carry a scarf for entering the prayer hall.
Avoid visiting during the main Friday midday prayer.
Remove your shoes before stepping onto the prayer hall carpets.
Ask before photographing people praying or washing.
Combine your visit with a walk along the city walls.
