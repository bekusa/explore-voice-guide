The guide

The story

Stand at the base of these basalt walls and run your hand along the dark volcanic stone. It feels warm in the afternoon sun, almost alive — layers of black and grey striped in bands, each one a different repair, a different century. The stone came from extinct volcanoes north of the city, dragged here block by block over millennia. Up close, the surface is pocked and scarred, smoothed by centuries of wind blowing off the Tigris plateau.

These fortifications stretch nearly six kilometres around the old city, making them among the longest and best-preserved defensive walls anywhere in the world. The oldest sections date back to Roman times, possibly the third century, though people have been building and rebuilding here for far longer. Archaeologists have found traces of Hurrian settlement beneath the current walls — this site has been fortified for at least five thousand years. What you see today is a palimpsest, Roman foundations supporting Byzantine towers, medieval Islamic repairs patching Seljuk breaches, Ottoman restorations filling gaps left by Mongol sieges.

Walk along the ramparts and count the towers. There are eighty-two in total, each one slightly different in shape and height. Some are square, some round, some polygonal. The variation tells a story of constant adaptation — military engineers across centuries solving the same problem in different ways. The tallest towers rise over fifteen metres, with arrow slits and machicolations still visible. Look for the inscriptions carved into lintels and gate arches: Armenian script, Arabic dedications, Syriac blessings, all layered on top of each other like graffiti across time.

From the top of the walls, the view opens eastward over the Hevsel Gardens, a long green ribbon of orchards and vegetable plots running down to the Tigris River. This fertile strip has fed the city for millennia, watered by springs and ancient channels that still flow. The gardens cover nearly seven hundred hectares, a patchwork of mulberry trees, apricot groves, and small family plots worked by the same families for generations. In spring, the whole valley turns white with blossom. In summer, the air smells of basil and wet earth.

The gardens and the walls were inscribed together as a single UNESCO World Heritage site in 2015, recognizing the deep relationship between defence and sustenance. Cities need walls to survive invasion, but they also need food and water — and Diyarbakır has both, side by side for five thousand years. The Tigris runs dark and fast below, cutting through the plateau, and the gardens cling to its banks like a lifeline. Without the river, the city would have been abandoned centuries ago. Without the walls, it would have been conquered and erased.

The gates are worth studying closely. The most famous is Mardin Gate on the southern side, a massive double portal with a vaulted passage wide enough for carts and livestock. The inner arch is Roman, the outer one medieval, and between them is a shadowy chamber where guards once checked every person entering the city. Stand in that chamber and listen — the acoustics are strange, voices echo and footsteps ring on the stone. It was designed this way, a deliberate trick to disorient invaders and amplify the sound of approaching armies.

Diyarbakır sits on the edge of the Mesopotamian plain, a strategic crossroads between Anatolia and the fertile crescent. Every empire that controlled this region — Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Seljuk, Ottoman — fortified this city and left their mark on the walls. The basalt itself is a kind of archive, each block a fragment of someone's labour, someone's war, someone's peace. The stone weathers slowly in this dry climate, so details survive that would have eroded elsewhere: chisel marks, mason's marks, the ghost of scaffolding holes.

The Hevsel Gardens below are still farmed using traditional methods. Farmers irrigate with gravity-fed channels, plant according to the lunar calendar, and harvest by hand. You will see donkeys carrying produce up narrow paths, women sorting apricots under mulberry shade, children running between rows of tomatoes. This is not a museum or a reconstruction — it is a working landscape, as functional today as it was three thousand years ago. The continuity is rare and fragile.

In the late afternoon, the light turns golden and the basalt glows. Swallows nest in the cracks between stones, darting in and out of the towers. The call to prayer drifts across the old city from a dozen mosques, overlapping and echoing off the walls. This is the hour to walk the ramparts slowly, watching the shadows lengthen across the gardens and the river beyond.

Before you leave, look for the small details that most visitors miss. There are carved reliefs of lions and bulls on some of the older towers, faint now but still visible if the light is right. There are Syriac crosses scratched into gate lintels, and Arabic calligraphy praising long-dead sultans. There are places where the basalt has been patched with lighter limestone, scars from battles no one remembers. Each mark is a voice, a hand, a moment preserved in stone.

Stand here long enough and the walls stop feeling like a relic. They feel like what they are — a living boundary between the city and the world, still doing the work they were built to do five thousand years ago. The gardens still feed the people inside. The gates still open at dawn and close at dusk. The Tigris still runs, dark and cold, carrying the plateau's water south toward the gulf.
