The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the wall. The stone is dark, almost black, and it holds the heat of the day long after the sun has moved on. This is basalt, a volcanic rock, and it gives Diyarbakır its colour. Swallows cut low along the battlements. Somewhere below, a car horn sounds, then a call to prayer drifts over the rooftops. You are standing beside one of the longest continuous city walls still standing anywhere in the world.

The circuit runs for close to six kilometres around the old city, the district now called Sur. Dozens of towers punctuate it, more than eighty by most counts. Some are round, some are square, and some have many sides. Walk any stretch for a few minutes and you will notice that no two sections look quite alike. That variety is not an accident. It is the record of many different hands, working across many centuries, on the same ring of stone.

Before it was Diyarbakır, this city was known as Amida. It sat on the eastern edge of the Roman world, facing the Persian empire across a contested frontier. In the fourth century, the Roman emperor Constantius the Second rebuilt and strengthened its defences. Much of the line you are following today follows that late Roman plan. Later rulers repaired it, raised it, and decorated it — but they rarely moved it.

Those defences were tested almost at once. In the year 359, the Persian king Shapur the Second laid siege to Amida. We know a great deal about it because a Roman officer named Ammianus Marcellinus was inside the walls. He later wrote one of the most vivid eyewitness accounts of siege warfare to survive from the ancient world. The city fell after weeks of fighting, and Ammianus escaped through a gap at night. It is a rare thing — to stand beside a wall and know someone described its last days from the inside.

Here is something many visitors miss. These walls are not only military architecture. They are also a library. Scattered along the towers and gates are dozens of carved inscriptions, many in Arabic script. They name the rulers who paid for repairs and the builders who carried them out. Read together, they track the city's changing masters — local Kurdish and Turkic dynasties such as the Marwanids, the Seljuks, and the Artuqids, followed later by the Ottomans.

The most celebrated towers stand on the southwestern side, near the Mardin Gate. Their names are Ulu Beden, which means roughly the great bastion, and Yedi Kardeş, the Seven Brothers. Both date from the early thirteenth century, under the Artuqid dynasty, a Turkic ruling family who controlled this region at the time. They are massive, rounded, and taller than the walls around them. And they carry something you might not expect on a fortress.

Look up at their faces. Among the bands of inscription, you will find carved animals — eagles, lions, and other creatures set into the dark stone. Medieval Anatolian builders often used such figures as symbols of power and protection. In the low evening light, when shadows fill the cuts in the basalt, the shapes stand out clearly. At midday they can almost disappear. It is worth coming back at a different hour just to see them change.

The walls once had four principal gates, each pointing toward a different road and a different world. The Mountain Gate, also called the Harput Gate, faces north. The Urfa Gate opens west. The Mardin Gate looks south. The New Gate faces east, down toward the river. Traffic and footsteps still pass through several of them. For centuries, merchants, pilgrims, soldiers, and farmers funnelled through these same narrow openings into the city.

In the northeast corner sits the İçkale, the inner citadel. Think of it as a fortress inside a fortress. This was the seat of authority, the last place to hold if the outer walls fell. Today its courtyards contain restored historic buildings and museum spaces, and the atmosphere is calmer than the streets outside. From its edge, the ground drops away sharply toward the east. That drop is the key to why the city was built exactly here.

Below the citadel runs the Tigris, one of the two great rivers of ancient Mesopotamia. Between the walls and the water lie the Hevsel Gardens — a broad green band of orchards, vegetable plots, and poplar trees. Families have farmed this fertile strip for a very long time, watered by the river. In 2015, UNESCO listed the fortress and the Hevsel Gardens together as a single World Heritage site. The idea is simple. The walls protected the city, and the gardens fed it. One makes no sense without the other.

If you look south along the river, you may spot a long stone bridge with many arches. This is the Ten-Eyed Bridge, named for its ten openings, built in the eleventh century during the Marwanid period. On a clear afternoon, the view from the walls takes in the river, the gardens, and the bridge all at once. It is the same landscape that medieval travellers saw as they approached the city, and it has changed less than you might think.

Walking the walls today is not always a smooth experience. Some sections are open and restored, others are closed or crumbling, and the old city behind them is dense and lived-in. Parts of Sur went through serious conflict and damage in the mid-2010s, and reconstruction has reshaped some neighbourhoods since. Go carefully, follow marked paths, and expect the route to change. The stone underfoot is uneven, worn smooth by centuries of feet.

Before you move on, stop for a moment and look along the line of the wall. Notice the small changes — a patch of lighter stone where a repair was made, a line of carved letters, a tower that turns from round to angular. Each one marks a moment when someone decided this city was worth defending. The basalt is still warm. The swallows are still circling. And the wall, as it has for well over a thousand years, keeps holding its ground.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the southwestern towers in late afternoon for low light.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven, worn basalt underfoot.
Check locally which wall sections are currently open to walkers.
Walk to the citadel edge for views over the Tigris.
Bring water and shade in summer, the basalt radiates heat.
