The guide

The story

Listen first. Small waves turn over pebbles on the shore. Wind moves through dry grass and olive trees. The ground underfoot is pale and dusty, and the light comes off the water of the Euboean Gulf. Few places look this quiet. Yet the low rise near the sea, and the fields around it, hold some of the most important evidence we have for one of the least understood chapters of Greek history.

You are on the island of Euboea, a short drive south of Chalkída. Today Lefkandi is a seaside village with holiday homes and tavernas. The name is modern. Nobody knows for certain what the people who lived here three thousand years ago called their town. Scholars have suggested several ancient names, including an earlier Eretria. None is proven. So the name you hear today is a modern label for a place that has lost its own.

To understand why this site matters, you need one piece of background. Around 1200 BC, the great palace kingdoms of Mycenaean Greece collapsed. Writing disappeared. Populations fell. Historians long called the next few centuries the Greek Dark Ages, a period when Greece seemed poor, isolated, and nearly silent. For a long time, many people pictured those centuries as an empty gap between the age of heroes and the age of classical cities.

Lefkandi changed that picture. British archaeologists excavated here over many seasons in the late twentieth century. They worked on the headland settlement, known as Xeropolis, and on several cemeteries nearby. What they found did not look poor or isolated. They found imported goods from Cyprus, the Levant, and Egypt. They found gold. They found evidence of people who traded across the eastern Mediterranean during the very centuries that were supposed to be dark.

The most famous discovery lies on a low hill called Toumba. Here, in the 1980s, excavators uncovered the remains of a long building with a rounded end. It dates to around the tenth century before Christ. Its walls stood on stone foundations, with mud brick above, and wooden posts held up the roof. For its time, it was very large. Nothing else of that scale from this period had been known in Greece before.

The building's purpose is still debated. Underneath its floor were two burials, side by side. One was a man whose cremated remains had been placed in a bronze vessel from Cyprus. That vessel was already old when it went into the ground, a treasured heirloom. Beside him lay a woman, buried with gold jewellery. Nearby, in a separate pit, were the remains of horses. This was a burial for people of very high standing.

Here is the first surprise. Soon after the burials, the great building appears to have been taken down or deliberately filled, and earth was heaped over it to form a mound. Some scholars see it as a house for a ruler. Others see it as a hero's shrine, a monument to the dead. The word archaeologists often use is heroon, a hero's tomb. The real answer may combine both ideas.

The mound then became a focus for later graves. Over the following generations, other wealthy people of Lefkandi chose to be buried around it. Their graves held more imported objects: faience from Egypt, bronze bowls from the Near East, and fine local pottery. It seems that families wanted to be close to the important couple under the mound. Status, memory, and ancestry were clearly linked here, long before the classical Greeks wrote about their heroes.

The second surprise is a small clay figure. In one of the cemeteries, excavators found a painted terracotta centaur, a creature that is half man, half horse. According to the excavation reports, its head and body were found in two different graves. Some think the figure was broken on purpose, so that two people could share it in death. It is one of the earliest known images of a centaur in Greek art, and its exact meaning remains open.

What happened to the town? The settlement on the headland was largely abandoned around 700 BC. A popular theory links this to the Lelantine War, a conflict between Chalkis and Eretria over the fertile Lelantine Plain behind you. Ancient writers mention this war, but its dates and details are unclear. So the link between the war and the end of Lefkandi is a possibility, not a proven fact.

When you visit, it helps to set expectations. Much of what was found here is not on the ground today. The most famous objects are displayed in museums, especially the Archaeological Museum of Eretria, further along the coast. The Toumba excavation has been protected by a modern shelter, and access can vary. What remains on site are traces: foundations, low walls, and the shape of the land itself.

So use the landscape. Look at the headland and notice how it pushes out into the gulf. It offers natural harbours on either side, which made sense for a town of traders and sailors. Across the water lies mainland Greece. Behind you stretches the plain that fed this community. Stand still for a moment and you can see why people settled here, and why rival cities later fought over this coast.

The people of Lefkandi left no written words. Everything we know comes from soil, stone, bronze, and clay, read by patient archaeologists. That is worth remembering as you look around. The past here is quiet, but it is not empty. Before you leave, look again at the low hill and the line of the shore. The sea still moves the pebbles, and the wind still crosses the plain, much as it did when this town was full of people.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit Eretria's Archaeological Museum to see the main finds.
Check locally whether the Toumba shelter is open to visitors.
Bring water and sun protection; the site has little shade.
Combine your visit with a swim at the village beach.
