The guide

The story

Start with the colour. The cliffs around you are not grey, and not brown. They glow a deep rust red, close to the colour of old brick or dried chilli. When the sun breaks through the mist, the stone warms to something nearer orange. Under your feet the path is gritty, and the air smells of wet leaves and river water. This is Mount Danxia, in the north of Guangdong Province, near the city of Shaoguan.

The name carries a picture. In Chinese, Danxia means something like rosy clouds, or red clouds at dawn. Look at the cliffs again with that phrase in mind. On a clear morning, the bare rock faces catch the low light and turn the colour of a sunrise sky. Poets and painters in China used images like this for centuries. Here, the image is made of stone.

Now for the first surprise. This mountain did more than earn a pretty name. It gave its name to a whole type of landscape. In the early twentieth century, Chinese geologists studied these red cliffs and towers. They used the word Danxia to describe this particular kind of scenery. Today, geologists around the world speak of Danxia landforms. When scientists describe red sandstone cliffs in other provinces, or even in other countries, they often borrow the name of the place where you stand.

So what is this rock? It is sandstone and conglomerate, a rough stone made of pebbles cemented together. Very long ago, in the age of the dinosaurs, rivers and lakes filled a basin here with sand and gravel. Over millions of years, that sediment pressed down and hardened into layers. Iron in the rock oxidised, much as a nail rusts in the rain. That iron is the reason for the red colour you see today.

The shapes came later. The earth slowly lifted this basin upward. Cracks opened in the rock, mostly running straight up and down. Rain and rivers worked into those cracks, year after year. The softer stone washed away, and the harder blocks stayed standing. The result is what you see around you — flat-topped peaks, sheer walls, narrow gorges, and lone pillars that rise from the forest like towers.

Look closely at any nearby cliff face. You will often see horizontal lines running across it, like the pages of a closed book. Each line marks a layer of old sediment. Some bands are pale, some darker, some full of small pebbles. Rain also carves shallow hollows and caves into the softer bands. Dark streaks run down from the cliff tops, where water has stained the stone for a very long time.

In 2010, UNESCO listed this mountain as a World Heritage Site. It was not listed alone. It joined several other red-rock landscapes across southern China, under one shared name — China Danxia. Together, these sites show the same geology at different stages of life. Some are young and sharp, with tall narrow peaks. Others are older and worn down into low rounded hills. Mount Danxia sits in the middle, a mature landscape of towers, walls, and valleys.

The area also holds status as a UNESCO Global Geopark. That label is for places where the rocks tell an important story, and where local communities help protect it. Around the park, you may notice signs and panels that explain the geology. They are worth a minute of your time. They turn a pleasant walk into a lesson in how mountains are built and slowly taken apart.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors arrive just for it. Among the rock formations are two stones known far and wide for their very human shapes. One tall pillar looks strikingly like a male body part. In another part of the park, a deep cleft in a cliff resembles the female form. Locals and visitors alike treat them with good humour. Some people see them as symbols of fertility and the balance of yin and yang, the paired forces of Chinese philosophy.

The mountain is not only stone. People have lived and prayed among these cliffs for a long time. Buddhist monks built a temple on the slopes, and it still stands among the trees. Elsewhere, the cliff faces carry old carved inscriptions, cut by scholars and travellers who walked here before you. Caves in the soft rock once gave shelter to hermits. As you climb, watch for steps cut straight into the stone, worn smooth by many feet.

Below the peaks, the Jinjiang River curves through the valley. From the water, the cliffs look even taller, and their reflections stretch red across the surface. The river also helps explain the scene. Running water is the main tool that carved this land. Stand by the bank for a moment and listen. The current is slow and quiet, yet over a very long time it cut a path through solid rock.

The forest adds its own layer. The warm, wet climate of southern China lets green life cling to almost every ledge. Trees grow on cliff tops, and ferns hang in the damp cracks. The contrast is part of the beauty here — red stone, green leaves, and white mist in the morning. In spring and summer you will hear insects and birds. In winter the air is cooler, and the colours feel sharper.

The climbs can be steep. Many paths use narrow stone stairs, and some sections have chains or railings to hold. Take your time, and pause on the landings. Each turn of the trail opens a new view of peaks rising out of the valley. From the higher viewpoints, the land looks like a red-and-green sea, with stone islands scattered across it.

Before you move on, look once more at the nearest cliff. Find one horizontal line in the stone, and follow it with your eyes. That thin band was once sand on the floor of an ancient lake. Rain, rivers, and time have shaped it into the walls around you. And as you walk away, notice the colour again — the same rosy red that gave this mountain its name.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear shoes with good grip for steep stone stairs.
Come early morning to see mist around the peaks.
Take a river trip for views up at the cliffs.
Bring water and rain gear for humid southern weather.
Pace yourself and rest on the stair landings.
