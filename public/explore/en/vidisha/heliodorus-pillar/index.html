The guide

The story

Put your palm against the stone. It is cool, slightly rough, and in places it carries a faint stain of orange-red. That colour is vermilion, the powder of devotion. Around you, the fields of Besnagar stretch out flat and quiet, just outside the town of Vidisha. Birds call from the trees. And in front of you stands a single column of pale stone, more than two thousand years old. It was raised by a Greek, in honour of an Indian god.

This is the Heliodorus Pillar. Locally, many people call it Khamba Baba — roughly, Father Pillar. For generations, it was not a monument in a guidebook. It was a living shrine. Villagers prayed here and coated the stone in vermilion. Under all that paint, almost nobody knew that words were carved into the shaft. Those words would turn out to be one of the most important inscriptions in early Indian history.

Let us go back to the beginning. Scholars usually date the pillar to the late second century BCE, around 113 to 110 BCE. At that time, this place was part of Vidisha, a prosperous city near the meeting of two rivers, the Betwa and the Bes. It sat on trade routes that linked the Ganges plains with western India. Merchants, monks, and envoys passed through. Not far away, at Sanchi, Buddhist stupas were already rising on a hilltop.

Now, imagine a visitor from the far north-west. His name was Heliodorus, son of Dion. Both names are Greek. The inscription tells us he came from Taxila, a city in what is now Pakistan. He was an ambassador, sent by the Indo-Greek king Antialcidas. These Indo-Greek kings were descendants of the Greek settlers who arrived in the region after the campaigns of Alexander the Great. Heliodorus travelled south to the court of a local ruler named Kasiputra Bhagabhadra.

Here is the first surprise. Heliodorus did not come to this court and stay apart from local religion. He took part in it. In the inscription, he calls himself a Bhagavata — a devotee of Vasudeva, a form of the god later closely identified with Krishna and Vishnu. He raised this pillar as a Garudadhvaja, a Garuda standard. Garuda is the great bird who carries Vishnu across the sky. A Garuda figure once crowned the column.

Look up to the top of the shaft. The bird is gone. It was lost long ago, and nobody knows exactly how it looked. What remains is the bell-shaped capital that once held it up. Even without its crown, the pillar gives you a clear picture. A foreign envoy stood here, in this field, and dedicated a monument to Vasudeva, whom the inscription calls the god of gods.

Now bring your eyes down and walk slowly around the shaft. Notice that it is not a simple round column. The lower part is octagonal, with eight faces. Above that, the faces double to sixteen. Higher still, they double again, to thirty-two, so the column looks almost rounded. It is a careful piece of stonework. Each stage catches the light a little differently, so the column seems to soften as it rises.

The inscription itself is carved in Brahmi, an early Indian script, and written in Prakrit, a spoken language of the time. The British archaeologist Alexander Cunningham recorded the pillar in the late nineteenth century. But the writing stayed hidden under the vermilion. Only in 1909, when the paint was cleared, did scholars read the words properly. A shrine that villagers had honoured for centuries turned out to carry a message from a Greek ambassador.

Here is the second surprise, and many visitors miss it. The inscription has a second part, a short moral teaching. It speaks of three immortal steps, or precepts. They are usually translated as self-restraint, generosity, and watchfulness, or care. When these are practised, the text says, they lead to heaven. So this is not only a political monument. It is also a small piece of ethical advice, carved in stone for anyone who stopped to read it.

Why does all of this matter so much to historians? First, the pillar is one of the earliest dated pieces of evidence for devotion to Vasudeva. It shows that this form of worship was already established more than two thousand years ago. Second, it records diplomacy between an Indo-Greek kingdom and an Indian court. Third, it shows a foreigner choosing to honour a local god in a public way. Cultures here were meeting, not only clashing.

The pillar did not stand alone. Later excavations around the site found the foundations of an early temple, with an unusual rounded, elliptical plan, which scholars connect with Vasudeva worship. There are also signs that other pillars once stood nearby. So try to imagine this field as it may once have been: a sacred enclosure, with a temple, standards rising into the sky, and pilgrims arriving from the busy city of Vidisha.

Today the scene is much quieter. The pillar stands on a low platform, inside a small protected enclosure. You may still see marks of worship: a flower, a smear of vermilion, a small offering at the base. For many local people, Khamba Baba is still a sacred presence, not only a historical object. Please move around it gently and respect any rituals you see. The same column belongs to archaeologists and to devotees at the same time.

If you have time, connect this visit with others nearby. The Udayagiri caves, with their later Hindu carvings, are not far away, and Sanchi is a short drive. Together, these places show how Buddhist, Hindu, and foreign visitors all shaped this region. The Heliodorus Pillar is one of the earliest pieces of that story. It is modest in size, but it carries a very large idea.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and look at the column against the open sky. Picture the missing Garuda at the top, wings spread. Think of Heliodorus, far from Taxila, reading these words for the first time. Then look at the vermilion on the stone, put there by people who live here now. As you walk back across the fields, listen to the birds and the wind. The pillar will stay right here, as it has for more than two thousand years.
