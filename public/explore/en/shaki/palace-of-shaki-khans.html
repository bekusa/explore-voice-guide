The guide

The story

Listen first. Somewhere above you, leaves move in the breeze. The courtyard is quiet. Gravel and stone shift under your feet. Ahead stands a long, two-storey building. From a distance its front looks like a piece of embroidery. Coloured light gathers in its tall windows, blue and red and amber. Even before you know anything about this place, the eye goes straight to that glass. That is exactly what its builders intended.

You are standing in front of the Palace of the Shaki Khans, in the old upper part of Shaki, in northern Azerbaijan. The palace sits inside the walls of the town's historic fortress, close to the green slopes of the Greater Caucasus mountains. It was built in the second half of the eighteenth century. Tradition links it to Hussein Khan, one of the rulers of the Shaki Khanate, a small local state that existed in this region for several generations.

A little context helps here. In the eighteenth century, the South Caucasus was a patchwork of small khanates. Each was ruled by its own khan, a local hereditary lord. These rulers balanced between larger powers, including Persia and, later, the Russian Empire. Shaki sat on old trade routes. Caravans carrying silk and other goods passed through this valley. That wealth from trade is part of the story of how a palace like this could be built and decorated so richly.

Now look more closely at the facade. This building was not a fortress or a large city residence. It is usually described as a summer palace, a place for receiving guests and holding court. It is quite compact. It has only a handful of rooms on each floor. What it lacks in size, it makes up for in detail. Almost every surface carries some kind of pattern, colour, or carving.

The windows are the first surprise. They are made in a technique called shebeke. This is a local craft of wooden lattice work filled with small pieces of coloured glass. The wooden pieces lock together like a puzzle. Traditionally, no nails and no glue hold them in place. The geometry itself keeps everything fixed. Step close to one window, if you can, and look at how tiny each piece of wood and glass is.

Think about the patience this required. A single panel contains a very large number of separate parts. Each one had to be cut by hand and fitted exactly. If one piece is wrong, the pattern does not close. Masters in Shaki still practise shebeke today, and you may find small workshops in town that make panels using the same method. So the palace is not only a monument. It is also a living craft tradition.

The second thing to notice is the painting on the facade itself. Look up at the upper levels. You will see deep blue backgrounds with flowing floral designs and geometric bands. Some sections show pointed niches carved in a honeycomb pattern. This style is called muqarnas. It is a form of decoration from Islamic architecture where small cells are stacked to create a shape like stalactites. Here it gives depth and shadow to the front of the building.

If you go inside, the mood changes. The rooms feel intimate, almost private. The walls and ceilings are covered with paintings. Some show flowers, birds, and vases in rich reds, golds, and greens. Others are more dramatic. There are hunting scenes with riders and animals. There are also battle scenes with soldiers and horses. Scholars see these images as a way for the khans to show power, taste, and their place in a wider Persian artistic world.

Now comes the third surprise. When sunlight passes through the shebeke windows, it throws coloured patterns across the painted walls and floors. The rooms are almost never the same twice. On a bright morning, the light can be sharp and lively. On a cloudy afternoon it becomes soft and dim. Stand still for a moment inside and watch how the colours move as the sun shifts or a cloud passes.

Back outside, turn your attention to the two enormous plane trees in front of the palace. Their trunks are wide and gnarled, and their branches spread over the courtyard. Locals say these trees are several centuries old, older even than the palace itself. Whatever their exact age, they shape the whole experience of this space. They give shade in summer, and they frame the facade in almost every photograph taken here.

The palace is also part of a larger story. In 2019, UNESCO added the historic centre of Shaki, together with the Khan's Palace, to its World Heritage List. The listing recognises the old town as a whole, with its traditional houses, tiled roofs, and streets that follow the hillside. So when you leave the palace grounds, you are still inside the protected landscape. The town around you is part of what made this place important.

As you walk out through the fortress walls, look at the stone. The walls are thick and built from river stones and brick, laid in rough bands. Below, the streets lead down towards the centre of Shaki. There you can find an old caravanserai, a large inn once used by traders and their animals. You can also find shops selling local halva, a layered sweet with nuts and syrup that Shaki is known for across the country.

The town also has a long connection with silk. For generations, silk production was an important part of life here. You might see scarves and textiles for sale in small shops. Some still use traditional patterns and natural colours. These patterns often echo the motifs on the palace walls, with flowers, birds, and repeating geometric shapes. The same visual language appears in wood, glass, paint, and fabric.

Before you go, turn back one last time. Look at the palace framed by the trees and the mountains behind it. Notice the colours of the glass and how they change with the light. Listen to the leaves above you and the quiet voices of other visitors. As you walk down into the town, keep an eye on doors, windows, and balconies. You may see small echoes of this palace in the everyday houses of Shaki.
