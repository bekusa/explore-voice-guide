The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel crunches underfoot, rooks call from the tall trees, and somewhere nearby water runs over stone. The air smells of cut grass and damp bark. Ahead of you rises a grey stone tower, solid and plain, with later wings gathered around it like a family around an elder. Welcome to Cawdor Castle, a short drive south-west of the coastal town of Nairn, in the Scottish Highlands.

Many people arrive here with one name already in their heads. Macbeth. In Shakespeare's play, the witches greet Macbeth as Thane of Cawdor, and so the castle and the tragedy have become tied together in the popular mind. A thane, by the way, was a Scottish noble who held land directly from the king. But here is the first surprise. The real Macbeth was a king of Scots in the eleventh century. This castle came centuries later. He never walked these rooms, and Duncan was never murdered here.

So what is the true story? The oldest part of the castle is the central tower house, a tall stone keep built for defence. It dates from the late Middle Ages, around the fifteenth century, when the Thanes of Cawdor received royal permission to fortify their home. A tower house was the classic Scottish answer to a dangerous world. You lived upstairs, kept your goods safe below, and made sure the door was very hard to break.

Look at the approach as you come in. The castle sits beside a stream, and you cross a drawbridge to reach the entrance. It is a small detail, but it changes how the place feels. You are not simply walking into a house. You are being admitted. For centuries, anyone arriving here would have felt the same moment of pause before the door.

Now for the legend that every guide here loves to tell. Locals say that the Thane of Cawdor once had a dream. In it, he was told to load a donkey with a chest of gold and let the animal wander. Wherever the donkey lay down to rest, he should build his castle. According to the tale, the donkey stopped beneath a holly tree. And so, the story goes, the tower was built right around it.

Here is the second surprise. That tree is real. Down in the vaulted ground floor of the tower, you can still see the remains of an old tree standing inside the stone walls. Scientific dating has shown that the tree died in the late fourteenth century, which fits the time the castle was taking shape. Whether a donkey chose the spot is another matter. But the tree is not a prop. It is genuinely ancient, and it has stood in the dark for over six hundred years.

The castle's later history belongs to the Campbells, one of Scotland's most powerful clans. The link came through a young heiress named Muriel Calder, who inherited the Cawdor lands as a child around the turn of the sixteenth century. Popular accounts say she was carried off by Campbell men, and that her nurse marked her so she could not be swapped for another girl. Treat the details as family lore. What is certain is that Muriel married a Campbell, and the castle passed into that family, where it has stayed for generations.

That continuity is what makes a visit here feel different. Cawdor is not a ruin and it is not a museum built from scratch. It has been a lived-in family home. As you move through the rooms, notice the mix of periods side by side. Old stone walls sit next to painted portraits, faded tapestries, heavy wooden furniture, and the small, personal clutter of people who actually lived here. Look out, too, for touches of humour in the written notes placed around the rooms.

Pay attention to the walls themselves as you climb. In the oldest parts, the stone is thick and the windows are narrow, built for safety rather than views. In the later wings, windows grow wider and rooms grow lighter. You can almost trace the centuries by how much daylight each room lets in. Peaceful times meant bigger windows. It is a quiet way to read a building without a single date on a sign.

When you step back outside, give yourself time for the gardens. There are several of them, each with its own character. The walled garden is the oldest, with stone walls that hold the warmth and shelter plants from the northern wind. You may find a holly maze there, a neat echo of the tree inside the tower. The flower garden is softer and more colourful, especially in high summer. Beyond lies a wilder garden that slopes towards the stream.

Beyond the formal gardens stretches Cawdor Big Wood, an old woodland of oak, beech and pine. Paths lead under the trees, where the light turns green and the ground is soft with moss and fallen leaves. It is a fine place to walk off a tour of the house. Listen for birdsong and the sound of the burn, the Scottish word for a stream, running somewhere below you.

The village of Cawdor sits close by, with stone cottages and a small parish church. Further away, the wider landscape opens up. The Moray Firth lies to the north, and the battlefield of Culloden, where the last Jacobite rising ended in 1746, is not far to the west. Nairn itself, with its long sandy beach, makes a gentle base for exploring this corner of the Highlands. Cawdor fits into that wider story of clans, kings and changing loyalties.

Before you leave, stand for a moment and look back at the tower. Notice how the grey stone darkens where rain has run down it, and how the newer wings lean against the old keep. Somewhere inside, in the cool dark of the ground floor, a tree still stands where it stood when the first stones were laid. As you walk away across the gravel, listen once more to the rooks and the water. They were here long before the castle, and they will be here long after this visit.
