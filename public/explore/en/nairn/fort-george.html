The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind comes off the water from almost every side, and gulls call above the ramparts. Underfoot there is short, tight grass, and under that grass lies a huge mass of earth and stone. Fort George sits on a low spit of land that pushes out into the Moray Firth, west of Nairn along the coast. Grey water stretches away in front of you. On a clear day, the hills of the Black Isle rise on the far shore.

This fort was built for one reason: fear. In 1746, the Jacobite rising ended at the Battle of Culloden, only a short distance inland from here. The Jacobites wanted to restore the Stuart royal line to the British throne. Many of their supporters came from the Highlands. After their defeat, the government in London decided it would never again lose control of this region. The answer was a fortress so strong that no one could ever take it.

There had been an earlier Fort George, in Inverness itself. The Jacobites captured it and blew it up during the rising. So the army chose a new site, away from the town and protected by the sea. Work began in the late 1740s and took about two decades. The design came from William Skinner, a military engineer. The Adam family of Scottish builders won the masonry contract. Members of that family later became some of the most famous architects in Britain.

Now look at the shape around you. The fort is not a simple square. It follows the ideas of the artillery age, with angled bastions that stick out from the walls like arrowheads. These angles let defenders fire along the face of every wall, so attackers had nowhere to hide. Low, thick ramparts absorbed cannon fire better than tall medieval towers. It was the most modern thinking in European fortification at the time, set down on a quiet Scottish shore.

Here is the first surprise. After all that money, labour and planning, Fort George never faced an attack. Not one shot was fired at it in anger. By the time it was finished, the Jacobite cause was effectively over. The Highlands had changed, and the threat had faded. Some people see that as a waste. Others see it as success... a fortress so strong that nobody ever tried.

If you came in through the main gate, you crossed the outer defences first. A ravelin, which is a detached triangular earthwork, guards the approach. Beyond it, a bridge crosses a deep ditch to the main entrance. Every step of that route was planned so that guns could cover it from several directions. Pause on the bridge if you can. Look down into the ditch and imagine trying to cross it under fire.

Inside the walls, the fort opens up into something like a small town. Long stone barrack blocks line the parade ground. There are officers' quarters, stores, workshops and a chapel. Thousands of people lived and worked here over the years, soldiers and their families among them. The scale is hard to judge from outside. Once inside, you realise how much space the walls enclose, and how carefully it was organised.

Some of the barrack rooms now show how soldiers lived in different periods. You can see narrow beds, simple wooden furniture and kit hung on the walls. Rooms were crowded, and privacy was rare. In earlier times, wives and children sometimes shared the same space, separated only by a blanket or a curtain. Take a moment in these rooms. They show a side of military life that grand walls and cannons usually hide.

Then there is the grand magazine, where the gunpowder was kept. Its walls are very thick, and its roof was built to resist a direct hit. Gunpowder was the most dangerous thing in the fort. One spark could destroy everything around it. So the builders took great care with ventilation and with keeping the store dry. Today the building holds a collection of weapons, and you can feel how cool and still the air is inside.

The second surprise is that Fort George has never stopped being a working military base. Soldiers have been stationed here for well over two centuries. That makes it unusual among great forts of its age, most of which became ruins or museums long ago. So as you walk, remember that parts of the site belong to the army, not to visitors. Some doors stay closed, and some areas are not open to the public.

The fort is also home to the Highlanders' Museum. It tells the story of Highland regiments and the soldiers who served in them, across many wars and many continents. There are uniforms, medals, letters and photographs. There is real irony in this. A fortress built to control the Highlands became a home for Highland soldiers. Over time, those regiments became a source of great pride across Scotland.

Before you leave, walk up onto the ramparts and look out across the firth. The narrow channel in front of you is one of the best places in Britain to see bottlenose dolphins. They often feed here when the tide is moving. Watch for a dark curved back breaking the surface, or a sudden splash. Look for a quieter corner too, near the officers' dog cemetery, where small headstones remember regimental pets.

So stand for a moment where the guns once pointed out to sea. Feel the wind again, and listen to the water against the shore. The walls around you were built in fear, for a war that never came here. Today they hold grass, birds, soldiers and visitors. As you walk back towards the gate, look at the long, low line of the rampart. Quiet strength, waiting for an enemy who never arrived.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the full rampart circuit for views over the Moray Firth.
Bring binoculars to scan the water for dolphins.
Pack a windproof layer; the exposed peninsula gets very breezy.
Allow extra time for the Highlanders' Museum inside.
Respect closed areas, as parts remain an active army base.
