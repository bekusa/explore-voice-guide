The guide

The story

Stand at the foot of the steps for a moment. The stone underfoot is smooth, polished by countless shoes. Behind you, traffic hums along the lower edge of the old city. Above you, a white façade rises between two square towers, bright against the Mediterranean sky. Somewhere close, a vendor calls out, a scooter climbs a narrow lane, and pigeons settle on a ledge. This is Ketchaoua Mosque, and few buildings in Algiers have carried so many identities.

You are at the lower gateway to the Casbah, the old hillside city of Algiers. The Casbah climbs steeply from here toward the heights, in a dense maze of white houses, stairways and covered passages. UNESCO lists it as a World Heritage Site. The mosque sits at the point where that old fabric meets the wider, flatter streets laid out later. In a sense, it stands on a border. That border runs between two cities, and between two chapters of history.

A place of prayer stood on this spot during the Ottoman era, when Algiers was a regency loosely tied to the Ottoman Empire. In the late eighteenth century, the ruler of the city, Hassan Pasha, had the mosque rebuilt on a grander scale. That rebuilding is usually dated to the 1790s. At that time, the building served the people of the lower Casbah, close to the port and its trading life.

The name itself is a small puzzle. Ketchaoua is often linked to Turkish words, and one popular explanation says it refers to a plateau of goats — perhaps an old open space where animals grazed or were sold. Treat this as a local tradition rather than settled fact. Linguists and residents do not all agree. Still, the image is a pleasing one: a quiet grassy rise, long before the paving stones and traffic arrived.

Then came 1830. French forces captured Algiers, and the city changed quickly. Within a few years, the new authorities turned this mosque into a Catholic church. Historical accounts describe strong local opposition to the seizure. For many Algerians, the loss of this building became a lasting symbol of colonial rule. It was not simply a change of use. It was a public statement about who now controlled the city.

The church became the Cathedral of Saint Philippe, the seat of the Catholic bishop of Algiers. Over the following decades, builders reshaped it heavily. Much of what you see today comes from that colonial reconstruction in the nineteenth century. The architects mixed styles on purpose. They combined Moorish forms from North Africa with elements of Byzantine design, the style of the old Eastern Christian empire centred on Constantinople. The result is unusual, and deliberately so.

Look up at the two towers on either side of the entrance. Many visitors assume they are traditional minarets. In fact, their form owes a great deal to the cathedral period, when church builders wanted a strong, symmetrical front. Look also at the arched doorway and the stepped approach. The staircase gives the building a sense of height and ceremony. It also lifts the entrance above the noise of the street below.

Here is a detail most people miss. This façade carries layers that do not fully agree with each other. Some lines feel North African, with horseshoe-like arches and geometric screens. Others feel European and formal, with balanced proportions and a grand frontal view. You are looking at a building where two traditions were forced together. The tension between them is part of what makes the face of the mosque so memorable.

In 1962, Algeria won its independence after a long and painful war. Soon after, the cathedral returned to Muslim worship and became Ketchaoua Mosque again. For many Algerians, this restoration carried deep emotional weight. The building had changed hands as a sign of conquest. Now it changed back as a sign of sovereignty. Few places in the city show the arc of the country's modern history so clearly in one structure.

Time, humidity and earthquakes took their toll. The building closed for a long restoration in the twenty-first century, with support from Turkey, which has strong historical ties to the Ottoman layer of Algiers. The work reopened the mosque in 2018. Restorers cleaned and repaired the stonework, the decoration and the interior spaces. When you look closely, the surfaces look fresh, yet the overall shape still follows its complicated past.

If you are able to go inside, step gently and respect the rhythm of prayer. Visitors are usually asked to dress modestly and remove shoes. Inside, you may notice rows of columns, carved plaster and soft light falling across carpets. The interior feels calmer than the street outside. The sound changes as soon as you cross the threshold. Footsteps soften, voices drop, and the air feels cooler against your skin.

After your visit, walk a little way up into the Casbah. The lanes narrow quickly. Doors are often painted in deep colours, and old wooden balconies lean over the steps. Children play, neighbours talk across doorways, and the smell of bread or coffee may drift from a small shop. This living neighbourhood gives the mosque its meaning. It was always a building for the people who lived in these streets.

Now turn back and look at the façade once more. You are seeing an Ottoman foundation, a colonial cathedral and an independent nation's mosque, all in a single wall of white stone. Notice how the light moves across the towers as the day goes on. As you walk away, listen for the city around you — the traffic below, the voices above. The mosque stands between them, as it always has.
