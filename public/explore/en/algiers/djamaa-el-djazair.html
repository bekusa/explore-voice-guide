The guide

The story

Feel the wind first. It comes off the Bay of Algiers and runs across a wide stone esplanade, warm and slightly salty. Above you, a pale square tower rises so high that you have to tilt your head right back to find the top. Below it, a broad prayer hall sits low and calm under a single great dome. This is Djamaa el Djazaïr, the Great Mosque of Algiers. It is one of the newest major religious buildings in the Mediterranean world, and one of the largest.

Let us begin with the number that most visitors already know. The minaret stands about 265 metres tall. That makes it the tallest minaret on Earth. It is taller than any mosque tower in the Gulf, taller than the minaret of the Hassan the Second Mosque in Casablanca. On a clear day, sailors see it long before they see the rest of the city. It has become a new landmark for ships entering the bay.

The mosque also ranks by size. It is widely described as the third largest mosque in the world, after the Grand Mosque in Mecca and the Prophet's Mosque in Medina. The whole complex is designed to hold well over one hundred thousand people when the halls, courtyards and esplanade are full. On ordinary days, though, you will often find it quiet. A few families walk slowly. A guard stands in the shade. The scale feels even larger when the space is almost empty.

Now look at where you are standing. This is the district of Mohammadia, on the eastern side of the bay, between the old city and the airport. During the French colonial period, this area carried a French name. After independence in 1962, it was renamed. Building the country's great mosque here was a deliberate choice. A place once marked by colonial rule now holds the largest religious building in Algeria. Many Algerians see this as a statement about national identity.

The project began under President Abdelaziz Bouteflika, who wanted a monument for the new century. Construction started in the early 2010s. A Chinese state construction company built it, while German architects led the design. The work took many years. Costs rose, and the project drew public debate inside Algeria. Some people asked whether the money should go to hospitals and housing instead. Others saw it as a lasting symbol for the nation. Both views are part of the building's story.

The mosque opened for prayer in stages, and the official inauguration came in 2024, under President Abdelmadjid Tebboune. So the stone around you is very young. There is no centuries-old patina here, no worn steps. The surfaces are clean and sharp. In a city where the old Casbah holds layers of Ottoman and earlier history, this building offers a different kind of experience. You are seeing a monument at the very start of its life.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. Algiers sits in an active earthquake zone. The region has suffered serious earthquakes in living memory. So the engineers placed the great prayer hall on a system of seismic isolators. These are large bearings under the structure. In an earthquake, they let the ground move while the hall above moves much less. You cannot see them from here. But the whole calm mass of the prayer hall rests on that hidden engineering.

Look again at the minaret. Its shape is square, not round or pencil-thin. This follows the tradition of the Maghreb, the western Islamic world that includes Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia. Old minarets across this region are square towers. The designers took that ancient form and stretched it upward with modern materials. Notice the fine geometric screens on its faces. They filter sunlight and give the tower a light, woven texture from a distance.

The second surprise is inside that tower. The minaret is not just a symbol. It holds many floors with a museum and research spaces devoted to the history of Islam and of Algeria. At the very top, there is an observation level. From there, the whole bay opens out below you, with the white city climbing the hills to the west. If it is open during your visit, it gives one of the widest views in Algiers.

Now turn toward the prayer hall. Its dome is large but low, and the roof line stays simple. Inside, the hall is a forest of slender columns under a patterned ceiling. Calligraphy and carved wood bring warmth to the cool stone. Light enters through screens, so the interior feels soft rather than bright. If you go in, remove your shoes, speak quietly and follow the guidance of the staff. This is a working place of worship first.

The complex is much more than one hall. Around the courtyards you will find a large library, a school for the study of the Quran, a cultural centre and gardens. The planners wanted a place of learning as well as prayer. Open galleries connect the buildings and give shade from the strong Algerian sun. Walk slowly along them. The repeated arches create long, quiet perspectives, and the sea breeze moves through the gaps.

Listen for the call to prayer. When it sounds here, it carries across the esplanade and out over the water. Traffic hums on the coastal road nearby. Gulls cry over the harbour. These ordinary sounds sit beside a very large monument, and that contrast is part of the experience. The mosque is new, but daily life around it continues as it always has in this busy capital.

Before you leave, stand for a moment at the edge of the esplanade and look back. Let your eye travel from the base of the minaret to its tip. Then turn and look at the bay, the ships at anchor, the hills of the old city in the distance. As you walk away, notice how often the tower appears again, above rooftops and between buildings. From almost anywhere along this coast, it will stay with you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Dress modestly; women should bring a scarf for the hair.
Avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers for tourism.
Check on arrival whether the minaret viewing level is open.
Come late afternoon when the sea breeze cools the esplanade.
Bring a bag to carry your shoes inside the hall.
