The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind comes off the Volkhov River and moves through the old trees inside these walls. Underfoot, gravel paths crunch, and somewhere a bell may be ringing from the cathedral. The brick around you is a deep, weathered red, warm in sunlight and almost purple under cloud. This is the Detinets, the fortress at the heart of Veliky Novgorod. Some people call it the Novgorod Kremlin. Both names point to the same place: the old citadel where this city kept its faith, its treasury and its power.

First, the name. In much of Russia, a fortified city centre is called a kremlin. Novgorod used its own older word, detinets. Where the word comes from is still debated. One popular idea links it to the Russian word for children, as if this were the place where families hid in times of danger. Scholars do not agree on that, so treat it as a story rather than a fact. What is certain is that a fortress has stood on this spot for nearly a thousand years. Chronicles mention it as early as the mid-eleventh century.

The walls you see today are not the first ones. Earlier defences here were built of wood and earth. The brick walls and towers date from the late fifteenth century. That timing matters. In 1478, Novgorod lost its independence to Moscow under Grand Prince Ivan the Third. Soon after, Moscow rebuilt this fortress in brick. So these walls carry a quiet irony. They protect Novgorod, but they were raised by the power that had just conquered it.

To understand what was lost, you need to know what Novgorod was. For centuries, this was a merchant republic. It traded with the cities of northern Europe and grew wealthy on furs, wax and trade routes running from the Baltic deep into Russia. Citizens gathered at a public assembly called the veche to debate and decide. When Ivan the Third took the city, he had the veche bell taken down and carried to Moscow. Without its bell, the assembly fell silent. It was a clear sign that the republic was over.

Now turn toward the cathedral with its cluster of domes. This is the Cathedral of Saint Sophia, built in the middle of the eleventh century, between 1045 and 1050. Prince Vladimir, a son of Yaroslav the Wise, ordered its construction. It is one of the oldest stone churches still standing anywhere in Russia. Look at its walls. They are not smooth and polished. They are thick, uneven and whitewashed, with small windows, built for weight and endurance rather than show. The central dome is gilded, while the others are grey, and the contrast catches the eye from far across the river.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look up at the cross on the main golden dome. A small figure of a dove sits on it. Legend has it that the dove is a guardian. Locals say that as long as the dove stays on the cross, the city will be safe. It is folklore, of course. But people in Novgorod have repeated the story for generations, and some still glance up at the dove when they pass.

Walk around to the west side of the cathedral and look for a set of bronze doors. They are covered with small panels of figures, saints and scenes, cast in metal. These are often called the Magdeburg Gates, because they were made in the German city of Magdeburg in the twelfth century. How they reached Novgorod is not fully known. Several theories exist, from trade to war trophy. Look closely and you will find figures of the craftsmen themselves among the panels. Northern European art is set into the door of a Russian cathedral, and that tells you how far Novgorod's connections reached.

In the open space nearby stands a very different monument. It is shaped a little like a giant bell. Bronze figures crowd around it in layers. This is the Monument to the Millennium of Russia, unveiled in 1862 to mark a thousand years since the legendary founding of the Russian state. It was designed by the artist Mikhail Mikeshin. The figures include princes, saints, soldiers, writers and statesmen. Walk slowly around it and you will see the history of a country told in bronze, from early rulers to the nineteenth century.

This monument also has a wartime story. During the Second World War, German forces occupied Novgorod and began to dismantle the monument, apparently intending to take it away. After the city was freed in 1944, the pieces were gathered and the monument was put back together. Much of Novgorod was badly damaged in the war, and many of its churches were left in ruins. When you look at the restored buildings around you today, remember how much patient rebuilding happened here after 1944.

Among the buildings of the archbishop's court is the Palace of Facets, also called the Faceted Chamber. It was built in the fifteenth century under Archbishop Euthymius the Second, a powerful church leader in the final decades of the republic. Its pointed vaults are in the Gothic style, the tall, ribbed style of medieval western Europe. German master builders are believed to have worked on it. A Gothic hall inside a Russian fortress is a surprise for many visitors, and it shows again that Novgorod looked west as well as east.

Now look at the towers along the walls. Each has its own shape and character. Some are round, some square, all with steep roofs. One of the tallest is the Kokui Tower. It was rebuilt in the late seventeenth century, and its upper levels have a lookout. On a clear day, the view from the top stretches over the river and the rooftops of the city. Also near the cathedral is the belfry, a long wall with openings for bells. Its rows of arches are best seen from the side, when light falls across them.

All of this is part of a larger whole. In 1992, the historic monuments of Novgorod and its surroundings were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list. The Detinets is the centre of that story, but it is only one part. Across the pedestrian bridge, on the other bank of the Volkhov, lies Yaroslav's Court, the old trading side of the city. There you will find more churches and the remains of the old market. The two banks once faced each other as rivals, the church and princes on one side, the merchants on the other.

Before you leave, walk out onto the riverbank below the walls. From here you can see how the fortress sits above the water. You can see why someone chose this spot. The river was a road, a border and a line of defence all at once. In summer, people sit on the sand and swim. In winter, the river turns white and still.

Take one last look back at the red walls and the golden dome above them. Notice the trees growing inside the fortress, the families walking the paths, and the sound of the river behind you. The power that built this place is long gone. The republic, the princes and the archbishops have all passed into history. But the walls are still standing, and the dove is still on its cross. As you walk on, listen for the bells.

Practical tips

Cross the footbridge afterwards to explore Yaroslav's Court.
Climb the Kokui Tower for views over the Volkhov.
Walk the riverbank below the walls for the best overview.
Circle the Millennium monument slowly to read each figure group.
Visit the cathedral early to enjoy a quieter interior.
