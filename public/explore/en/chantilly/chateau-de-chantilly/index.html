The guide

The story

Listen first to the water. It laps softly against pale stone walls, and the walls seem to rise straight out of it. Swans drift past the foundations. Gravel crunches under your feet, and the air carries the green smell of wet lawns and old trees. Before any date or name, this is what Chantilly is: a castle that sits on its own reflection, surrounded on every side by water.

You are standing roughly forty kilometres north of Paris, at the edge of a wide forest that was once a royal hunting ground. The château is not one building. It is two, joined together. The smaller wing to one side is the Petit Château, from the Renaissance. The larger, more decorated mass is the Grand Château. It looks old, but much of what you see is younger than it appears. That contrast is the first surprise of this place.

The story begins in the sixteenth century with the Montmorency family. Their most powerful member, Anne de Montmorency, was Constable of France. That title made him the commander of the king's armies. He was one of the richest men in the kingdom, and he made Chantilly a showcase. The Petit Château dates from his era. It has elegant windows and carved stone, and it has survived every disaster that came later.

In the seventeenth century, the estate passed by inheritance to the Condé family, cousins of the French kings. The most famous of them was Louis de Bourbon, remembered as the Grand Condé. He was a brilliant and difficult general. After years of war and political rebellion, he settled here and turned Chantilly into a gathering place for writers, thinkers and the court. Conversation, music and theatre filled these rooms.

Look out across the gardens now. The long straight lines of water and the wide flat terraces are the work of André Le Nôtre. He was the same garden designer who later shaped Versailles. Here he worked with the flat, wet land and did not fight it. Instead of tall fountains, he used broad canals that hold the sky like mirrors. Many people consider this one of his favourite projects.

Chantilly also has a famous and sad story about food. In 1671, the Grand Condé hosted King Louis the Fourteenth for several days of feasts. The man in charge of the kitchens was François Vatel. According to the well-known account, the fish delivery seemed to be late, and Vatel took his own life from shame. The fish, people say, arrived shortly afterwards. It remains one of the most retold kitchen stories in French history.

And yes, Chantilly cream carries this name. Sweetened whipped cream is linked to the estate in popular tradition. Some tales credit Vatel. Others place it later, in the dairy of the little rustic hamlet in the park. Food historians do not agree on the true origin, so treat these as legends rather than records. Still, the name has travelled to almost every pastry shop in the world.

Now for the hard part of the story. During the French Revolution at the end of the eighteenth century, the Condé family fled. The Grand Château was seized, and most of it was demolished. Its stone was taken away. For decades, only the Petit Château and the great stables stood near an empty platform beside the water. The building you see today grew from those ruins.

The man who rebuilt it was Henri d'Orléans, Duke of Aumale. He was a son of King Louis-Philippe and inherited the estate from the last of the Condés. He spent years in exile in England and became a passionate collector of paintings, drawings and rare books. In the 1870s and 1880s he rebuilt the Grand Château in a Renaissance style. He designed it to hold his collections. So this new castle was planned as a museum from the beginning.

Here is the second surprise. The duke had no surviving heirs, so he gave the entire estate to the Institut de France, the country's great academic body. He set strict conditions. The collections must stay open to the public. They must not be lent elsewhere. And the paintings must hang as he arranged them. That is why the picture galleries of the Musée Condé look so crowded. Canvases hang close together, frame to frame, from floor to high ceiling, exactly as a nineteenth-century collector wanted them.

Inside, the collection of old paintings is often described as the second most important in France, after the Louvre. You will find works by Raphael, Poussin and Ingres, among many others. The library holds rare manuscripts. The most famous is the Très Riches Heures of the Duke of Berry, a medieval prayer book full of painted calendar scenes. The original is very fragile and is rarely displayed, so visitors usually see a careful reproduction.

Walk a little away from the castle and you will reach the Great Stables. They are a long, grand building from the eighteenth century, and many visitors first mistake them for a palace. A popular legend says the duke who built them believed he would be reborn as a horse, and so he wanted stables fit for a prince. Whether or not that is true, horses still live here, and the stables now house a museum about horses and riding. Next to them lies the racecourse, and Chantilly remains an important centre for horse racing in France.

Before you move on, stand still for a moment at the water's edge. Look at how the castle doubles itself in the moat, its roofs and chimneys turned upside down in the grey-green surface. Notice the different colours of stone between the old wing and the new. You are looking at four centuries of ambition, loss and rebuilding in a single view. As you walk away along the gravel, listen again for the swans and the soft sound of the water.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan a full day for castle, gardens and stables.
Wear comfortable shoes for long gravel garden paths.
Visit the Great Stables early, before larger groups arrive.
Try Chantilly cream at a café on the estate.
Check race days, when the town gets much busier.
