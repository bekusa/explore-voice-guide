The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere beyond the gates, a bird gives a sharp, rising call, and the gum trees overhead move in the breeze. Underfoot, the paths are warm and dusty in summer and dark with leaf litter after rain. The air smells of eucalyptus, damp earth, and the animals themselves. This is Melbourne Zoo, and you are standing inside one of the oldest zoos in the world that is still open today.

The story begins in the early 1860s, in a young colonial city made rich by the gold rushes. A group called the Acclimatisation Society of Victoria had an idea that sounds strange now. They wanted to bring animals from Europe and Asia to Australia, and to send Australian animals the other way. The goal was partly scientific and partly practical. Some settlers hoped familiar birds and game would make the new land feel more like home.

That idea had real consequences. Across Australia, introduced animals such as rabbits and foxes later caused enormous damage to native wildlife. It is one of the quiet ironies of this place. A zoo that started with a mission to bring in foreign species now spends much of its energy protecting the native animals those introductions put at risk. The modern zoo describes itself first as a conservation organisation, not simply a place to look at animals.

The zoo settled here in Royal Park, in the inner suburb of Parkville, and it has stayed on this site ever since. For many decades, one family shaped its life. Albert Le Souef became director in the late nineteenth century, and members of the Le Souef family went on to lead zoos in other Australian cities too. Under their watch, the gardens grew, and the collection expanded into something recognisably modern.

Walk a little further, and look at how the paths curve. Older zoos often lined animals up in rows of bars and concrete. Melbourne Zoo still has some heritage structures from those years, but most enclosures here have been rebuilt to feel like habitats. The planting is dense on purpose. Bamboo, ferns, and tall grasses hide the fences, so the boundary between you and the animal becomes hard to see.

Here is a surprise many visitors miss. The zoo sits on land that belonged long before the city to the Wurundjeri people, one of the Aboriginal nations of the Kulin alliance around Melbourne. You may see acknowledgements of Traditional Owners on signs as you walk. These are not decoration. They recognise that people cared for this country, and its animals, for tens of thousands of years before any zoo existed.

If you head toward the Australian animals, slow down. This is where the zoo feels most like home ground. You can look for kangaroos and wallabies, koalas tucked high in the forks of trees, and the platypus, which is often a highlight. A platypus is a mammal that lays eggs, has a bill like a duck, and hunts underwater with its eyes closed. It finds food by sensing tiny electrical signals from its prey.

That platypus is also a reminder of why the zoo works the way it does. Zoos Victoria, the organisation that runs this site, also manages Healesville Sanctuary in the hills east of the city and Werribee Open Range Zoo to the west. Together they run breeding and recovery programs for threatened Victorian species. Some of the smallest animals here — frogs, lizards, insects — matter more to those programs than the big crowd favourites.

Of course, the big animals still draw people in. Near the rainforest areas, you can find gorillas and orangutans in planted, leafy enclosures. Watch how the orangutans use their long arms to swing and climb. Their name comes from Malay words that are often translated as person of the forest. Spend a few minutes with them, and you will notice how carefully they watch the people watching them.

The lions draw a different kind of crowd. On a quiet day, you may hear a low, rumbling call carry across the grounds, deeper than you expect. Nearby, the zoo has spaces for seals and penguins, where you can see the animals move underwater through viewing windows. Children press their faces to the glass. Adults often do too, when they think nobody is looking.

Now for a gentler corner. The Butterfly House is warm and humid inside, like stepping into a tropical greenhouse. Hundreds of butterflies drift between flowering plants, and they will sometimes land on your shoulder or your hat. Move slowly, and check your clothes before you leave. Staff ask visitors to make sure no butterfly hitches a ride out the door.

The zoo also tries to connect what you see here with choices you make at home. Over the years, Zoos Victoria has run public campaigns about the plastic, packaging, and products that can harm wildlife. Signs near some enclosures explain how small changes, such as choosing sustainable seafood, can help animals in the wild. It is an easy message to walk past. It is also the heart of what the modern zoo is trying to say.

In summer evenings, the atmosphere changes. The zoo has a long tradition of outdoor concerts called Zoo Twilights, when people bring picnic rugs and listen to music on the lawns as the light fades. Even on an ordinary day, late afternoon is a good time to be here. The shadows grow long, the crowds thin out, and many animals become more active as the heat drops.

Before you go, stand still for one more moment. Notice the layers of sound around you — birds in the canopy, children calling to each other, a keeper's bucket clanking somewhere out of sight. Beyond the fences, Royal Park stretches out in open grass and old trees, and the city carries on. As you walk back toward the gates, listen once more for that first bird call. It was here long before the zoo, and it will still be here after you leave.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early or late afternoon when animals are more active.
Take the tram to the zoo stop on Royal Park's edge.
Royal Park station on the Upfield line sits close by.
Check your clothes for butterflies before leaving the Butterfly House.
Bring a hat and water for warm summer days.
