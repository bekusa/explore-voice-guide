The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the path, Beaver Creek runs over stones, low and steady. Wind moves through the pale branches of the sycamore trees. Their bark peels away in patches of white, grey and green. Now lift your eyes. High in the cliff, tucked into a deep hollow, sits a building of stone and mud. It looks like part of the rock itself, the same warm, sandy colour. For a moment, it is hard to tell where the cliff ends and the walls begin.

This is Montezuma Castle, in the Verde Valley of central Arizona. And here is the first surprise. The name is wrong twice over. It is not a castle, and it has nothing to do with Montezuma, the Aztec ruler of Mexico. When American settlers came to this valley in the nineteenth century, they saw the dwelling and assumed the Aztecs had built it. The idea was mistaken, but the name stayed.

The real builders were a people archaeologists call the Sinagua. The name comes from Spanish, meaning "without water". It was given in the twentieth century, not by the people themselves. We do not know what they called themselves. The Sinagua farmed this valley for centuries. They grew corn, beans and squash. They also gathered wild plants and hunted in the hills. Their story is woven into the wider history of the ancient Southwest.

Most of the building you see went up during roughly the twelfth to fourteenth centuries. That makes it several hundred years old. It rises about five stories and holds around twenty rooms. Think about the work for a moment. Every stone, every basket of mud, every heavy roof beam had to be carried up the cliff face. The builders used ladders and handholds to reach the alcove. There were no cranes and no pack animals.

The materials came from close by. The walls are made of limestone blocks set in mud mortar. Look closely at the upper walls and you may see the ends of wooden beams. Many of these beams came from the sycamore trees that still grow along the creek. Across the beams, builders laid smaller sticks, grasses and mud to make the ceilings and floors. It is a simple recipe, but it has lasted for centuries.

Why build so high? Nobody can say for certain, but there are good guesses. The alcove gives shelter from rain and snow. In summer, the overhang casts shade over the rooms. In winter, the low sun reaches deep inside and warms the stone. The height also kept the rooms safe from flooding when Beaver Creek rose. And a high position gave a long view over the valley and the fields.

Life here was not only inside the walls. Families likely spent much of the day outside. They worked in the fields along the creek, ground corn on stone, and made pottery and woven goods. The Sinagua also traded widely. Archaeologists have found objects in this region that came from far away, such as shells from the coast. These finds show that this quiet valley was connected to a large network of people.

Now turn your attention a little way along the cliff, to the base of the rock. Here are the remains of a second, larger building, often called Castle A. Today it is mostly low walls and foundations. This structure was once bigger than the dwelling above, with many more rooms. Archaeologists believe fire damaged it. Many visitors walk past these ruins without stopping. They are worth a pause.

Around the early fifteenth century, the Sinagua left the Verde Valley. Again, the full reason is not known. Drought, conflict, disease or changing ways of life may all have played a part. But the people did not simply vanish. Hopi people and the Yavapai and Apache peoples of this region hold traditions that connect them to this place. For them, it is not an abandoned ruin. It is part of a living history.

In 1906, President Theodore Roosevelt declared Montezuma Castle a national monument. It was among the very first places protected under a new law called the Antiquities Act. That law was written to stop the looting of ancient sites across the American West. And looting had already happened here. Before protection, many objects were carried away. What remains in the alcove survived partly because the cliff is so hard to reach.

For decades, visitors were allowed to climb ladders up into the dwelling. In the 1950s, the park closed it to the public. The reason was simple. Thousands of feet and hands were wearing down the walls. Today, you see the building from the trail below, as the builders' neighbours once did. Inside the visitor centre, a model shows what the rooms look like from the inside.

As you walk the short paved loop, notice the other life of this place. The creek draws birds and other animals. You may hear ravens calling from the cliff, or see lizards on warm rocks. Look up at the limestone above the alcove. Dark streaks and small holes mark the cliff, shaped over a very long time by water and weather. The same forces that carved the hollow also gave the Sinagua their home.

Before you go, stand still for one more minute. Hear the creek again, and the sycamore leaves moving. Look at the alcove and imagine smoke from a cooking fire drifting out. Imagine voices, children, and someone climbing a ladder with a basket of corn. Then look at the trail under your own feet. You are standing where people have looked up at this cliff for many hundreds of years.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the short paved loop trail at a slow pace.
Visit the museum model to see the interior rooms.
Bring binoculars to study the walls and roof beams.
Come early in summer to avoid the strongest heat.
Pair your visit with nearby Montezuma Well, a separate site.
