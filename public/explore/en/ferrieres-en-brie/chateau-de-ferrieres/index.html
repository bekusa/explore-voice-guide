The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and wind moves through tall old trees at the edge of the lawn. Ahead of you rises a pale stone palace, square and solid, with towers at each corner and rows of tall windows catching the light. It looks like a building that belongs in the English countryside. That impression is correct, and it is the first clue to the story of this place.

This is the Château de Ferrières, in the forested country of Brie, east of Paris. It was built in the middle of the nineteenth century for Baron James de Rothschild. He was the founder of the French branch of the banking family, and one of the most powerful financiers in Europe. He wanted a country house that would show the world exactly who he was. He did not choose a French architect. He chose an Englishman.

That Englishman was Joseph Paxton. Paxton was not a classical architect by training. He was a gardener, and he became famous for designing the Crystal Palace, the enormous glass and iron hall built in London for the Great Exhibition of 1851. Paxton had already designed Mentmore Towers in England for another member of the Rothschild family. Ferrières followed that model closely. So the building in front of you is, in a sense, an English design planted on French soil.

Construction ran through the second half of the 1850s. The style is often called neo-Renaissance. That means it borrows the forms of sixteenth-century European palaces — the towers, the carved stone, the balanced windows — but builds them with nineteenth-century money and technology. Look at the façades as you walk around. Each side has its own character. The towers anchor the corners, and the roofline is busy with chimneys, dormers, and decoration.

Now here is something many visitors miss. Paxton the glass designer did not forget his specialty. The heart of the château is a great central hall, and it is lit from above through a glass roof. Instead of a dark interior surrounded by rooms, the house has a bright core. Daylight falls into the middle of the building. It is a small Crystal Palace hidden inside a Renaissance shell.

The Rothschilds filled the house with paintings, sculpture, furniture, and fine objects collected from across Europe. This was a working stage for power. Bankers, aristocrats, and politicians came here. The most famous early guest was the Emperor Napoleon III, who visited in 1862 for a day of hunting. For that visit, the composer Gioachino Rossini, a friend of the family, wrote a hunting chorus. Music, politics, and money all met in these rooms.

There is a second surprise, and it concerns your nose. Grand houses of this period had a problem. Kitchen smells drifted into the reception rooms. At Ferrières, the kitchens were placed in a separate building, away from the main house. Food was carried through an underground passage, so guests never saw the work and never smelled the cooking. It is a quiet reminder of how many servants made this life possible.

Only a few years later, history arrived at the door in a much harsher way. In 1870, during the war between France and Prussia, Prussian forces moved toward Paris. The Prussian king and his chancellor, Otto von Bismarck, used Ferrières as their headquarters. In September of that year, the French minister Jules Favre came here to meet Bismarck and discuss terms. The talks failed. The fighting continued, and Paris went under siege. A house built for celebration became a room for hard negotiation.

The twentieth century brought more disruption. During the Second World War, German forces occupied the château, and the family was forced away. After the war, the house stood under a heavy shadow for some years. Then, in the 1950s, Baron Guy de Rothschild and his wife, Marie-Hélène, brought it back to life. They restored the rooms and reopened the house to society.

Marie-Hélène became known for her extravagant parties. The most talked-about was a Surrealist ball in the early 1970s. Surrealism was an art movement that loved dreams, strange images, and impossible combinations. Guests reportedly came in elaborate masks and costumes, and the decorations played with the absurd. Photographs from that night still circulate today. It is strange to imagine those dreamlike figures moving under the same glass roof where emperors once dined.

In the 1970s, the family made a generous decision. They gave the château and much of its park to the universities of Paris. Later, the estate passed into public management, and today it is open to visitors. The outbuildings have found a new purpose too. A school for cooking and hospitality now works on the estate. So the old idea of fine food at Ferrières continues, though now students learn the craft openly instead of hiding it underground.

Before you leave, give the park some time. It was laid out in the English landscape style. That style avoids strict geometry. Instead, it uses open lawns, curving paths, groups of trees, and water to create views that look natural, even when every one was planned. Walk toward the lake and turn back. From there the château sits in its frame of trees, exactly as its designers intended you to see it.

So stand still for one more moment. Notice the colour of the stone in this light, and the shape of the towers against the sky. Think of the gardener who became an architect, the banker who wanted a palace, the emperor at the hunt, and the minister who came here to ask for peace. Then listen again to the gravel under your feet as you walk on. Many people have walked this same ground before you, each with their own reason for coming.
