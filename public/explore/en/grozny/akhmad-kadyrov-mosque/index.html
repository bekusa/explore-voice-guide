The guide

The story

Listen first. Water moves in the fountains around you, a soft and steady sound under the noise of the avenue. Then look up. White stone rises in front of you, pale against the sky, and four slender minarets frame a wide central dome. In strong daylight the facade can almost hurt the eyes. In the evening it glows. Either way, the first thing most visitors feel here is scale — and a sense of quiet that the city traffic cannot quite reach.

You are standing at the Akhmad Kadyrov Mosque, in the centre of Grozny, the capital of the Chechen Republic in Russia's North Caucasus. Many locals know it by another name: the Heart of Chechnya. That name belongs to the whole complex, not only to the prayer hall. It opened in 2008, and it was one of the first great public buildings to rise in a city that had been badly damaged only a few years before.

That context matters. During the wars of the 1990s and early 2000s, much of Grozny was destroyed. Whole districts were reduced to ruins. When reconstruction began, the authorities wanted symbols that people could see from far away — buildings that said the city had returned. This mosque became the most visible of those symbols. So when you look at the clean white stone, remember that the ground under it carries a much harder story.

The mosque is named after Akhmad Kadyrov, a former mufti of Chechnya who later became head of the republic. He was killed in a bomb attack in Grozny in 2004. His son, Ramzan Kadyrov, now leads the republic, and the family name appears on many streets and buildings across the city. For many Chechens the name carries deep emotion. For outside observers it is also tied to complex political debates. Both views exist, and you will hear both.

Now look at the shape of the building. If you have ever visited Istanbul, it may feel familiar. The design follows the classical Ottoman style — the architecture of the Turkish empire that ruled much of the Middle East for centuries. Builders often compare it to the Sultan Ahmed Mosque, better known as the Blue Mosque. You can see the same idea here: one large central dome, smaller domes and half-domes around it, and tall pencil-thin minarets at the corners.

Those four minarets are the first surprise for many visitors. They are among the tallest minarets in Russia, and from the ground they seem even taller because they are so narrow. Each one ends in a sharp pointed cap, the classic Ottoman form. Stand at one corner of the park and try to fit all four into your view at once. You will probably need to walk back further than you expect.

The second surprise is the stone itself. The exterior is faced with a pale stone that was brought from abroad, and much of the craftsmanship came from Turkish specialists. The stone is chosen to keep its light colour, and in the hot Caucasus summer it stays cooler than you might think. Put your hand on a sunlit wall, if you can reach one, and feel the difference from the paving under your feet.

Before you go inside, some practical points. This is a working mosque, not a museum. Men should wear long trousers, and women should cover their hair, arms and legs. At the entrance, women are often offered long robes and headscarves to borrow. Everyone removes their shoes. Visitors are usually welcome outside prayer times, but during prayers the hall belongs to the worshippers. Move quietly, keep your voice low, and ask before you photograph people.

Inside, the light changes completely. The white of the exterior gives way to soft carpet, painted patterns and marble. Look up into the dome. Blue, gold and white designs run in circles toward the centre, and Arabic calligraphy curls around the rims. Below hang large crystal chandeliers. Their forms are said to recall the great holy sites of Islam, though the meaning of each detail is best explained by the staff, who are often happy to talk.

Take a moment to notice the floor. The carpet is designed with repeated rows, so each worshipper has a clear place to stand. Every row faces the mihrab, the decorated niche in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca. Near it stands the minbar, the high stepped pulpit from which the Friday sermon is given. Even when the hall is empty, these rows show you how many people can gather here shoulder to shoulder.

Faith in Chechnya has its own local character. Most Chechens are Sunni Muslims, and many follow Sufi brotherhoods — mystical traditions that emphasise remembrance of God. One well-known practice is the zikr, in which men chant and move together in a circle. You may not see it here, but it helps to know that this grand Ottoman-style building sits within a much older mountain tradition of prayer, family clans and hospitality.

Step back outside and walk into the park. The Heart of Chechnya complex includes gardens, fountains and an Islamic university nearby. Families come here in the evening to stroll, and children run along the paths. Across the area you can also see the glass towers of Grozny City, built a few years after the mosque. Old Ottoman forms and new glass skyscrapers stand in the same view — a clear picture of how fast this city has been rebuilt.

If you can, stay until dusk. As the sky darkens, lights come on across the facade, and the minarets turn into lines of light. The fountains catch the glow. For photographers this is the moment to frame the dome with the reflections in the water. For everyone else it is simply a calm time to sit on a bench and watch the city change from day to night.

Before you leave, turn around once more. Look at the white dome, the four minarets, the families walking slowly under the trees. Not long ago this place was part of a broken city. Now it is where people meet, pray and rest. Listen again to the water in the fountains... and carry that sound with you as you walk back into the streets of Grozny.
