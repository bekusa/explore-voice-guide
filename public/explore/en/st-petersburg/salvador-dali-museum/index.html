The guide

The story

Listen first. Gulls call over the harbour, and a light wind comes off the water of Tampa Bay. In front of you stands a pale concrete box. From one side, a great wave of glass seems to burst out of it, like something trying to escape. On a sunny day, the triangles of that glass flash white and blue. This is the Dalí Museum in Saint Petersburg, Florida, and it may be the strangest home a Spanish painter ever found.

It is fair to ask why it is here at all. Salvador Dalí was born in Catalonia, in northeast Spain, and he spent much of his life between Spain, Paris and New York. He never lived on this coast. The answer is a love story about collecting. It involves a couple from Ohio, a first purchase made in the 1940s, and a search for a home that drew interest from cities across the United States.

The couple were A. Reynolds Morse and his wife Eleanor. They saw Dalí's work, admired it, and bought their first painting early in their marriage. Over the following decades they kept buying. They also became friends with Dalí and his wife Gala, and they met the artist many times. Slowly their house filled with canvases, drawings, prints and objects. In time, they owned one of the largest collections of Dalí's work anywhere, outside of Spain.

By the late 1970s the collection had outgrown any private home. The Morses wanted it kept together, and they wanted it open to the public. Many places hesitated. Saint Petersburg made an offer, and the collection arrived on this waterfront. The first museum opened in 1982 inside a converted building near the water. For almost three decades, visitors came to see surrealism in a modest space that was never designed for art.

The building in front of you replaced it, and it opened in 2011. Look at how it works as two ideas at once. The concrete box is the reason. It has very thick walls, built to protect the paintings from the hurricanes that strike this coast. The glass bubble is the dream. Locals call it the Enigma. It is made of more than a thousand triangular glass panels, each one cut to fit its own place in the curve.

That contrast is very Dalí. He liked to set hard logic against wild imagination. He studied science, mathematics and optics with real enthusiasm, and then he painted melting clocks. So the architects gave him a building that is half bunker, half soap bubble. Here is something many visitors miss — the glass is not decoration. It lets daylight into the lobby and frames a view of the bay. Walk inside and look up to see how the panels fit together.

Now find the staircase. A white concrete spiral rises through the atrium and turns as it climbs, with no visible central post. Dalí was fascinated by spirals. He saw them in snail shells, in the horns of a rhinoceros, and, later in his life, in the double helix of DNA. The stair is a quiet tribute to that obsession. Take it slowly. As you climb, the view of the lobby and the glass changes with every turn.

The galleries upstairs move broadly through Dalí's life. His early work can surprise people. Before surrealism, he painted landscapes, portraits and quiet scenes of the Catalan coast. Some are calm and almost traditional. You can see a young painter learning from the masters and experimenting with new styles. Then the dream images arrive... long shadows, empty beaches, soft objects, and the rocky coast of his home region repeated again and again.

The largest rooms hold some of his huge late canvases, and they are worth time. Look for The Hallucinogenic Toreador. At first it seems to show many copies of the Venus de Milo, the famous armless Greek statue. Stand back, and the shapes form the face of a bullfighter. A green scarf becomes his tie, and a shadow becomes his cheek. Dalí loved these double images, where one picture hides inside another. Your eye keeps switching between them.

Nearby, The Discovery of America by Christopher Columbus fills a wall. It shows a young Columbus stepping ashore, with a banner that carries the face of Gala, Dalí's wife and lifelong muse. Look closely, and you may find Dalí himself, kneeling in the shadows as a monk. Gala appears again and again in his work. She was his partner, manager and model, and he often signed paintings with both their names. Finding her face becomes a small game in these rooms.

When the galleries feel intense, step outside into the garden. It is a softer place, with plants that grow well in the Florida heat and small surprises along the paths. You may find a bench shaped like a melting clock, a nod to his best-known image. There is also a spiral path set into the ground. Children often walk it to the centre, and adults usually follow them. The sea air helps after so much colour indoors.

Look for the Wish Tree, too. It is a living tree, and its branches are covered in small paper wristbands. Visitors write a wish on their admission band and tie it to a branch. On a breezy day, hundreds of bands move together, and they make a soft rustling sound. It is not an artwork by Dalí, but it feels close to his spirit — ordinary objects, gathered together, until they become something strange and personal.

The museum also changes with time. It hosts temporary exhibitions, often linking Dalí with other artists, or with science and technology. In recent years it has used digital tools to bring the artist's voice and humour to new audiences. Check what is on when you arrive, because the special shows can fill a whole floor. The permanent collection stays at the heart of the visit, but the second half of your day may look very different from someone else's.

Before you leave, stand outside once more and face the building. Notice how the glass now holds the evening sky, or the reflection of a passing cloud. Behind you, the boats sit in the harbour, and people walk along the waterfront. A couple from Ohio started all of this with a single painting. As you walk away, look back at the bubble one last time... it never looks quite the same twice.
