The guide

The story

Listen first. Gravel shifts under your shoes, and somewhere beyond the wall the cicadas keep up their dry, steady rattle. The air smells of warm pine and lavender. In front of you, pale limestone glows in the Provençal light, and the jagged grey ridge of the Alpilles hills rises just to the south. This is a quiet place. It has been a place of retreat for a very long time, and in some ways it still is.

This is the Monastery of Saint-Paul de Mausole, on the southern edge of Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. Most visitors come here for one reason, and we will get to him soon. But the stones around you are far older than any painting. The church and the cloister date back to the Middle Ages, to roughly the eleventh and twelfth centuries. For centuries, this was a religious community, living by the rhythm of bells and prayer.

The name itself holds a small surprise. "Mausole" is generally thought to come from the Roman mausoleum that still stands a short walk up the road. That monument, together with a Roman triumphal arch, sits at the entrance to Glanum, an ancient town whose ruins lie at the foot of the hills. So when you stand here, you are standing on a very old route. Gauls, Romans, monks and pilgrims all passed this way long before the tourists did.

Now walk into the cloister, if you can. This is the heart of the monastery, and it is a fine example of Romanesque building. Romanesque is the style of Europe before the great Gothic cathedrals, with round arches, thick walls and modest, heavy forms. Look at the small columns that support the arcades. Their capitals are carved with leaves, animals and simple figures. Each one is a little different. The stonework is calm and human in scale, and that calm is part of why this place mattered later.

In the early nineteenth century, the monastery took on a new role. It became an asylum, a private institution for people living with mental illness. That changed everything about its story. The cloister, built for prayer and silence, became a place for rest and treatment. Treatment then was very limited. Patients often had little to do except wait, walk in the garden and take long baths, which doctors believed would calm the nerves.

In May 1889, a Dutch painter arrived here of his own free will. His name was Vincent van Gogh. A few months earlier, in the town of Arles, he had suffered a severe crisis, the episode in which he cut off part of his own ear. Frightened by his attacks, he asked to be admitted. His brother Theo, an art dealer in Paris, paid for his stay. The director at the time was Doctor Théophile Peyron, who allowed Vincent something unusual for a patient — a second room, to use as a studio.

Here is the fact that surprises many visitors. Van Gogh stayed for only about a year, until May 1890. Yet in that time he produced around one hundred and fifty paintings, plus many drawings. That is roughly one painting every two or three days, between periods when illness stopped him from working at all. Some of his most loved works were made right here, including The Starry Night, painted in June 1889, and the famous Irises, painted from the flower beds in the garden.

Think about the view he had. From his bedroom window, he looked over a walled wheat field toward the Alpilles. He painted that field again and again — in green spring, in golden summer, under rain, with a reaper at work, with the sun rising. He was not allowed to paint in his bedroom, so he made sketches there and worked them into paintings in the studio. The Starry Night itself combines the view from his window with memory and imagination. The village and its church spire came from his mind, not from what he could see.

When his health allowed, he walked outside the walls, often with a staff member beside him. He found the olive groves and the dark cypress trees that became central to his work from this period. In his letters to Theo, he wrote about the cypresses with real fascination, describing their dark green against the bright southern light. Look around as you walk and you will still see those flame-shaped trees, and the silvery olives, growing in the fields nearby.

Inside, you can visit a reconstruction of a room like the one he lived in. It is small and plain, with a narrow iron bed, a chair and a barred window. Take a moment there. It is a modest space, and it is easy to imagine both the loneliness and the intense focus of the man who slept in it. Out in the garden, the flower beds and paths are kept in a way that echoes his paintings, and reproductions of his works are placed near the spots he painted.

Here is a second thing many people miss. Saint-Paul de Mausole is still a working psychiatric clinic today. The part you visit is only one section of a living institution. Patients continue to receive care beyond the areas open to the public. The clinic has also developed an art therapy programme, and work by current patients is sometimes shown and sold here. So the connection between this place, mental illness and painting did not end with Van Gogh. It continues quietly, out of sight.

That is worth remembering as you move through the cloister and the garden. This is not a museum frozen in 1889. It is a place where people still come for help, and where silence still has a purpose. Speak softly. Respect the boundaries and the closed doors. The calm you feel here is not decoration. For some people on the other side of these walls, it is part of their recovery, just as it was for a painter more than a century ago.

Before you leave, stand for a moment in the garden or by the field wall and look south. The ridge of the Alpilles is still there, pale and broken against the sky, exactly where he painted it. Notice the light on the stone, the movement of the olive leaves, the dark upright shape of a cypress. As you walk back toward town, you may find yourself seeing the landscape a little differently — brighter, more restless, a little more alive. That is his gift, still at work on the road outside.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit in early morning for soft light and fewer crowds.
Walk the signposted path showing sites of his paintings.
Combine your visit with the nearby Roman ruins of Glanum.
Keep voices low out of respect for current patients.
Come in late spring to see irises in bloom.
