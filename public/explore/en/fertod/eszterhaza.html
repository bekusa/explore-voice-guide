The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your feet. It crunches softly as you cross the wide forecourt, and ahead of you the palace opens its arms. Two long wings curve forward in a horseshoe shape, painted a warm, buttery yellow. In the afternoon the facade almost glows. The air here is flat and open, with the wide plain of western Hungary all around. Somewhere beyond the trees lies a broad, shallow lake. And in this quiet country corner, one of the great musical courts of Europe once came to life.

This is Eszterháza, the palace of the Esterházy family, in the small town of Fertőd. The Esterházys were among the richest and most powerful noble families in the Kingdom of Hungary. They held vast estates, many castles, and close ties to the Habsburg court in Vienna. Many people call this place the Hungarian Versailles. The comparison is not only about size. It is about ambition — the wish to build, far from any capital, a world of art, music and display.

The man behind that ambition was Prince Nikolaus Esterházy, the first of that name to rule the family. History remembers him as Nikolaus the Magnificent. He earned the name. From the 1760s he turned a more modest hunting lodge into this grand residence. Swampy ground had to be drained and reshaped. Gardens, fountains and side buildings followed. The prince spent enormous sums here. For him, Eszterháza was not a holiday house. It became the centre of his life.

Stand still for a moment in front of the gates. The ironwork is fine and delicate, a lace of scrolls and curls in dark metal. It frames the palace like the edge of a stage. That feeling is not an accident. Everything here was designed to impress a guest arriving by carriage. You see the curving wings first. Then the central block rises behind them, with its balconies and its double staircase. The eye moves forward step by step, toward the main door.

The style mixes late Baroque and Rococo. Baroque means grand, theatrical architecture with strong symmetry. Rococo is its lighter, more playful cousin, full of curves, gilding and pale colours. Inside, many rooms show that lightness. You will see painted ceilings, carved and gilded panels, mirrors, and decorated stoves. The grand hall on the upper floor was built for receptions and music. Imagine it full of candlelight, silk, and the sound of a small orchestra tuning up.

And that orchestra had a famous leader. Joseph Haydn, one of the founders of the classical symphony and string quartet, served the Esterházy family for decades. He spent much of that time here, at Eszterháza. He composed, rehearsed, conducted, and managed the musicians. Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Some of the music that shaped modern concert life was written for this remote palace, for one prince and his guests.

Haydn himself said that isolation helped him. Cut off from the wider world, he could experiment freely. He had a skilled orchestra at hand, and he could test new ideas and hear them at once. There was no one nearby to copy, and no fashion to follow. He later said, in words his early biographers recorded, that he was forced to become original. It is worth remembering that line as you walk these quiet halls.

The best-known story from this court is about a symphony. One summer, the prince stayed at Eszterháza much longer than usual. The musicians missed their families, who were not allowed to live here. So Haydn wrote a clever ending into a new work. In the final movement, the players stopped one by one. Each blew out his candle and left. At the end, only two violins remained. The prince understood the hint. The court left for home soon after. Today we call it the Farewell Symphony.

Music was not the only entertainment. The palace grounds once held an opera house, where Haydn staged many operas, and a marionette theatre for puppet opera. Princes, generals and diplomats came to watch. In 1773 the Empress Maria Theresa visited, and the court staged great celebrations in her honour. A popular saying attributed to her claims that if she wanted to hear good opera, she went to Eszterháza. The words may be legend, but the reputation was real.

That golden age did not last. When Prince Nikolaus died in 1790, his successor had little interest in this remote palace. The orchestra was dismissed, and the court moved away. The theatres have not survived. Over the following generations, the palace fell into long neglect. In the twentieth century, war and its aftermath damaged it further. Here is the second surprise. Much of the beauty you see today is the result of careful restoration, carried out over many decades.

The town around you also carries this story in its name. For a long time the settlement was simply called Eszterháza, after the palace. In the middle of the twentieth century it merged with a neighbouring village and took a new name, Fertőd. That name comes from Lake Fertő, which Austrians call the Neusiedler See. The palace now belongs to the wider Fertő and Neusiedler See cultural landscape, which UNESCO lists as a World Heritage Site shared by Hungary and Austria.

Before you leave, walk out into the gardens behind the palace. The formal French-style layout runs out from the central building in long, straight lines. Paths, lawns and clipped hedges create order on the flat land. Turn around and look back. From here the garden facade is calmer and more private than the grand entrance side. Listen for birds in the trees and the low sound of the wind across open country. It is easy to understand why a composer could work here undisturbed.

So take one last look at the yellow walls and the tall windows. Think of the candles that once burned behind them, and of musicians walking out one by one into the summer night. Today the music still returns, because concerts are held here again. As you walk back across the gravel, notice how quiet the forecourt becomes. That silence is part of the story too. Out of it, two centuries ago, came some of Europe's most joyful sound.
