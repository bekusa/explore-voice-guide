The guide

The story

Listen first. Beneath your feet, the gravel crunches softly, and somewhere ahead you can hear water moving slowly around stone. The long avenue of trees opens, and the light changes. Then the château appears. It does not sit beside the river like most castles. It stands across it, pale stone resting on arches, with the green water of the Cher sliding underneath. Few buildings in France feel so balanced between land and water. Take a moment here before we walk closer.

This is the Château de Chenonceau, in the heart of the Loire Valley. People often call it the Château des Dames, the Castle of the Ladies. The name fits. Over five centuries, a series of remarkable women shaped this place. They built it, fought over it, mourned in it, filled it with conversation, and protected it in dangerous years. As we walk, you will meet them one by one. Each one left a mark you can still see in the stone.

Before the château, there was a mill and a fortified manor here, owned by the Marques family. Look at the round tower standing alone in the forecourt. That is the Marques Tower, the last large piece of the older castle. In the early sixteenth century, a royal finance official named Thomas Bohier bought the estate. While he was often away on royal service in Italy, his wife, Katherine Briçonnet, oversaw much of the building work. So the very first lady of Chenonceau was, in practice, its builder.

Their new house rose on the stone foundations of the old mill, right at the edge of the river. It was modern for its time, with a straight central staircase rather than the usual spiral. That was a fresh Italian idea in France. But fortune did not last. After Bohier's death, the family's debts to the crown caught up with them. King Francis the First took the château. And so Chenonceau passed into royal hands, and into the most famous chapter of its story.

King Henry the Second gave the estate to Diane de Poitiers, the woman he loved for most of his life. Diane was older than the king, intelligent, and a skilled manager of land. She made Chenonceau hers. She laid out a large formal garden beside the river, and she had a bridge built from the château across the Cher. For a few years, this was her private world. Then, in 1559, everything changed. Henry was wounded in a jousting tournament and died.

Here is the second woman of power: Catherine de Medici, Henry's wife and now queen regent. For years she had watched her husband's mistress hold this beautiful house. Now Catherine acted. She made Diane give up Chenonceau in exchange for another castle, the Château de Chaumont, a little further up the Loire. Diane left. Catherine moved in. She created her own garden, facing Diane's, and she held lavish festivities here. You can walk both gardens today, side by side, rivals still.

Catherine's greatest addition stands right in front of you. On top of Diane's bridge, she built a long gallery, two storeys high. It is the part of Chenonceau that makes the building look like it is walking across the water. Inside, the gallery runs for about sixty metres, with a floor of black and white tiles and tall windows on both sides. Stand in the middle and look out. You see the river on your left and on your right, as if you were on a ship.

Now a quieter story. After King Henry the Third was assassinated in 1589, his widow, Louise of Lorraine, came to live here. She wore white, the royal colour of mourning in that time, and people called her the White Queen. Her bedroom was decorated in black, with symbols of grief and tears painted on it. The room you can visit is a later reconstruction. But it carries the same sombre mood. It is a strange contrast with the bright river outside.

In the eighteenth century, a very different lady arrived. Louise Dupin turned Chenonceau into a place of ideas. Writers, scientists and thinkers of the Enlightenment came to talk here. The young Jean-Jacques Rousseau worked for the family as a secretary and tutor, and he wrote warmly about his time at the château. When the French Revolution came, many noble houses were damaged. Chenonceau survived. Locals say Madame Dupin's good relations with the villagers helped protect it.

Here is something many visitors miss. Go down into the kitchens. They are built inside the massive stone piers that hold the château above the river. You will find copper pots hanging in rows, a butcher's room, and old ovens. There was once a small landing where boats could deliver food straight from the water. It is easy to imagine cooks working here, with the sound of the current coming through the thick walls. It is the working heart of a house built for show.

The twentieth century brought the hardest chapter. The Menier family, famous for making chocolate, bought the château in 1913, and it still belongs to them today. During the First World War, they turned the gallery into a hospital ward for wounded soldiers. During the Second World War, the line between occupied and free France ran along the Cher. According to many accounts, the gallery's far door became a way for people to slip across to the southern bank and escape.

As you move through the rooms, notice the flowers. Fresh arrangements fill the fireplaces, tables and corners, and they change with the seasons. Look, too, for royal initials carved and painted around the château. One monogram joins an H with a letter that can be read as a C for Catherine, or as a D for Diane. People have argued about it for centuries. Perhaps the ambiguity suits a house where two women once claimed the same king.

Before you leave, walk back toward the edge of the garden and turn around. Look at the arches again, and the reflection of the gallery in the slow river. Listen for the water, the birds in the trees, the gravel under other feet. These stones have held queens, writers, cooks, nurses and people running for their lives. Now they hold you, for a moment. As you walk back down the avenue, look over your shoulder once more.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to walk the gallery before tour groups fill it.
Walk the riverside paths for a side view of the arches.
Allow time for both formal gardens, not only the house.
Visit the kitchens below; many people skip the lower level.
Bring comfortable shoes for the long tree-lined approach avenue.
