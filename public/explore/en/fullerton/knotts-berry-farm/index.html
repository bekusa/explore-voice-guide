The guide

The story

Listen for the hiss of steam. Somewhere ahead, a narrow-gauge locomotive is building pressure, and the smell of coal smoke drifts over weathered boards and dusty paths. Underfoot, the ground feels like a frontier town, though you are standing in Buena Park, just a short drive from Fullerton, in the middle of suburban Orange County. Welcome to Knott's Berry Farm, a place that grew from a roadside fruit stand into one of America's oldest theme parks.

The story begins with a couple, not a corporation. Walter and Cordelia Knott came to this area in the 1920s and farmed berries on rented land. They sold fruit, jams, and pies from a simple stand beside the road. This was flat farm country then, with fields and orchards where the parking lots and freeways now run. Nothing about it suggested what would come next... except, perhaps, the stubbornness of the people working it.

Here is the first surprise. The boysenberry, the fruit that made this place famous, did not come from the Knotts. It was a hybrid created by a grower named Rudolph Boysen, who had given up on his experiment. In the early 1930s, Walter Knott heard about the lost berry. With help from an agricultural researcher, he tracked down a few neglected vines on Boysen's old farm. He nursed them back to health, planted them, and began selling the large, dark, tart fruit.

Then came the Great Depression. Money was short, and the family needed more income. In 1934, Cordelia Knott began serving fried chicken dinners on her wedding china, with biscuits, boysenberry pie, and preserves. Word spread quickly. Within a few years, the lines of hungry customers stretched outside the little tea room for hours. Mrs. Knott's Chicken Dinner Restaurant still operates today, just outside the park gates, and many families treat it as part of the visit.

Those long lines created a problem — and Walter Knott saw an opportunity. Waiting diners were bored. So he began building attractions to keep them entertained. Around 1940, he started creating a replica of an Old West town, with wooden storefronts, a saloon, and a jail. This was the beginning of Ghost Town. At first, there was no admission charge at all. People simply wandered through while they waited for a table.

Walter Knott had a deep love for the American West and its pioneer stories. Ghost Town was his tribute to that history. Some of its structures were brought here from real western settlements, while others were built to look old from the start. As you walk the streets, notice the wear on the boards, the hand-painted signs, and the small details in shop windows. This was not designed in an office. It was assembled piece by piece by someone who cared.

Here is the second surprise. Walter Knott did not only build a fake ghost town. In the 1950s, he bought a real one. Calico, an old silver-mining town in the Mojave Desert, had fallen into ruin. Knott purchased much of it and restored it, and he later helped place it in public hands. Its name lives on here, in the Calico Mine Ride and in the Ghost Town and Calico Railway, whose vintage steam equipment once worked real mountain lines in the West.

The railway is worth your time. It carries passengers on a slow loop around the park, past cactus, rock, and painted scenery. Actors sometimes stage a train robbery along the route, with masked bandits stepping aboard. It is theatre, of course, but it carries an older style of entertainment — live performers, real steam, and a sense of humour. Few modern parks still run trains like this.

Nearby, look for the Timber Mountain Log Ride, which opened in 1969. It is often described as one of the earliest log flume rides of its kind, and it helped make water rides popular across the industry. You float through a sawmill scene filled with figures and machinery before a final drop. On a warm Southern California afternoon, the splash at the end feels like a gift. Expect to get damp, especially in the front seat.

As the decades passed, the farm kept growing. In 1966, the Knotts built a full-size replica of Independence Hall from Philadelphia, across the boulevard from the main park. In 1968, the park finally began charging admission. Later came Camp Snoopy, a land for younger children featuring the Peanuts characters, and bigger thrill rides. GhostRider, a long wooden roller coaster, opened in 1998 and now roars above Ghost Town with a sound you can hear across the park.

The Knott family ran the business for most of the twentieth century. In 1997, they sold the park to Cedar Fair, a company that operates amusement parks across North America. More recently, Cedar Fair merged with Six Flags. Even so, the family's fingerprints remain everywhere — in the berry products, the chicken dinners, and the Old West streets. The park is a strange and pleasant mix: a modern thrill park wrapped around a Depression-era family farm.

The seasons also change the mood. Each autumn, the park transforms for Knott's Scary Farm, a Halloween event that has run since the 1970s and is widely credited as one of the first of its kind. Fog machines, costumed monsters, and haunted mazes fill the same streets you are walking now. In spring, the park often celebrates the boysenberry itself, with special foods and drinks built around the fruit that started everything.

For a moment, stop and look around. Notice how the old and the new sit side by side. A wooden storefront stands in the shadow of a steel coaster track. A steam whistle cuts through the screams from a thrill ride. Families queue for funnel cakes where, nearly a century ago, farmers picked berries by hand. This place did not follow a master plan. It grew in layers, one good idea at a time.

As you walk on, listen again for that steam whistle. Taste a little boysenberry jam if you can, and think of Walter tending those half-dead vines. Look at the faded boards of Ghost Town and imagine diners in the 1940s, waiting for chicken, wandering these streets with nothing to do but wonder. The farm is still here... just wearing a different coat. Enjoy the rest of your day.

Before you go

Practical tips

Ride the Calico Railway early for a relaxed park overview.
Sit away from the front on the Log Ride to stay drier.
Book the chicken restaurant ahead on busy weekends.
Look for seasonal boysenberry treats during spring visits.
Check whether Scary Farm nights need a separate ticket.
