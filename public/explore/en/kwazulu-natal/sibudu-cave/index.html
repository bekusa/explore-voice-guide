The guide

The story

Listen first. Below the cliff, the uThongathi River runs over stones, and the air feels warm and damp. The sandstone under your hand is rough and slightly gritty. Above you, the rock curves outward like a half-open shell. This is not a deep, dark cave. It is a rock shelter, an overhang open to the light. For tens of thousands of years, people chose to live right here.

Sibudu sits in the hills of KwaZulu-Natal, inland from the coast and north of Durban. Today the land around it is a mix of sugar cane fields, scrub and patches of remaining forest. From the shelter you look down over the river valley. It is easy to see why people liked this spot. The overhang gives shade and shelter from rain. The river gives water. The valley would have held animals, plants and stone for tools.

What makes this place matter is the floor beneath your feet. Over a very long time, people lit fires, slept, ate and worked here. Each visit left a thin layer of ash, bone, plant remains and broken stone. Those layers built up, one on top of another, into a deep and detailed record. Archaeologists read these layers the way you might read the pages of a book, from the most recent at the top to the oldest at the bottom.

Most of that record belongs to a period called the Middle Stone Age. In southern Africa, this is the long stretch of time when early modern humans, people like us, made tools from carefully shaped stone flakes. At Sibudu, the deposits cover many thousands of years within that period. The oldest layers here reach back well beyond 70,000 years. That is far older than farming, writing or any city on Earth.

The site was first tested by archaeologists in the 1980s. The major work began in 1998, led by Professor Lyn Wadley of the University of the Witwatersrand in Johannesburg. Her team dug slowly and with great care. They recorded each thin layer, sampled the soil and studied tiny remains under the microscope. That patience is the reason Sibudu became so important to scientists around the world.

Here is the first surprise. In layers dated to about 77,000 years ago, the team found the remains of bedding. People had gathered sedges and rushes from the river and laid them on the floor to sleep on. These are among the oldest known plant beds anywhere in the world. Picture it — a family settling down for the night on a soft mat of green stems, firelight on the rock above.

And there is a detail most visitors never hear. Some of that bedding included leaves from a local tree in the laurel family. Those leaves contain natural chemicals that repel mosquitoes and other biting insects. It seems the people here understood the plants around them well. They may have chosen those leaves on purpose to keep pests away. Researchers also found signs that old bedding was sometimes burned, perhaps to clean the floor and get rid of insects.

The second surprise is about tools. At Sibudu, researchers found stone points with tiny traces of glue still attached. Analysis showed that people mixed plant gum with powdered ochre, a red iron-rich earth, to make a strong adhesive. They used it to fix stone tips onto wooden handles or shafts. Getting that mixture right takes planning, testing and skill. It is a kind of early chemistry, carried out beside a fire around 70,000 years ago.

Some small stone points from the site show the kind of damage that happens when a point hits a target at speed. Bone points were found here too. Together, these clues have led some researchers to argue that people at Sibudu may have used bows and arrows more than 60,000 years ago. Not everyone agrees on every detail. But the evidence has made Sibudu a central place in the debate about when hunting with arrows began.

There are also signs of something beyond survival. Small perforated shells, likely worn as beads, have been found in the deposits. Pieces of ochre show scraping and grinding. These finds suggest that people here decorated themselves and used colour. Things like beads carry meaning. They can show who belongs to which group, or mark a person as special. In that sense, the people who sheltered here were thinking in symbols, much as we do.

As you stand here, it helps to remember how much has changed. The coastline, the climate and the animals of this valley have all shifted many times since those first fires. Bones from the site show that people hunted a range of animals, from small creatures to larger antelope. The forest and grassland around the river grew and shrank over time. The layers in this shelter hold a record of those changes too.

A practical note. Sibudu is an active research site, not a developed tourist attraction. Access is limited, and visits usually need to be arranged in advance. The deposits are fragile and cannot be replaced. So please keep to any marked paths, do not touch exposed sections and never take anything away, not even a small stone. What looks like loose soil may be part of a layer that took a thousand years to form.

Before you leave, look again at the curve of the rock above you and the light falling across the floor. Listen to the river below. These are, in a simple way, the same things the people of Sibudu saw and heard. They chose this shelter for the shade, the water and the view. As you walk back down the slope, notice the plants along the riverbank. Some of them might be the same kinds people once gathered here for a good night's sleep.
