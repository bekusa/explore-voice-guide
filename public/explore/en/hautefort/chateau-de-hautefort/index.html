The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel first. It crunches under your shoes as the path climbs toward the gate, and then the sound softens as you reach the lawns. Above you, pale stone walls catch the light. The rooftops rise in steep slate slopes and rounded domes. On a clear day the air smells of cut grass and box hedges warming in the sun. Take a breath here. You are standing on one of the great hilltops of the Périgord, in southwest France.

The château sits on a high spur of land above the village of Hautefort. The name itself comes from that position, meaning something like the high fortress. From up here you can see why people chose this rock. The ground falls away on several sides, and the green countryside of the Dordogne spreads out in every direction. For centuries, whoever held this hill could watch the roads, the farms and the valleys below.

The earliest story of this place belongs to the Middle Ages, and to a poet. In the twelfth century the castle was linked to Bertran de Born, a lord and a troubadour. Troubadours were poet-musicians who wrote songs in Occitan, the old language of southern France. Bertran is remembered for his songs about love, and also for his songs in praise of war. He was not a gentle man. His verses celebrate the clash of swords and the sight of banners on the battlefield.

Bertran did not hold the castle peacefully. He quarrelled with his own brother over who should control it. That family feud became tangled with much larger politics. At that time, much of this region was ruled by the English crown through the Plantagenet family, the dynasty of King Henry the Second. Henry's sons fought each other and their father, and Bertran stirred their rivalries in his songs. The medieval walls here saw real sieges as a result.

Here is something many visitors do not know. Bertran's reputation travelled far beyond the Périgord. The Italian poet Dante placed him in his Inferno, the first part of the Divine Comedy. In Dante's vision, Bertran is punished for setting a father against his son. So a lord from this quiet hill became a figure in one of the most famous poems in Europe. Very little of his medieval castle survives today, but his story still hangs over the site.

The building you see now is mostly much later. In the seventeenth century, the Hautefort family transformed the old fortress into an elegant residence. They kept the strong position and some defensive features, but they added long wings, tall windows and a sense of order. The result sits between two worlds. It still has towers and a moat, yet it also looks like a home for courtiers. Look at the contrast between the solid lower walls and the refined upper levels.

One member of this family became famous at the royal court. Marie de Hautefort was born here and later served the queen in Paris. She caught the attention of King Louis the Thirteenth. Historians usually describe their bond as a deep but chaste devotion rather than a love affair. Marie was known for her beauty and her sharp, independent spirit. She also had the courage to oppose the powerful Cardinal Richelieu, which cost her for a time her place at court.

Now turn your attention to the domes. The round towers are capped with curved roofs and small lanterns on top. They give the château its unusual silhouette, which looks almost Central European from a distance. Domes like these were a fashion of the period, and they make the skyline here easy to recognise. Notice how the slate roofs change colour as clouds pass over. In bright sun they shine silver. Under grey skies they turn nearly black.

The gardens deserve a slow walk. Below the terraces, clipped box and yew form patterns of curves and geometric shapes. This is the French formal garden tradition, where nature is shaped into lines and order. Some beds are planted with seasonal flowers, so the colours change through the year. Further out, the grounds open into a landscaped park with large trees and shaded paths. The contrast is deliberate: control near the house, and a wilder feeling as you move away.

The twentieth century brought both rescue and disaster. In 1929 the estate was bought by Baron Henry de Bastard and his wife, Simone. They began a long restoration of the house and its gardens. Then, in 1968, a severe fire swept through the château. Much of the interior and the roof was destroyed. It could have been the end of the building. Instead, Simone de Bastard dedicated herself to rebuilding it, and her work is the reason you can walk through it today.

So when you go inside, remember that many rooms are careful reconstructions. They are furnished to show how a great house of the past may have looked. Look closely at the floors, the fireplaces and the woodwork. Some pieces survived the fire, while others were restored or brought in later. Knowing this does not make the rooms less interesting. It makes them a record of two stories at once: the noble family who lived here, and the people who refused to let it disappear.

One more surprise lies down in the village. The writer Eugène Le Roy was born here in the nineteenth century. His parents worked in service at the château. Le Roy later wrote novels about the hard lives of peasants in the Périgord, including Jacquou le Croquant, a story of a poor boy who rebels against a cruel noble. It is striking that a writer so critical of the aristocracy came into the world beside one of its grandest homes.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the terrace and look outward. Below you are the roofs of the village, the fields and the long wooded ridges of the Périgord. The view has changed less than you might think since Bertran's time. Listen again for the gravel under your feet, the birds in the hedges and the wind across the slate. You are standing where poets, courtiers and rebuilders all stood... each of them looking at this same wide horizon.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for gravel paths and sloping lawns.
Visit the gardens first while the morning light is soft.
Walk down to the village to see its old streets.
Bring water on summer days because shade is limited.
Allow extra time to explore the outer landscaped park.
