The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Underfoot, the cobbles are uneven, worn smooth in the middle and rougher at the edges. A tram hums somewhere nearby, but here, in the old lanes of Durlach, the sound softens. Window boxes hang from timber-framed houses. A bakery door opens, and the smell of fresh bread drifts into the street. Welcome to the town that was here long before Karlsruhe was even an idea.

That is the first surprise. Today Durlach is officially a district of Karlsruhe, reached in a few minutes by tram from the city centre. But the relationship once ran the other way. Durlach is the elder sibling, by centuries. Karlsruhe, the planned city with its streets spreading out from the palace like the ribs of a fan, grew out of decisions made by the rulers who lived here first.

Durlach's roots reach back to the Middle Ages. Historians usually connect its rise as a town with the Hohenstaufen, the powerful dynasty of German kings and emperors, around the late twelfth century. The town grew at a useful spot, where the flat Rhine plain meets the first hills of the Black Forest foothills, and where roads met and crossed. That geography still shapes what you see. Look east, and the land begins to climb.

In the sixteenth century, Durlach received its moment in the spotlight. In 1565, Margrave Karl the Second moved his residence here from Pforzheim. A margrave was a regional ruler, a prince of a border territory, and his court changed the character of the town. His line became known as Baden-Durlach. For a century and a half, this small town was a capital, with officials, craftsmen, and courtiers filling its streets.

The seat of that court was the Karlsburg, the palace complex near the old centre. What survives today is only part of what once stood, but it still carries the weight of those years. Inside, the Pfinzgau Museum tells the story of the town and the surrounding region. The name comes from the Pfinz, the small river that runs past Durlach, and from the old word for a district or territory.

Then came disaster. In 1689, during the long war between France and an alliance of European powers, French troops set Durlach on fire. Much of the town, and much of the palace, burned. This is why so many of the buildings around you look younger than the town's medieval story might suggest. What you are walking through is, in large part, a town rebuilt in the decades after the flames.

And here the second surprise begins. The margraves did rebuild, but one of them, Karl Wilhelm, had bigger plans. In 1715, he laid the foundation of a new residence in the forest to the west, and named it after himself. That was Karlsruhe, which means roughly "Karl's rest". Locals still tell a popular tale that the margrave fell asleep during a hunt and dreamed of a palace at the centre of radiating streets. It is a legend, but a lovely one.

Within a few years the court left Durlach for good. The capital was gone, and the town became a quieter market and craft town in the shadow of its new neighbour. For more than two centuries Durlach kept its own independence and its own town hall. Only in 1938 was it joined to Karlsruhe. Many residents still call themselves Durlachers first, and you may hear that pride in conversation.

Now, look around the market square. The town hall faces the open space, and a fountain stands at its heart. On market mornings the square fills with stalls, with flowers, vegetables, and the steady murmur of people buying and chatting. Cafés push their tables out onto the stones when the weather allows. This is the everyday rhythm of Durlach, and it has a pace quite different from the wide avenues of Karlsruhe.

Take time in the side streets. The old town follows a medieval pattern, so the lanes bend and narrow without warning. Some houses lean slightly, their beams painted dark against pale plaster. Look for carved door frames, small inscriptions above entrances, and old shop signs hanging on iron brackets. Keep an eye out, too, for the Basler Tor, a surviving tower from the old town fortifications. It is a reminder that this place once had walls.

Above the town rises the Turmberg, the hill that gives Durlach its skyline. On top stands the remains of a medieval castle tower, which gave the hill its name, "tower mountain". You can walk up through gardens and vineyards, or take the Turmbergbahn, a small funicular railway. It opened in the late nineteenth century, and it is often described as the oldest funicular in Germany that is still running. The ride is short and steep.

From the top, the view opens across the whole Rhine plain. On a clear day, you can see Karlsruhe spread out below, and beyond it the flat land stretching towards the Rhine and the hills of the Palatinate on the far side. Stand there and the story of this place becomes easy to read. The old town sits at the foot of the hill. The newer city fans out across the plain, planned by the family that once ruled from here.

So, before you move on, pause where you are. Notice the height of the rooftops, the colour of the plaster, the sound of footsteps on stone. Durlach did not become the grand capital. Instead, it kept something smaller and more human: a market, a hill, a set of crooked lanes. As you walk away, glance back once at the Turmberg above the roofs... it has watched over this town far longer than the city next door has existed.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take the tram from central Karlsruhe for an easy trip.
Visit on a market morning to see the square busiest.
Ride the funicular up and walk down through the vineyards.
Climb the Turmberg on a clear day for longer views.
Wear sturdy shoes for cobbles and the steep hillside paths.
