The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment on the wide forecourt and listen. The fine gravel crunches under passing feet. Somewhere a fountain splashes, and bicycles tick past along the paths. In front of you the palace opens its two long wings like arms. It is painted a soft, pale yellow, and a slender tower rises from its centre. That tower is the point where this whole city begins, quite literally. Everything around you was drawn outward from it.

This is Karlsruhe Palace, and it is older than the city that surrounds it. In 1715, the Margrave Karl Wilhelm of Baden-Durlach decided to leave his crowded old residence at Durlach, a few kilometres to the east. He chose a stretch of open forest on the plain of the Rhine. Here he laid the foundation of a new seat of power. The city was planned around it afterwards, like a setting made for a single jewel.

The name itself comes with a story. Karlsruhe means, roughly, Karl's rest. Legend has it that the margrave fell asleep under a tree while out hunting. He dreamed of a radiant city spreading out from the spot where he lay. When he woke, he resolved to build it. It is a charming tale, and locals still enjoy telling it. Historians treat it as folklore, though. The more likely reasons were practical ones: space, prestige, and a fresh start away from old quarrels.

Here is the first surprise, and the tower is the best place to grasp it. Karlsruhe is known as the fan city. From the palace tower, streets run outward in straight lines, like the ribs of an open fan. The southern rays form the town, with its markets and houses. The northern rays cut through the forest and the park behind you. Look along any of the avenues in front of the palace and you will see that it points directly back at the tower.

This layout was more than decoration. It was a statement about power in the age of absolute rulers. Every street, every house, and every citizen was arranged in a line of sight to the ruler's residence. The prince sat at the centre, and the world radiated from him. Many European courts of the eighteenth century loved geometry like this. Few cities, however, built the idea so completely into their streets. Here the plan is still visible on any map.

The building you see is not the first palace on this spot. The original residence was built quickly, and much of it was made of timber. By the middle of the eighteenth century it was in poor condition. Under the next ruler, Margrave Karl Friedrich, the palace was rebuilt in stone from the late 1740s onward. Several architects worked on the designs over the years. The result is the long, calm, symmetrical building in front of you, in the late Baroque style.

Karl Friedrich had a remarkably long reign, and it changed the fortunes of his small state. In the Napoleonic era, Baden grew in size and in rank. In 1806 it became a Grand Duchy, and this palace became the seat of the Grand Dukes. Their court lived here for more than a century. That era ended in 1918, after the First World War. The last Grand Duke gave up his throne, and the monarchy in Baden came to an end.

The second surprise is hidden in plain sight. Much of what you see is a careful reconstruction. During the Second World War, bombing raids struck Karlsruhe heavily, and the palace burned. The outer walls survived, but the interiors were largely destroyed. In the decades after the war, the exterior was restored to its historic appearance. Inside, however, the rooms were rebuilt for a new purpose. So the face is eighteenth century, while much of the body behind it is modern.

That new purpose is the Baden State Museum, which fills the palace today. Its collections cover a very long span, from ancient finds to modern design. One section many visitors remember is the so-called Turkish booty. These objects were brought back by Margrave Ludwig Wilhelm of Baden, a general who fought the Ottoman armies in the late seventeenth century. Weapons, textiles, and ornate equipment from those campaigns are displayed here. They show how war, trade, and fascination mixed together at European courts.

If you can, climb the palace tower. The steps are narrow, and the climb takes some effort. But from the top, the fan plan finally makes sense to the eye. You can follow the streets running south into the city. To the north you see the green canopy of the park and the forest beyond. On a clear day, the low hills of the Black Forest and the Palatinate edge the horizon. It is the view the margrave imagined, now filled with trams and rooftops.

Now turn around and walk behind the palace into the palace garden. It was later reshaped as an English landscape park, a style that favoured gentle lawns, groups of old trees, and winding paths instead of strict formal beds. On warm days, students lie on the grass with books. Families feed ducks at the pond. Near the palace you will also find the botanical garden with its glasshouses. Further out along one of the fan's rays stands the State Majolica Manufactory, where ceramics are still made.

Karlsruhe still uses its palace district as a stage for important institutions. Just beside the grounds stands the Federal Constitutional Court of Germany, the country's highest court for constitutional questions. There is a quiet irony here. The city was laid out to glorify a single ruler. Today, one of the guardians of German democracy works within sight of that ruler's tower. If you walk south along the main axis, you will also reach the Market Square. A sandstone pyramid there marks the tomb of the city's founder.

Before you leave, stand once more in the centre of the forecourt and look up at the tower. Then look down any of the long avenues in front of you. Notice how your eye travels straight back to this point, every time. That is the margrave's plan, still working three centuries later. As you walk away along one of those streets, glance back once or twice. The tower will stay exactly in the middle of your view, quietly holding the whole city together.
