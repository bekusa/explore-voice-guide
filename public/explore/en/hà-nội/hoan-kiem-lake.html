The story

In the middle of the old city lies a long, calm lake. Willow trees lean over the green water. An old stone tower stands on a tiny island near the southern end. In the early morning, people walk and stretch along the shore. This is Hoan Kiem Lake, the quiet heart of Hanoi. Scooters stream past on the roads that ring the water.

The name Hoan Kiem means the Lake of the Returned Sword. It comes from a legend told for hundreds of years. The story is set in the 15th century, a time when foreign armies from the north had taken control of Vietnam. The people longed to be free again. The lake is not large, and you can walk around it in under an hour.

According to the legend, a man named Le Loi found a magic sword. Some say it came to him from the Golden Turtle God, a spirit of the lake. With this sword, Le Loi led the people in a long fight against the invaders. In the end, he drove them out and became emperor. His fight lasted about ten years before it was won.

One day, after the war, the new emperor was boating on this lake. Suddenly a great golden turtle rose from the water. It came to take back the sword, which had only been lent to him. The turtle carried the blade down into the depths and vanished. From that day, the lake had its name. The water closed over the spot, calm again.

Whether or not the story is true, it shaped how people see this place. The lake became a symbol of peace after war. The turtle became a guardian spirit. For centuries, large soft-shell turtles really did live in these waters. The last famous one died in 2016, and many people mourned it. They could grow longer than a grown man is tall.

Near the southern end of the lake stands the Turtle Tower. It rises from a small islet, just out of reach. The tower is small, with arched windows and a worn stone surface. It was built in the late nineteenth century. You cannot go inside, but its shape against the water is one of the best-known sights in the city. At night, it is lit with a soft golden light.

At the northern end sits another island, with a temple upon it. This is Ngoc Son Temple, the Temple of the Jade Mountain. It was built in the nineteenth century. The temple honors several figures, including Tran Hung Dao, a general who turned back Mongol invaders in the 13th century. Incense burns inside its halls. A gate and a tall brush-shaped tower stand near the entrance.

To reach the temple, you cross a bright red wooden bridge. It is called The Huc Bridge, a name linked to the light of the rising sun. The bridge was built in 1865, during a restoration led by a scholar named Nguyen Van Sieu. Its curved shape and deep red color draw many visitors. Its name is often read as the place where morning sunlight rests.

Red is not chosen by accident. In Vietnamese culture, it is a color of luck and protection. As you walk across the bridge, the water shines on both sides. The crossing is meant to feel like a passage, leaving daily life behind and stepping onto sacred ground.

Inside Ngoc Son Temple, you can see the preserved body of one of the giant turtles that once lived in the lake. It is kept in a glass case. Standing before it, the old legend feels closer. The turtle of the story and the turtles of the water seem to meet in this one room. People lean close to the glass to take it in.

The lake is the center of daily life in Hanoi. In the morning, people practice exercises along the paths. In the evening, families and couples walk slowly around the water. On weekends, the surrounding streets close to cars, and the whole area fills with music, games, and people on foot. Vendors sell tea and snacks from the edges of the square.

Find a bench by the water before you leave. Watch the willow branches move in the breeze and the red bridge glow at the far end. A story about a sword and a turtle has kept this lake at the soul of the city for centuries. Sitting here, between the noise of the streets and the calm of the water, it is easy to see why.
