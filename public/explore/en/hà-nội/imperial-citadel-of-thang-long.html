The guide

The story

Listen first. Beyond the old walls, Hanoi's motorbikes hum like a distant river. Inside, the sound drops away. You hear sandals on worn brick, a bird in the frangipani trees, and the soft knock of your own steps on stone. The air smells of damp earth and leaves. Ahead rises a broad gateway of dark, weathered brick, with three arched openings at its base. You are standing at the edge of a place that served as a seat of power for roughly a thousand years.

This is the Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long. In 2010, UNESCO added the central sector to its World Heritage List. That same year, Hanoi marked its thousandth birthday as a capital. The timing was not an accident. The story of this citadel and the story of the city begin at the same moment, in the same place, with the same name.

That moment came in 1010. The emperor Lý Thái Tổ, founder of the Lý dynasty, decided to move his capital. He left Hoa Lư, a fortress tucked among limestone hills to the south, and came to the wide plain of the Red River. Legend has it that as his boats approached, a golden dragon rose from the water and climbed into the sky. He took it as a sign. He named the new capital Thăng Long, which means Ascending Dragon.

The emperor did not build on empty ground. Before him, this area held a fortress from the period when Chinese dynasties ruled the region. Its foundations reach back to around the seventh century. So the ground beneath your feet is layered — one city on top of another. Over the following centuries, the Lý, the Trần and the Lê dynasties all ruled from here. Each one rebuilt, repaired and added new halls, so the site grew like a stack of pages.

The gateway in front of you is Đoan Môn, the main southern gate into the old forbidden zone. Look at its arches. The central opening was reserved for the emperor. Officials and others used the side passages. Walk through, and look down. In places, glass panels or open sections show older brick pavements below the modern ground level. These are earlier surfaces, from earlier courts. You are walking, quite literally, above history.

Continue north along the central axis, and you reach a raised stone platform. This is the foundation of Kính Thiên Palace, the main audience hall of the Lê dynasty. The wooden hall itself is long gone. What remains is the stone base and its steps. Pay close attention to the balustrades on the central stairway. Carved stone dragons run down the slope, their bodies curling, their heads resting at the bottom. They date from the fifteenth century, and they rank among the finest stone carvings in Vietnam.

Here is the first surprise. After 1802, the new Nguyễn dynasty moved the capital south to Huế. Thăng Long was no longer the imperial seat. Then, in the late nineteenth century, French colonial forces took the citadel. They demolished many of the old palaces and used the grounds as a military base. Some of the yellow colonial buildings you see around the site come from that period. The French also put up structures directly on the old foundations.

At the northern end of the axis stands Hậu Lâu, the Rear Pavilion, sometimes called the Princesses' Pavilion. Tradition says it housed women of the court. The building was heavily altered under French rule, so its present form mixes old and newer elements. Its tiled roof and small upper floor give it a quieter, more domestic feel than the grand gate. Stand back from it, and you can see how the whole citadel lines up along one straight north-south line.

Now the second surprise, and it is a very different century. After independence, this enclosure became the headquarters of the Vietnamese People's Army. During the American War, military leaders planned campaigns here. Look for a plain, low building known as House D67, named for the year 1967. Inside, you can see the meeting room with its long table and chairs. Beneath it, steep stairs lead down into a reinforced underground bunker. Few visitors expect a Cold War command centre inside an imperial palace.

That military use helps explain why so much survived underground. For decades, the public could not enter, and little was built over the deepest layers. Then, in the early 2000s, construction for a new National Assembly building began across the street, at 18 Hoàng Diệu. Archaeologists started to dig. What they found changed the understanding of the old city — foundations of palaces, ancient wells, drainage channels, and huge quantities of pottery and roof decorations.

The excavation site sits just across Hoàng Diệu Street from the main entrance, and it is worth the short walk. Under protective roofs, you can look into open trenches. Different eras sit side by side and on top of each other. You can see round stone bases that once held wooden columns, and lines of brick that marked old walls. In the display areas, look for ceramic dragon heads and phoenix ornaments that once crowned the palace roofs. Some show traces of green and yellow glaze.

As you walk, notice how the site feels. It is not a single preserved palace like you might find elsewhere. It is more open, more like a park built over memory. Old trees throw wide shade over the paths. Families sit on low walls. Students sometimes come in school uniforms to sketch or to take graduation photos at the gate. The calm here comes partly from that mix: an archaeological site, a war memorial, and a quiet public garden, all inside the same walls.

Before you leave, return to the stone dragons at Kính Thiên for a moment. Rest your eyes on their curling bodies, smoothed by rain and by countless hands. Then look up, past the treetops, toward the city around you. The traffic, the cafés and the government buildings of modern Hanoi all grew outward from this ground. When you walk back out through the old gate, the noise of the street will return. Listen for that moment — the edge where the old citadel ends and the living city begins.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early in the morning for softer light and cooler air.
Wear comfortable shoes for uneven brick and stone paths.
Cross Hoàng Diệu Street to see the 18 Hoàng Diệu excavation.
Bring water and sun protection; shade is patchy in places.
Start at Đoan Môn and walk the central axis northward.
