The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Traffic hums along Jefferson Avenue. Glass towers catch the light. Somewhere below, the Detroit River moves past, wide and steady, toward Lake Erie. None of it looks like a frontier. Yet this ground, the slight rise above the water, is where the city began. More than three centuries ago, there was no concrete here. There was forest, river mist, and the sound of axes cutting logs for a wooden wall.

That wall was Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit. It was a French outpost, founded in the summer of 1701. Its leader was Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac, an officer and fur-trade entrepreneur with large ambitions. He arrived by canoe with a party of soldiers and settlers, roughly a hundred people in all. They chose this spot with care. The bank was high enough to see along the river in both directions. Any boat passing through would pass beneath their eyes.

The name holds two stories. Pontchartrain honours a powerful minister at the court of King Louis the Fourteenth in France. Cadillac knew that naming a fort after a patron in Versailles could help win support and funding. The second word, Détroit, is simpler. It is French for strait, a narrow passage of water. Here is a surprise many visitors miss. The city's name does not describe a place. It describes the river itself.

Why this strait mattered becomes clear on a map. The river links Lake Huron and Lake Saint Clair in the north with Lake Erie in the south. Whoever held this narrow channel controlled a key route through the Great Lakes. For France, that meant the fur trade. Beaver pelts moved east toward Montreal and on to European hat makers. Cadillac also hoped the fort would block British traders pushing west from their colonies.

Picture the fort as it first stood. It was not stone. It was a palisade, a fence of tall wooden stakes set upright in the earth. Inside were small log houses, storehouses, and a chapel. The streets were narrow, little more than lanes between buildings. Fire was a constant fear, and with good reason. Wood, candles, and cooking hearths sat close together inside a wall made of timber.

The chapel inside the walls is part of a story that still continues. The Catholic parish of Sainte Anne traces its founding to the first days of the settlement. It is often described as one of the oldest continuously operating Catholic parishes in the United States. The church building has moved and been rebuilt several times. But the parish community has never stopped meeting since those early years.

The French were not alone here. Indigenous nations had travelled and traded along this river long before any European arrived. Cadillac invited several of them to settle near the fort, including the Wyandot, also called the Huron, along with the Odawa and Potawatomi. Their villages stood close to the walls. Trade, alliance, marriage, and tension all shaped daily life. The fort survived because of these relationships, not in spite of them.

Families also came. According to the familiar account, Cadillac's wife, Marie-Thérèse, made the long journey from Quebec soon after the founding, along with another officer's wife. Their arrival signalled that this was meant to be a town, not only a military camp. Over the following decades, French farmers laid out long, narrow farms. Each strip ran back from the riverbank so that every family had access to the water. Some old street names in Detroit still follow those ribbon-shaped lots.

The flag over the fort changed in 1760. During the war between Britain and France for control of North America, the French garrison here surrendered to British forces. Only a few years later, in 1763, the Odawa leader Pontiac led a siege against the British-held fort. It lasted for months and became one of the best-known episodes of Indigenous resistance in the region. The walls held, but the siege showed how fragile European control remained.

After the American Revolution, the British still kept Detroit for more than a decade. American troops did not take possession until 1796. By then, the old French stockade had been changed, expanded, and partly replaced. The British had built a newer fortification, called Fort Lernoult, on higher ground a short distance inland. Piece by piece, the original fort faded into the growing town.

Then came the event that erased it completely. In 1805, a fire swept through Detroit and destroyed nearly the entire settlement in a single day. The town of tight wooden streets was gone. A priest named Father Gabriel Richard offered words that became the city's motto. In Latin, they mean, roughly, We hope for better things; it will rise from the ashes. Those words still appear on the city flag and seal today.

So what remains of Fort Pontchartrain? Almost nothing you can touch. No wall survives above ground. The site lies beneath the streets and buildings of the downtown riverfront. Historical markers and plaques recall the founding. Archaeologists have found traces of colonial Detroit in excavations around the city, but the fort itself lives mostly in records, maps, and memory. That absence is part of the story. This city rebuilt itself again and again on the same ground.

As you stand here now, look toward the river. That water is the reason for everything around you. Turn and look at the street grid behind you, and imagine it smaller, wooden, enclosed by a fence of stakes. When you walk away, notice the city flag if you see it. Two short Latin phrases sit on it, written after a fire more than two centuries ago. They began with a small wooden fort on a bank above the strait.
