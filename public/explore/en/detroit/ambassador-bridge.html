The guide

The story

Stand near the water and listen for a moment. A low, steady hum comes down from above. It is the sound of tyres on steel, hundreds of trucks rolling between two countries. The river below moves fast and dark, and the long green-grey span hangs over it on two slender towers. It looks light from here, almost delicate. Then a freighter slides underneath, and you understand how high it really sits.

This is the Ambassador Bridge. It links Detroit, in the American state of Michigan, with Windsor, in the Canadian province of Ontario. Between them runs the Detroit River, a working waterway that carries water from Lake Saint Clair down toward Lake Erie. For most of a century, this single suspension bridge has been one of the most important crossings between the United States and Canada. By the value of goods that pass over it, it is often described as the busiest border crossing on the continent.

Here is the first surprise, and it catches almost everyone. Look across the water. That city on the far bank is Canada, and it lies to the south of you. Detroit is one of the few places where you travel south from the United States to reach Canada. The river curves here, and the border follows it. Locals enjoy watching visitors check the map twice.

The bridge opened in November 1929. The timing is remarkable. Only a few weeks earlier, the stock market had crashed, and the Great Depression was beginning. The project had been planned in the confident years of the nineteen-twenties, when Detroit's car factories were growing fast and the city was hungry for a faster link to Canada. Before the bridge, people and goods crossed mainly by ferry. The car age needed something permanent.

When it opened, the Ambassador Bridge had the longest main span of any suspension bridge in the world. It held that title only briefly. The George Washington Bridge in New York opened a couple of years later and took the record. Still, for a short moment, the longest suspended roadway on Earth crossed this river between Detroit and Windsor. Few people driving over it today know that.

The construction story has a tense chapter. During the building work, engineers became worried about the steel wire in the main cables. The type of wire first chosen had shown dangerous weaknesses on another bridge built around the same time. So the builders took a hard decision. They replaced the cable wire with a different, more reliable kind before the bridge opened. It cost time and money, but it meant the bridge you see today stands on a sounder foundation.

Now look at the shape itself. A suspension bridge works by hanging the road from two great cables. Those cables drape over the tall towers and are anchored firmly into the ground at each end. Thin vertical ropes, called suspenders, drop from the cables and carry the deck. The weight of every truck travels up into the cables, over the towers, and down into heavy anchorages hidden at both ends. It is a simple idea, and on a clear day you can follow the whole path with your eyes.

The second surprise is about who owns it. Most major border bridges belong to governments. This one does not. The Ambassador Bridge has been privately owned for its entire life. Since the late nineteen-seventies, it has been controlled by a Detroit business family. That private ownership has made the bridge the centre of long political and legal arguments about trade, tolls, and who should control a crossing this important.

Those arguments are one reason a second bridge has been built a few kilometres downstream. The new Gordie Howe International Bridge, named after the hockey legend who played for the Detroit Red Wings, is a public project shared by Canada and Michigan. If you look down the river, you may be able to see its tall towers. For decades the Ambassador stood mostly alone. Now it has company on the skyline.

This crossing has also seen moments of national drama. In February 2022, protesters blocked the Canadian approach to the bridge for several days. Traffic stopped. Car factories on both sides of the border slowed or paused production, because so many parts move across this span every day. The blockade showed how closely the two economies are joined, and how much depends on this one road over the water.

That connection goes far beyond trucks. Families live on one side and work on the other. Hockey fans, shoppers, and old friends cross back and forth. For generations, people in Detroit and Windsor have watched the same river, the same sunsets, and the same freighters. The bridge is a border, but it is also a kind of shared street between two cities that grew up facing each other.

Spend a little time watching the river traffic. Long lake freighters move slowly beneath the deck, carrying cargo across the Great Lakes. Their horns carry across the water. In the evening, the bridge lights come on and trace the curve of the cables against the darkening sky. From the Windsor shore, the Detroit skyline glows behind the span. From the Detroit side, the bridge frames the quieter lights of Canada.

Before you move on, look up once more. Follow one cable from the far anchorage, over the nearest tower, and down to the deck. Listen again for that steady hum of tyres above the water. Every one of those trucks is carrying something from one country to another, right now, over a road that has hung here since 1929. As you walk away, notice how often the bridge stays in view... a long grey line between two nations, still doing its daily work.

Before you go

Practical tips

Bring a passport if you plan to cross into Canada.
Pedestrians cannot walk the bridge, so view it from shore.
Visit at dusk to see the cable lights switch on.
Watch from the Windsor riverfront for skyline views behind it.
Check border wait times before driving across.
