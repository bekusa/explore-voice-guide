King's Chapel

Dark gray stone rises from the corner of Tremont Street.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

Dark gray stone rises from the corner of Tremont Street. The building looks heavy and solid. It is square in shape, with thick stone columns across the front. But there is no tall spire above it. The top looks oddly flat against the sky. There is a reason for that missing steeple. This is King's Chapel, and its story is one of stone, faith, and change.

The first church on this spot was small and made of wood. It was built in 1686 for the Anglican faith, the official church of England. Many of the Puritan colonists disliked it greatly. They had crossed the ocean to escape the English church. Now that same church stood in the middle of their own town. To them, it was an unwelcome sight.

By the middle of the next century, the wooden church had grown too small for its congregation. The members wanted a grand new building made of stone. They hired a man named Peter Harrison to design it. Harrison is often called the first trained architect in America. He gave the church the strong, classical shape that you see today. His design drew on the styles of Europe.

The way they built it was clever. Between 1749 and 1754, the new stone walls were raised right around the old wooden church. The old church stood inside the new shell. Services continued indoors while the stone rose outside. When the stone walls were finished at last, workers took the old wooden church apart. They carried the pieces out through the windows of the new building.

The tower was meant to hold a tall steeple. But the money ran out before it could be built. The steeple was never added. That is why the building looks unfinished at the top, flat and square against the sky. The row of columns at the front is called a portico. It was added later, in 1789. It softened the plain face of the church.

Step inside, and the mood changes at once. The space is high, calm, and full of light. The walls are painted in soft, pale colors. Tall windows line the sides. Wooden box pews stand in neat rows on the floor. At the front is the pulpit, the raised stand where the minister speaks. This pulpit dates from 1717. It is one of the oldest pulpits still in use in the country, saved from the earlier wooden church.

The church changed its faith as well as its building. After the American Revolution, the congregation broke away from the Church of England. In the 1780s, it became the first Unitarian church in America. This was a major change in belief. Yet the church kept its old royal name. So a building called King's Chapel came to follow a very American faith.

There is a bell in the tower with a famous maker. Paul Revere was known as a silversmith and a patriot rider. He also ran a foundry that cast bells from metal. The bell that hangs here came from his works. Revere is said to have called it the sweetest-sounding bell he ever made. Its tone has rung out over this corner for many generations.

Step outside again to the small graveyard beside the church. This is the oldest burial ground in all of Boston. It is even older than the church itself. Its first graves date back to 1630. That was the very year the town of Boston was founded. The people buried here were among the city's first residents. Their stones have weathered nearly four centuries.

Among the worn markers rest early settlers and leaders of the colony. The carvings on the stones are simple and old. Most are cut from slate, a soft gray rock. They show the plain, careful style of the first New England stone carvers. Time and weather have softened many of the letters. Some names can barely be read now.

Together, the church and its graveyard hold many layers of time. A wooden church became a stone one. An English faith became an American one. A small corner of a young colony became a corner of a large, modern city. The walls have watched all of these changes in silence. Each layer of that story is still here, if you take the time to look.

Before you leave, look once more at the flat, towerless top. What might seem like a failure is really a kind of record. It shows a congregation that ran out of money but kept its faith. They could not finish their steeple. Yet they built something that has lasted far longer than any tower they had once planned to raise.

Key facts

First Anglican church in Boston, founded in 1686
Stone building constructed from 1749 to 1754
Designed by Peter Harrison, an early American architect
Became America's first Unitarian church in the 1780s
Adjoining burying ground dates from 1630

What to look for

Flat, towerless top where a steeple was planned
The 1717 wooden pulpit still in use
Tall columns of the porch added in 1789
Soft slate carvings on the oldest graves

Practical tips

Enter at the corner of Tremont and School Streets
Check the schedule for organ and music recitals
Walk the small burying ground beside the church
Sit quietly in a box pew to feel the scale
