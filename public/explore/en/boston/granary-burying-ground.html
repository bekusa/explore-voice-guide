Granary Burying Ground

Slate stones lean in long rows.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

Slate stones lean in long rows. The grass is thin under the old trees. Cars and buses move past the iron fence on Tremont Street. People talk and walk on the sidewalk just outside. But inside this small ground the noise seems to soften. The stones are gray and worn. Some tilt forward, and some lean back. You are standing in one of the oldest burial places in the city of Boston.

The cemetery opened in 1660. It is the third-oldest burial ground in the city. The name comes from a building that once stood next door. A granary is a place where grain is stored. Such a building sat on the spot where Park Street Church stands today. Over time, people began to call the burial ground by the name of its neighbor. The granary is long gone, but the name has stayed for centuries.

Many people lie here. The stones you see number around 2,300. But the ground holds far more than that. Historians believe that more than 5,000 people rest beneath your feet. In early Boston, space was limited. Families were often buried together in the same plot. One stone might carry many names, parents and children side by side. Some graves were never marked at all.

Some of the names here are well known. Three of the men who signed the Declaration of Independence are buried in this ground. They are John Hancock, Samuel Adams, and Robert Treat Paine. Hancock was the first man to sign that famous document. Samuel Adams helped lead the push for independence. These men once walked the streets of Boston, and now they rest in its soil.

Paul Revere is buried here as well. He was a silversmith by trade, skilled at working with metal. He was also a messenger for the patriot cause. His night ride in 1775 helped warn that British troops were on the move. For many years his grave was marked only by a simple stone. Later, admirers placed a larger marker beside it, so that visitors could find him more easily.

Near the entrance you will find the victims of the Boston Massacre. On a cold night in 1770, British soldiers fired into a crowd of colonists. Five men were killed. One of them was Crispus Attucks, a man of African and Native descent. He is often named as the first person to die for the American cause. The five men share a single resting place here.

At the center of the ground stands a tall stone shaft. This is an obelisk, a thin tower that rises to a point. It honors the parents of Benjamin Franklin. Franklin himself is not here. He is buried in Philadelphia, far to the south. But his mother and father lived and died in Boston. The people of the city raised this monument so that their names would not be lost.

Look closely at the old stones. Many of them are carved from slate, a soft gray rock. You may see strange images cut into the surface. There are winged skulls, hourglasses, and angels. These were common symbols in early New England. The skull was a reminder that death comes to everyone. The hourglass showed that time runs out. The wings suggested the soul rising up.

There is something you should know about the neat rows. The stones were not always lined up like this. In the nineteenth century, workers moved many of them into straight lines. This made the grass easier to cut. But it created a strange problem. The markers no longer always sit above the right grave. The name you read on a stone may not match the person buried below it.

The ground is small, but the story it holds is wide. Famous patriots and ordinary citizens share the same soil. Merchants, ministers, sailors, and children rest side by side. In life they belonged to different worlds. In death they are equal here, under the same trees and the same sky. Rich and poor lie together now, divided only by the worn stones above them.

Walk slowly along the narrow paths. Read the old spellings and the worn dates on the stones. Some are so faint that you must lean close to make them out. Each marker was carved by hand for someone who was loved and missed. For the people who made them, the carving was a way to hold a name against the passing of time.

When you step back through the gate, the city returns at once. Cars, voices, and tall buildings press in from every side. The modern world is loud and quick. But for a few quiet minutes inside this fence, the past felt close enough to touch. That stillness, in the middle of a busy city, is the gift this small ground still offers.

Key facts

Established in 1660, Boston's third-oldest burial ground
Over 5,000 buried beneath about 2,300 surviving markers
Three Declaration signers buried: Hancock, Adams, and Paine
Named for a grain granary that stood nearby
Headstones rearranged into neat rows during the nineteenth century

What to look for

Winged skulls and hourglasses carved into slate
The tall central obelisk for Franklin's parents
Paul Revere's plain stone beside a larger memorial
The shared marker for the Boston Massacre victims

Practical tips

Enter from Tremont Street, beside Park Street Church
Come early to avoid the Freedom Trail crowds
Keep to marked paths to protect fragile stones
Read the worn stones gently and do not climb
