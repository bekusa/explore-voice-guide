The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the hum of the city, the floorboards here creak softly as people move between the glass cases. The light is low and even, kept that way to protect old fibres and feathers. The air carries a faint smell of wood and dust. You are in one of the oldest museums in the world dedicated to the study of human cultures.

First, a small note on geography. The Peabody Museum of Archaeology and Ethnology is not in Boston proper. It sits in Cambridge, just across the Charles River, on the Harvard University campus. Most visitors think of it as part of a Boston day, and the two cities run into each other. But this red brick building belongs to Harvard, and its history is tied closely to the university.

The museum was founded in 1866, with a gift from George Peabody. He was a merchant and banker born in Massachusetts, and he made his fortune largely in London. Peabody gave money to many causes in his lifetime, including other museums that carry his name. That is why travellers sometimes confuse this place with the Peabody Museum at Yale. They are separate institutions. This one was built to study archaeology and ethnology — the study of how different peoples live, make things, and organise their lives.

In its early decades, one of the central figures here was Frederic Ward Putnam. He was a curator who helped shape American archaeology as a professional field. Under his guidance, the museum sent people to dig, record, and collect, rather than simply buying curiosities. That shift matters. It is part of the story of how anthropology became a science, with field notes, careful drawings, and records of where each object was found.

Now look around the ground floor. Many visitors begin in the hall devoted to Indigenous peoples of North America. Here you can see clothing, tools, baskets, and objects used in ceremonies. Take your time with the materials. Notice how quills, beads, hide, and plant fibres were worked by hand. Some pieces are plain and practical. Others carry fine patterns that would have taken weeks of patient work.

Here is a detail many people walk past. Some objects in this museum have been linked by researchers to the Lewis and Clark expedition — the journey across western North America in the early nineteenth century. Over time, these items passed through earlier collections before arriving here. Scholars still discuss which pieces can be firmly connected to the expedition. That uncertainty is honest, and it is part of the story. Labels in the galleries explain what is known and what is still debated.

Next, climb upstairs to the Maya gallery, one of the most memorable rooms in the building. Late in the nineteenth century, the museum supported expeditions to Copán, an ancient Maya city in what is now Honduras. Large plaster casts were made from carved stone monuments. Stand close to them. You can see faces, headdresses, and rows of glyphs — the signs the Maya used for writing. Because the casts were made long ago, they sometimes keep details that weather has since worn away on the original stones.

That is the second surprise. A copy can sometimes help scholars as much as an original. Rain, plants, and time keep working on stone in the open air. The casts in this room hold a record of how certain carvings looked more than a century ago. Researchers have used old casts and photographs like these to study inscriptions. So when you look at the plaster, you are also looking at a moment in time.

The museum's story also has difficult chapters, and today the institution speaks about them openly. Like many older museums, it gathered human remains and sacred objects in ways that would not be accepted today. A federal law passed in 1990 requires museums to work with Native American nations on returning ancestral remains and certain cultural items. The museum has been part of that process for decades. In recent years, Harvard has also reviewed the history of human remains in its collections, including remains of people who were enslaved.

This is worth keeping in mind as you walk. A museum like this does more than display objects. It also raises questions. Who made these things? Who decided they should travel here? Who should care for them now? You will notice that some newer labels include the voices of community members and descendants. Those voices change how an object reads. A basket becomes a family's knowledge, not only a specimen.

The building connects with the Harvard Museum of Natural History, and many visitors move between the two in one visit. Next door you will find fossils, minerals, and the famous collection of glass models of plants. The contrast is interesting. On one side, the natural world. On this side, the ways people have lived within it. Walking from one to the other feels like turning from the land to the people who shaped it.

Before you leave, slow down once more. Choose one object that caught your eye, and spend a full minute with it. Look at the tool marks, the wear on a handle, a repair someone made long ago. These small marks show real hands at work. They are the closest thing in the room to a conversation with the person who made it.

When you step back outside, notice the quiet of Divinity Avenue and the brick and ivy around you. Students cross the paths with backpacks. Bicycles lean on railings. The Cambridge streets carry on with their ordinary rhythm. Inside, thousands of objects wait in drawers and cases, each one made by someone with a name, even when that name is lost. Carry one of those objects with you as you walk on.
