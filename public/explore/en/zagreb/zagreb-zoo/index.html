The guide

The story

Listen first. Under the tall oaks of Maksimir, the gravel crunches softly with each step. Somewhere ahead, a peacock lets out a sharp cry. Ducks splash on dark green water, and the air smells of wet leaves, cut grass, and animals. Light falls through the trees in broken patches. Before you see a single enclosure, you are already inside an old forest, and that is the first thing to understand about Zagreb Zoo. It does not sit on the edge of the city like a separate world. It lives inside a park, among lakes and woodland.

That park is Maksimir, and it is older than the zoo by more than a century. It opened to the public at the end of the eighteenth century, in the 1790s. The idea came from a Bishop of Zagreb named Maksimilijan Vrhovac, and the park still carries his name. At that time, a large public park was a new idea in this part of Europe. Gardens usually belonged to nobles and the church. Here, ordinary people could walk among the trees.

Over the nineteenth century, later bishops reshaped Maksimir in the English landscape style. This style imitates nature instead of forcing it into straight lines. So the park has winding paths, open meadows, and a chain of artificial lakes. There are small pavilions and viewpoints too. The goal was to make each turn of the path show a new picture. As you walk to the zoo, you are walking through this designed landscape.

The zoo itself opened in 1925. That makes it one of the older zoos in this region of Europe. It began very modestly. According to the story often told in Zagreb, the first residents were just a handful of small local animals, foxes and owls among them. From that small start, the collection grew slowly over the decades. Today it holds animals from many continents, from big cats to tiny birds.

Here is something many visitors miss. The zoo was built around the lakes of Maksimir, and water still shapes the whole place. Paths follow the shorelines. Bridges cross narrow channels. Some enclosures use water instead of fences as a barrier. When you stand still on one of the bridges, look down. You may see fish moving slowly, or wild ducks that are not part of the collection at all.

That is the second surprise. Not every animal here is an exhibit. Maksimir is a protected natural area, and it is full of wild life. Herons, woodpeckers, squirrels, frogs, and many songbirds live in the park freely. They often wander into the zoo grounds. A grey heron standing on a log may simply be a visitor, like you. Locals enjoy pointing this out. The line between the zoo and the forest is softer than it looks.

The zoo is compact, and that is part of its charm. You can walk the main loop at an easy pace in a few hours. The paths are mostly flat and shaded, which helps on hot summer days. Families with strollers move easily here. Benches stand under the trees, and you will often see grandparents resting while children run ahead to the next enclosure.

As you walk, notice how the enclosures have changed over time. Older zoos in Europe often kept animals in small cages with bars. Many zoos, including this one, have spent recent decades rebuilding. Newer spaces tend to be larger and more natural, with rocks, plants, pools, and places for animals to hide. You can sometimes see the difference side by side. An older structure may stand near a newer, more open space.

This change reflects a wider shift in what zoos are for. In the past, the main purpose was display. Today, modern zoos also focus on education, animal welfare, and the protection of endangered species. Many European zoos share animals through breeding programmes. The goal is to keep healthy populations of rare species. Information panels near the enclosures often explain where each animal comes from and why it needs protection.

The best time to watch animals is usually early in the day, or during feeding times. Many animals rest in the afternoon heat, especially the big cats. A lion asleep in the shade is still impressive, but a lion pacing at feeding time is something else. Staff sometimes give short talks at these moments. Check the boards near the entrance when you arrive, because schedules change with the seasons.

The zoo also changes with the seasons. In spring, the park turns bright green and the birds are loud in the trees. In summer, the shade is the gift. Autumn brings gold and red leaves floating on the lakes. In winter, some animals from warm climates stay indoors, but animals from cold regions often become more active. Each season shows you a slightly different zoo.

For people in Zagreb, this place is part of ordinary life. Many adults in the city remember coming here as children with their parents. Now they bring their own children. School groups arrive on weekday mornings. On weekends, the paths fill with families, and the sound of laughter mixes with birdsong. Just across the road stands Maksimir Stadium, home of the football club Dinamo Zagreb. On match days, you may hear the crowd from far away.

After your visit, give yourself time for the rest of the park. Maksimir is large, more than three hundred hectares, and much of it is quiet old forest. Some of the oak trees are very old. The paths lead to more lakes, to small pavilions, and to open meadows where people lie on blankets. It is easy to spend a whole afternoon here, moving from the zoo into the woods without a clear boundary.

So pause for a moment before you go. Look at the water near you. Listen to the birds in the trees above. Some of them belong to the zoo, and some belong only to the forest. A bishop opened these woods to the public more than two hundred years ago. A small collection of animals arrived a century ago. Today, you are walking where both stories meet, on a quiet path under the oaks of Maksimir.
