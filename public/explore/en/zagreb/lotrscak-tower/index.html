The guide

The story

Listen for the click of shoes on stone, the low rumble of the funicular car arriving, and the wind that comes up the slope from the city below. You are standing at the edge of Zagreb's Upper Town, beside a square, pale tower with small windows and a crenellated top. The walls feel solid and cool if you touch them. This is Lotrščak Tower. For centuries, it has watched over the point where the old hilltop town meets the busy streets beneath it.

To understand the tower, picture Zagreb as two separate settlements. On one hill stood Kaptol, the seat of the bishop and the cathedral clergy. On this hill stood Gradec, a town of merchants and craftsmen. In 1242, King Béla the Fourth of Hungary and Croatia issued a charter called the Golden Bull. It made Gradec a free royal town. With that status came duties. The townspeople had to build walls and towers to protect themselves.

Lotrščak Tower is one of those defences. It dates from the thirteenth century and guarded the southern gate of Gradec. Its style is Romanesque, which means the heavy, simple architecture of medieval Europe, with thick walls and small openings. Look at the narrow windows on the lower levels. They were made for watching and defending, not for letting in light. Most of the old town walls have gone, but this tower survived and kept its place on the edge of the hill.

The name has a curious story, and it is one most visitors never hear. It comes from the Latin phrase campana latrunculorum, which means the bell of thieves. Each evening, a bell in the tower rang to warn people that the town gates would soon close for the night. Anyone still outside had to hurry in, or stay out until morning. Those who stayed out, so the thinking went, were probably up to no good.

Today, the tower is known for a different sound. Every day at noon, a cannon fires from the tower. The tradition began in the late nineteenth century and has continued for well over a hundred years. The boom rolls across the rooftops of the Lower Town. People in cafés below check their watches, and pigeons rise from the squares. For many people in Zagreb, this sound simply means midday. It is part of ordinary life.

The cannon has its own legend — and it is important to say clearly that it is a legend. According to a popular tale, Ottoman forces once camped across the Sava River, threatening the town. At noon, the defenders fired the cannon. The ball flew across the river and struck the rooster that the Ottoman commander was about to eat for lunch. Shocked, the army withdrew. No historian treats this as fact, but locals still enjoy telling it.

The tower you see today is not exactly the medieval one. Over the centuries, it changed as the town changed. In the nineteenth century, it was altered. An extra floor and larger windows were added, and the top was given its present shape. So the lower walls carry the older, defensive character, while the upper part shows a later, calmer age. If you look closely, you can follow this story in the stone, from bottom to top.

Inside, a stairway climbs up through the tower to a viewing platform. The steps are steep and narrow in places, so take your time. At the top, the city opens up below you. You can see the long red rooftops of the Lower Town, the green squares, and on a clear day, the hills beyond. Many people come here for this view alone. It helps you understand how high the old town really sat above the plain.

Now look down at the small railway beside the tower. This is the Zagreb funicular, a little cable-pulled carriage that links the Upper Town with Ilica, the long shopping street below. It is famous for being one of the shortest funiculars in the world. The ride lasts barely a minute. Many visitors walk up the stairs instead, but riding it is a small pleasure. It connects the medieval hilltop with the modern city in a few quiet seconds.

Along the edge of the hill, next to the tower, runs the Strossmayer Promenade. It is a tree-lined walkway built along the line of the old southern walls. In warm months, people sit on benches here and look out over the rooftops. Couples stroll, children run ahead, and street musicians sometimes play. In the evening, the gas lamps of the Upper Town come on. Zagreb is one of the few cities that still lights some of its old streets this way.

From the tower, it is a short walk deeper into Gradec. A few minutes away stands Saint Mark's Church, with its roof of coloured tiles showing two coats of arms. Further on is the Stone Gate, another old entrance to the town, where people stop to light candles at a small shrine. The streets between them are narrow and quiet. Here you can feel how compact the medieval town was, and how carefully it guarded its entrances.

It is worth thinking about the tower's job across the centuries. First, it guarded a gate against enemies. Then it rang a bell to close the town each night. Now it fires a cannon to mark the middle of the day. Its purpose moved from defence to timekeeping. The building stayed in the same place, but the city around it grew from a fortified hilltop into a capital of nearly a million people.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment by the wall. Feel the stone under your hand, rough and worn smooth in places by countless other hands. Listen to the funicular, the voices on the promenade, and the traffic far below on Ilica. If it is close to noon, wait for the cannon. And as you walk away, look back once. The tower will still be there, at the edge of the hill, keeping its watch over Zagreb.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive shortly before noon to hear the cannon fire.
Ride the funicular up from Ilica to save your legs.
Take the tower stairs slowly; some sections are steep and narrow.
Walk the Strossmayer Promenade at dusk when gas lamps light up.
Continue to Saint Mark's Church and the Stone Gate nearby.
