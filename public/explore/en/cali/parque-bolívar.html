Parque Bolívar

Something moves in the tree above you.

The story

Something moves in the tree above you. Not a bird. A long green tail hangs down from a branch, and a scaled head turns slowly to study you. Down the path, another one crosses at its own unhurried speed. This is Parque Bolívar, in the center of Guayaquil, and its most famous residents are iguanas. Nobody here finds this strange.

The park fills a single block downtown, directly in front of the white towers of the Metropolitan Cathedral. Officially it is named Parque Seminario, after the merchant family that paid for its remodeling at the end of the nineteenth century, in the years when cacao money flowed through this port. Most locals use a simpler name: Parque de las Iguanas. The iguana park.

The square is older than either name. It served the city as an open plaza from early colonial days, used for markets, militia drills, and public celebrations. In the late nineteenth century it took its present shape: iron fences, shade trees, flower beds, an ornamental pond, and neat walkways in the style of a European garden.

At the center, Simón Bolívar rides a bronze horse, sword at rest. The equestrian statue of the Liberator was erected in 1889. Bolívar's tie to Guayaquil is direct: he met José de San Martín in this city in 1822, a private conversation between two liberators that helped decide the future of a continent.

But the true rulers of the park ignore the statue completely. Dozens of green iguanas live here in full freedom. They stretch along branches, doze in the flower beds, and cross the paths with slow, ancient patience. Some grow to more than one meter long, tail included, and they fear no one in this square.

No one can say exactly how the colony began. Iguanas are native to this coast, and the park's old trees, warm stone, and steady supply of admirers give them everything a reptile could want. Generations of iguanas have been born inside these iron fences, photographed from every possible angle, and none of them has ever hurried.

Watch them for a while. On sunny mornings they gather in open spots and lie still as carved wood, warming their blood for the day ahead. When the shade moves, they move with it. In the small pond, iguanas sometimes swim among the fish and turtles, tails waving like green ribbons in the dark water.

Caretakers watch over the colony, and the animals are used to people. Still, they are wild animals. Do not touch them, and keep food sealed and out of sight. An iguana that smells fruit can move surprisingly fast, and its claws are built for climbing bark, not for gentle manners.

The park keeps quieter company too. Pigeons strut along the railings. Turtles pile on the stones of the pond. Outside the fence, vendors sell juice, lottery tickets, and shaved ice. The sounds of downtown, engines and whistles and church bells, float over the trees and settle softly among the leaves.

Now lift your eyes across the street. The Metropolitan Cathedral rises in white neo-Gothic style, all pointed arches, ribbed windows, and twin towers. Churches stood on that site for centuries, built of wood, and fire took them more than once. The present concrete building was completed in the middle of the twentieth century.

Step inside if the doors are open. The air cools at once, and stained glass throws color across the floor. Then come back to the park benches, where office workers eat lunch and shoe shiners tap their brushes, all under the calm gaze of a species far older than any cathedral.

If you stand still for five minutes, the park rewards you. An iguana descends a trunk headfirst, claws clicking on the bark. Another lifts its head to catch the sun. A tourist laughs in disbelief. A local on the next bench does not even look up.

The park is small, and you can cross it in two minutes, but few places hold the city's layers so closely together: the Liberator cast in bronze, the cathedral in pale stone, the merchants' garden in iron and flowers, and the iguanas in their patient green skin. Look up into the branches one last time and count the tails. There are always more than you think. They were here first, in a way. They intend to stay.

Practical tips

Do not touch or feed the free-roaming iguanas
Keep snacks sealed; the animals approach food quickly
Visit midmorning when iguanas gather in sunny spots
Bring a camera with zoom for close reptile portraits
