The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves through dry grass and wild thyme. Somewhere a cicada starts up, then stops. Underfoot, the ground is hard and pale, scattered with chips of pottery that catch the sun. Across this long, flat-topped hill, low walls of stone run in straight lines, crossing at right angles. They look almost too orderly for ruins. That order is the first clue to what this place once was: not a village that grew by accident, but a city that someone planned.

You are standing at Olynthus, in the Chalcidice peninsula of northern Greece, a short drive from the modern town of Polygyros. Today it is quiet farmland and open sky. In the fifth and fourth centuries before Christ, it was one of the most important cities in this part of the Greek world. It led a federation of local towns known as the Chalcidian League. For a time, its power worried both Athens to the south and the rising kingdom of Macedon to the west.

The site sits on two hills. The southern hill holds the older settlement, with streets that follow the shape of the land. The northern hill, where most visitors walk, is different. Here the city expanded in the later fifth century, around 432 BC, when people from smaller towns nearby moved together into one stronger centre. Greeks had a word for this kind of merger — synoecism, which simply means living together in one home.

The new quarter was laid out on a grid. Long avenues ran north to south, crossed by narrower streets. The blocks between them were divided into houses of roughly equal size. This style of planning is often linked with Hippodamus of Miletus, a Greek thinker who became famous for orderly city layouts. Walk along one of the main streets, and you can still feel the logic of it. Each block repeats the last, like the squares on a chessboard.

Now, here is the first surprise. Olynthus matters to historians mostly because of how it ended. In 348 BC, Philip the Second of Macedon — the father of Alexander the Great — besieged the city and captured it. The town was destroyed. Ancient writers report that its people were sold into slavery. The city was never rebuilt on any large scale. Life here stopped, more or less, in a single moment.

That sudden end left behind something rare. In most ancient cities, later builders reused the stones, dug new foundations, and erased the homes of earlier people. At Olynthus, that did not happen. The houses stayed where they fell, with their floors, their hearths, and many of their household objects still in place. For archaeologists, it became one of the clearest windows into everyday Greek domestic life in the classical period.

Before the fall, Athens watched these events closely. The Athenian orator Demosthenes gave a series of speeches urging his city to send help to Olynthus against Philip. These speeches are still known as the Olynthiacs. Athens did send some aid, but it was not enough. When you read those speeches today, you are reading the political argument over the fate of the very streets around you.

The large-scale excavation of Olynthus began in 1928, led by the American archaeologist David Robinson. His teams worked here through several seasons into the 1930s. They uncovered many houses on the northern hill, and their published reports became a basic reference for how Greek families lived. More recently, an international research project has returned to the site, using newer methods like soil sampling and geophysical survey to ask fresh questions.

Look closely at the houses as you pass. Many followed a similar plan. A door from the street led into an open courtyard, which brought light and air into the home. Rooms opened around it. Some houses had a covered porch along one side of the court. Inside, archaeologists found places for cooking, storage, and work. The house was not only a home — it was also a small workshop, where weaving, food preparation, and trade could all take place.

Here is the second surprise. In several houses, the excavators found floors decorated with pebble mosaics. These are made not from cut tiles, but from small natural river pebbles, black and white and sometimes coloured, set into mortar to form pictures. They are among the earliest known mosaics in Greece. Scenes include mythological figures and patterned borders. One well-known mosaic, from a house just outside the city, shows the hero Bellerophon riding the winged horse Pegasus.

Many of these mosaics were placed in a special room called the andron, which means the men's room. This was where the master of the house hosted guests for dinner and drinking parties. Couches lined the walls, and the decorated floor sat in the centre where everyone could see it. So the most beautiful floor in the house was also the most social one. It showed visitors the taste and status of the family.

Small finds tell the human side of the siege. Among the objects recovered here are lead sling bullets, the ammunition of ancient slingers. Some carry stamped inscriptions, including names linked to Philip and his commanders. Others bear short, rough messages meant for the enemy. Holding that idea in mind changes how this calm hill feels. The quiet you hear now was, for a short time, the sound of a city under attack.

As you walk back, take a slow look along one of the straight streets toward the horizon. Notice how the low walls line up, block after block, across the hilltop. Notice the pottery fragments at your feet, and the open fields beyond, where olive trees and grain now grow. Thousands of people once crossed this ground every day, cooking, arguing, weaving, and hosting dinners. The grid they lived in is still here, written in stone, waiting for you to read it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strong midday sun.
Bring water and a hat; shade on the hilltop is scarce.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone and loose gravel paths.
Walk the northern hill first to see the planned grid.
Pair your visit with the archaeological museum in Polygyros.
