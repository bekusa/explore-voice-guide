The guide

The story

Feel the air first. Up here it is cooler than the city below. It is damp, too, and it smells of wet leaves and pine. A ribbon of gold curves away from your feet, and on a misty morning its far end simply disappears into cloud. Somewhere behind you a cable car hums on its wire. Ahead, people murmur in a dozen languages. And beneath the walkway, two enormous grey hands rise out of the mountainside... holding the whole thing up.

Welcome to the Golden Bridge, known in Vietnamese as Cầu Vàng. It sits high in the Ba Na Hills, west of Da Nang, well over a thousand metres above the coast. The bridge opened in 2018. Within weeks, photographs of it spread across the internet. Many people who had never heard of Da Nang suddenly wanted to stand exactly where you are standing now. It is a remarkably young landmark for a country with such a long past.

Let us start with the walkway itself. It runs for about one hundred and fifty metres. It is not a straight line. It bends in a long, gentle arc, like a loop of thread dropped across the slope. The designers wanted it to feel as if the mountain were offering it up. The gold colour is paint and finish, not metal. Look closely at the railings and you will see rows of flowers planted along the edges, softening the line with colour.

Now the hands. They are the reason most people come. From a distance they look like ancient stone, cracked and weathered, patched with moss and lichen. Here is the first surprise. They are not stone at all. They were built on a steel frame, then wrapped in wire mesh and finished with fibreglass. The ageing is deliberate. The cracks were sculpted, and the colours were painted. The goal was to make a new object look as if it had waited on this ridge for centuries.

That illusion is clever because of where you are. The Ba Na Hills have a strong visual identity, with mist, forest, and old European-style buildings. A shiny, modern bridge might have looked out of place. Instead, the designers chose something that feels like a ruin or a myth. The hands suggest a giant, or perhaps a mountain god, lifting a golden thread from the earth. You are free to choose your own story. The designers left that open on purpose.

The bridge was designed by TA Landscape Architecture, a firm from Ho Chi Minh City. Vietnamese designers, working on a Vietnamese mountain, created one of the most photographed structures in Southeast Asia. That matters to many people here. For years, the region's famous images were temples and rice terraces. This bridge added something new. It is playful, modern, and theatrical, and it was made at home.

To understand why a bridge stands on a mountaintop at all, go back about a century. In the early twentieth century, French colonial officials looked for an escape from the heat of the lowlands. They found it here. The Ba Na Hills became a hill station, which was a cool retreat with villas and gardens. Similar retreats existed across colonial Asia. Later, during decades of war, the old settlement fell into ruin, and the forest moved back in.

In more recent years, the mountain was transformed into a large resort, now operated as Sun World Ba Na Hills. That is why you arrived by cable car rather than on foot. The ride itself is part of the experience. You float above dense jungle and waterfalls, and the cabin climbs steeply as the air cools. The Golden Bridge was built as part of this development. It links points in a garden area on the slope, and it gives visitors a reason to pause between stations.

Here is the second surprise. For all its fame, the bridge is not really a crossing. It does not connect two villages or carry traffic. Its purpose is the walk itself. It is a viewing platform shaped like a bridge. Once you know that, you might slow down. There is nowhere you need to get to. The whole point is the view over the valley and the feeling of stepping out over the trees.

The weather changes everything here. On a clear day, you can look down over green ridges toward the lowlands. On a foggy day, which is common, the world shrinks to a few metres of gold and grey. The hands loom out of the cloud like something half remembered. Some visitors feel disappointed by the mist. Others think it is the best version of the place. The bridge seems to float, and the crowds become soft shapes.

And there will be crowds. This is one of the busiest spots on the mountain. Everyone wants the same photograph, framed between the fingers. Be patient and be kind. Let people take their moment, and they will usually give you yours. If you walk to the far end of the curve, you often find a little more space. You also get a clearer angle back toward the hands.

When you are ready to move on, the rest of the mountain is close. Nearby you will find flower gardens, old wine cellars from the French period, and a recreated French village with cobbled lanes and pastel facades. It is a strange and memorable mix. There is a Vietnamese mountain, a European fantasy town, and between them a golden thread held up by giant hands.

Before you go, stand still for a moment. Put one hand on the railing. Feel the cool, damp metal. Look down at the fingertips below you, with their sculpted cracks and painted moss, and remember that they are younger than most of the phones pointed at them. Listen to the wind in the trees and the cable cars humming overhead. Then walk slowly along the curve, and watch the gold fade back into the mist behind you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Take an early cable car to beat the crowds.
Bring a light jacket because the mountain air is cool.
Walk to the far end of the curve for space.
Pack a rain layer since mist and showers are common.
Allow extra time for the long cable car ride.
