The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment under the arcade outside. The pavement is cool and smooth, polished by countless shoes. Shade falls across your shoulders. Then the street opens, and a tall wall of pale stone and warm plaster rises in front of you. Traffic hums along the avenue behind you. Bicycles tick past. And just above the noise, the cathedral of Saint Peter stands calm and upright, as if it has been waiting for you to look up.

Here is the first surprise. Many visitors assume the great church on Piazza Maggiore is Bologna's cathedral. It is not. That vast unfinished brick basilica belongs to Saint Petronius, the city's patron saint. The seat of the archbishop is here instead, on Via dell'Indipendenza, a few minutes' walk to the north. This is the Metropolitan Cathedral of Saint Peter. It is quieter, less photographed, and it carries a much longer story than its smooth front suggests.

A church has stood on this site since the Middle Ages. The building around you, however, is mostly a product of later rebuilding. Over the centuries the older church suffered damage and was remodelled again and again. What you see today belongs largely to the Baroque age — the style of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, known for grand scale, strong light, and dramatic space. So think of this cathedral as layers, not as a single moment frozen in stone.

Look at the facade. Its columns, pediments, and clean vertical lines belong to the eighteenth century. This work is closely linked to Cardinal Prospero Lambertini. He served as archbishop of Bologna and then, in 1740, was elected pope, taking the name Benedict XIV. He was a learned man, known for his wit and his interest in science. The Bolognese still remember him fondly as one of their own who reached the highest office in the Church.

Now notice where the cathedral sits. It does not command a wide open square. Instead, it stands in line with the street, framed by the long covered walkways that run beside it. These are Bologna's porticoes — arcades that shelter pedestrians from sun and rain. The city has dozens of kilometres of them, and in 2021 they were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list. Here, the cathedral seems to grow straight out of daily city life.

Step inside, and the temperature drops. The noise of the avenue fades almost at once. The interior is a single wide nave, bright and orderly, with side chapels opening along each wall. White and grey surfaces reflect the light from the high windows. There is little clutter. The eye travels forward along the nave, almost without effort, toward the far end of the church. This was the Baroque idea: one great space, one clear direction.

At that far end, above the main altar, look up into the curved apse. There you will find a large painting of the Annunciation — the moment the angel Gabriel tells Mary she will bear a son. It is the work of Ludovico Carracci, a central figure in Bologna's painting school. Ludovico, together with his cousins Annibale and Agostino, helped steer Italian art away from cold artificial styles toward warmth, feeling, and natural observation.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. This Annunciation is usually described as one of Ludovico's last works, painted late in his life, around the end of the 1610s. Art historians have long discussed a flaw in it — some say a problem with the angel's foot. A popular story claims the painter was so upset by the criticism that it hastened his death. Treat that as a local legend, not documented history. But it shows how seriously Bologna took its painters.

Before you leave the church, look for the stairs down to the crypt, if they are open. Below the main floor the atmosphere changes. The space is lower and more enclosed. Among the works kept in the cathedral is a terracotta group of mourning figures gathered around the body of Christ, by the Renaissance sculptor Alfonso Lombardi. Terracotta — baked clay — was a favourite material in this region, where good building stone is rare and clay is everywhere.

That clay is the reason Bologna looks the way it does. The city is often called la rossa, the red one, because of its brick and its terracotta-coloured plaster. Even this cathedral, with its smooth Baroque front, sits on a city built of fired earth. When you walk outside again, touch a column under the arcade. Under the plaster, there is very often brick — the same humble material that built the medieval towers nearby.

Now listen. If you are lucky, you may hear the bells. The bell tower rises behind the main body of the church, and it is much older than the facade, with roots in the Romanesque period — the style of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, with thick walls and rounded arches. It is one of the tallest bell towers in the city. Bologna also has its own tradition of ringing, called the Bolognese style, in which teams swing the bells full circle by hand.

This ringing is not a recording or a machine. Volunteer ringers still practise the art, passing the technique from one generation to the next. When the bells turn in their frames, the sound comes in rolling waves rather than single strokes. It is one of those things that connects the cathedral to the living city. Here, an old craft continues not in a museum case, but in the air above the street.

So take a last look before you go. The pale facade. The arcades running away in both directions. The people walking under them with shopping bags and phones, barely glancing up. This cathedral has never needed a grand square to matter. It simply stands on its street, as it has for centuries, part of the ordinary rhythm of Bologna. As you walk away under the porticoes, listen for the bells, and feel the cool stone underfoot once more.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk north from Piazza Maggiore along Via dell'Indipendenza.
Dress modestly; shoulders and knees covered in church.
Ask whether the crypt is open during your visit.
Linger outside around service times to hear the bells.
Stay quiet and avoid flash photography during services.
