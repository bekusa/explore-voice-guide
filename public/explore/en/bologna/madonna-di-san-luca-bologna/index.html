The guide

The story

Listen to your footsteps for a moment. Under the arches, the sound comes back to you from the brick and plaster, close and hollow. Outside the portico, the air feels open and bright. Inside, the light falls in stripes across the paving, arch after arch, rising toward the green hill above Bologna. Somewhere above you, out of sight for now, stands a church with a pale dome. You are walking to it the way pilgrims have walked for centuries.

The sanctuary of the Madonna di San Luca sits on a hill called the Colle della Guardia, the Hill of the Guard, southwest of the old city. From almost anywhere in Bologna, look toward the hills and you can find it: a warm, rose-coloured building with a rounded dome, sitting on the ridge. Locals say that when the Bolognese come home by train or by car, the first sight of San Luca tells them they are back. For many people here, it is less a monument than a landmark of belonging.

The heart of the place is a painting. It is a small icon of the Virgin Mary holding the Christ Child, painted in the Byzantine style, the Greek Christian style of the eastern Mediterranean, with dark faces and gold. According to tradition, Saint Luke the Evangelist painted it himself. That is where the sanctuary takes its name. Art historians see it differently. They place the image in the Middle Ages, roughly the twelfth or thirteenth century. Both stories matter here, for different reasons.

The legend continues. One popular tale says a pilgrim, a hermit from the East, received the icon in Constantinople with an instruction: carry it to a hill called the Guard. He searched for years until he heard the name in Bologna. Whatever the truth, a small community of religious women kept the image on this hilltop in the Middle Ages. Over the centuries, the shrine grew. The church you will see today replaced earlier buildings in the eighteenth century.

Now — the walk itself. This covered walkway is the Portico of San Luca, and it is the longest portico in the world. It runs close to four kilometres, from the old city gate at Porta Saragozza to the church door. It has more than six hundred arches. The most commonly quoted figure is 666. Building began in the 1670s and continued into the early eighteenth century. It was made so that pilgrims, and the icon itself, could climb the hill sheltered from rain and sun.

Here is a detail most visitors miss. That number, 666, is the number of the beast in the Book of Revelation. Some people read the whole portico as a giant serpent, the devil, twisting up the hill. At the top, the Virgin waits to crush it underfoot, as she does in many Christian images. Treat this as a popular interpretation, not a documented plan. Still, once you hear it, the long, curving line of arches is hard to unsee.

The portico was not paid for by one rich patron. Families, guilds, religious groups, and ordinary citizens funded the arches, often one at a time. As you climb, look at the walls and pillars. Many carry painted coats of arms, inscriptions, and plaques naming the people who paid. Some are faded almost to nothing. Others were restored and look freshly painted. Together they work like a long book of names, written by a whole city across many decades.

The route has two clear parts. The first stretch runs flat, almost straight, through the neighbourhood outside Porta Saragozza, past cafés and apartment buildings. Then you reach the Arco del Meloncello, a curved, theatrical arch that lifts the portico over the road. From here the climb begins in earnest. The path turns into steps and steep ramps, zigzagging up the hillside. Along the way, small chapels mark the mysteries of the rosary, a sequence of prayers and meditations on the life of Christ and Mary.

The church at the top was designed by the architect Carlo Francesco Dotti in the eighteenth century. He gave it an oval shape inside, under a large dome, with curved side wings reaching out at the front like open arms. The style is late Baroque, a style that loves curves, movement, and drama. Inside, the space feels light and gathered. Your eye goes straight to the high altar, where the icon sits behind glass, dark and small among the gold.

The icon does not always stay on the hill. Every year, in the week of the Feast of the Ascension in late spring, it is carried down the portico into the city. It stays in the Cathedral of Saint Peter for several days, then returns. According to tradition, this began in the fifteenth century, after a long period of rain threatened the harvest. The procession is said to have ended the rains. Today crowds still line the streets to see the Madonna pass.

In 2021, UNESCO added the porticoes of Bologna to its World Heritage List. The city has dozens of kilometres of covered walkways, and this one was part of that recognition. But the portico is also simply a place where people live their lives. Early in the morning, you will see joggers running the steps, cyclists climbing the road outside, and older walkers taking it slowly. For many Bolognese, walking up to San Luca is a habit, a challenge, or a quiet promise.

When you reach the top, turn around. On one side, the city spreads out below: red roofs, the old towers, and the flat plain beyond. On the other side, the hills of the Apennines roll away in soft green lines. On a clear day, the view runs a very long way. Inside the sanctuary, a route also leads up toward the dome, with views from above. Check on site, because access can change.

Take a moment before you head back down. Notice the breeze on the hill, the bells, perhaps the voices of other walkers arriving out of breath. The portico waits below you, arch after arch, ready to carry you back into the city. As you walk down, watch the light change between the pillars, and read a few more names on the walls. Each one belongs to someone who helped build this long road to the hill.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start early in the morning to avoid heat and crowds.
Wear comfortable shoes for the steep steps after Meloncello.
Bring water; the final climb is long and steady.
Come during Ascension week to see the icon's procession.
Respect services inside; dress modestly for the sanctuary.
