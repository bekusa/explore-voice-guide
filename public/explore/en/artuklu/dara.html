The guide

The story

Listen first. The wind moves across the limestone with a dry, steady hiss, and somewhere a dog barks from a village roof. Under your feet the ground is pale and broken, scattered with cut stones that once belonged to something much larger. The light here is hard and white at midday, softer and honey-coloured toward evening. Around you, ordinary houses stand among ancient walls. This is Dara, a frontier city of the late Roman world, still lived in, still half buried.

To understand why a city stands here at all, look south. The land falls away toward the wide Mesopotamian plain, the flat country between the great rivers. For centuries this plain was a border. On one side stood the Eastern Roman Empire, which historians often call the Byzantine Empire. On the other side stood the Sasanian Persians, the last great Iranian dynasty before the arrival of Islam. Dara was built to watch that border, and to hold it.

The story begins with a loss. In the fourth century, Rome gave up the nearby city of Nisibis, today's Nusaybin, to Persia under a peace treaty. That left the Roman frontier without a strong base in this region. In the early sixth century, the emperor Anastasius the First ordered a new fortress city here. For a time it carried his name, Anastasiopolis. Builders worked quickly, because the Persians did not welcome a new stronghold so close to their own lands.

The next emperor, Justinian, strengthened the city further. The Byzantine historian Procopius wrote about Dara in some detail, and his accounts are one of the main reasons we know so much about this place. He described the walls, the towers, and above all the careful management of water. A city on a frontier must survive a siege. That means it must keep water flowing inside the walls, and keep it away from the enemy outside.

In 530, the plain below became a battlefield. The Roman general Belisarius faced a large Persian army just outside the city. He used trenches and careful positioning to break the Persian attack. The Battle of Dara is still studied today as an example of clever defensive tactics. Belisarius later became one of the most famous commanders of his age. But his career, in a sense, began on this dusty ground in front of you.

Victory did not last forever. In 573, after a long siege, the Persian king Khosrow the First captured Dara. The fall of the city shocked the Roman court. Later chroniclers wrote that the news deeply affected the emperor Justin the Second. Dara changed hands again in the following decades. Then, in the seventh century, Arab armies swept through the region, and the old frontier between Rome and Persia disappeared altogether.

Now, let us go underground. One of the most remarkable sights here is a huge cistern carved and built beneath the ground. Step down into it, and the temperature drops at once. The air feels cool and damp, and your voice comes back to you from the stone. Tall arches support the roof. Light falls in through openings above, cutting pale shafts through the dark. Locals say the space was once used as a dungeon, though this is local tradition rather than certain history.

Think about what this cistern meant. Thousands of people, soldiers and families and animals, needed water during a siege. A space like this could store enough to keep a city alive for a long time. Procopius also described works on the stream that runs through the site. Engineers controlled its flow so that floods would not damage the walls, and so that attackers could not easily cut off the supply. Water, here, was a weapon of defence.

Walk out toward the western edge of the site, and you reach the necropolis — the city of the dead. Here the hillside was cut back like a quarry, and the stone faces were carved with burial chambers. Dark doorways open into the rock. Some chambers hold several shelves or niches for the dead. Standing in front of these cliffs, you can feel how much effort went into honouring the dead in a place built mainly for war.

The quarry and the tombs are connected. Builders likely took stone from these slopes to raise the walls and buildings of the city. Then the cut faces became a place for graves. It is a simple and practical idea. Nothing was wasted. When you look closely at the rock, you can still see the marks of the tools that shaped it, long straight lines left by workers whose names nobody recorded.

Here is something many visitors miss. The modern village around you is part of the story, not separate from it. For generations, people have lived among these ruins. Some houses use old cut blocks in their walls. Look at a garden wall or a doorway, and you may notice a stone that is too smooth, too square, too carefully finished for a simple village house. It almost certainly came from the ancient city.

Archaeologists have been excavating here for several decades, and the work continues. Much of Dara still lies under the soil and under the village. Each season of digging adds new details — streets, buildings, foundations of churches. This means the Dara you see today is not finished. The visitor who returns in ten years may walk past walls that are invisible now. That is part of the quiet excitement of this place.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Look at the line of the old walls against the sky. Look at the plain to the south, where armies once gathered and where farmers work today. Listen again to the wind over the stone, to the voices from the village houses. Empires argued over this ground for centuries. Now children play near the ruins, and the stones that once held a frontier hold up garden walls instead.

Practical tips

Carry water and sun protection on the open site.
Combine your visit with a day trip from Mardin.
