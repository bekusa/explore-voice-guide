The guide

The story

Leave your shoes at the door and step onto the carpet. It is soft and cool underfoot, a welcome change from the heat of the stone outside. Overhead, the light comes in pale and even through high windows. Somewhere a pair of footsteps crosses the hall, and then a low murmur of recitation. This is Masjid al-Qiblatayn, the Mosque of the Two Qiblas, in the northwest of Medina.

That name is the key to everything here. The qibla is the direction Muslims face when they pray. Today, every mosque on Earth points toward the Kaaba in Mecca. But it was not always so. In the earliest years of Islam, the Prophet Muhammad and his followers prayed facing Jerusalem. This mosque is remembered as the place where that direction changed.

To understand why that matters, picture Medina in the early 620s. The Prophet had recently arrived from Mecca, in the journey Muslims call the Hijra, the migration. Medina was then a scattered oasis of date palms, wells and farming clans. Small prayer places stood among the gardens. This one belonged to a local clan called the Banu Salamah. For a time, people knew it simply as the mosque of that clan.

The tradition tells the story like this. In the second year after the migration, the Prophet was leading a prayer here. Midway through, a revelation came, instructing believers to turn toward the Sacred Mosque in Mecca. The verse appears in the second chapter of the Quran, known as al-Baqarah, or The Cow. According to the accounts, the Prophet turned, and the worshippers behind him turned with him. They finished the prayer facing the opposite way.

Think about the geometry for a moment. Jerusalem lies to the north of Medina. Mecca lies to the south. So this was not a small adjustment of a few degrees. It was close to a full reversal. A row of people standing shoulder to shoulder had to turn around and reform their lines, all in the middle of a single act of worship. It is a strikingly physical image for a spiritual change.

Here is something many visitors do not realise. Early Muslim sources do not all agree on the exact setting. Some accounts place the moment of the change at a different mosque, or describe the news reaching other congregations in Medina while they were praying. Historians still discuss these differences. What is certain is that this particular mosque has carried the story, and the name, for well over a thousand years.

The change of direction meant much more than orientation. It gave the young community a focus of its own, centred on the Kaaba, the ancient cube-shaped shrine in Mecca. Every prayer, five times a day, would now point there. When you see millions of pilgrims on television circling the Kaaba, you are seeing the result of the moment remembered in this room.

Now, look around you. The building you are standing in is not the original. The first mosque would have been very simple — walls of mud brick, a roof of palm trunks and fronds, a floor of earth or sand. Over the centuries it was rebuilt and repaired many times. Rulers of different eras, including the Ottomans, who governed this region for several centuries, left their mark on it.

The current mosque dates from a major rebuilding in the 1980s, under the Saudi state. Its design is credited to the Egyptian architect Abdel-Wahed El-Wakil. He was known for drawing on traditional Islamic building forms — domes, arches, thick walls — rather than glass and steel. Notice the clean white surfaces outside, the paired minarets, and the domes rising above the roofline. The style feels calm and deliberate, not showy.

There is a second surprise here. For a long time, the mosque was said to contain two prayer niches. A prayer niche, or mihrab, is the arched recess in the wall that shows the direction of Mecca. One niche faced Mecca, and an older one faced Jerusalem. In the modern rebuilding, the old Jerusalem-facing niche was not kept as a working feature. Today, all prayer here faces one direction only, toward Mecca.

Step back outside, and the city returns. Traffic hums along the wide roads nearby. Tour buses pull in and out, because this mosque is a regular stop on the visiting routes of Medina, often alongside the Quba Mosque and the Prophet's Mosque in the centre of the city. You will hear many languages in the courtyard — Urdu, Indonesian, Turkish, Arabic, English — as groups gather and pilgrims share the story with one another.

Take a moment to watch how people behave here. Many visitors pray two short units of prayer as a greeting to the mosque. Others simply sit against a pillar, phone put away, and look toward the front of the hall. Some families photograph each other outside by the white walls. For most people who come, this is not a monument to be studied. It is a place to stand inside a story they have known since childhood.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute and face the front of the prayer hall. That is south, toward Mecca. Now imagine turning to face the back wall, toward Jerusalem, and then turning again. As you walk out into the sunlight, notice how every mosque you pass in this city, and every prayer you hear, points the same way — the direction that settled here, in this quiet room.

Practical tips

Check current access rules for non-Muslim visitors before arriving.
Dress modestly, with arms and legs fully covered.
Women should look for the separate women's entrance.
Avoid congregational prayer times if you only want to look.
Combine your visit with the Quba Mosque on one route.
