The guide

The story

Listen first. Sandals shuffle across smooth white stone. Water runs at the washing taps. Somewhere a child laughs, and then the call to prayer rises over the date palms. The light here is bright and dry. The white walls of the mosque hold it so strongly that you may want to narrow your eyes. Take a moment before you look for any history. Feel the warmth coming up from the ground, and notice the calm that settles over the crowd as people walk through the gates.

You are standing at Quba Mosque, on the southern edge of Medina, a few kilometres from the Prophet's Mosque at the city's heart. For Muslims, this place holds a special rank. It is widely regarded as the first mosque built in Islam. Its story begins with a journey, the Hijra, the migration of the Prophet Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to this oasis in 622. That migration is so important that the Islamic calendar counts its years from it.

Picture the land as it was then. Quba was a village of farms, wells and palm groves outside the main settlement, then known as Yathrib. Early Muslim sources say the Prophet arrived here first. He stayed for several days before he travelled on into the town. During that stay, he and his companions laid the foundations of a simple place of prayer. Tradition says the Prophet carried stones with his own hands, working beside the others.

That first building was nothing like what you see today. Accounts describe walls of mud brick and a roof of palm trunks and fronds. There was no dome, no marble and no minaret. It was an open, shaded space where a small community could stand together in rows. Keep that image in your mind as you look at the white walls in front of you. The modern building stands on ground connected to that first, very humble act of building.

Many Muslims connect this mosque with a verse of the Quran. It speaks of a mosque founded on piety from the first day. Scholars have discussed exactly which mosque the verse means, and some apply it to the Prophet's Mosque as well. But the link with Quba is old and deeply felt. For visitors who come here, the idea of a beginning is part of the experience. This is where the story of the mosque as a building starts.

Here is something many visitors do not know until they arrive. According to well-known hadith, the recorded sayings and actions of the Prophet, he returned to Quba regularly, often on Saturdays. Sometimes he came riding, and sometimes on foot, to pray here. Another widely cited saying teaches that a person who purifies himself at home and then prays here receives a reward like that of an Umrah, the lesser pilgrimage to Mecca. That is why so many people make the trip.

Those sayings shape the crowd around you. Pilgrims who come to Medina after the Hajj or Umrah often add Quba to their days in the city. Many perform their washing at their hotel before they set out, following that tradition. Then they pray two units of prayer inside. On Saturday mornings, you may notice more families and more buses. In a quiet way, people are repeating a habit that is fourteen centuries old.

Across the centuries, the mosque was rebuilt and enlarged many times. Different rulers and governors repaired it, extended it and added new features as Medina grew. Little of the early fabric survives above ground. That is normal for a building in constant use. A place of prayer that people use every day is maintained, replaced and improved. It is not kept like a museum piece. The continuity here is in the practice, not in the stones.

The building you see now dates mainly from a major reconstruction in the 1980s. The design is credited to the Egyptian architect Abdel-Wahed El-Wakil. He was known for his respect for traditional building forms. Look at how the design uses old ideas. There are rounded domes, tall minarets, arches and inner courtyards. The colours are mostly white and soft cream, so the building stays cool to the eye even at midday.

Now look up at the domes. Their pale curves sit against the deep blue Arabian sky, and the outlines are clean and simple. The minarets rise at the corners like tall white towers. Notice the shaded arcades as well. These are the rows of arches along the walkways. In this climate, shade is not decoration. It is how a building cares for the people inside it. The arcades let air move and protect worshippers from the strong sun.

Turn around and look at the neighbourhood. Quba is no longer a separate village. Roads, hotels and shops now surround the mosque. But you can still see date palms nearby, a reminder of the farmland that once fed this oasis. Stalls often sell dates, prayer beads and perfume oils. A long pedestrian avenue connects this area with the centre of Medina. Some visitors walk it, choosing to arrive on foot as the Prophet sometimes did.

The mosque continues to change. In recent years, Saudi authorities have announced large expansion plans to welcome many more worshippers. You may see construction fences, cranes or new paving around the site during your visit. This is part of the same long pattern. Each generation has made room for the people who come next. If parts of the area look unfinished, you are watching one more chapter in a story that began with mud brick and palm fronds.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment near the gates. Feel the warm stone under your feet again. Listen to the water at the taps, the soft voices and the slow footsteps. Look once more at the white domes. As you walk away, notice the palms along the road and the people still arriving. Each of them has come to the same place, for a reason that is very old.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Expect larger crowds on Saturday mornings.
Dress modestly and bring a bag for your shoes.
The prayer hall is open to Muslim worshippers only.
Walk the pedestrian avenue from central Medina in cooler hours.
