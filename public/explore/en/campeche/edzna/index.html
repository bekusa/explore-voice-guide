The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, away from the city, the loudest sound is often the insects in the dry scrub. The air smells of warm limestone and cut grass. Underfoot, the ground is pale and dusty, and the heat rises from it in slow waves. Ahead of you, a great stepped pyramid climbs out of a flat green valley. This is Edzna, an ancient Maya city in the state of Campeche. It lies inland from the coast, about an hour's drive from the walled port where many visitors begin their day.

Before we walk further, a word about the name. The most common interpretation is that Edzna means something like House of the Itzas. The Itzas were a Maya lineage known from other parts of the Yucatan Peninsula. Scholars still debate this reading, so treat it as a likely explanation, not a settled fact. What is certain is that people lived in this valley for a very long time. The settlement grew over many centuries, from modest farming villages into a planned city of stone.

Archaeologists generally place the first occupation here several centuries before the common era. The city kept changing for a remarkably long period after that. It reached its greatest size and power during the Classic period of Maya history, roughly the middle of the first millennium. People continued to live here, in smaller numbers, well into later centuries. So the buildings around you are not from one moment. They are layers. Builders often placed new structures directly over older ones, wrapping the past inside the present.

Now look at the open ground in front of you. This is the Main Plaza, a wide, flat space framed by long platforms. Imagine it full of people. Plazas like this were the stage for public life in Maya cities. Ceremonies, markets and political gatherings could all happen here. On the western side stands a very long building known as the Nohochna, a Maya name meaning Big House. Its broad steps face the plaza like rows of seats, and some researchers think people watched events from them.

Turn toward the tallest structure, on the eastern side. This raised complex is called the Great Acropolis. On top of it stands the building most visitors come to see: the Building of the Five Stories. It rises around thirty metres above the plaza. Each of its five levels is a stepped terrace, with rooms set into the lower floors. A steep central staircase runs straight up the front to a temple at the summit. Maya architecture rarely stacks rooms like this, which makes the building unusual.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look closely at the risers of the lower steps on that central staircase. Some of them once carried carved hieroglyphs, the written script of the ancient Maya. Weather and time have worn most of them away, but traces remain. The Maya wrote on stairways, stone slabs and walls to record rulers, dates and important events. At Edzna, carved stone slabs called stelae have also been found around the site. Several of them are now protected under shelters, so look for them as you walk.

The style of the buildings tells another story. Edzna sits at a crossroads between several regional traditions of Maya architecture. Some features recall the cities of the southern lowlands, in what is now Guatemala. Others look more like the Puuc style of the hills to the north, known for neat, cut-stone facades. A third influence, called Chenes, is famous for doorways shaped like the open mouths of creatures. Standing here, you see a city that borrowed ideas from many neighbours and mixed them in its own way.

Now for the second surprise, and perhaps the most important one. This valley has no river. The soil holds water badly in the dry season and floods in the rainy season. The people of Edzna solved this problem with engineering. They dug a large network of canals and reservoirs across the valley floor. The canals drained the floods, stored rainwater, and probably helped with farming and transport. Many researchers describe it as one of the most ambitious water systems known in the Maya world.

One part of that system is especially striking. Archaeologists identified a channel that surrounded a raised area, a little like a moat around a castle. Its exact purpose is still discussed. It may have been defensive, practical, or both. You will not see much of the canal network from the plaza, because grass and soil now cover most of it. But when you look out across the flat green land, remember that it was once shaped by hand, channel by channel.

If you have time, walk south toward a smaller group of buildings to find the Temple of the Masks. Here, under protective roofs, two large faces made of stucco survive on the facade. Stucco is a plaster made from burnt limestone, which the Maya shaped and painted. The faces are often interpreted as images of the sun, perhaps one rising and one setting. Look at their eyes, their ear ornaments and the shapes around their mouths. Traces of original colour may still be visible.

The sky mattered here. Like many Maya cities, Edzna seems to have been laid out with careful attention to the movements of the sun. Many observers note that, on certain days of the year, the setting sun lines up with the Building of the Five Stories. These dates connect to the agricultural calendar and the coming of the rains. Crowds sometimes gather for these moments today. Whether every alignment was intended is still debated, but the connection between buildings, sun and season is clear in Maya thought.

As you explore, notice the small things. There is a ball court, two parallel sloping walls where a ritual ball game was once played. You will see the grey and cream tones of the limestone, patched in places by modern restoration. Lizards sun themselves on warm steps. Birds call from the trees at the edge of the clearing. On many days the site is quiet, and you may have whole buildings to yourself. That stillness is part of the experience here.

Before you leave, stand once more in the Main Plaza and face the Great Acropolis. Let your eyes travel up the five terraces to the temple at the top. Then look down at the dust and grass at your feet. People walked this same ground for many centuries, carrying water, building, watching the sun. As you walk back toward the entrance, listen again to the insects in the scrub. The valley still floods and dries with the seasons... just as it did when this city was alive.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Bring plenty of water, a hat and sun protection.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven stone steps and dusty paths.
Hire a car or join a tour from Campeche city.
Use insect repellent, especially near the trees and grass.
