The guide

The story

Step inside, and the air changes. It is cooler here, and very quiet. The traffic of Niš fades behind the chapel doors. In front of you stands a squat, pale structure of stone and mortar. At first it looks like any old wall. Then your eyes adjust... and you see the rows of hollow shapes pressed into its surface. Some are empty sockets in the mortar. Others still hold what the tower was built from: human skulls.

This is Skull Tower, known in Serbian as Ćele Kula. It is one of the most unusual monuments in the Balkans. It is not a ruin, a fortress, or a church in the usual sense. It is a war memorial made from the bodies of the defeated. To understand why it exists, we need to go back to the early nineteenth century, and to a hill just outside this city.

In 1804, Serbs rose against Ottoman rule in what is now called the First Serbian Uprising. For years, rebel forces fought to push the Ottoman army out of their lands. By 1809, the rebels had advanced toward Niš, which was then an important Ottoman stronghold on the road between Belgrade and Constantinople. On a hill called Čegar, northeast of the town, Serbian fighters dug into trenches and waited.

One of their commanders was a man named Stevan Sinđelić. He led the defence of one of the trench positions on Čegar. When the Ottoman army attacked, it came in overwhelming numbers. The Serbian lines held for a time, then began to break. Reinforcements did not arrive. Sinđelić and his men were surrounded, and the enemy poured into their entrenchment.

What happened next is the moment every Serbian schoolchild learns. According to the accounts that have come down to us, Sinđelić fired his pistol into the gunpowder store. The explosion killed him, most of his remaining fighters, and a large number of the Ottoman soldiers who had overrun the position. It was an act of defiance — a refusal to be captured. In Serbia, the Battle of Čegar is remembered as a defeat that became a symbol.

The Ottoman commander, Hurshid Pasha, wanted the opposite kind of symbol. He ordered the heads of the fallen Serbian rebels to be collected. Their skin was stripped, and the skulls were set into a tower of stone and lime beside the main road into Niš. Anyone travelling to or from the town would pass it. The message was meant to be simple: this is what happens to those who rebel.

The figure most often given is 952 skulls, set in neat rows on all four sides. Today only a small fraction remain — the usual count is fifty-eight. Over the decades, many were lost to weather and time. Many others were taken away by families who wanted to give their relatives a proper burial. Look at the empty hollows in the mortar. Each one marks a place where a skull once sat.

Here is something many visitors do not expect. The tower did not stay a symbol of fear for long. Travellers passing through began to write about it. The French poet Alphonse de Lamartine saw it in the 1830s on his journey to the East. He wrote about the tower with deep emotion, and he described it as a monument to the courage of a people seeking freedom. In his eyes, the tower honoured the men inside it — not the men who built it.

That shift in meaning grew stronger as Serbia gained its independence. Niš became part of the Serbian state in 1878. A few years later, in the 1890s, a chapel was built around the tower to protect it. That is the building you are standing in now. Its plain walls and simple roof were designed to shelter the remains from rain and frost, and to turn the site into a place of remembrance.

So this small room holds two stories at once. One is the story the pasha meant to tell, about punishment and power. The other is the story Serbia chose to tell afterwards, about sacrifice and resistance. The structure is exactly the same. Only the meaning changed. It is a quiet lesson in how monuments can outlive the people who build them, and end up saying something entirely different.

Take a moment with the details. Look closely at the skulls that remain. Each one is set slightly differently into the mortar — some tilted, some deeper, some cracked. These were real men, many of them farmers and villagers who had joined the uprising. Near the top of the tower, inside a glass case, there is a skull that is traditionally said to be Sinđelić's own. Whether that is certain is hard to know, but locals treat it with special respect.

Outside, the setting is ordinary. The chapel sits in a small, green plot beside a busy road, with apartment blocks and passing cars all around. That contrast is part of the experience. Niš is a living city, with cafés, markets, and an old fortress on the river. The tower is not hidden away on a mountain. It stands where the old road ran, just as it did when travellers first saw it.

If you want to complete the story, the battle site at Čegar is a short drive away. A memorial monument rises on the hill, with wide views over the Niš valley. Standing there, you can picture the trenches and the lines of soldiers. Then you can come back here and see what that day left behind — not in words, but in bone and lime.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment. Listen to how quiet the chapel is, even with the city just outside. Notice the light falling on the pale mortar and the empty sockets. Around two hundred years have passed since these stones were laid. As you step back onto the street and the noise returns, carry a little of that silence with you.
