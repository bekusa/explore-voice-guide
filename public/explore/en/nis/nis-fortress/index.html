The guide

The story

Start at the river. The Nišava runs slow and green beside the walls, and its water is the first sound you hear here. Then there is the grit of gravel under your shoes. The stone in front of you is pale and rough, warmed by the sun. A broad arched gate opens into shade. Beyond it, people are walking dogs, children are chasing each other, and coffee cups clink on a café terrace. This is a fortress, but these days it works as the city's living room.

The walls you see were built by the Ottoman Empire in the early eighteenth century. The ground beneath them is much older. Engineers chose this spot for a simple reason. The river gives natural protection on one side. The great road between Central Europe and Constantinople passed right here. Whoever held this crossing controlled one of the main routes through the Balkans. That road brought traders, pilgrims and armies for nearly two thousand years.

Long before the Ottomans, the Romans knew this place as Naissus. It was a military and trading town on that same road. Its most famous son is Constantine the Great. He was born here in the third century, and he later became the emperor who legalised Christianity across the Roman world. Many visitors walk straight past this connection. Yet this quiet city on the Nišava produced one of the most important rulers of the ancient world.

In 2013, Niš held large celebrations for the 1,700th anniversary of the Edict of Milan. That was the agreement of 313 that granted religious tolerance in the empire. Constantine was one of its authors, and the city still treats him as part of its identity. Within the fortress grounds, archaeologists have uncovered traces of Roman and Byzantine buildings. Every Ottoman wall here stands on layers of earlier towns.

The main entrance is the Stambol Gate, the Istanbul Gate. Its name points south and east, toward the old imperial capital at the far end of the road. Pause inside the arch for a moment. Notice how the passage turns cool and how sound changes under the vaulted stone. Ottoman builders often placed carved panels and inscriptions above their gates. Look up, and check what survives above this one.

The gate was designed for defence first and for show second. The walls are thick and low compared with medieval castle towers. That was a deliberate choice. By the eighteenth century, cannon fire had changed how fortresses were built. Tall, thin walls cracked under artillery. Lower, heavier walls with angled sections could absorb a hit and give defenders clear lines of fire. Walk along the outside, and the walls follow sharp angles rather than smooth curves.

Step through the gate, and the mood changes at once. Inside you find lawns, plane trees and wide paths. The noise of the city fades. Close to the entrance are the remains of an Ottoman hammam, a public bath house. In Ottoman towns the bath was a social centre, with steam rooms, gossip and business deals. Look for the low domed shapes. Those domes once held small glass openings that let soft light fall into the steam.

Further in, you reach the Bali Bey Mosque, a modest building in pale stone with a rounded dome. It dates from the sixteenth century, so it is older than the walls around it. Today it is no longer used for prayer. It serves as an art gallery, and exhibitions change through the year. That makes it a good example of how this fortress keeps finding new uses for old buildings.

Not far away lies the lapidarium, an open-air collection of carved stone. Here you will find Roman tombstones, altar fragments and pieces of inscriptions gathered from Naissus and its surroundings. Take your time with them. Some carry Latin letters cut nearly two thousand years ago. Others show portraits of the dead, worn soft by weather. This is the closest you will come to the people of Constantine's city.

The fortress also has a darker chapter. For centuries Niš sat on the frontier between empires, and it changed hands more than once. Ottoman rule here ended in 1878, when the Serbian army took the city during the wars of that decade. After that, the fortress stayed in military use for many years. Only later did it slowly open to the public and become the park you see today.

Here is something many visitors miss. Each summer, these old walls host Nišville, an international jazz festival. Musicians from across the world have played on stages set up inside the fortress grounds. Picture brass and drums echoing off Ottoman stone on a warm night, with crowds spread across the lawns. A place built to keep armies out now draws thousands of people in. Few defensive walls in Europe have had such a cheerful second life.

On an ordinary day the rhythm is gentler. In the morning, older residents sit on benches and read the paper. In the afternoon, students meet friends and families push prams along the paths. Toward evening, the light turns golden on the western walls, and the cafés near the gate fill up. Walk the top of the ramparts where the paths allow it. From there you can see the river, the bridges and the city centre across the water.

As you leave, cross back toward the river and look once more at the Stambol Gate. Notice how the stone changes colour as the light moves, from grey to cream to soft gold. Listen for the Nišava again, still running past the walls as it did for Roman soldiers and Ottoman guards. Then walk over the bridge into the modern city. You are following the same road that travellers have used here for two thousand years.
