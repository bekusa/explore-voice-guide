The guide

The story

Listen first. Out here in the Médoc, the loudest sound is often the wind moving across the vine rows. Under your shoes there is gravel. It is pale, rounded and loose, and it crunches with every step. Look down between the vines and you will see the same stones covering the soil. They look poor and dry, and that is the point. This thin, stony ground is what wine people have travelled here to understand for centuries.

You are standing at Château Lafite Rothschild, at the northern edge of Pauillac. Pauillac is a small town on the left bank of the Gironde estuary. The Gironde is the wide, brown tidal water formed where the Garonne and Dordogne rivers meet before they reach the Atlantic. Just north of this estate, the commune of Saint-Estèphe begins. Lafite sits right on that border, and its vineyards rise gently above the flat land near the water.

That gentle rise may explain the name itself. Many historians link "Lafite" to an old Gascon expression, "la hite", which means a small hill or rise. Gascon is the old regional language of southwest France. In a landscape this flat, even a few metres of height matters. Water drains away faster. The vine roots must push deep through gravel to find moisture. Stressed vines tend to give smaller grapes with more concentrated flavour.

The estate's reputation grew under the Ségur family in the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. One of them, the Marquis Nicolas-Alexandre de Ségur, held several of the Médoc's leading vineyards at once. His wines reached the French court, and people gave him a famous nickname, "the Prince of Vines". It was during this era that wine from Lafite began to travel. Merchants in London and Paris knew the name.

Here is a story you will hear often, but treat it with care. According to a popular tale, the Maréchal de Richelieu, an aging nobleman at the court of Louis the Fifteenth, credited Lafite with keeping him youthful. In the story, the wine became fashionable at Versailles as a kind of royal tonic. It is a charming anecdote. But it is court gossip passed down through retellings, not a documented medical claim.

Another story crosses the Atlantic. Thomas Jefferson, later the third American president, lived in France in the 1780s and was an eager student of Bordeaux wine. He wrote about the top estates of the Médoc, and Lafite was among them. Decades ago, some old bottles engraved with his initials appeared at auction and sold for very high sums. Many experts later questioned whether those bottles were genuine. The story is now as much about wine fraud as about Jefferson.

In 1855, Bordeaux prepared a ranking of its red wines for the Universal Exhibition in Paris. Brokers grouped the estates into five levels, called growths, mostly according to the prices their wines fetched. Only four wines reached the top tier of First Growth. Lafite was among them, and it was listed first. That ranking has barely changed since. It still shapes how the world talks about Bordeaux today.

The Rothschild chapter began in 1868. Baron James de Rothschild, of the French branch of the banking family, bought the estate at auction that year. And here is the surprise. He died only a few months later and barely had time to enjoy his new property. His heirs kept it. More than a century and a half later, the estate is still in the hands of the same family.

Now look at the buildings around you. The château itself is not a vast palace. It is a fairly modest country house with steep roofs and a small turret, set among trees and lawns. Many visitors expect grandeur and are surprised by its calm scale. The real wealth of this place is in the soil and in the cellars below ground. The house itself is mostly a symbol, and you will find its outline on every bottle label.

If you join a tour, look for the most unusual space on the estate. Lafite has a circular underground barrel cellar designed by the Spanish architect Ricardo Bofill and opened in the 1980s. Rows of oak barrels form rings around the centre. The low vaulted ceiling rests on a ring of stout columns. The light is soft, the air is cool and still, and the room smells of damp stone and toasted oak. Speak quietly here and listen to how the sound travels.

Those barrels deserve a second look. The estate has its own cooperage, a workshop where craftspeople shape new oak barrels by hand. They bend the wooden staves over fire, and the heat toasts the inside of the wood. That toasting gives the wine some of its spice and vanilla notes as it ages. Few wineries still make their own barrels, so this is a detail many visitors miss.

What goes into those barrels? The main grape here is Cabernet Sauvignon, which suits warm, well-drained gravel. Merlot adds softness, and small amounts of Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot often complete the blend. The estate also makes a second wine called Carruades de Lafite. It takes its name from a plot of vineyard land. Watch for one more symbol on the bottles. Five arrows stand for the five sons of the family's founder, who built banks across Europe.

As you walk back out toward the road, take a moment at the edge of the vines. To the east, the land slopes toward the estuary, and on clear days you may catch a pale shine of water. Further south lie neighbouring estates whose names appear on wine lists everywhere. Pauillac is a working village of tractors, pruning crews and low stone walls, not a museum. Harvest arrives in early autumn, and the air then smells of crushed fruit.

So before you leave, look down once more at the gravel under your feet. Pick up one stone in your mind and hold it. Something this ordinary, poor and grey, worn smooth by ancient rivers, lies behind one of the most famous names in wine. The vines in front of you have pushed their roots through it, season after season. When you open a bottle of Bordeaux someday, you might remember this quiet rise, the wind in the rows and the crunch of stone.
