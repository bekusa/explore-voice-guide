The guide

The story

Listen to the gravel under your shoes. Here in the Médoc, those small, rounded stones are the reason you came. Ancient rivers carried them down from the mountains long ago, and today they hold the warmth of the day after the sun has dropped. Around you, low vines run in straight rows toward the horizon. On a still morning the air smells of damp earth. In autumn, it smells of crushed fruit. Welcome to Château Mouton Rothschild, in Pauillac. This is one of the most carefully watched pieces of farmland in France.

First, a quick sense of where you are standing. Pauillac sits on the left bank of the Gironde, the wide estuary that runs north from Bordeaux to the Atlantic. The land here looks almost flat, but it is not. It rises in gentle gravel slopes, and those slopes drain rainwater quickly. Vines prefer this. Their roots must dig deep to find water, and that struggle tends to give smaller, more concentrated grapes. Pauillac is known above all for Cabernet Sauvignon, a grape with thick skins and firm structure.

Now, the name. In French, a mouton is a sheep, and the estate has embraced that meaning fully. You will see rams on gates, on labels, and in the decoration of the buildings. But many historians suggest a more down-to-earth origin. They link the name to an old local word for a small hill or mound — a reference to the raised gravel ground under your feet. Both explanations live side by side here, and the ram has long since won the public's affection.

The Rothschild chapter began in the mid-nineteenth century. In 1853, Baron Nathaniel de Rothschild, from the English branch of the famous banking family, bought the property. At the time it was known as Brane-Mouton. He gave it his family name, and the estate has stayed with his descendants ever since. That alone is unusual in Bordeaux, where many great properties have changed hands again and again. Here, the same family has guided the vines for more than a century and a half.

Only two years after that purchase came a moment that shaped everything that followed. For the Paris Universal Exhibition of 1855, Bordeaux wine brokers drew up a ranking of the region's best red wines, based largely on reputation and price. They sorted the estates into five tiers, called growths. Mouton was placed at the top of the second tier — not among the first growths. The family felt this was unfair. A proud motto followed, which roughly means: first I cannot be, second I do not deign to be, Mouton I am.

The person who turned that pride into action was Baron Philippe de Rothschild. He took charge of the estate in 1922, when he was only about twenty years old. He was restless, inventive, and fond of theatre and poetry. Within a few years he made a decision that sounds simple today but was bold then. From 1924, Mouton bottled its entire harvest at the château, instead of selling wine in barrels to merchants who bottled it themselves. It was a guarantee of authenticity, and other leading estates soon followed.

If your visit takes you into the great barrel hall, pause at the entrance. This long, dim room was created in the 1920s, during Philippe's early years. Rows of oak barrels stretch away in neat lines, and the light is kept low and even. The air is cool and slightly damp, with a smell of wood, wine, and stone. Wine rests here quietly for many months while it develops. Notice how silent the space feels compared with the open vineyard outside.

Here is the detail that many visitors know before they even arrive. In 1945, to celebrate the end of the Second World War, Philippe commissioned a label design featuring a large letter V, for victory. The artist was Philippe Jullian. From that year on, a different artist has created the label for each new vintage. It became an annual tradition, and a small square of paper on a bottle became a kind of gallery. According to a popular account, artists are paid not in money but in wine.

The list of contributors reads like a history of modern art. Joan Miró, Marc Chagall, Salvador Dalí, Andy Warhol, Francis Bacon, and Pablo Picasso have all appeared on Mouton labels. Not every design passed without trouble. In 1993, the painter Balthus created a drawing of a young girl. Some American authorities found it inappropriate, so bottles sold in the United States carried a blank space instead. That vintage now exists in two versions — and collectors seek out both.

The long campaign of the 1855 ranking finally ended in 1973. That year, after decades of effort by Philippe, Mouton was officially raised to first growth. It remains a rare case of promotion within that original ranking, which otherwise has barely moved since the nineteenth century. The label for the 1973 vintage honoured Picasso, who had died that spring. The motto changed too. It now says, roughly: first I am, second I was, Mouton does not change.

The estate also looks beyond its own bottles. Philippe and his wife, Pauline, created a museum here devoted to wine in art. It brings together objects from many centuries and many cultures — drinking vessels, tapestries, paintings, and sculpture — all connected to the vine and to wine. Alongside it, the original artworks behind the labels are displayed. Seeing them up close is a surprise for many visitors. Some are tiny sketches; others are full paintings that were reduced to fit a label.

After Philippe died in 1988, his daughter, Baroness Philippine de Rothschild, led the family business. She had been an actress before turning to wine, and she kept the artistic spirit of the estate alive. The family's ambitions also reached far beyond Pauillac. Together with the American winemaker Robert Mondavi, Philippe had already started Opus One, a joint venture in California's Napa Valley. Today the family's work spans several countries, but this gravel ridge remains the centre of it all.

A practical note about how a visit here works. Mouton is a working estate, not a theme park, so visits are arranged in advance and usually follow a guided route. Tours often move from the vineyard to the cellars and then to the art collections. Tasting may be part of the experience. In September and October, the harvest changes the mood completely. Pickers move along the rows, tractors pass on the narrow roads, and the smell of fermenting grapes drifts out of the buildings.

Before you leave, look once more at the vines around you. Each plant is small, twisted, and pruned close to the ground. Nothing here looks grand at first glance. Yet these rows, this gravel, and one family's stubborn pride produced a wine that changed a famous ranking. As you walk back toward the road, notice the rams on the gates and the long, quiet lines of the Médoc stretching toward the estuary. The story continues in every new vintage — and in every new label.
