The guide

The story

Listen first. Under this high roof, sound behaves differently. Footsteps echo off the concrete. Children's voices carry up into the rafters. Somewhere a guide is talking about wheels and pistons. Around you sit great machines of iron and steel. Some are painted deep green, some crimson, some a rich, glossy blue. They are perfectly still, yet each one looks as if it could move at any moment. Welcome to the National Railway Museum in York, a place built around motion.

This is not a building designed as a museum. Much of it began as working railway buildings. The Great Hall stands on the site of an old locomotive depot, where engines once came to be cleaned, repaired and turned. In that sense, these trains have come home. York was a major railway city. Lines from London, Scotland and the north of England met here, and the station sits just next door. You can often hear modern trains pass nearby while you look at their ancestors.

The museum opened in the 1970s. Today it belongs to the Science Museum Group, the same family of museums as the Science Museum in London. Its job is simple to describe and huge to carry out. It looks after the story of Britain's railways — the engines, the carriages, the signals, the posters, the tickets, the uniforms, and the people who made the whole system work. Many visitors come for one famous engine. Most leave talking about something they did not expect.

Start in the Great Hall, if you can. At its centre sits a turntable, a large rotating platform set into the floor. In the age of steam, many locomotives ran best facing forward. So depots used turntables to spin an engine around and send it back out. Around this one, locomotives stand in a ring, like spokes on a wheel. At certain times, staff turn the table and explain how it works. It is a slow, heavy movement, and oddly satisfying to watch.

Now look for a streamlined blue engine with a sloping, rounded nose. This is Mallard. In 1938, on a stretch of track in Lincolnshire, Mallard reached 126 miles per hour. That is about two hundred kilometres per hour. No steam locomotive has officially gone faster since. Mallard was designed under Sir Nigel Gresley, a leading engineer of his day. The smooth casing was not just for looks. It helped the engine cut through the air at speed.

Here is the first surprise. Steam locomotives are complicated inside, but most of that machinery is hidden. In the Great Hall, one large engine has been cut open down the side. It is a sectioned locomotive, sliced so you can see inside the boiler, the firebox and the cylinders. Painted surfaces show where fire, water and steam travelled. Spend a few minutes here. After this, every other engine in the building will make more sense.

The second surprise sits nearby, and it does not look British at all. It is a Japanese Shinkansen, the high-speed train often called the bullet train. Its white body and rounded blue-trimmed nose stand out among the steam engines. This early Shinkansen car came to York from Japan, and it is the only one of its kind on display outside that country. You can usually step inside. It is a quiet, clever reminder that railway history is a world story.

Next, walk through to Station Hall. This building was once a goods station, where freight arrived and left the city. Today its platforms hold carriages rather than crates. The light here is softer. The space feels more like a real station, with long platforms and old signs. It is a good place to slow down. The machines here are less about speed and more about the experience of travelling, from crowded third-class benches to elegant private saloons.

Among the most popular exhibits are the royal carriages. British monarchs travelled by train for well over a century, and several of their carriages are here. One belonged to Queen Victoria. Look through the windows and you will see padded fabric, careful woodwork and small domestic comforts. It feels more like a sitting room than a train. Compare it with an ordinary passenger carriage nearby. The difference shows how the railway carried every level of society, though not in the same comfort.

The museum also tells the story of the people behind the trains. Drivers and firemen worked in heat and coal dust. Signal workers controlled traffic with levers and bells. Station staff sold tickets, moved luggage and answered endless questions. Look out for uniforms, lamps, clocks and signalling equipment. These small objects can be more moving than the giant engines. They show the daily routine of a railway that once employed huge numbers of people across the country.

Do not miss the Warehouse, if it is open during your visit. Here, shelves and cases hold thousands of smaller objects, packed together much as a museum store might keep them. You might find railway crockery, model trains, nameplates, badges and signs side by side. It feels less curated, and more like a treasure hunt. Take your time. Some of the best details here are only a few centimetres across, and easy to pass by.

As you walk, notice how the museum changes your sense of scale. A steam engine looks large in a photograph. Standing next to one, the driving wheels can be taller than you. The buffers are at chest height. The cab sits high above the floor. Then look up at the roof and the long lines of the halls. These buildings were made for very big machines, and they still feel like working spaces.

Before you leave, return for a moment to the engine that interested you most. Look at it again, now that you know a little more. Notice the wear on the metal, the polish on the brass, the small repairs on the paintwork. Then listen once more to the echo under the roof. Outside, trains still run in and out of York station. The story in these halls has not ended — it is still running past the door.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start in the Great Hall to see the turntable.
Ask staff about turntable demonstration times on arrival.
Allow at least half a day for both halls.
Step inside the Shinkansen carriage when it is open.
Combine your visit with York station right next door.
