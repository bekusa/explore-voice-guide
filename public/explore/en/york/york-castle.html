The guide

The story

Start at the foot of the mound. The grass rises steeply in front of you, and the slope is far sharper than it looks from the road. At the top, pale stone walls curve in and out like the petals of a flower. Around you, traffic hums along Tower Street, and somewhere behind the old prison buildings the River Foss moves slowly toward the Ouse. This is York Castle. What remains today is a tower, a green, and a group of handsome Georgian buildings.

The story begins with conquest. In 1068, two years after the Battle of Hastings, William the Conqueror marched north to control a city that did not welcome him. His men raised an earth mound here and topped it with a wooden tower. It was a classic Norman design called a motte and bailey. The motte is the raised mound, and the bailey is the enclosed yard below it. A second castle soon appeared across the Ouse. Its mound, Baile Hill, still survives.

The early years were violent. Northern rebels rose against the Normans soon after the castle was built. William answered with the brutal campaign later known as the Harrying of the North. During this campaign, much of the surrounding countryside was laid waste. Engineers also dammed the River Foss to flood the low ground around the castle. This created a defensive lake and a moat. The water shaped this part of York for centuries, and the Foss still runs close by.

The darkest chapter came in March 1190. A wave of anti-Jewish violence spread across England, and in York a mob turned on the city's Jewish community. Families fled to the royal tower on this mound for protection. They were besieged there. Faced with the mob outside, many chose to take their own lives rather than be killed or forced to convert. The wooden tower burned. Others who surrendered were murdered. It is one of the worst massacres in medieval English history.

Look at the base of the mound for a memorial plaque. Each spring, daffodils cover the slope. They were planted as a living memorial, and they bloom around the anniversary of the massacre. Their six-pointed petals recall the Star of David. Many visitors climb past them without knowing why they are there. If you come in late winter or early spring, stop for a moment on the steps. The flowers carry a meaning that is easy to miss.

The stone tower above you came later. In the thirteenth century, King Henry III ordered a new keep in stone to replace the timber structures. Its plan is unusual. Instead of a square or a circle, it has four rounded lobes that overlap, a shape called a quatrefoil. Very few castles in England use this design. From the outside it looks almost soft, but the walls are thick. The design gave defenders wide views and few blind corners.

The name Clifford's Tower is a later puzzle. One popular explanation links it to Roger de Clifford, a nobleman executed in York in 1322 after he rebelled against King Edward II. Legend says his body was hung in chains from the tower. Another idea links the name to the Clifford family, who held local offices. Historians are not certain which story is true. What is certain is that the name stuck, and most people in York use it instead of 'the keep'.

Here is a surprise. In the late sixteenth century, the tower nearly disappeared. A keeper of the castle began to sell off its stone as building material. The people of York objected strongly, and the work was stopped. You can still see damage on some walls. Then, in 1684, a fire gutted the interior. Many accounts link the fire to a gun salute that went wrong. After that, the tower stood as an open, roofless shell for a very long time.

Turn now and look across the round lawn below the mound. This green is called the Eye of York. The medieval bailey once stood here. Over the eighteenth century, the castle became a centre of law and punishment. Courts and prisons replaced the old defences. Elegant buildings in honey-coloured stone rose around the green. They look calm and orderly, almost like a college. Behind those classical columns, however, people faced trial, imprisonment, and sometimes a death sentence.

The building on one side was the Debtors' Prison, built in the early eighteenth century. Its most famous inmate was the highwayman Dick Turpin. He was held at York Castle before his execution in 1739. Popular stories turned him into a romantic hero on horseback, but the real man was a thief and a violent criminal. Facing the green is the court building, which still serves as a working court today. The legal life of this site has continued for centuries.

The old prison buildings now house York Castle Museum. It opened in the 1930s and grew from the collection of a local doctor who saved everyday objects that others threw away. Its best-known display is a recreated Victorian street with shopfronts, cobbles, and gas lamps. You can walk inside it as if you are shopping a century and a half ago. The museum also lets you see the old prison cells. That museum and the tower are run separately.

Clifford's Tower itself has changed recently. In 2022, after a major project by English Heritage, it reopened with a new timber structure inside the walls. Walkways now let you move through the interior, and a roof deck sits above the old stone. From the top, you can look out over York's rooftops toward the Minster. You can also follow the line of the rivers. For the first time in centuries, visitors can stand on a floor inside the shell.

Before you leave, stand still for a minute on the green. Look up at the four curved walls, then down at the slope. You are standing where a Norman garrison camped, where a community died, and where courts and prisons followed. Listen to the traffic, the voices, and the birds on the grass. As you walk away, look back once more at the mound. In spring it will be yellow with daffodils, quietly holding a memory.

Before you go

Practical tips

Climb the tower steps early to avoid crowds on top.
Visit in early spring to see the daffodil memorial.
Wear sturdy shoes for the steep steps up the mound.
Allow separate time for the Castle Museum next door.
Use the roof deck for views toward York Minster.
