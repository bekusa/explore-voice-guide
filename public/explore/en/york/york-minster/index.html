The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Inside the nave, footsteps do not simply fall. They rise, scatter and return a moment later from somewhere far above. The air is cool, even in summer. It carries a faint smell of stone dust and candle wax. Pale light drops through tall windows and lands on the floor in coloured patches that slowly move with the sun. This is York Minster, the cathedral that has shaped the skyline of this city for centuries.

The word minster comes from an old English term for a church that was a centre of mission and teaching. Here, it is also a cathedral, the seat of the Archbishop of York. That is one of the two most senior posts in the Church of England. So the building you are standing in is not only a parish landmark. It is a church of national importance, and for much of English history it was the religious heart of the whole north.

The story begins long before the stone. In the year 627, a missionary bishop named Paulinus baptised Edwin, King of Northumbria, in a small wooden church on or near this spot. Northumbria was one of the early Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, stretching across much of northern England. That simple timber building was replaced again and again. Fire, war and ambition each played a part. What you see today is the last and largest version of a church that kept being rebuilt.

And beneath all of it lies Rome. York was the Roman fortress of Eboracum, and the Minster sits over its headquarters. Here is the first surprise. In the year 306, a young commander named Constantine was proclaimed emperor in York by his troops. He later became the first Christian emperor of Rome. Outside, near the south transept, a bronze statue shows him seated with a broken sword. Many visitors walk straight past it on their way in.

The present Gothic cathedral took roughly two and a half centuries to build, from the early thirteenth century to the late fifteenth. Gothic is the medieval style of pointed arches, ribbed ceilings and huge windows. Because the work lasted so long, the style changes as you walk. The transepts are earlier and plainer. The nave and the eastern end are later, lighter and more open. In one building, you can trace how English masons grew bolder over generations.

The glass is the real treasure here. The Minster holds one of the largest collections of medieval stained glass in Britain. Look for the Great East Window, behind the high altar. It was made in the early fifteenth century by a glazier from Coventry named John Thornton. It shows the beginning and the end of the world, from the Book of Genesis to the Book of Revelation. It is roughly the size of a tennis court, set upright in stone.

That window was taken apart, panel by panel, during a long conservation project that finished in the late 2010s. Specialists cleaned each piece and added protective outer glazing. If you stand close, look at the small scenes near the bottom. They are full of detail: tiny faces, animals and stars. Thornton knew most people would never see them clearly from the floor. He painted them with care anyway.

Now turn to the north transept and find the Five Sisters Window. It is made of five tall, narrow lancets, which are slender pointed windows. Unlike most medieval glass, it is almost colourless. It uses a grey and silver style called grisaille, built from thousands of small pieces in delicate patterns. It dates from the thirteenth century. In soft light it glows like frost. Today it also serves as a memorial to women of the British Empire who died in the First World War.

Here is a second surprise. In 1984, a fire broke out in the south transept after a lightning strike in the night. The roof collapsed. The heat cracked the glass of the great Rose Window into tens of thousands of pieces. Yet the window did not fall. Thanks to earlier releading, it held together. Restorers later repaired it. The rose design celebrates the marriage that joined the royal houses of Lancaster and York at the end of the Wars of the Roses.

The rebuilt south transept roof carries a gentle twist. As part of the restoration, a children's television programme invited young viewers to design new roof bosses. A boss is the carved knob where ceiling ribs meet. Several winning designs were carved and painted. One shows a man landing on the moon. It sits high above you among saints and leaves, a small modern joke hidden in a medieval ceiling. Binoculars help.

Between the nave and the choir stands the stone screen known as the King's Screen. It carries fifteen carved kings of England, running from William the Conqueror to Henry the Sixth. Their crowns and robes still hold traces of colour. Then walk to the Chapter House, an eight-sided meeting hall where church leaders once gathered. Its vaulted roof needs no central pillar to hold it up. A Latin line painted there compares the room to a rose, calling it the house of houses.

The Minster nearly lost its footing in the twentieth century. In the 1960s, engineers found that the central tower's foundations were failing. Over several years they reinforced them with concrete and steel. That work opened up spaces below the floor. Today the Undercroft shows Roman walls, Norman foundations and modern supports side by side. Down there, almost two thousand years of building stand within a few paces of each other.

For those with steady legs, the central tower can be climbed. The stair is narrow, winding and long, with nearly three hundred steps. At the top, the whole city spreads out below. You can see red roofs, the line of the medieval walls and green country beyond. Back on the ground, take a slow walk around the outside. The pale magnesian limestone shifts from cream to honey to grey as the light moves across it.

Before you leave, stand once more in the nave and look up. Notice how small the people around you seem. The builders wanted exactly that feeling. Listen for the echo of your own step. Watch a patch of coloured light settle on someone's sleeve. As you walk out into the narrow streets, glance back over your shoulder. The towers will follow you for a long way, rising above the rooftops of York.

Practical tips

Walk the full outer circuit to see the stone change colour.
Check service times, as touring pauses during worship.
