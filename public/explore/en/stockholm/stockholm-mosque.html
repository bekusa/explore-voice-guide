The guide

The story

Stand for a moment on the edge of the park and look up at the red brick. It is warm in colour, even on a grey Stockholm afternoon. The arches above the windows curve into the soft horseshoe shape you might expect in Granada or Cairo. Pigeons settle on the ledges. Traffic hums from the square nearby. And somewhere inside, behind thick walls, a quiet hall is waiting. This is the Stockholm Mosque — and the first surprise is that it was not built as a mosque at all.

This building began life in the early twentieth century as an electrical substation. Its old name was Katarinastation, after the Katarina parish here on Södermalm, the large island that forms the southern part of central Stockholm. In those years the city was racing to bring electric power to its streets, trams and homes. Machines inside this hall transformed and distributed that power. It was industrial infrastructure — the kind of building most cities hide behind plain walls.

Stockholm did not hide it. The city gave the commission to Ferdinand Boberg, one of the most celebrated Swedish architects of his generation. Boberg worked in a style that mixed Art Nouveau, the flowing decorative movement popular around 1900, with older influences from many places. Later in his career he designed some of central Stockholm's best-known buildings, including the grand Rosenbad on the waterfront and the department store NK. But here he did something unusual.

Boberg had travelled, and he was fascinated by the architecture of North Africa and the Islamic world. For this substation he borrowed openly from that tradition. Look at the tall arched openings, the patterned brickwork and the strong, almost fortress-like mass of the walls. They carry clear echoes of Moorish design — the style of the Muslim kingdoms that once ruled much of Spain. So, a century ago, a Swedish architect dressed a power station in Islamic forms, simply because he found them beautiful.

Here is the second surprise. When the machines eventually fell silent and the building lost its original purpose, it stood waiting for a new life. Stockholm's Muslim community was growing, and it needed a central place to pray. The old substation, with its large open hall and its Moorish arches, seemed almost made for the role. After conversion work, it opened as a mosque in 2000. The architecture that Boberg imagined as decoration became, a hundred years later, something closer to a homecoming.

The conversion kept the protected exterior largely as Boberg left it. The building is a listed part of Stockholm's architectural heritage, so the brick, the proportions and the arches remain. A small minaret and a dome-like crown were added as signs of the new use, but they sit lightly on the older structure. Walk slowly along the façade. Notice how hard it is to tell where the 1900s end and the 2000s begin. That blending was deliberate.

Inside, the great machine hall has become the main prayer hall. Where heavy equipment once stood, there is now open floor covered with soft carpet. Rows on the carpet guide worshippers to stand shoulder to shoulder, facing Mecca. The ceiling is high, a reminder of the hall's industrial past, when space was needed for large machinery. Light falls through tall windows. The effect is calm and generous — a working building turned into a place of stillness.

The mosque is run by the Islamic Association in Stockholm, and it serves a very diverse community. On a Friday, the most important day of congregational prayer, you may hear many languages in the entrance hall. Arabic, Somali, Bosnian, Turkish, Persian, Swedish — the voices mix as people take off their shoes and greet one another. For many families, this is not just a place of prayer. It is also a meeting point, a place for classes, advice and community events.

If you would like to go inside, approach with the same courtesy you would bring to any house of worship. Shoes come off before the carpeted areas. Modest clothing is expected, with shoulders and knees covered, and women are usually asked to cover their hair. Avoid the main prayer times if you want a quieter visit. Volunteers and staff are often happy to answer questions, and guided visits for groups can sometimes be arranged in advance.

Now turn around and look at the setting. The mosque faces Björns trädgård, a small public park, and just beyond it is Medborgarplatsen, one of the busiest squares on Södermalm. Its name means Citizens' Square. In summer the square fills with outdoor café tables. In winter it often holds an ice rink. Teenagers skate, office workers cut across with coffee, and the mosque sits calmly at the edge of it all — part of the daily rhythm of the island.

Södermalm itself has changed many times. It was once a working-class district of small wooden houses, workshops and breweries. Over the twentieth century it slowly became one of Stockholm's most creative neighbourhoods, known for its independent shops, music venues and cafés. The story of this building follows that same arc. It began as a piece of practical industry, sat quietly through changing decades, and then found a new purpose that reflects the city as it is today.

There is something worth pausing on here. Many European cities have argued, sometimes bitterly, about where mosques should go and how they should look. In Stockholm, one of the city's most visible mosques lives inside a protected Swedish landmark, designed by a Swedish architect who loved Islamic art. The building does not have to choose between belonging to Stockholm and belonging to its worshippers. It holds both stories in the same red brick.

Before you walk on, look up once more at the arches. Notice how the light changes on the brick as clouds move over the square. Listen to the skateboards, the buses, the voices from the park. Then picture the hall as it was a century ago — full of humming machines carrying power into the city. Today it carries something quieter. As you leave, glance back from the far side of the park. The building looks exactly like what it has become.
