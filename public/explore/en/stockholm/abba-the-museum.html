The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Somewhere nearby, a roller coaster rattles past, and a wave of screams rises and fades. Gulls call over the water. Then, from a doorway, a few bars of a familiar chorus drift out. Maybe it's 'Dancing Queen', maybe 'Mamma Mia'. Almost everyone recognises it within two notes. That is the strange power of the place you are about to enter, a museum built around four Swedish musicians and the songs that carried them around the world.

You are standing on Djurgården, a green island in the heart of Stockholm. For centuries this was royal hunting ground. Today it holds museums, parks, old wooden villas and the Gröna Lund amusement park right next door. It makes sense that a museum about pop music sits here. This island has always been a place where Stockholmers come to relax, to stroll by the water and to have a little fun. ABBA The Museum opened here in 2013, and it fits the island's playful mood.

First, a quick introduction to the four people behind it all. Agnetha Fältskog and Anni-Frid Lyngstad were the two lead voices. Björn Ulvaeus and Benny Andersson wrote the songs and played on them. The band's name comes from their first initials. In the early years the four were also two couples, which gave many of their later songs an honest, sometimes painful edge. Keep that in mind as you walk through. The cheerful melodies often carry quite sad words.

The turning point came in 1974. ABBA entered the Eurovision Song Contest, the large annual television competition between European countries, with a song called 'Waterloo'. The contest took place in Brighton, in England, and ABBA won. Overnight, a Swedish group became an international act. Inside, you will find this moment treated as the start of the story. Look for the costumes from that night. They are bold, shiny and theatrical, and they set the tone for everything that followed.

Here is something that surprises many visitors. This is not a quiet museum where you stand behind a rope and read labels. It is built for you to take part. You can record your own voice in a studio booth. You can step onto a stage and perform alongside digital versions of the band. You can try mixing a track yourself. Many people arrive a little shy and leave having sung loudly in front of strangers. That is very much the idea.

The costumes deserve a slow look. ABBA's stage clothes from the seventies were famous for their platform boots, wide sleeves, glitter and bright colours. Some say the outfits were deliberately extravagant. According to a story the band members have told in interviews, Swedish tax rules at the time allowed costumes to be deducted only if they could not be worn on the street. Whether that was the full reason or just part of it, the result is clothing that nobody could mistake for everyday wear.

As you move through the rooms, notice the recreated spaces. There are reconstructions connected to the band's working life, including a studio setting that recalls where so many of their records took shape in Stockholm. There is also a feel of seventies interiors, with wooden panels, soft lighting and period details. These rooms help you imagine the long hours of writing and recording. Pop songs that sound effortless were, in fact, built slowly, layer upon layer, often late into the night.

Now listen for a telephone. Somewhere in the museum is a red phone, and it is not just decoration. Only the four band members are said to have its number. If it rings while you are nearby, you are invited to pick it up. It might really be one of them on the line. It does not happen often, so most visitors never hear it. But the possibility adds a small thrill. Keep one ear open as you walk.

Another detail many people miss is a piano. Benny Andersson's piano here is connected to one he plays elsewhere. When he sits down and plays at his own instrument, the keys of this piano can move on their own. It may be silent when you pass, but if you happen to hear it play, you are hearing a musician at work somewhere else in real time. It is a small, slightly magical idea, and very typical of the museum's sense of humour.

The story does not stop in the early eighties, when the group stepped away from performing together. The songs kept living. A stage musical and later films built around ABBA's music brought the band to new generations. Then, in 2021, the four released a new album called 'Voyage', decades after their last one. A concert show using digital avatars of the band followed in London. The museum reflects this long afterlife. Many visitors here were born long after ABBA stopped touring.

Why did this music last so long? Part of the answer is craft. Björn and Benny wrote tightly built melodies, with hooks that return at just the right moment. Agnetha's and Frida's voices blended so closely that they often sound like one bright voice. The production was clean and detailed. Listen as you walk. Under the sparkle, there are clever chord changes and careful harmonies. It is easy to dismiss pop as simple, but these songs reward close attention.

The building also holds a wider story. The Swedish Music Hall of Fame shares this space, and it celebrates other Swedish artists and songwriters. This is a useful reminder. ABBA did not appear from nowhere. Sweden has a long tradition of choirs, folk music and songwriting, and in later decades Swedish writers and producers shaped a great deal of international pop. Seen this way, ABBA was the first big wave of something much larger, a small country with an outsized voice in music.

When you step back outside, take a moment by the water. The roller coaster is still rattling. The gulls are still calling. Perhaps a chorus is still playing in your head, and perhaps you just sang it out loud in a booth inside. Look across at the city on the other shore, the boats moving between the islands. Four musicians from here once filled radios across the world. And as you walk on along the island, notice how many people are quietly humming.
