Riddarholm Church

A dark spire of iron lace rises high above a small island.

⏱ 6 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

A dark spire of iron lace rises high above a small island. It is not solid like stone or copper. The daylight passes straight through its open framework, so it seems to be drawn against the sky rather than built. Stand below it on the worn cobbles, tilt your head back, and look up. You are looking at one of the oldest buildings in all of Stockholm, and the resting place of its kings.

This is Riddarholm Church. It stands on Riddarholmen, a small, quiet island just to the west of the old town. The water is close on every side, and the noise of the city falls away. Set apart from the crowds, the place feels still and a little solemn. Yet under its stone floor and within its many chapels lies a great part of the history of an entire nation.

The church began its life as a monastery. In the late thirteenth century, friars of the Franciscan order settled here on the island. They were known as the grey friars, after the plain colour of their robes. They raised a simple brick church, in keeping with their vows of poverty and humble living. Parts of that early medieval building still stand within the walls you see today, hidden among the later additions.

Over the long centuries, the plain church changed and grew. Kings and great noble families added burial chapels along its sides, one after another. Each chapel was built to hold the dead of a single royal house or a single reign. And so the building spread outward, chapel by chapel, like a record of shifting power written slowly in brick, in marble, and in carved stone over many generations.

Riddarholm Church became the burial place of the Swedish monarchs. Almost every king and queen from the seventeenth century onward was laid to rest here, beneath these roofs. Among them lies Gustavus Adolphus, the warrior king who died far from home on a battlefield in 1632. His remains were brought back across the sea and placed within one of the great vaults, where they rest still.

Walk slowly through the interior and let your eyes adjust. The light is soft and grey. Stone slabs cover the floor, worn smooth and shallow by centuries of passing feet. Coats of arms hang in rows along the walls. They belong to the knights of a royal order, and when one of those knights dies, his arms are hung here in his memory. The dead are remembered in bright colour and dark metal.

The sarcophagi themselves are works of art. Some are carved from pale marble, cold and smooth to the eye. Others are cast in heavy bronze, gone dark with the slow passing of time. Kings and queens lie in great stone boxes shaped with enormous care by the finest hands of their day. All around them the air stays cool and quiet, and your own footsteps echo upward against the high stone vaults.

Step outside again into the island's narrow lanes and look up once more at the spire, and notice that it is made of iron. This was not the first spire to stand here. The earlier one was built of wood, as such towers usually were. In 1835, during a summer storm, lightning struck the tower and set it ablaze. The fire spread quickly, and the old wooden spire was completely destroyed.

What rose in its place was something new for its time. The fallen spire was rebuilt as an open framework of cast iron, light and dark against the clouds. Cast iron was a modern material then, a proud product of the early industrial age. And so the medieval church gained a tall crown of nineteenth-century engineering. The two distant ages now stand joined together in a single tower, stone below and iron above.

The church is no longer used for ordinary worship. There are no regular Sunday services held here now. Instead the building serves as a memorial and a quiet museum, carefully kept as a place of remembrance for the crowned heads of the country. It opens its heavy doors to visitors through the warmer months of the year, when the northern days are long and the island is easy to reach on foot.

Outside, the small open square around the church rewards a pause before you move on. From the stone edge of Riddarholmen you can look out across the water toward the great city hall, with its tall tower, and the wide lake beyond. The view is among the calmer ones in all of Stockholm. Boats pass slowly. The light shifts across the surface of the water through the changing hours of the day.

Before you go, stand once more beneath the iron spire and be still. Think of the grey friars who came here first, so long ago. Think of the kings and queens who followed them into the ground. Think of the lightning that changed this skyline in a single summer night. Many centuries are gathered together on this one small island. Here, very quietly, a whole country keeps and guards its dead.

Key facts

Founded as a Franciscan monastery in the late thirteenth century
Among the oldest surviving buildings in Stockholm
Burial place of Swedish monarchs since the seventeenth century
King Gustavus Adolphus, who died in 1632, lies here
Lightning destroyed the wooden spire in 1835

What to look for

Carved marble and dark bronze royal sarcophagi
Knights' coats of arms hung along the walls
Floor slabs worn smooth by centuries of feet
Soft light falling through the high stone vaults
