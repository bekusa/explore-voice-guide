The guide

The story

Listen for a moment before you go inside. Out here in Frescati, on the northern edge of Stockholm, the sounds are soft. You hear wind in the trees, a few students cycling past, and perhaps a train in the distance. Ahead of you stands a long brick building with a stately, formal front. It does not look like a modern science centre. It looks like a palace for knowledge, and that is close to the truth.

This is the Swedish Museum of Natural History. In Swedish it is called Naturhistoriska riksmuseet, which means roughly the national museum of natural history. It is one of the largest museums in the country. It is also a working research institution and a government agency. Behind the galleries, scientists study collections that run to millions of specimens. Visitors see only a small part of them. The rest sit in storage rooms, drawers and jars, and they are used for research.

The museum's story begins with the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, which was founded in the eighteenth century. One of its founders was Carl Linnaeus. He was the Swedish botanist who gave science the system of two-part Latin names that we still use today, like Homo sapiens for humans. The Academy gathered specimens from across Sweden and the wider world. Over time that collection grew too large for a learned society to manage. It became the foundation of a separate national museum.

For much of the nineteenth century, the collections lived in central Stockholm, close to the busy heart of the city. Space ran out again and again. Eventually the decision was made to move them to a new, purpose-built home here in Frescati. The building you see opened in 1916, during the First World War. Sweden stayed neutral in that war, and the country still found the will to build a grand house for science.

Take a moment to look at the facade. The brick gives the building a warm, earthy colour, especially when the low northern sun hits it. The design is symmetrical and ceremonial. There is a strong central section and long wings stretching out to either side. The architect, Axel Anderberg, designed it in the early twentieth century, when public museums were meant to impress. The scale tells visitors that the study of nature deserves the same respect as a royal court.

Here is something many visitors miss. The location was not chosen at random. Frescati sits beside the Royal National City Park. This is a long green belt of forest, meadows and water that runs through the northern side of Stockholm. It is one of the first protected urban national parks in the world. So the museum of nature stands next to real nature. Lake Brunnsviken lies nearby, and the Bergianska Botanic Garden is only a short walk away.

Step inside, and the atmosphere changes. The ceilings rise high above you, and voices echo softly against stone and plaster. On a weekday morning you may hear school groups everywhere — children calling out as they spot a skeleton or a stuffed animal. On a quiet afternoon the halls feel calm. You hear footsteps, the hum of display lighting and the low murmur of people reading labels together.

The permanent exhibitions cover a wide sweep of life on Earth. Fossil galleries take you back through deep time, to the age of dinosaurs and even earlier. Other halls focus on the story of human evolution. Some of the most loved rooms show Swedish nature itself. Here you meet the animals of the forests, mountains, coasts and seas of Scandinavia. For many Swedish families, these rooms are a childhood memory passed down through generations.

Pay attention to the older displays when you find them. Some animal dioramas follow a classic museum tradition. Mounted animals stand in front of painted landscapes, so that the foreground blends into a scene that seems to stretch for kilometres. This craft took great patience. A painter and a taxidermist had to work together to make the light, the grass and the animal feel like one moment in the wild. Look closely at the point where the real objects meet the painted backdrop.

Now for a second surprise. Inside this historic building there is a dome theatre called Cosmonova. It opened in the early 1990s. It works as a planetarium and also as a giant-screen cinema. You sit beneath a curved dome, and the film fills almost your whole field of vision. Films here take you into space, to the deep sea or back in time. Shows usually need a separate ticket. Many visitors only discover this when they arrive.

It is worth remembering what happens out of sight. The collections in storage are not simply kept for display. Researchers compare specimens collected decades or even centuries apart. This helps them see how species, climates and landscapes have changed. Old bird skins, pressed plants and preserved fish can contain evidence about pollution, disease and evolution. Collectors of the past could not have imagined some of these modern uses. In this way, a drawer of old specimens becomes a record of the planet.

Plan your visit with some care. The museum is large, and it is easy to feel tired halfway through. Choose two or three galleries that interest you most and give them real time. The metro station called Universitetet is close by, so the trip from the city centre is simple. If the weather is kind, combine your visit with a walk in the park outside. The museum and the green spaces around it belong to the same idea.

Think about who stood in these halls before you. Generations of Stockholm children have pressed their faces against these glass cases. Students from the university next door have come here to sketch bones and study minerals. Scientists have worked quietly in back rooms while families explored the galleries in front. The building has served all of them for more than a century. It is a public place and a laboratory at the same time.

When you step back outside, pause on the path for a moment. Turn and look at the brick front once more. Notice how the trees frame it and how the light falls across the wings. Then listen again — the wind, the birds, the bicycles. The museum holds a record of the natural world, but the natural world is also right here around you. As you walk away, keep looking. The next specimen worth noticing might be a bird in the branches above your head.
