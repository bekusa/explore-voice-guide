The guide

The story

Stand on the cobbles of the outer courtyard for a moment and listen. Gulls cry over the water. Boots scrape on stone somewhere behind you. A cold wind comes off the Baltic and runs along the long, flat walls of the palace. The building in front of you does not rise like a fairy-tale castle. It sits low and heavy, like a block of carved stone, with rows of tall windows that seem to go on forever.

This is Stockholm Palace, the official residence of the King of Sweden. It stands at the northern edge of Gamla stan, the Old Town, on the island where Stockholm first grew. From here, the city spreads out across islands and bridges in every direction. Water is never far away. On a clear day, the light bounces off the channels and turns the stone pale gold. On a grey day, the whole building looks like it was cut from the sky itself.

But the story of this place begins with a fire. For centuries, a castle called Tre Kronor, which means Three Crowns, stood on this exact spot. It had started life as a medieval fortress and grew over hundreds of years into a royal residence with towers and spires. Then, in 1697, it burned. The fire destroyed most of the old castle in a matter of hours. Sweden, at that time a major power in northern Europe, suddenly had no proper home for its kings.

Here is the first surprise. The architect of the new palace was already working on Tre Kronor before the fire. Nicodemus Tessin the Younger had been rebuilding the old castle's north wing in a modern style. Part of that wing survived the flames. So when the decision came to build something completely new, Tessin was the natural choice. He did not try to copy the lost castle. He wanted something different — something Roman.

Tessin had travelled in Italy and France, and he admired the grand palaces of Rome. That is why this building looks the way it does. Notice the flat roofline, hidden behind a low stone balustrade. Notice the strict symmetry and the calm, repeating windows. This style is called Roman Baroque, a seventeenth-century style that used order, weight and dramatic interiors to show power. It was a statement that Sweden belonged among the great courts of Europe.

The work was slow. Wars and empty treasuries stopped construction for long periods. Tessin died in 1728 with the palace still unfinished. Another architect, Carl Hårleman, carried the project forward and completed many of the interiors in a lighter, more elegant taste. The royal family finally moved in during the 1750s, more than half a century after the fire. Imagine waiting that long for a new home — and imagine the kings who lived in temporary quarters all that time.

Now the second surprise. This is the official residence of the monarch, but the royal family does not actually live here. Since the early 1980s, they have made their home at Drottningholm Palace, west of the city. This building remains a working palace, though. The King has his offices here. Official dinners, state visits and ceremonies happen inside these walls. So the windows above you are not only museum rooms. Some of them belong to people at work.

The palace is enormous. It holds more than six hundred rooms, which makes it one of the largest royal palaces in Europe still used for its original purpose. Many of those rooms are open to visitors. The Royal Apartments show the state rooms where kings received guests. Karl the Eleventh's Gallery is a long banqueting hall, inspired by the great galleries of France. The Hall of State holds a silver throne made for Queen Kristina's coronation in the seventeenth century.

Go down below ground, and the mood changes completely. The Tre Kronor Museum sits in the vaults beneath the palace. Here you can see the thick old walls of the medieval castle that burned. The stone is rough and dark, and the air is cool. It is a strange feeling to stand under this ordered Baroque building and touch the remains of the fortress it replaced. Above you is the eighteenth century. Around you is the Middle Ages.

There is more to explore. The Treasury holds the Swedish regalia — the crowns, the orb and the sceptre used in royal ceremonies. Gustav the Third's Museum of Antiquities displays classical sculptures that the king collected on his travels in Italy. It opened in the 1790s and is one of the oldest public museums in Europe. Nearby, the Royal Armoury keeps carriages, armour and royal clothing, including garments with real stories behind them.

Back outside, take time to walk around the building. On the north side, a wide ramp called Lejonbacken, the Lion Slope, leads down toward the water and the bridge to the parliament. Bronze lions guard it. On the south side, the palace faces a sloping square and the old cathedral, Storkyrkan, where Swedish coronations once took place. Each side of the palace feels different, because each one faces a different part of the city's life.

If you are here at the right time, you may see the changing of the guard. Soldiers of the Royal Guards march into the outer courtyard, often with a military band. The music echoes off the walls. Crowds gather in a wide circle and children climb onto shoulders to see. The guards here are real soldiers, not actors. They have protected the royal residence in Stockholm for centuries, and the ceremony keeps that tradition visible to everyone.

The palace also changes with the seasons. In summer, the light lasts late into the evening, and the stone glows warm against a pale sky. In winter, darkness comes early, and the lit windows shine over black water. Snow sits on the balustrade and on the backs of the bronze lions. The building does not change, but the city around it changes almost every hour.

Before you leave, turn back and look at the long facade one more time. Count the windows if you can... you will probably give up. Think of the castle that burned here, and the decades it took to build this one in its place. Then walk down toward the water. Listen to the gulls again, and feel the cobbles under your feet — the same ground where Stockholm began.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see the state rooms before tour groups.
Check if state events close the Royal Apartments on your day.
Find a front spot early for the changing of the guard.
Walk the full exterior circuit, not just the courtyard.
Bring a warm layer for the cool underground vaults.
