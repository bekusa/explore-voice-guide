The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and look at the concrete. It is grey, heavy and patterned with the grain of the wooden boards it was poured against. Run your eye along the facade and you will see the faint lines of each plank, pressed into the stone like a fingerprint. Around you, the wind comes off the open fields of Gärdet, and the city feels further away than it really is.

This is Filmhuset, the Film House. It is the home of the Swedish Film Institute, the national body that supports, preserves and shows Swedish cinema. From the outside, the building does not try to charm you. It is solid, angular and serious. Inside, though, it holds one of the most important collections of moving images in Northern Europe.

The Film Institute was founded in 1963. Its creation came out of an agreement between the Swedish state and the film industry. Under that deal, a share of cinema ticket income went back into making new Swedish films. The man most closely linked to the idea was Harry Schein, a writer, engineer and passionate cinephile. He became the Institute's first leader, and he shaped its character for years.

At that time, Swedish cinema already had a strong reputation abroad. Ingmar Bergman was making the films that would define him for audiences around the world. Schein knew Bergman well. The new Institute aimed to protect that kind of artistic ambition. It wanted Sweden to make films for their quality, not only for the box office.

The building around you came a few years later. It was designed by the Swedish architect Peter Celsing and opened around 1970. Celsing is also known for Kulturhuset, the large glass culture centre on Sergels torg in central Stockholm. Here, however, he chose a very different language. Instead of glass and openness, he worked with raw concrete and deep, sheltering forms.

This style is often called Brutalism. The name does not mean brutal in the everyday sense. It comes from the French phrase béton brut, which simply means raw concrete. Brutalist architects left their materials honest and visible. They did not hide the structure behind decoration. Walk slowly along the walls and notice how the shapes step in and out, creating shadows that shift through the day.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The building was designed to bring several parts of the film world under one roof. Over the years, it has held offices, archives, a large film library, screening rooms and spaces for education. For a long time, it also housed training for future filmmakers. Writers, students, archivists and audiences all passed through the same doors.

One of the real treasures here is Cinemateket, the Institute's cinematheque. A cinematheque is a cinema dedicated to film history and film culture, rather than to new releases alone. The screening rooms in this building carry the names of two pioneers of Swedish silent cinema, Victor Sjöström and Mauritz Stiller. In the early twentieth century, these two directors helped put Swedish film on the world map.

Sjöström is remembered for silent dramas full of landscape, weather and moral struggle. Many years later, Bergman cast him as the old professor in Wild Strawberries, one of Bergman's best-known films. Stiller, for his part, discovered a young actress named Greta Garbo and took her with him to Hollywood. So when you sit in these rooms, you are sitting under the names of the people who started it all.

The Institute does much more than screen films. It also gives out the Guldbagge Awards, Sweden's national film prizes. The name means the Golden Beetle, and the trophy is shaped like one. The Institute also funds new productions, supports film education in schools, and works to restore and digitise older films. Much of Sweden's film heritage would be far harder to see today without this work.

Now look at the setting. Filmhuset stands on the edge of Gärdet, a wide, open area in the Östermalm district. For centuries, much of this land was used for military exercises and grazing. Today it is a green space where people walk dogs, fly kites and go running. Nearby you will also find buildings for Swedish public radio and television. This corner of Stockholm became, in a sense, a home for the country's media.

If you go inside, notice how the mood changes. The thick walls block out the wind and much of the street noise. The light becomes softer and more controlled, which suits a building made for watching images in the dark. The film library is a quiet place, with books, magazines and research material about cinema. It feels less like a monument and more like a working house for people who love film.

Opinions about the architecture have always been divided. Some people find the concrete cold and unwelcoming. Others see it as one of the finest modern buildings in Stockholm, honest and confident in its form. Over time, appreciation for this style has grown. Many architecture lovers now travel specifically to see buildings like this one, and to study how the concrete holds the light.

Before you leave, take one more look at the wall closest to you. Find the marks of the wooden boards in the concrete again. Somewhere inside, a film is perhaps being restored, catalogued or projected for a small, attentive audience. As you walk back across the open fields, the building settles quietly behind you, grey against the sky, still keeping its stories safe.
