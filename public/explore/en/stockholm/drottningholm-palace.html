The guide

The story

Water laps quietly against the stone of the quay. Beyond a wide formal garden, a long pale facade rises into the northern sky, mirrored almost perfectly in a calm stretch of lake. You may have come here by boat, gliding past wooded shores, or by road across the bridges west of the city. This is the home of the Swedish royal family. The place is called Drottningholm.

The name means the queen's islet. A queen gave the place its name back in the sixteenth century, when an earlier royal house stood on this ground. That first house burned down one winter night. What you see today rose in its place, larger and far grander than the building it replaced, shaped to impress all who arrived across the water.

Work on the present palace began in 1662. The architect was Nicodemus Tessin the Elder, one of the great builders of his age. He did not live to see it finished. His son, also an architect, carried the long work onward. Together, father and son, they shaped a palace in the baroque style, with sweeping wings, tall bright windows, and a roofline that draws and holds the eye from far across the lake.

The gardens stretch out before the palace in careful, geometric patterns. Gravel paths divide low beds of clipped hedge and smooth lawn. Fountains play, and bronze statues stand still among the greenery. Many of these sculptures were carried here from abroad, long ago, as the spoils of old wars in distant lands. They have weathered in this cool northern light for three centuries, and still they hold their poses.

Step inside, and the rooms unfold one after another in a long sequence. There are grand staircases of stone, ceilings painted with gods and battles, and halls hung with portraits of kings and queens long dead. The decoration changed with the fashions of each generation. You can read the passing of the centuries in the styles around you, from the heavy gold of the baroque to lighter, more delicate later work.

Drottningholm holds a rare treasure within its grounds. It is a theatre, built in 1766 for the pleasure of the court. When later generations lost interest in it, the building was simply closed and very nearly forgotten. Its old stage machinery sat untouched in the dark for well over a hundred years. That long neglect, strange as it sounds, turned out to be a kind of gift to the future.

Because it was left alone, the theatre survived almost unchanged. The wooden machines that move the scenery still work today. Painted backdrops slide smoothly on, canvas waves roll across the stage, and trapdoors open and close, all worked by hand with rope and wheel, just as they were in the eighteenth century. Very few theatres anywhere in the world have kept so much of their original craft alive.

Out in the gardens you will also find a smaller palace in a wholly different mood. It is called the Chinese Pavilion. In the eighteenth century, much of Europe was fascinated by the art and design of East Asia, and the court built this light, airy retreat in answer to that taste. It was a surprise, a birthday gift from a king to his queen, hidden among the trees until the day it was unveiled.

The Chinese Pavilion glows softly with red and gold. Inside are lacquered panels, fine porcelain, and furniture made in Europe to imitate the styles of far-off lands. It was built as a place to escape the weight and formality of the main palace, somewhere lighter and more playful. A short walk through the park brings you to it, set apart and quiet among the greenery.

In 1991, Drottningholm was named a World Heritage Site. The palace, the theatre, and the Chinese Pavilion were honoured together, as a single connected whole. They were recognised as the finest surviving example in northern Europe of a royal residence built in the grand French manner of the time, with all of its different parts still standing side by side, none of them lost.

Remember, as you walk, that this is still a living home. The royal family lives in one wing of the palace to this day. Those private rooms are closed to visitors, and rightly so. The rest of the building is open, and so the place does two things at the same time. It keeps the long past on display, and it shelters the everyday present behind the same old walls.

Find a quiet spot at the edge of the water before you leave. Look back at the long pale facade and its reflection lying still on the lake. The fountains murmur somewhere behind you. A swan may drift slowly past, pushing a line across the glass. For three hundred years this same view has changed very little, and standing here, you become a small part of its long and patient calm.
