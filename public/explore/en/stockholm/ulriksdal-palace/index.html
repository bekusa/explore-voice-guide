The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Gravel crunches under your shoes. Water laps softly somewhere close by, and the wind moves through old trees. You are only a short drive from central Stockholm, yet the city noise has almost gone. In front of you stands a pale, low palace with long wings. It faces the quiet water of Edsviken, a narrow inlet of the Baltic that reaches inland here.

This is Ulriksdal Palace. It sits in Solna, just north of the capital, inside the Royal National City Park. This green belt of forest, meadow and shoreline curves around the north of Stockholm. It holds several royal estates, and Ulriksdal is one of the gentlest of them. It was never a fortress and never a great seat of government. For most of its life, it was a place to rest, garden and spend the summer.

The story begins in the seventeenth century, when Sweden was a great power in northern Europe. A powerful noble, Jakob De la Gardie, had a country house built here in the 1640s. He named it Jakobsdal, which means Jakob's valley. The De la Gardie family belonged to the inner circle of the Swedish court. A house this close to the capital made sense for a man who needed to be near the king.

The name changed when the estate passed into royal hands. In 1669, Queen Hedvig Eleonora bought the property. She was the widow of King Charles the Tenth and one of the strongest figures at the Swedish court for decades. Later she renamed the palace Ulriksdal, after her grandson, Prince Ulrik. Here is a sad detail many visitors miss. The little prince died as a baby. So the palace carries the name of a child who never grew up to see it.

Over the following centuries, many royal owners changed and extended the building. Each generation added rooms, removed others and followed the fashion of its own time. That is why the palace does not feel like a single design. As you walk around it, notice how the wings and details do not quite match. The building shows layers of change, much like an old family home that each owner adapted to their own needs.

In the nineteenth century, King Charles the Fifteenth grew very fond of Ulriksdal. He was an artistic king who painted and collected objects from earlier centuries. He loved the romantic idea of the past. Under him, the palace filled with historic furniture and decoration. He also ordered a chapel for the estate in the 1860s. Look for it near the water. It is built of brick in a style that borrows from Dutch Renaissance buildings, with gables and warm red tones.

The chapel is a surprise for many people. It looks almost too cheerful for a royal chapel. It is still used today, and couples sometimes choose it for weddings. The setting explains why. On a calm day the water lies flat, the trees lean over the shore, and the bells carry across the inlet. It feels more like a village church than part of a royal palace.

The twentieth century brought the most personal chapter. Crown Prince Gustaf Adolf, later King Gustaf the Sixth Adolf, made Ulriksdal his family home for the summer. He used it for decades, until his death in 1973. He was a serious amateur archaeologist and a passionate gardener. He was also a quiet collector of art and Chinese ceramics. Many Swedes remember him as a gentle, scholarly king rather than a grand one.

Inside, one room tells his story especially well. In the 1920s, the furniture designer Carl Malmsten created a living room for the Crown Prince and his wife, Louise. Malmsten became one of Sweden's best-known designers of the twentieth century. His furniture is light, practical and full of natural wood. The room does not look like a throne room. It looks like a comfortable family space, and it shows the start of a modern, simpler idea of royal life in Sweden.

Now turn toward the gardens and the orangery. An orangery is a large greenhouse that grand houses built to protect citrus trees and other delicate plants through the hard northern winter. In Sweden, keeping orange trees alive through months of frost and darkness was a sign of wealth and skill. At Ulriksdal, the orangery also holds a collection of Swedish sculpture. Marble figures stand among the plants, so art and gardening share the same bright space.

Take a moment to think about the light. In midsummer, the northern sky barely goes dark, and evening can last for hours. In winter, daylight is short and low, and the palace walls can look almost silver. These extremes shaped how people used the estate. Summer was the season for long walks, boating and gatherings outside. Winter was for warm rooms, candles and plants kept safely under glass.

Outside the palace itself, the wider park gives the visit a lot of its character. Paths lead along the shore and into the woods. Joggers, dog walkers and families with prams pass by, because this is a local park as much as a royal one. Near the palace, an old inn serves visitors. It continues a long tradition of travellers stopping here for food on the way into or out of the city.

Before you leave, stand still for one more minute. Look at the pale facade, then at the water, then at the trees behind you. Many people have stood near this spot. A seventeenth-century noble, a determined queen, an artistic king and a modest gardener-king all knew this view. As you walk back along the gravel, listen again to the water and the wind. They are probably much the same sounds that each of them heard here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with a walk along Edsviken's shoreline.
Check in advance which palace rooms are open.
Wear comfortable shoes for the gravel paths and park trails.
Visit on a summer evening for long, soft northern light.
Leave time for the orangery's sculpture collection.
