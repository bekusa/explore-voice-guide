The guide

The story

Listen first. Wind moves through low, grey-green scrub. Somewhere beyond the dunes, Atlantic waves break and pull back over the sand. The air smells of salt, dry earth, and something faintly resinous from the shrubs at your feet. The light here is wide and pale, softened by the sea haze that often hangs over this coast. This is Souss-Massa National Park, a long strip of Moroccan shoreline where desert, river, and ocean meet.

The park runs along the Atlantic coast south of Agadir, in southwestern Morocco. Morocco created it in 1991 to protect a rare mix of habitats in one place. There are cliffs, sand dunes, coastal steppe, argan woodland, and two river mouths. The Souss River reaches the sea near the northern end. The Massa River reaches it further south. Between them lies a narrow band of land that holds more life than first appears.

Here is the first surprise. This quiet coast is home to one of the rarest birds on Earth — the northern bald ibis. It is a dark, glossy bird with a bare red face, a long curved red bill, and a ragged crest of feathers at the back of its head. Centuries ago it lived across North Africa, the Middle East, and even the Alps of Europe. Today, the coast around Souss-Massa holds the main wild population left anywhere.

The ibis is not elegant in the usual sense. Up close, it can look almost prehistoric. Yet conservationists treat every chick born here as important news. The birds nest on ledges of the sea cliffs and fly inland to feed. They walk across open steppe and fields, probing the ground for beetles, larvae, and small lizards. If you see a loose group of dark birds moving slowly over dry ground, look again. You may be watching a species that came very close to vanishing.

And the story has a hopeful turn. Decades of protection here, along with work by local wardens and international partners, helped the population grow. In 2018, the global conservation body known as the IUCN moved the species from critically endangered to endangered. That is still a serious status. But it is one of the few times a bird this rare has moved in the right direction. Much of that change is linked to this stretch of coast.

Now turn your attention to the river mouths. Where the Massa meets the sea, fresh water spreads into a shallow lagoon behind a sand bar. Reeds line the banks. The water is often still, and it reflects the sky. This is where many birdwatchers gather, especially in the cooler months. Greater flamingos sometimes stand in the shallows, their pink bodies bright against brown water. Spoonbills sweep their flat bills side to side. Herons wait without moving.

This coast lies on a major flight path for migrating birds. Each year, many species travel between Europe and Africa, and they stop here to rest and feed. That means the cast of birds changes with the seasons. Waders run along the mud at low tide. Gulls and terns work the surf. Small songbirds shelter in the scrub. Even if you cannot name a single species, you can feel the movement of the place. The wetlands are protected under the international Ramsar agreement for important wetlands.

The second surprise is not a bird at all. Inside fenced reserves within the park, Morocco has helped bring back desert animals that had disappeared from much of North Africa. These include dorcas gazelles, the larger dama gazelle, the addax with its spiral horns, and the scimitar-horned oryx, whose long curved horns once inspired stories of unicorns. Red-necked ostriches are part of this effort too. Many of these animals came from breeding programmes abroad. These enclosures act as a kind of bridge between captivity and the wild.

Look at the plants around you. The argan tree grows in this region and almost nowhere else in the world. It is a tough, thorny tree with small leaves and a twisted trunk. Its fruit gives argan oil, which local women have pressed by hand for generations. Nearby you may also see spiny, cactus-like euphorbia plants. They are not true cactuses, but they solved the same problem in a similar way. They store water and defend themselves with thorns. Everything here is shaped by heat, wind, and salt.

People are part of this landscape too. Small villages sit near the park edges. Fishermen launch boats from beaches and coves, and you may see nets drying in the sun. Farmers grow crops on the river plains, where the soil is richer. Shepherds move goats across open ground. The park is not an empty wilderness. It is a working coast, and protecting it means balancing the needs of wildlife with the daily lives of the families who live here.

Near the mouth of the Massa River lies the small settlement of Sidi Rbat. It carries a remarkable local tradition. Legend has it that this is the beach where the prophet Jonah was thrown back onto land after his time inside the great fish. Many cultures place this story in different locations, so treat it as folklore rather than history. Still, it is easy to understand why such a tale would settle here. On a misty morning, the shore feels like the very edge of the known world.

A few practical thoughts as you explore. Distances here are larger than they look, and shade is rare. The best wildlife viewing usually comes early in the morning or late in the afternoon, when animals are active and the light is soft. Move slowly near the water. Birds tolerate a quiet, still person far better than a moving group. Keep to paths, because the dunes and steppe are fragile. A footprint in crusted soil can last a surprisingly long time.

Before you leave, pause where you are standing. Notice the colours — the sandy gold, the pale green of the scrub, the blue-grey line of the ocean. Listen again for the wind and the surf. Somewhere nearby, a bird you may never see is feeding, nesting, or resting on a long journey. This coast asks for patience more than effort. As you walk back, keep your eyes on the cliffs and the open ground. The rarest thing here may be moving quietly just ahead of you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Come early morning for active birds and softer light.
Bring binoculars for watching birds across the lagoon.
Carry water and sun protection; shade is scarce.
Stay on paths to protect fragile dunes and soil.
Visit the Massa estuary in cooler months for waterbirds.
