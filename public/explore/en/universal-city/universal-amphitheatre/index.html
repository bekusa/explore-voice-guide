The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Under the noise of the crowds, the theme music, and the tram tours rolling past, there is a quieter sound up here on the hill: the warm wind coming off the dry slopes of the Santa Monica Mountains. It smells faintly of sage and hot asphalt. This hillside in Universal City once held a concert venue where thousands of people sat under the California night sky. That venue is gone now. But its story is still part of this ground.

First, some orientation. Universal City is not a typical city. It is an unincorporated patch of Los Angeles County. It sits at the top of the Cahuenga Pass, the low gap in the hills that links Hollywood to the San Fernando Valley. The name comes from the film studio. Carl Laemmle, the founder of Universal Pictures, opened a studio town here in 1915. He even invited the public to come and watch films being made. That idea of letting visitors inside the dream factory has shaped this hill ever since.

The Universal Amphitheatre opened in 1972. At first it was an open-air venue built into the slope of the studio lot. The seats rose steeply in a curved bowl, so even people in the back rows felt close to the stage. Picture a summer evening here. The sun drops behind the hills, the sky turns orange and then deep blue, and the first chords ring out across the valley. For a decade, that was the experience: music under open air, with the lights of the Valley spread out below.

The open-air design had a problem, though. Southern California evenings can turn cool, and sound travels far in the hills. Nearby residents could hear the shows. Performers and audiences also had to deal with wind and the occasional rain. So in 1982 the amphitheatre was enclosed with a roof. It became an indoor theatre, but it kept its steep, curved seating. Many concertgoers said this gave it a rare feeling. It held thousands of people, yet it felt almost intimate.

That balance is the reason the venue earned a loyal following. Larger arenas in Los Angeles could hold far bigger crowds. But in a big arena, a performer can look like a small figure far away. Here, the steep rake of the seats pulled the audience toward the stage. Musicians, comedians, and touring shows all used this room. For many people who grew up in Los Angeles, a night at the amphitheatre was a first concert, a birthday treat, or a first date.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Most people who come to Universal City today come for the theme park and its rides. They do not know that for more than forty years, this hill was also one of the region's important live music rooms. People drove up the pass not to see film sets, but to hear a band. The studio, the tour, and the concert hall all shared the same slope, side by side.

In 2005 the venue took a new name. The guitar maker Gibson bought the naming rights, and the hall became the Gibson Amphitheatre. Naming deals like this became common across American venues in that period. For regulars, though, the old name often stayed in conversation. You can still hear older Angelenos call it "the Universal Amphitheatre" when they remember a show. Names on a building can change quickly. The names people use in memory change much more slowly.

During those later years, the hall also hosted televised events and award shows. Cameras, red carpets, and bright stage lighting brought the Hollywood side of Universal City into the concert room. It was a fitting mix. On this hill, entertainment has always moved between the screen and the stage. A venue that started as a place for live music also became a place where television was made in front of a live crowd.

Then the story turned. In 2013 the amphitheatre closed, and it was later demolished. The studio needed the land for a large new attraction. That attraction was The Wizarding World of Harry Potter, which opened at Universal Studios Hollywood in 2016. So the ground where concert seats once climbed the hill now carries snowy rooftops, a castle on a ridge, and shop windows full of wands and sweets. The music hall did not move. It simply gave way.

This is the second surprise for many visitors. When you walk through the village streets of the wizarding area, you are walking across land that once held a concert stage. The crowd noise you hear now is different. It is the sound of families, the hiss of drinks being poured, and the music from the park speakers. But the basic purpose of the land is the same. People still come here to gather, to be entertained, and to share an evening.

It is worth thinking about what was lost and what remains. Fans of the old hall felt real sadness when it closed. A good concert room is hard to replace, because its sound and its shape become part of people's memories. At the same time, Universal City has always rebuilt itself. Sets go up and come down. Attractions open and close. Change is part of the character of this hill, from Laemmle's first studio to the park you see today.

As you look around, notice the landscape that has not changed. The brown and green hills still rise around the lot. The freeway still runs through the pass below. On a clear day, you can look out over the San Fernando Valley, a wide grid of streets and rooftops stretching toward distant mountains. Concertgoers in the 1970s saw much the same view on their way in. The buildings change, but the hills and the light stay.

So before you move on, take one more look at the ground beneath your feet and the slope around you. Imagine rows of seats curving up this hill. Imagine the hush before the first song, and the roar after the last one. Then listen again to the sounds of today. As you walk down toward the rest of the park or the shops of CityWalk, you are carrying a small piece of this hill's memory with you.
