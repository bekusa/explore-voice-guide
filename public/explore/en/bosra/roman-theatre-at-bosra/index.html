The guide

The story

Put your palm flat against the stone at the entrance. It is dark, almost black, and slightly rough, like cooled lava. That is exactly what it is. This is basalt, the volcanic rock of the Hauran plain in southern Syria. Above you, the passage curves into shadow. Somewhere ahead, light spills down onto rows of seats. It is cool in here, even on a hot day.

You are entering one of the best-preserved Roman theatres anywhere in the world. Many visitors expect a ruin open to the sky, with broken seats and scattered columns. Instead, they find a theatre that is nearly whole. It sits inside the walls of a medieval fortress. That strange pairing is the story of this place. A building made for comedy and tragedy survived because soldiers turned it into a castle.

First, the city itself. Bosra lies in the south of Syria, close to the border with Jordan. Before the Romans, it belonged to the Nabataeans, the Arab trading people best known for Petra. In the early second century, the emperor Trajan absorbed their kingdom into the Roman Empire. Bosra became the capital of the new province of Arabia. Caravans, soldiers and officials passed through its streets. A capital needed a grand theatre, and this is it.

Historians generally date the theatre to the second century. It was a public building on a very large scale. Estimates of its capacity vary between sources, but it held many thousands of people. Imagine the seats full. Officials in the lower rows, ordinary citizens higher up, everyone facing the same stage. Plays, music, speeches and civic ceremonies would all have taken place here, in front of the whole city.

Now look at the seating. The rows rise in a wide half-circle, cut and fitted from the same dark basalt. Roman builders usually set theatres into a hillside for support. Bosra sits on flat land, so this one stands freely on its own vaulted structure. Beneath your feet run corridors and arches that carry the weight of the seats. They also moved crowds in and out quickly, a little like the tunnels of a modern stadium.

Here is the first surprise. Basalt is extremely hard to carve. It is dense and heavy, and it dulls tools quickly. Builders in Rome often used softer stone, brick and concrete faced with marble. In the Hauran, basalt was the local material, so craftsmen learned to shape it with real skill. The clean edges of the seats and the crisp profiles of the column bases show that work. Run your eye along a row and notice how tightly the blocks meet.

Turn toward the stage. Behind it rises the stage wall, which the Romans called the scaenae frons. It is a tall decorated facade, not a plain backdrop. Columns stand in tiers, many of them with Corinthian capitals, the style carved with curling acanthus leaves. Some columns are pale stone, which stands out sharply against the black basalt. Doorways in this wall let actors enter and leave. The whole facade worked like permanent scenery for every performance.

Now stand quietly for a moment and listen. Roman theatres were designed so a voice from the stage could carry to the upper rows. The curve of the seating, the height of the stage wall and the hard stone surfaces all help. If the theatre is calm, ask a companion to speak from the stage at a normal level. You may be surprised how clearly the words reach you. Two thousand years later, the design still works.

So how did this theatre survive so well? After the Roman and Byzantine periods, Bosra came under Islamic rule. Over the following centuries, rulers fortified the theatre. The major work came under the Ayyubids, the dynasty founded by Saladin, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. They wrapped the theatre in thick walls and towers. The seating bowl became the core of a fortress. Stone that might have been taken for other buildings stayed in place, protected.

This is the second surprise. For centuries, much of the theatre was hidden. Houses, rubble and later structures filled parts of the interior. Travellers saw a castle, not a stage. Only in the twentieth century did archaeologists clear the space and restore what we see today. So the theatre you are standing in was, in a sense, rediscovered. Its protection and its concealment came from the same fortress.

Bosra holds other layers of history too. The wider ancient city is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site. Around the fortress are Roman streets, baths, arches and some of the earliest mosques in the region. There is also a well-known tradition about the town. According to Islamic tradition, the young Muhammad travelled here with a trading caravan and met a Christian monk named Bahira. Locals still point out a building linked with that story.

The recent past has been hard for Bosra. Like much of Syria, the town was affected by years of conflict, and Syrian heritage sites were placed on UNESCO's list of World Heritage in Danger. Before the war, the theatre hosted a festival of music and performance, and audiences filled these seats again. Conditions on the ground can change, so it is worth checking local advice about access and any restored areas before you explore further.

As you walk around, notice the contrast between the two builders. The Roman parts are smooth, curved and symmetrical, made for watching. The medieval parts are blunt, thick and defensive, made for holding ground. Look up at the towers, then down at the seats. One culture built for an audience. Another built for a siege. Here, they share the same walls.

Before you leave, find a seat in the upper rows and sit for a minute. Feel the stone under you, warm where the sun touches it and cool in the shade. Look down at the stage and the pale columns against the black wall. Many thousands of people sat in these rows long ago. Now you are one of them. As you walk back out through the dark passage, listen to how your footsteps echo... and then fade into the open air.
