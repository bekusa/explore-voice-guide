The guide

The story

Start with the stone. Soft, dark, almost greasy to the eye, it is the colour of wet slate with a faint green sheen. Carved into its surface, a scorpion stands upright on two legs. Beside it, an eagle spreads its wings, and a snake coils around a bull. These shapes are more than four thousand years old. They come from the valley of the Halil River, in the far south of Kerman Province, and they belong to a civilisation that most of the world did not know existed until this century.

This is the Jiroft culture. The name comes from the modern town of Jiroft, a farming centre surrounded by date palms and citrus groves. The land here is warm and low, and it sits well below the cold desert plateau of central Iran. Mountains rise in the distance, and the river brings water down from them. For a Bronze Age people, this was a good place to live. It had fresh water, fertile soil, and routes that led toward the sea and toward distant cities.

The story of how the world found Jiroft is unusual. Around the start of the 2000s, heavy floods along the Halil River cut into the ground and exposed ancient graves. Local people found carved stone vessels inside. What followed was painful. Looting spread quickly, and thousands of objects were dug up without any record of where they came from. Many of them left Iran and appeared on the international antiquities market.

This matters for every object you see. When an artefact is taken from the ground without care, archaeologists lose its context. They lose the layer of soil, the objects buried next to it, and the date that context could give. So the beauty of these vessels comes with a gap in knowledge. Iranian authorities later recovered and confiscated large numbers of pieces, and some objects held abroad have been returned. Many of the finest pieces you can see today came back this way.

Scientific work began soon after the looting. A team led by the Iranian archaeologist Yousef Majidzadeh started excavations in the early 2000s, and international researchers joined the effort. Their main focus was a pair of large mounds called Konar Sandal, a few kilometres from the modern town. One mound lies to the north, and one lies to the south. Together they mark the remains of a substantial settlement from the third millennium before Christ.

At Konar Sandal South, the excavators found something that surprised many people. It is a massive raised platform built of mud brick. Some researchers compare it to the stepped temple towers of Mesopotamia, which are called ziggurats. Others prefer more cautious words. Either way, it shows a society that could organise labour on a large scale. Building it required planning, food surpluses, and leaders who could direct many workers over a long time.

Now return to the stone vessels, because they are the heart of this culture. Most are carved from chlorite, a soft stone that is easy to cut but holds fine detail. The carvers made bowls, jars, and flat weights with handles. Some pieces once held inlays of coloured stone or shell set into small hollows. Look closely at any vessel on display, and you may still see these empty sockets, where a bright stone once sat.

The imagery on these vessels repeats a set of powerful themes. You see eagles with spread wings and figures that are half human and half animal. Some show a man with a scorpion's body, and others show bulls with heavy humps on their shoulders. Snakes appear again and again, often wrapped around other creatures or fighting them. Palm trees, rivers, and wild cats fill the scenes. Scholars still debate what these stories meant, so treat any single interpretation with care.

One motif surprises many visitors. Certain vessels carry a pattern that looks like the front of a building. It shows rows of small doorways, or perhaps windows, with a slanted roof above. Some researchers call this the hut motif. It may show a real type of house, a temple, or a symbolic shape. Whatever it represents, it gives a rare glimpse of how these people pictured the built world around them.

Here is a second surprise. Vessels in this same carving style were found long before Jiroft was known. They turned up in Mesopotamia, in Syria, and around the Persian Gulf. Archaeologists had a name for them, the intercultural style, but nobody knew where they came from. The discoveries in the Halil valley gave a strong candidate for their homeland. Suddenly, objects in old museum collections across the region had a possible origin.

This suggests that Jiroft was part of a wide trading network. The Bronze Age world was more connected than we often imagine. Goods moved between the Iranian plateau, the Indus Valley, the Gulf, and the cities of Mesopotamia. Majidzadeh has proposed that Jiroft might be Aratta, a rich land described in ancient Sumerian poems. It is an attractive idea. But it remains a hypothesis, and many scholars disagree, so it is best to hold it lightly.

There is also the question of writing. A few inscribed objects from the area carry signs that some researchers link to an early script of the Iranian plateau. This claim is still debated, and the evidence is limited. So when you hear that Jiroft had its own writing, remember that the full answer is not yet known. Much about this civilisation remains open, and new seasons of excavation could change the picture.

As you stand here, look again at the carved surfaces. Notice how the artist cut the feathers of the eagle in neat rows. Notice the muscles of the bull and the tension in the coiled snake. Someone in this river valley held a soft stone and a sharp tool, and made these shapes with great patience. Outside, the palms still grow, and the river still comes down from the mountains. The land that fed this civilisation is the same land under your feet today.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the Jiroft archaeological museum before going to Konar Sandal.
Plan mound visits for early morning to avoid the southern heat.
Bring a hat and plenty of water for the open mounds.
Ask museum staff which objects were recovered from looters.
Use zoom or close viewing to study fine carving details.
