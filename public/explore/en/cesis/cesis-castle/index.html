The guide

The story

Listen for the gravel first. It shifts under your shoes as you walk toward the ruins. Then comes the stone itself: rough, grey, patched with darker blocks where centuries of repair have been laid over older work. On a damp day the walls hold a cool, mineral smell, like the inside of a well. Above you, the towers rise unevenly. Some edges are sharp, and some are worn soft by wind and rain. This is Cēsis Castle, in the heart of a small Latvian town about ninety kilometres northeast of Riga.

The story begins in the early thirteenth century. Crusading knights from German lands were pushing into the Baltic region. A military order called the Livonian Brothers of the Sword chose this hill for a stronghold. The site already had value. Local tribes, ancestors of today's Latvians, had fortified the area before the knights arrived. The newcomers built in stone, and they built to last. In German records the town and castle appear under another name, Wenden. You may still see that name on old maps inside the museum.

The Brothers of the Sword did not survive long as an independent order. After a heavy defeat in battle, they were absorbed into the Teutonic Order. Their Baltic branch became known as the Livonian Order. Over time, Cēsis became one of its most important seats. For long periods it served as the residence of the Order's Master, its highest leader in the region. That is a surprise for many visitors. This quiet town was once the administrative centre of a knightly state.

Take a moment to look at the scale of what remains. The castle grew in stages over several centuries. Walls were thickened. Towers were added. Gunpowder changed warfare, and the builders adapted, making the defences heavier and more rounded. When you study the masonry, you can sometimes see these changes in the stone itself. A line of different blocks, a filled-in window, a doorway that now leads nowhere. Each one marks a new owner, a new threat, or a new idea about defence.

The most dramatic chapter came in 1577, during the Livonian War. The armies of Ivan the Fourth, the Russian tsar known as Ivan the Terrible, laid siege to the castle. The defenders were trapped and had no hope of relief. According to the accounts that have come down to us, they chose not to surrender. They gathered in the castle with stores of gunpowder and set it alight. Many died in the explosion. The story is still told in Cēsis today, and it shapes how the town remembers this place.

The castle was repaired and changed hands more than once after that. Polish, Swedish and Russian powers all fought over this region. The final blow came in the early eighteenth century, during the Great Northern War, a long conflict between Sweden and Russia. The castle was badly damaged and was never fully rebuilt as a fortress. From then on, it slowly became what you see now. It is a ruin, but a remarkably complete one, with walls and towers you can still climb.

Now turn your attention to the newer building beside the ruins. It looks calm and domestic compared to the old fortress. This is the New Castle, a manor house built in the eighteenth century. It belonged to the Sievers family, Baltic German nobles who owned the estate for generations. They lived here in comfort while the medieval walls stood as a romantic backdrop. Today the manor houses the town's history and art museum. Inside, the collections follow Cēsis from prehistory to the twentieth century.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look up at the tower of the New Castle. You will often see the Latvian flag flying there, dark red with a narrow white stripe. Cēsis has a special place in the story of that flag. A medieval chronicle describes a red banner with a white stripe carried by local warriors from this region. Historians still debate the details. But Latvians often point to Cēsis as one of the flag's earliest homes, which is why the flag over this tower carries extra meaning.

If you go into the old ruins, you may be offered a candle lantern at the entrance. Take it. Parts of the towers are dark, and the lantern is not just decoration. The light flickers on the walls as you climb narrow spiral stairs. The steps are uneven and worn into shallow dips by many feet. Take your time here. Hold the handrail where there is one. At the top, the dark gives way to open air and a view across the rooftops and the green slopes of the town.

From above, notice how the castle connects to the town around it. Cēsis grew in the shadow of the fortress. The old streets bend around the hill, and the low houses sit close together. Nearby you will find the castle park, with old trees, open lawns and a pond. In summer the park is full of birdsong and the smell of cut grass. In autumn the leaves turn yellow and copper against the grey stone. It is a good place to sit and let the history settle.

Cēsis carries one more layer of memory, from the twentieth century. In 1919, during the Latvian War of Independence, Estonian and Latvian forces fought a decisive battle near the town. They defeated German forces who wanted to control the young republic. This Battle of Cēsis is still commemorated in both countries. In the town centre you can find a monument connected to this victory. It reminds visitors that this hill has been fought over not only in medieval times, but also within living family memory.

What makes this place feel alive is the mix of eras. Medieval knights, Baltic German nobles, a modern museum and a national flag all share one small hilltop. In summer, the castle grounds often host events, from music to medieval-style festivals. Even on a quiet weekday, you may hear children echoing their voices in the towers, or a guide's footsteps on the stairs. The castle is not sealed behind glass. It is a place where people still walk, climb and touch the stone.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment in the courtyard. Feel the gravel under your feet again. Look at the joins between the stones, the dark gaps where windows once held glass, the green moss in the shaded corners. Then look across to the calm manor and the flag moving in the wind. As you walk back into town, notice how often the towers appear between the houses. They stay in sight for a long time... just as they have for every traveller who passed this way before you.
