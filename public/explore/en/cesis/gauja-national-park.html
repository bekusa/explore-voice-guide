The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Somewhere below you, water is moving over stones. The air smells of pine resin and damp earth, and the ground underfoot is soft with needles. Through the trees you may catch a flash of rust-red rock. That colour belongs to an ancient seabed. This is Gauja National Park, and the town of Cēsis sits on its eastern edge, like a gate into the forest.

The park was created in 1973. That makes it the oldest national park in Latvia, and it is also the largest. It covers more than nine hundred square kilometres of forest, meadow, and river valley. The park is not a fenced wilderness. People live inside it. There are farms, villages, and whole towns, including Cēsis, Sigulda, and Līgatne. Nature and daily life share the same ground here.

The heart of the park is the Gauja River itself. It is the longest river that flows entirely within Latvia's borders. Over thousands of years, the water cut a deep valley through soft rock. In places the banks rise as steep walls above the current. The river bends again and again, so each turn opens a new view. In summer, canoes drift slowly downstream. In autumn, the valley fills with yellow birch and red maple.

Now, about that red rock. The cliffs here are sandstone from the Devonian period — a time hundreds of millions of years ago, long before the dinosaurs. Back then, this land lay under shallow water. Sand settled layer by layer and slowly hardened. Today you can still see those layers as fine stripes in the cliff faces. Touch the stone if you can reach it. It feels gritty, and it crumbles easily. That softness is why the river carved so deeply.

Here is something many visitors miss. Because the sandstone is soft, people have scratched names and dates into it for generations. Some of the carvings near the larger caves are old. Locals treat them as a kind of folk record. But please do not add your own. The rock is fragile, and every new mark speeds up erosion. Today the park asks visitors to look and not to carve.

Close to Cēsis, one of the best-known outcrops is Ērgļu klintis, the Eagle Cliffs. The name means exactly that in Latvian. A walking trail runs along the top of the cliffs, high above the river. From the edge you look straight down onto the water and across to the forested far bank. Early in the morning, mist often hangs low in the valley. The view changes minute by minute as it lifts.

Further down the valley, near Sigulda, lies Gūtmaņa ala, often called Gutman's Cave. It is described as the largest cave in the Baltic states, although it is more of a wide grotto than a deep tunnel. A small spring flows out from inside. Legend has it that the water once had healing powers. The cave is also tied to one of Latvia's most loved tales — the story of the Rose of Turaida.

According to the popular tale, a young woman called Maija lived near Turaida Castle in the seventeenth century. She was loved by a local gardener. A soldier tried to take her by force, and she chose death rather than dishonour. Her grave lies near the castle, and couples still leave flowers there. Historians debate how much of the story is true. But the legend is now a quiet part of local identity.

The park is also a landscape of castles. Many date back to the thirteenth century, when German crusading orders came to this region. Cēsis Castle was one of the main strongholds of the Livonian Order, a military order of knights that ruled much of present-day Latvia and Estonia. Turaida, Sigulda, and Krimulda have their own fortresses and ruins. Standing on the castle walls, you can see why they chose these spots. The river valley was a natural road.

Here is the second surprise. Near Līgatne, inside the park, a secret Soviet bunker was hidden under an ordinary-looking building. It was built during the Cold War as a shelter for the Communist Party leadership of the Latvian republic. For years, almost nobody knew it was there. Today it is open to visitors on guided tours. So in one day you can see medieval knights, Devonian rock, and Cold War concrete.

The wildlife is shy, but it is here. The forests shelter deer, elk, wild boar, and foxes. Along the river, look for beavers. You may not see the animals, but you will often see their work — tree stumps chewed into sharp points near the water. Woodpeckers drum in the pines. In spring, the meadows fill with wildflowers, and the dawn chorus of birds can be surprisingly loud.

Latvians sometimes call this area Latvian Switzerland. The name is a little playful, since there are no Alps here. But the deep valley, the forest, and the steep banks do give the land a sense of height that is rare in the flat Baltic countryside. The park is crossed by many marked trails. Some are short loops of a few kilometres. Others link towns together and take a full day.

So take a breath before you move on. Notice the light coming through the pine branches, and the cool air that rises from the river. Look again at the red stone, and remember the old sea that made it. As you walk back towards Cēsis, the forest will thin, and the town's rooftops and castle towers will appear. The river will keep running behind you, cutting the valley one grain of sand at a time.

Before you go

Practical tips

Walk the Eagle Cliffs trail at dawn for valley mist.
Wear sturdy shoes; sandstone paths get slippery after rain.
Book the Līgatne Soviet bunker tour in advance.
Rent a canoe to see the cliffs from the river.
Bring insect repellent for summer forest walks.
