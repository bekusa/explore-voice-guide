Mausoleum of Shah Rukn-e-Alam

The dome appears before the city does.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

The dome appears before the city does. From far across the flat plain of southern Punjab, a white half-sphere floats above the heat haze. Beneath it stand bands of deep red brick, dark timber, and glazed blue tile. This is the tomb of Shah Rukn-e-Alam, the Pillar of the World. It has stood over Multan for seven centuries, and it is still the first thing many travelers see.

The man inside was born in Multan in 1251. His name was Rukn-ud-Din Abul Fath, but people called him Rukn-e-Alam. He was the grandson of Bahauddin Zakariya, the great Sufi teacher who made Multan a center of learning. Rukn-e-Alam led the Suhrawardiyya order of Sufis after his father. Sultans asked him for advice. The poor asked him for bread and blessings. He died in 1335, mourned across Punjab.

The building is older than the man's death, and that is a strange story. Most historians believe Ghiyas-ud-Din Tughluq built it between 1320 and 1324, while he governed this frontier before founding the Tughluq dynasty in Delhi. Many think he intended it as his own tomb. History changed his plans. He died near Delhi, and his successor gave this building to the saint's family. Rukn-e-Alam was buried here in 1335.

Walk around the outside first. The tomb is an octagon, a shape with eight sides, and it rises in three levels. The lowest level has walls that lean inward as they climb, like the base of a mountain. Round towers stand at each corner, and they slope too. The shape is not only beautiful. Sloped walls resist floods and tremors, and they carry the great weight of the dome above.

The whole structure rises about 35 meters above the mound. Because it stands on the highest ground in the city, the tip of the dome is the highest point in Multan. In the fourteenth century, this dome was among the largest in South Asia. Builders talked about it from Delhi to Central Asia. Travelers wrote that they could see it from a full day's ride away.

Come close to the walls and read the crafts of Multan. Red brick is laid in careful courses. Beams of dark shisham wood run through the brickwork, tying the walls together and absorbing shocks. Between brick and timber sit panels of glazed tile in white, sky blue, and deep cobalt. This is Multani kashi work, a craft the city still practices today. Look for stars, flowers, and flowing Arabic script.

The doors are low and made of carved wood, so every visitor bows a little on the way in. Inside, the light is soft and the air is cool. A single high room holds the cenotaph of the saint, covered with cloth and rose petals. Relatives and followers rest in graves around him. A mihrab marks the direction of prayer. Voices stay low under the great curve of the dome.

Watch the pilgrims for a moment. Some tie colored threads to the lattice screens and whisper a wish. Some bring sweets to share after a prayer was answered. The air smells of rosewater and incense. Nothing here is performed for tourists. These are the same quiet habits that visitors brought seven hundred years ago.

By the twentieth century, the great tomb was tired. Tiles had fallen and timber had cracked. Between 1971 and 1977, craftsmen carried out a complete restoration using traditional methods, firing new tiles in local workshops and cutting timber as the first builders did. In 1983, the project received an Aga Khan Award for Architecture, one of the highest honors for building in the Islamic world.

Multan is called the City of Saints, and this tomb is the heart of that name. Around 100,000 pilgrims come here every year. During the urs, the festival that marks the anniversary of the saint's death, the courtyard fills with prayer, song, and shared food. For many families of Punjab, no journey to Multan is complete without climbing this hill.

Before leaving, stand at the edge of the platform. The old fort mound spreads around you, and below lie the packed lanes of the walled city, the domes of other shrines, and the flat green plain. In the evening light, swallows circle the dome and the brick turns the color of embers.

Seven centuries of heat, floods, and wars have passed over this hill. Empires rose and dissolved. Even the river moved away. The tomb remains, doing the same patient work it has always done: holding one man's memory, and giving an entire city a shape to look up to.

Key facts

Built between 1320 and 1324 by Ghiyas-ud-Din Tughluq
Saint Rukn-e-Alam lived from 1251 to 1335
Structure rises about 35 meters above the fort mound
Restoration from 1971 to 1977 won 1983 Aga Khan Award
Around 100,000 pilgrims visit the tomb each year

What to look for

Sloping corner towers on all eight sides
Blue and white kashi tile bands with star patterns
Shisham timber beams running through the brickwork
Colored threads tied to the lattice screens

Practical tips

Remove shoes and cover your head before entering
Visit near sunset for cooler air and glowing brickwork
Combine with the Bahauddin Zakariya mausoleum nearby
Ask permission before photographing pilgrims inside
