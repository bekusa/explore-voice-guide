The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the music drifting from the speakers, you can hear water moving, the hum of crowds, and somewhere a low mechanical rumble. Then look up. Sunlight strikes angled glass, and the buildings around you break into sharp shards of sky. Some look like crystals pushed up from the ground. Others look like giant spheres or tilted cubes. This is Futuroscope, on the edge of Chasseneuil-du-Poitou, a short drive north of Poitiers. And it is a theme park built around one idea — the future.

It is worth pausing on how unusual that is. Most theme parks in Europe draw on fairy tales, cartoon characters, or a romantic picture of the past. Futuroscope went the other way. Its subject is image, technology, science, and invention. When it opened in 1987, the park did not promise princesses or pirates. It promised large screens, new forms of cinema, and a glimpse of what might come next.

The idea came from a politician, not an entertainment company. René Monory led the general council of the Vienne department, the local administrative region around Poitiers. In the 1980s, this was a largely rural area of farmland and small towns. Monory wanted something that would bring jobs, visitors, and modern industry here. His answer was bold. Build a park about the future in a field beside the motorway... and see who comes.

People came. Within a few years, Futuroscope became one of the most visited leisure sites in France. That is the first surprise for many visitors. This park was not only a tourist project. It was regional planning. Around it grew a technology district, with research centres, schools, and offices. If you drive in from the main roads, you pass this cluster of buildings before you reach the gates. The park and the technology district were planned to grow together.

Now look again at the architecture, because it is the real signature of this place. Much of the early park was designed by the French architect Denis Laming. His buildings do not try to look friendly or familiar. They look like objects from a science-fiction film. Glass, steel, and white surfaces dominate. Lines run at odd angles. Each building is shaped to suggest what happens inside it, so the outside works like a poster for the show within.

The most photographed example is the building known as the Kinemax. Its walls are made of tall, slanted panels of mirrored glass. They rise in sharp points, like a cluster of rock crystal. On a clear day, the panels hold reflections of the clouds and the water, so the building seems to change from hour to hour. Inside, it was built for large-format cinema. From outside, it is simply one of the strangest silhouettes in rural France.

That focus on the screen explains a lot about the park's history. In its early decades, Futuroscope was known above all for experimental cinema. Visitors sat in front of giant screens, domes, and screens that surrounded them on several sides. Some theatres had seats that moved in time with the film. At the time, many people had never seen anything like it. A trip here felt like a trip to a laboratory of images.

Here is the second surprise. A park about the future has a problem that other parks do not have — the future keeps arriving. Technology that looked astonishing in the late 1980s looks ordinary today. Large screens are now in every living room. So Futuroscope has had to reinvent itself again and again. Over the years, older shows have closed, and new attractions have replaced them, with more physical rides, interactive experiences, and stronger storytelling.

You can feel that change as you walk. Some attractions still lean on the original spirit of film and image. Others are closer to classic thrill rides, with a science or space theme on top. One attraction uses large industrial robot arms to lift and swing riders through a dance-like sequence. Another sends visitors on a ride themed around a mission to Mars. The mix is deliberate. It keeps the park's identity while giving families a reason to come back.

Water is the thread that ties the park together. Pools, channels, and a central lake run between the buildings. They cool the air on hot summer days, and they soften all that glass and steel. When the evening comes, the lake becomes a stage. The park has long staged night-time shows here, using water jets, projections, light, and music. The programme changes over the years, but the setting stays the same — dark water, bright images, and a crowd on the shore.

If you have time, stand still near the water for a few minutes. Watch how the people move. Children run ahead toward the next queue. Grandparents sit on benches in the shade. Teenagers compare which ride made them dizziest. Futuroscope is a family park at heart, and much of its audience comes from across France. Many French adults first came here on a school trip. Now some of them return with children of their own.

The park's location also matters. It sits beside a major motorway and close to a high-speed railway line, with its own station nearby. That access was part of the original plan. Monory and the regional leaders wanted a destination that people from Paris, Bordeaux, and beyond could reach easily. Over time, hotels, restaurants, and other leisure facilities have grown up around the site. Today, the area around the park works almost like a small town built for visitors.

Before you move on, take one more look around you. Notice the reflections in the glass, the sound of water under the music, and the shapes that still look strange after nearly four decades. Think of the farmland that stood here before 1987. Someone looked at those fields and imagined crystals, spheres, and giant screens. Whatever the future really brings, this park is a record of how one region once pictured it... and then decided to build it.
