The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. There is the scrape of gravel underfoot, perhaps a jackdaw calling from the roofline, and the quiet of a small Elbe town. The walls in front of you are pale and solid, with tall Renaissance gables and rows of windows. At first glance this looks like any old Saxon palace. But this place carries a second history, and it is a hard one. Here, in the town of Prettin, now part of Annaburg, the Nazi regime ran one of its earliest concentration camps.

Let us begin with the building itself, because it came first. Lichtenburg was built in the late sixteenth century as a residence for the Electors of Saxony. The Electors were the powerful rulers of this region, princes with a vote in choosing the Holy Roman Emperor. The castle is closely linked to Electress Anna, the wife of Elector August, and she spent time here. Look at the long wings and the courtyard. They were designed for court life, for hunting parties and comfortable living, not for punishment.

That changed long before the Nazis arrived. In the early nineteenth century, the castle was turned into a prison. For roughly a hundred years, the old palace rooms held convicts behind bars and locked doors. The prison closed in the late 1920s, partly because conditions were judged too poor. Remember that detail. A building considered unfit for ordinary prisoners was soon reopened for people whose only crime was their politics.

In 1933, only months after Adolf Hitler became chancellor, the new regime moved fast against its opponents. Communists, Social Democrats, trade unionists and others were arrested in great numbers. The authorities needed space, and they needed it quickly. Empty prisons and old buildings across Germany became so-called early camps. Lichtenburg was one of them. From the summer of 1933, men were brought here and held without trial, under a system called protective custody.

That phrase, protective custody, was a lie in plain sight. It did not protect the prisoners. It removed them from the courts and placed them under the control of the political police and the guards. Here, the guards came first from the SA, the Nazi brownshirt militia, and later from the SS. Survivors described beatings, humiliating drills, and punishment cells. The courtyard you may be standing near was used for roll calls, where men stood in rows for long hours in any weather.

One prisoner held here was Hans Litten, a young Berlin lawyer. Before 1933, Litten had cross-examined Hitler himself in a courtroom, and made him look foolish. The regime never forgave him. He was moved through several camps, and Lichtenburg was one stop on that long road. He died at Dachau in 1938. His story shows how personal the persecution could be. Lawyers, writers and politicians were targeted not by chance, but by name.

Over time, the men's camp at Lichtenburg also held other groups. Jehovah's Witnesses were imprisoned for refusing to give the Nazi salute or to serve the state. Others were labelled as criminals or as so-called asocials, a vague term the regime used against people who did not fit its idea of a useful citizen. Homosexual men were also held in the camps of this period. Each group wore different treatment, and many suffered greatly.

Now comes a detail that surprises many visitors. In 1937, the men were moved out, and Lichtenburg became a concentration camp for women. It was one of the first central women's camps in Nazi Germany. Women arrived from across the country: political opponents, Jehovah's Witnesses, Jewish women, and others the regime wanted to remove from society. Female guards worked here, and some of them later served at larger camps. In this sense, Lichtenburg was a training ground for what came next.

Among the women held here was Lina Haag, a Communist from southern Germany. After the war, she wrote a memoir about her imprisonment and her husband's arrest. Another prisoner was Olga Benário, a German-Jewish Communist who had been handed over to Germany by the Brazilian government. She was later moved on, and she was murdered in 1942. These are only a few names. Many women who passed through these rooms left no written record at all.

In 1939, the women's camp closed, and the prisoners were transferred north to a new, purpose-built camp at Ravensbrück. Ravensbrück became the largest women's concentration camp in the Nazi system. So when you stand here, you are standing at an early chapter of that story. The methods, the guards, and many of the prisoners moved from this castle to a much larger and deadlier place. The road from Lichtenburg ran straight into the war years.

For decades after 1945, the history of the camp was not easy to see here. The castle had other uses, and memory took different shapes in East Germany, where official remembrance focused mainly on Communist resistance. In more recent years, the memorial site was developed with a permanent exhibition. It presents the stories of the men and the women held here, with documents, photographs and personal accounts. The aim is simple: to show who these people were, not only what was done to them.

As you move through the site, take your time. Some of the most powerful places are the smallest. Former cells and plain corridors hold no decoration and need none. Notice the thickness of the walls and the narrow windows. Notice how sound behaves in these rooms, how footsteps return to you. It is worth reading the individual biographies slowly. One life, read carefully, often stays with a visitor longer than any number.

When you step back outside, look again at the castle from a little distance. The gables and the old stone are still elegant. That contrast is the heart of this place. A palace built for princes became a prison, and then a camp, within ordinary sight of the town. The people of Prettin could see these walls every day. That is worth thinking about as you stand here now.

Before you leave, pause for a moment in the courtyard or by the outer wall. Listen again to the gravel, the birds, the distant traffic of the town. These are the same sounds the prisoners may have heard from inside. Then walk on slowly toward the streets of Prettin, and carry the names with you.
