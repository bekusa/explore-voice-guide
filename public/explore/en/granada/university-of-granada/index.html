The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Footsteps on stone, a bicycle bell, two students arguing in Spanish and a third answering in Italian. The University of Granada does not sit behind a single gate. It runs through the city like a long thread, from medieval courtyards near the cathedral to modern glass blocks on the northern edge of town. Wherever you are standing right now, you are probably within a short walk of one of its buildings.

That spread is the first thing worth understanding. This is not a campus in the American sense, with lawns and a fence around it. Faculties live in former hospitals, old convents, a Jesuit college, and purpose-built blocks from the twentieth century. Students cross the old town between classes. Lecture halls share streets with bakeries and tapas bars. In Granada, the university and the city are hard to separate... and that is the point.

The story begins in 1531. The Holy Roman Emperor Charles the Fifth, who was also King of Spain, wanted a centre of learning in the city his grandparents had taken from its last Muslim rulers in 1492. A papal bull, a formal decree from the Pope, confirmed the foundation. The new university had a clear purpose. It was meant to train clergy, lawyers and administrators for a Christian kingdom that had only recently absorbed the Emirate of Granada.

But here is the first surprise. Granada already had a university, of a kind, long before Charles the Fifth. In the fourteenth century, the Nasrid sultans, the dynasty that built the Alhambra, founded a madrasa in the heart of the city. A madrasa is an Islamic college, a place for teaching law, theology and the sciences. Its building still stands, opposite the Royal Chapel, and today it belongs to the university.

That building is called the Palacio de la Madraza. From the street, its painted baroque façade looks entirely Christian. Step inside, though, and you find a small prayer hall from the Nasrid period. It has a mihrab — the niche that points worshippers toward Mecca — and a high, carved ceiling above it. Few visitors walking past to the cathedral realise that this room is one of the oldest teaching spaces in Spain still owned by a university.

Now move north in your mind, toward the Hospital Real. This large building was founded by the Catholic Monarchs, Ferdinand and Isabella, in the early sixteenth century. It was built as a royal hospital to care for the sick. Its plan follows the shape of a cross, with four courtyards set between the arms. The style mixes late Gothic detail with the newer ideas of the Renaissance, which arrived in Spain from Italy in those same decades.

Today the Hospital Real is the seat of the rectorate — the university's central administration — and it houses an important library. That means the heart of a modern institution beats inside a building designed for medicine five centuries ago. Walk through its courtyards and you will notice the quiet. The thick walls hold back the traffic. Arcades throw long bands of shade across the paving. It is a good place to sit for a few minutes.

Back in the centre, the Faculty of Law occupies a former Jesuit college beside the Church of Saints Justo and Pastor. The Jesuits were a Catholic religious order famous for their schools. When the Spanish crown expelled them in the eighteenth century, their buildings passed to other uses, and here the university moved in. Law students now gather on the steps where Jesuit pupils once waited for class.

The second surprise is about who the students are. Granada has long been one of the most popular destinations in Europe for the Erasmus programme, the European Union scheme that lets students spend a term or a year abroad. Tens of thousands of students study here in total, and a large share come from other countries. On any evening in the old town, you will hear a mix of languages at the café tables. The university has given Granada a young, international population that few cities of its size can match.

That student life shapes the city's rhythm. Bars in neighbourhoods near the faculties fill late. Granada is also known for its free tapas tradition, where a small plate arrives with each drink, and students are among its most loyal customers. Bookshops, copy shops and cheap lunch counters cluster near the teaching buildings. Watch the streets in late afternoon and you will see backpacks, folders and laptop bags everywhere.

The modern university has grown far beyond the old town. The Cartuja campus climbs the hills to the north, near a famous Carthusian monastery. The Fuentenueva campus, closer to the centre, holds the sciences. A newer health sciences campus lies to the south. Each area has its own character, but they are linked by buses and by the same steady flow of students moving across the city every day.

Think about the layers here. A Nasrid college from the age of the sultans. A royal hospital from the reign of Ferdinand and Isabella. A Jesuit school from the Counter-Reformation, the period when the Catholic Church reorganised itself after the Protestant split. And glass-fronted laboratories from recent decades. Few universities in Europe let you walk through so many chapters of history between one lecture and the next.

So before you move on, look around you once more. Notice the people with books under their arms, the notices pinned to old wooden doors, the bicycles chained to iron railings. This is a living institution, not a museum. The stones are old, but the conversations are new every morning. As you walk away, listen again for those mixed languages in the street... they are the university, carrying on.
