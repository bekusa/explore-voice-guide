The guide

The story

Step off the narrow lane and the noise of the city drops away. The air inside is cool and slightly dry, with a faint smell of old stone and candle wax. Your footsteps sound softer here. Look up, and pale Gothic ribs fan across the ceiling. Look ahead, and a great screen of gilded iron catches whatever light falls through the high windows. Beyond it lie the people who changed the map of Europe.

This is the Royal Chapel of Granada, the burial place of Isabella of Castile and Ferdinand of Aragon. Most people know them as the Catholic Monarchs. Their marriage joined two Iberian crowns. In 1492 they took Granada, the last Muslim kingdom on the peninsula. In the same year they backed the voyage of Christopher Columbus. They could have chosen Toledo or another old royal city for their tomb. Instead, they chose the city they had conquered.

That choice was deliberate. Granada was their great prize, and they wanted to rest inside it forever. A royal order in 1504 set the project in motion, the same year Isabella died. The architect was Enrique Egas, a master of late Gothic building. Work ran through the following years and was largely complete by about 1517. Ferdinand did not live to see it finished. He died in 1516, and the couple waited elsewhere for their final home.

At first, both bodies lay in a Franciscan convent inside the Alhambra, the palace complex on the hill above you. In 1521 they were carried down into the city and placed here. Picture that procession moving through these streets. The style of the building has a name of its own — Isabelline Gothic. It is late Gothic architecture with a Spanish accent, full of heraldry, twisting carved detail, and royal symbols worked into the stone.

Those symbols are worth hunting for. Watch for two letters, F and Y. The Y stands for Ysabel, the old spelling of Isabella's name. Look also for a yoke and a bundle of arrows. In Spanish, yoke is yugo and arrows are flechas. The yoke begins with Y, for Isabella. The arrows begin with F, for Ferdinand. Each monarch adopted the other's initial. It was a small piece of royal propaganda about a partnership of equals.

Now turn to the screen in front of the altar. This tall iron grille was made by a craftsman known as Master Bartolomé de Jaén. It is wrought iron, painted and gilded, and it rises in tiers of saints, scenes, and coats of arms. Many visitors walk straight past it to reach the tombs. Pause instead. Iron of this scale and fineness was hard to make. Every leaf and figure was shaped by hand, with heat and hammer.

Behind the grille stand two marble monuments, pale and almost glowing. The first, for Isabella and Ferdinand, was carved in Italy by the Florentine sculptor Domenico Fancelli, in white Carrara marble. That marble comes from the same quarry region that gave Michelangelo his stone. The king and queen lie side by side, crowned, hands folded, faces calm. Carved figures of saints and virtues crowd the sides of the base below them.

Look closely at the two heads. Many visitors notice that Isabella's head seems to press more deeply into her marble pillow than Ferdinand's does. Locals love to explain it with a story. Legend has it that the queen's head sinks lower because she did more of the thinking. It is a tale, not history. Still, it says a lot about how Spain remembers her: as the sharper mind of the pair.

The second monument holds their daughter Joanna and her husband, Philip of Habsburg, often called Philip the Handsome. History gave Joanna a harsh nickname — Juana la Loca, Joanna the Mad. After Philip's early death, she was kept away from power for decades, and historians still debate how much of her madness was real. Her tomb was carved by Bartolomé Ordóñez, a Spanish sculptor trained in the Italian manner. Their son became the emperor Charles the Fifth.

Here is the surprise most people miss. The marble figures are not where the bodies lie. Find the small stairway that leads down into the crypt. Below the grand monuments, the royal coffins rest in a low, plain chamber. They are simple lead caskets, dark and unadorned. A child's coffin is among them, for Prince Miguel, a grandson who died very young. Upstairs, the marble shows royal glory. Downstairs, the caskets show only death.

Back upstairs, look past the tombs to the main altarpiece. It is a dense wall of carved and gilded wood, attributed to the sculptor Felipe Bigarny. Near the base, small painted reliefs show scenes from the fall of Granada. In one, the last Muslim ruler hands over the city. In another, Muslims receive Christian baptism. Read those panels carefully. They show the conquest the way the victors wanted it remembered, and many Granadans of Moorish descent paid heavily for it.

The sacristy, now a small museum, holds some of the most personal objects in Spain. Here you can see a crown and sceptre linked to Isabella, and a sword linked to Ferdinand. There is also part of the queen's own painting collection. She loved Flemish art, and you will find works by masters such as Rogier van der Weyden and Hans Memling. These are small panels, rich with colour and detail, painted for private prayer.

One more story belongs here. According to a popular tale, their grandson Charles the Fifth visited and felt the chapel was too modest for such great monarchs. True or not, the next generation did build bigger. The vast cathedral rose right next door, and later kings chose a different burial place near Madrid. That makes this chapel unusual. It is the resting place of one dynasty's beginning, set apart from everything that came after.

When you step back out, pause on the narrow street outside. Notice the carved stone portal, the tight lane, and the smell of incense from the shops nearby. Listen for the cathedral bells and the voices of the old commercial streets around you. Five centuries ago, crowds gathered here to see two monarchs carried home. Today the lane is quieter. The kings and queens are still inside, behind iron and marble, in the city they chose.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with the cathedral right next door.
Keep voices low; this remains a consecrated royal burial place.
Allow extra time for the paintings in the sacristy museum.
Come early in the day for a quieter look at the tombs.
