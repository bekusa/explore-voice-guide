The guide

The story

Listen first to the wind. Up here on the limestone cliff, it comes across open fields and pushes against grey stone walls. Below, out of sight, the small Koporka River runs through a deep green ravine. Under your feet the ground is uneven, worn into hollows by centuries of boots and cartwheels. Before you learn a single date, notice how high you are. This is a place chosen by soldiers who wanted to see everything coming.

You are standing at Koporye Fortress, in the northwest of Russia, west of Saint Petersburg. The coast of the Gulf of Finland lies only a short distance to the north. For hundreds of years this low, flat country was a borderland. Russian, German and Swedish powers all wanted it. The fortress in front of you is the record of that long contest, written in stone instead of ink.

The story begins in the thirteenth century, in the time of the Northern Crusades. These were campaigns by German military religious orders to conquer and convert the lands around the Baltic Sea. Around 1240, knights of the Livonian Order came into this region and built a fortified post near this spot. It was probably made of wood and earth. It did not last long.

The next year, the prince of Novgorod, Alexander Nevsky, came with his army. Today Russians remember him as one of their great medieval heroes, and as a saint of the Orthodox Church. According to the old chronicles, he took the crusaders' stronghold and destroyed it. Here is a detail many visitors miss — this small battle at Koporye came just before his much more famous victory over the knights on frozen Lake Peipus.

After that, the city of Novgorod held this ground. At the end of the thirteenth century, the Novgorodians built a stone fortress here. That was unusual for the time. Most frontier forts in this part of the world were still made of timber. Stone was slow and expensive. Building in stone told everyone that Novgorod planned to stay.

Most of what you see now is younger. In the early sixteenth century, Novgorod had become part of the growing state of Muscovy. The Moscow rulers rebuilt Koporye for a new kind of war — a war of cannons. Look at how thick the walls are, and how the round towers push outward. That shape lets defenders fire along the face of the walls. It was a practical design for the age of gunpowder.

Now walk toward the main gate. This is the part that photographers love, and it is easy to see why. A long bridge on stone supports crosses the ditch and leads to a tall gate tower. In the past, part of the crossing could be raised or removed, so enemies could not simply march in. Stand at the start of the bridge and look up. The tower is designed to make you feel small, and it still works.

But walls cannot stop history. In the late sixteenth century, Swedish forces captured Koporye. It went back to Russia by treaty, then fell to Sweden again. In 1617, a peace treaty gave this whole region to the Swedish crown. For almost ninety years, Koporye was a Swedish fortress. The Russian state had lost its access to the Baltic Sea, and Koporye was one of the places that lost it.

That changed during the Great Northern War, the long conflict between Peter the Great and Sweden. In 1703, Russian troops under Field Marshal Boris Sheremetev besieged Koporye and took it back. The same year, Peter founded his new city at the mouth of the Neva River — the city that became Saint Petersburg. Once the new capital stood on the coast, this old border fortress was no longer on the front line. Its military life came to an end.

And here is the second surprise. Peter gave Koporye and its lands to his closest friend, Alexander Menshikov. Menshikov had risen from humble beginnings to become one of the richest men in the empire. For a time, this fortress was part of a private estate. Later, without soldiers to care for it, it slowly fell into ruin. Rain, frost and local builders looking for free stone did the rest.

Inside the walls, the mood is very different. The courtyard is quiet and grassy. You will find a small Orthodox church here, with a simple white body and a modest dome. It is much later than the fortress, and it gives the place a softer, village character. Nearby, people from the surrounding area come to light candles and to walk. For them this is not only a monument. It is part of home.

This region also has an older human layer. Long before the knights and princes, small Finnic peoples lived here, including the Votes, who spoke a language related to Estonian. Some scholars think the name Koporye may come from a local word, while others link it to a Russian root. Nobody knows for certain. What is certain is that this was always a meeting place of languages and cultures.

Before you leave, walk to the edge of the cliff, where it is safe, and look down into the valley. The river is small, but it cut this deep ravine through the soft limestone. That ravine is the reason the fortress exists. Nature drew the first line of defence, and people only finished the work. Feel the wind again, and look at the fields that run toward the sea.

As you walk back across the bridge, turn once more toward the gate. Look at the patchwork in the stone — older masonry, newer repairs, grass growing from the joints. Every army that passed here left a mark of some kind. Now the only sounds are the wind, the birds, and maybe the voices of other visitors. Take one last look. Then follow the old road down, the same way so many travellers have gone before you.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear sturdy shoes for the uneven ground inside the walls.
Bring a windproof layer; the clifftop is exposed and breezy.
Photograph the gate bridge from the road for full framing.
Visit early or late in the day for softer light.
Stay back from the cliff edge above the river ravine.
