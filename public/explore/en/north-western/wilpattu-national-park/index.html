The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you see anything, you will hear the forest. Peacocks call from somewhere unseen. Insects hum in the dry scrub. The jeep's tyres crunch over pale sand and rust-red earth, and dust hangs in the slanting light. The air smells of warm leaves and, near the water, of mud and grass. This is Wilpattu, and it rewards patience more than speed.

The name gives you the key to the whole landscape. Wilpattu is usually translated as the land of lakes. The lakes here are called villus. They are shallow, natural basins that fill with rainwater, often ringed by bands of white sand and short green grass. They are not reservoirs built by people. As you drive, the forest opens again and again onto these glinting pools. Each one draws animals, so each one is worth a pause.

Wilpattu is the largest national park in Sri Lanka. It stretches across the north-western side of the island, inland from the coast near Puttalam and reaching toward the old royal city of Anuradhapura. It is also one of the oldest protected areas in the country, declared a national park in 1938. That long history of protection matters. Large stretches of this forest have stayed wild for generations, and you can feel the scale as the tracks run on for hour after hour.

The park also carries a more difficult story. During Sri Lanka's long civil conflict, Wilpattu lay close to contested ground. From the late 1980s, the park was closed to visitors for many years. Rangers faced real danger, and the forest went quiet to the outside world. It reopened gradually in the following decades. Today, the calm you feel on these tracks is relatively new. Many local guides remember when no visitors came here at all.

Now look at the forest itself. This is the island's dry zone, where rain arrives in seasons and the land waits in between. The trees are mostly low and tangled, with thorny shrubs and patches of taller hardwoods. In the dry months, leaves fade to olive and gold, and the soil cracks around the edges of the villus. After the rains, everything turns green very quickly. The same track can look like two different parks, depending on the month you come.

Many visitors come hoping for one animal above all — the Sri Lankan leopard. This is a subspecies found only on this island. Wilpattu is known for leopards, but here they are often shy, and the dense forest hides them well. That is part of the thrill. A sighting is never guaranteed. Watch the low branches, the sandy track ahead, and the shade under bushes. A leopard resting in the open can look like a patch of dappled light.

The park also shelters the sloth bear, a shaggy, black, slightly clumsy-looking animal with a pale mark on its chest. Sloth bears feed on termites and fruit, and they use long claws to tear open termite mounds. Keep an eye out for those mounds, which rise like small red towers beside the track. Asian elephants move through the park too, though often in smaller numbers than in some other Sri Lankan reserves. Spotted deer, wild boar, and water buffalo are much easier to find.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. The villus are not all the same. Some hold fresh rainwater, while others near the coast can be salty. That difference changes which plants grow at the edges and which animals gather there. At the water, look for mugger crocodiles lying still in the shallows, and for painted storks, herons, and eagles. The birdlife alone can fill a morning, even on a day when the larger animals stay hidden.

The second surprise is human, and very old. Inside the park area, at a place called Pomparippu, archaeologists have found ancient burial urns. These clay vessels held the remains of people who lived in this region long before written records began here. You will not see a grand monument. But the find is a reminder that this wilderness was once a lived-in landscape. People walked these same sandy plains many centuries before it became a park.

On the coastal edge of Wilpattu stands a red cliff called Kudiramalai Point. Its name is often linked to horses. Legend has it that this coast is connected with the arrival of Prince Vijaya, the figure that Sri Lankan tradition names as the founder of the Sinhalese people, and with Kuveni, a local queen in the same tale. Treat this as story rather than proven history. Still, it shows how deeply this shoreline is woven into the island's memory.

Safaris here run by jeep, and the rhythm is slow. The tracks are sandy and bumpy, and long stretches pass with nothing but forest. Then, suddenly, the driver stops and turns off the engine. In that silence, you hear birds, wind, and perhaps a branch moving. Early morning and late afternoon usually bring the most animal activity and the softest light. The middle of the day can be hot, still, and bright.

A good safari here depends on respect. Stay inside the vehicle, keep your voice low, and let the animals decide the distance. A calm, quiet jeep often sees more than a loud one. Your guide or tracker knows the habits of the park. Ask them what the animals have been doing lately. Many trackers read the sand like a newspaper, pointing out paw prints, drag marks, and fresh droppings that most visitors would pass without noticing.

Before you leave, stop at the edge of one more villu. Look at the line where the sand meets the water. Notice the tracks pressed into the mud, the ripples from a fish or a bird, the way the light lies flat across the surface. Somewhere in the scrub behind you, something may be watching. As the jeep starts again and the dust rises, listen once more to the forest... It keeps going long after you have gone.

What to look for

Mugger crocodiles lying motionless in shallow water.
Paw prints pressed into the sandy track surface.
Painted storks wading at the edges of the lakes.
Before you go

Practical tips

Book an early morning safari for cooler air and activity.
Bring binoculars for distant birds and shy leopards.
Carry water, a hat, and sun protection.
Wear dull-coloured clothes and keep voices low.
Ask your tracker to point out fresh animal prints.
