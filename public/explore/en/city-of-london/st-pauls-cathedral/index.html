St. Paul's Cathedral

The story

Rising above the rooftops of the old City, a vast grey dome catches the eye from bridges and streets all across central London. Beneath it stands St Paul's Cathedral, the masterwork of one architect, raised from the ashes of disaster, and for centuries the stage on which the nation has marked its grandest and saddest occasions.

The cathedral you see was born out of fire. The Great Fire of 1666 destroyed the medieval church that stood here, and the task of replacing it fell to Sir Christopher Wren. His new cathedral rose slowly over the following decades and was largely complete by 1710, a confident design in the English Baroque style with the dome as its crown.

That dome is the heart of the achievement. One of the largest in the world, it is really a clever set of shells, one inside another, so that the soaring shape outside and the painted ceiling inside both work without one ruining the other. Wren solved a problem that had defeated many builders, and the result still defines the skyline.

Inside, the scale is calm rather than overwhelming. Pale stone, long sightlines, and the great space beneath the dome draw the eye upward to paintings and mosaics. It is a working cathedral, with daily services and music, so you may well arrive to find a choir rehearsing or an organ filling the building with sound.

The climb is the experience many remember most. A long series of steps leads first to the Whispering Gallery, a circular walkway inside the dome where a word murmured against the wall can be heard on the far side. Higher still are outdoor galleries that ring the dome and give some of the finest views over London.

The cathedral has stood at the centre of national life. It has held the funerals of admired figures such as the admiral Nelson and the duke of Wellington, and more recently of a wartime prime minister, as well as great services of celebration. To sit inside is to sit where the country has gathered to mourn and to mark its turning points.

Its survival through the Second World War became a symbol in itself. During the bombing of London, while fires raged around it, the dome stood firm, and a photograph of it wreathed in smoke but intact lifted spirits across the country. Volunteers who fought to save it are part of the cathedral's modern story.

Down in the crypt, beneath the main floor, lie the tombs and memorials of many notable figures, including the architect Wren himself. A plain inscription there invites visitors who seek his monument simply to look around them at the building, which remains the truest memorial he could have.

The setting has changed around it. For centuries the dome towered over everything; now glass office buildings rise nearby, and rules still protect certain distant views of it across the city. The contrast between the old cathedral and the new towers is part of how the City of London looks today.

A few practical notes. Entry for sightseeing is ticketed, and the same ticket covers the dome climb and the crypt; attending a service is free, and a quieter, more moving way to experience the space. Booking online ahead of time usually saves both money and waiting.

It sits at the top of Ludgate Hill, linked to the river by a footbridge that lines up neatly with the dome from the south bank. That alignment makes for one of the best approaches on foot, walking across the river with the cathedral growing larger ahead of you.

Music is central to the life of the cathedral. A choir has sung here for centuries, and evensong, a sung service in the late afternoon, lets you hear that tradition in its proper setting without a sightseeing ticket. For many visitors it is the most moving way to experience the building.

The cathedral also looks outward to the city it serves. Its steps and the space around it have seen protests, celebrations, and quiet remembrance, and the building has come to stand for London's endurance through hard times. It is a working church and a public symbol at once.

Before you leave, stand under the centre of the dome and look straight up. One man imagined this space rising from a burnt city, and it has watched over London for three centuries since. When you step back out onto the hill and the modern street returns, the dome stays with you, visible again from the next bridge along the river.

Key facts

Designed by Sir Christopher Wren after the 1666 fire.
Largely completed in 1710 in English Baroque style.
Its dome is among the largest in the world.
Site of the funerals of Nelson and Wellington.
Its dome survived the wartime bombing of London.

What to look for

The great dome rising over the City.
The Whispering Gallery inside the dome.
Wren's tomb and inscription in the crypt.
Mosaics and paintings high above the floor.

Practical tips

Book sightseeing tickets online to save money.
Attend a service to enter free and quietly.
Climb to the galleries for wide city views.
Approach on foot across the river footbridge.
