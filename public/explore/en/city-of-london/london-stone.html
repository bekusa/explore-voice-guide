The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Buses grind past on Cannon Street. Footsteps hurry towards the station. Somewhere a delivery van reverses with a steady beep. And here, set low into the front of an office building, behind a pane of glass, sits a battered lump of pale stone. Most people walk straight past it. Some glance down, see a rock in a box, and keep going. But you have stopped, and that already puts you in good company with centuries of curious Londoners.

This is London Stone. It is not tall. It is not carved with faces or inscriptions. It is roughly the size of a small travel case, worn smooth in places and chipped in others. Its surface is a creamy limestone, with small rounded grains that you can sometimes make out if you lean close to the glass. Whatever it once was, it is now a fragment of something larger. And nobody can say for certain what that something was.

That uncertainty is the heart of the story. The stone appears in written records from around the year 1100, in a list of London properties that mentions a place called London Stone. So it was already old and already a landmark nine hundred years ago. One of the earliest figures in the city's civic history, Henry Fitz-Ailwin, the first Mayor of London in the late twelfth century, is described in records as Henry son of Ailwin of London Stone. He apparently lived nearby, and the stone was enough to identify his address.

So what was it? Historians and archaeologists have offered several theories. One popular idea is that the stone was Roman. The Romans founded Londinium on this ground, and the remains of a large Roman building were found close by, under and around today's Cannon Street station. Some think the stone was part of that complex. Others suggest it was a Roman marker, perhaps the point from which road distances in the province were measured. These are educated guesses... not proven facts.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. The stone did not always stand where you see it now. For most of its recorded life, it stood on the other side of the street, on the south side of what was then a narrow medieval lane. In 1598, the Elizabethan historian John Stow described it as fixed deep in the ground and fastened with bars of iron. He noted that carts sometimes struck it. In his day it was already a puzzle, and already protected.

In 1450, the stone had its most dramatic moment. A rebel leader named Jack Cade marched on London with an army of discontented men from Kent. According to the chronicles, he entered the city and struck London Stone with his sword, declaring himself lord of the city. The gesture suggests that, by the fifteenth century, the stone was seen as a symbol of the city's authority itself. Touching it meant claiming London.

Shakespeare liked the scene enough to put it on stage. In his play Henry the Sixth, Part Two, Cade sits on London Stone and announces that he is now lord of the city. So this unassuming rock became a theatre prop in one of the most performed playwrights in the English language. Cade's rebellion, by the way, did not end well for him. He was captured and killed within weeks.

Now for the legends — and it is worth being careful here. A well-known tale links the stone to Brutus of Troy, a mythical founder of Britain from medieval storytelling. Legend has it that the stone was brought by Brutus, and a saying claims that so long as the stone of Brutus is safe, so long shall London flourish. It sounds ancient. In fact, that saying appears to have surfaced only in the nineteenth century. It is a romantic invention, not a medieval prophecy.

The stone's later life was quieter but full of movement. In the eighteenth century, as traffic grew and the street was widened, it was moved to the north side of the road. Eventually it was set into the south wall of the Church of Saint Swithin, which stood on this very spot. For generations, Londoners passed it in a niche on the church front, protected by an iron grille. William Blake, the poet and painter, mentioned London Stone in his long visionary poem Jerusalem.

Then came the Second World War. During the Blitz, the German bombing of London, Saint Swithin's church was gutted by fire. The church was never rebuilt. Yet the stone survived in the ruins, intact as ever. After the war, an office building rose on the site, and the stone was placed in a recess in its street front. For decades it sat at ankle height, behind a grille, beside shop windows. It was easy to miss, and many people did.

A second surprise: the stone recently went away for a while. When the building around it was redeveloped in the 2010s, the stone was taken to the Museum of London for safekeeping and displayed there. In 2018 it returned to Cannon Street, to the setting you see now. The new surround is cleaner and better lit. A short inscription explains what you are looking at. The stone is also a listed structure, which gives it legal protection as part of the nation's heritage.

Take a moment to think about scale. Around you are glass towers, a railway terminus, and some of the most expensive offices in the world. And in the middle of it all is a stone that was already a puzzle when the Normans ruled England. The City of London has burned, been rebuilt, been bombed, and been rebuilt again. Streets have moved. Churches have vanished. This one fragment has stayed within a few steps of where it was first recorded.

Before you walk on, look at the stone once more. Notice the rough top, the smoother edges, the marks of old damage. Then lift your eyes to the street, the station entrance, the stream of people passing. Very few of them know that a rebel once struck this rock with a sword, or that a mayor took his name from it. As you move on, you carry that small secret with you — a piece of the city that has outlasted almost everything around it.
