The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. The wind moves through the aspen leaves with a dry, papery rattle, like light rain that never quite arrives. Somewhere out in the grass, something large is breathing. You may smell it before you see it: warm earth, crushed sage, and the heavy animal scent of bison. Welcome to Elk Island National Park, a short drive east of Edmonton, where some of the most important animals in North America walk past your car window.

First, a small puzzle. This is not an island, and there is no sea anywhere near. The name comes from an older idea. When the park began, it was a fenced refuge in a wide sea of farmland that was spreading across the prairie. Inside the fence, the animals were safe. Outside, they were not. So in a sense, it really was an island... an island of wild land, surrounded by ploughed fields and new settlements.

The story starts in the first years of the twentieth century. By then, the great herds of the prairies were almost gone. Elk had been hunted hard across this region. In 1906, a group of local residents pushed the federal government to set aside land to protect a small surviving herd of elk. A fence went up around it. That fenced reserve became one of the earliest federal wildlife sanctuaries in Canada, and later it grew into the national park around you.

Then came an unexpected chapter. In 1907, the Canadian government bought a large herd of plains bison from ranchers in Montana. These were some of the last big groups of plains bison anywhere on the continent. The animals travelled north by train. Many of them were kept here for a time, while a larger bison park was prepared further east. When the round-up came, not every bison could be caught. A few dozen stayed behind in the aspen woods. Their descendants are the bison you may see today.

Here is something many visitors miss. The park actually holds two different kinds of bison, and they are kept apart. Plains bison live north of the Yellowhead Highway, the main road that cuts across the park. Wood bison live to the south. Wood bison are larger and darker, with a taller, squarer hump over the shoulders. Plains bison have a rounder shape and a thick woolly cape of fur. Keeping the herds separate protects each one from mixing with the other.

And this quiet park has an influence far beyond its fence. For decades, bison raised here have been moved to start or strengthen herds in other places. Elk Island animals have gone to other parks in Canada, to the United States, and even to Russia. When bison returned to Banff National Park in 2017, the founding animals came from here. In a way, this small corner of Alberta has become a nursery for a species that once came very close to disappearing.

Now look at the land itself. The ground is not flat. It rolls in small hills and hollows, one after another. Geologists call this knob-and-kettle terrain. At the end of the last ice age, melting glaciers left behind mounds of gravel and blocks of buried ice. When the ice melted, the ground sank and formed hollows. Many of those hollows filled with water. That is why the park is scattered with ponds, marshes, and small lakes, each one a different shade of blue or green.

This landscape is called aspen parkland. It sits between the open prairie to the south and the great northern forest. Trembling aspen dominates, with its pale, almost white bark. Look closely at the trunks and you may see dark scars and marks low on the bark. Some come from elk and moose feeding in winter. Others come from bison rubbing against the trees. Between the groves, meadows of grass and wildflowers open up, and in the wet places, cattails and sedges crowd the shoreline.

The ponds belong to another engineer: the beaver. Beavers are everywhere here, and they shape the park as much as any glacier did. Watch for their lodges, low domes of sticks and mud rising from the water. Look for gnawed stumps with pointed tops along the shore. Their dams hold back water, which keeps wetlands alive in dry years. Those wetlands then support ducks, grebes, herons, and many other birds. In early morning, the water can be loud with their calls.

If you head toward Astotin Lake, the largest lake in the park, you reach its main gathering place. There are picnic areas, a beach, a campground, and the start of several walking trails. The lake is shallow and dotted with small islands. On calm evenings, the surface becomes almost perfectly still, and the reflections of the aspen line the edge. Nearby, you can also find a reconstructed Ukrainian pioneer home, a reminder of the settlers from Eastern Europe who farmed the land around this region.

The park is also part of something larger. It sits inside the Beaver Hills, a wider area of rolling, wooded country that has been recognised by UNESCO as a biosphere region. That means local communities, farmers, and conservation groups work together to protect the landscape while people continue to live and work in it. The area is also a dark sky preserve. Lights are kept low, so on a clear night the sky fills with stars, and sometimes with the green ribbons of the northern lights.

A word about safety, because it matters here more than in most parks. Bison look calm, even sleepy. They are not. An adult can weigh as much as a small car and can run faster than any person. If bison are on the road, stay in your vehicle and let them pass. Give them time. Never walk toward them for a photograph. The same is true of moose, especially mothers with calves in spring. Here, the animals have the right of way.

And so, wherever you are standing or sitting right now, take one more look. Notice the fence line if you can see it, the thin border that made all of this possible. Notice the white aspen trunks, the still water, the dark shapes in the grass. As you leave, listen again for that papery rattle of leaves. It is the same sound the first bison here would have heard, more than a century ago, when this small island of wild land first began.

Before you go

Practical tips

Drive slowly at dawn or dusk for the best wildlife viewing.
Stay inside your vehicle whenever bison are near the road.
Bring binoculars to watch beavers and waterbirds from shore.
Stay after dark on clear nights for stargazing.
Carry insect repellent in summer near ponds and marshes.
