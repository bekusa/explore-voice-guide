The guide

The story

Feel the ground under your shoes. It is dry, pale earth, packed hard and dusted with fine grit that the wind lifts and drops again. Around you, the plain of northern Afghanistan runs flat toward the horizon. In front of you rises a low, worn hill, and nothing here suggests what came out of it. This is Tillya Tepe. The name means Golden Hill in the local languages. For most of its long life, that name was just a name. Then, in the late 1970s, archaeologists opened the ground here. They found a burial ground so rich in gold that the name became a description.

The mound sits just outside the town of Sheberghan, in Jowzjan Province. This region was once part of ancient Bactria. Bactria was the fertile land between the Hindu Kush mountains and the Amu Darya river, which the Greeks called the Oxus. Armies, merchants and nomads crossed it for centuries. Persian, Greek, Indian and steppe cultures all left traces here. Keep that crossroads in mind, because the hill under your feet holds all of them at once.

The story begins in 1978. A joint Soviet and Afghan expedition, led by the archaeologist Viktor Sarianidi, was working on the mound. They first came for something older. The hill held the ruins of a fortified Iron Age temple, far older than the graves. But in the upper layers, cut into those ancient walls, the team found something nobody expected. They found graves.

There were six of them. Five held women. One held a man. They were buried roughly two thousand years ago, around the turn from the first century before Christ to the first century of the Common Era. They seem to have been nomads of high status. Scholars still debate exactly who they were. Some link them to the Yuezhi, a people who moved west from the borders of China. Others point to the Saka, horse-riding nomads of the steppe.

What the team lifted from the earth is now known as the Bactrian Gold. Here is the first surprise. It was not a handful of treasures. It was more than twenty thousand individual objects. Many were tiny gold plaques and spangles sewn onto clothing. When these people were laid to rest, their garments must have shimmered in the lamplight. Their burial cloth would have carried thousands of small points of light.

The individual pieces are just as remarkable as the quantity. One woman wore a crown of thin gold sheet, decorated with trees and birds. It could be taken apart and folded flat. That is exactly what a nomad on the move would need. Another piece shows a winged female figure, often compared to Aphrodite, the Greek goddess of love. There are pendants showing a figure holding two dragon-like creatures. There are clasps, daggers and inlays of turquoise and other coloured stones.

Now the second surprise. These graves held the whole ancient world in miniature. Excavators found a Chinese bronze mirror. They found a gold coin of the Roman emperor Tiberius, minted thousands of kilometres to the west. They found objects in Indian, Greek and steppe styles. Standing here, on a plain in northern Afghanistan, you are standing where all those roads met. One small graveyard gathered treasures from China to Rome.

The excavation ended suddenly. In late 1979, war came to Afghanistan, and work on the hill stopped. Sarianidi believed there were more graves still in the ground. Reports suggest that at least one further burial was later dug up by looters. Its contents disappeared. That is the quiet sadness of this place. Part of the story was taken away before anyone could record it.

The gold that was recorded had its own dramatic journey. For years it was feared lost, melted down or sold during Afghanistan's long conflicts. In fact, in the late 1980s, the collection was hidden in a bank vault in Kabul. A small group of trusted keepers protected it. They kept the secret through years of war and changing governments. In 2003, the vault was opened. The treasure was still there, and largely intact.

After that, the Bactrian Gold travelled the world in a touring exhibition, seen in museums across Europe, North America and Asia. Millions of people saw the folding crown and the winged goddess. Very few of them ever stood where you are standing now. The objects belong to the National Museum of Afghanistan in Kabul. That is the important thing to understand about this visit. The gold is not here. Only the hill remains.

So look at the hill itself. It is low and rounded, softened by wind and time. In places you may see uneven ground and old cuts in the earth. These are traces of excavation, and sadly, of later digging by looters. Listen for the wind across the open land, and perhaps a voice or a vehicle from the direction of town. Notice how ordinary it all seems. That ordinariness is part of why the graves stayed hidden for two thousand years.

Think, for a moment, about the people who chose this spot. They buried their dead in the ruins of a temple that was already ancient to them. Perhaps the hill was a landmark on the plain. Perhaps it felt sacred. We do not know. We only know that they laid their noblest dead here, dressed in gold, with mirrors and coins from distant empires beside them.

Before you walk away, turn slowly and take in the whole horizon. The same flat light fell on the mourners who stood here long ago. When you think of the Bactrian Gold now, behind glass in a museum, remember this quiet mound of pale earth. Remember the wind and the dust on your shoes. This is where the treasure slept, and this hill still keeps part of the story.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current security advice before travelling to Jowzjan Province.
Hire a local guide familiar with the site and area.
Bring water and sun protection; the plain offers little shade.
See the actual gold at Kabul's National Museum of Afghanistan.
Respect the mound; never dig or remove anything.
