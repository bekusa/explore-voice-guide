The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Wind moves through dry grass on the hilltop. Far below, the Răut River curls slowly through its valley, and somewhere a dog barks in a village courtyard. Under your feet is pale limestone, worn smooth in places by many generations of footsteps. This is Old Orhei, about an hour's drive north of Chișinău, in the heart of Moldova. Before we talk about monks, khans or princes, take in the shape of the land. Here the landscape tells the first part of the story.

Look at the way the river bends. The Răut has cut a deep, looping gorge into the limestone plateau, almost doubling back on itself. That loop creates a long, narrow promontory with steep cliffs on several sides. For people in the past, a site like this was a gift. The river supplied water and fish. The cliffs gave natural protection. And from the top, you could see anyone approaching across the valley long before they arrived.

Because of that, people have lived here for a very long time. Archaeologists have found traces of settlement from many different eras across this valley. Among the oldest major remains are earthworks and fortifications linked to the Geto-Dacians, the Thracian peoples who lived in this region long before the Roman period. What you see today is not one monument. It is a layered landscape, where one age sits on top of another, sometimes only a few spades deep.

Now the first surprise. In the fourteenth century, this quiet valley held a city of the Golden Horde, the Mongol-led state that controlled much of the steppe from the Black Sea eastward. The city is known from coins and records as Shehr al-Jedid, which means simply "New City". Coins were minted here. Excavations have uncovered the foundations of a mosque, a caravanserai, and bathhouses with heating systems. Imagine it: a steppe trading town with domes and baths, here on the edge of today's Moldova.

That city did not last long. As the power of the Golden Horde weakened later in the fourteenth century, the settlement declined. The site was taken up again by the young Principality of Moldavia, the medieval state that stretched across today's Moldova and northeastern Romania. A Moldavian fortress town grew here, and it was an important stronghold in the region. Over time, however, the centre of life moved away, and the name Orhei passed to the modern town a short distance upstream.

Now turn to the cliff face itself. If you look closely along the limestone walls, you can see dark openings — small caves and hollows cut into the rock. Many of these were made or enlarged by monks. For centuries, hermits and small monastic communities chose this gorge for prayer and isolation. The soft limestone was easy to carve, and the cliffs kept them apart from the world. Some of these cells can only be seen from below; others are lost or have collapsed.

The best known of these spaces is the cave monastery, which you can still enter today. A narrow passage leads into the rock. Inside, the air is noticeably cooler, and the light drops to a soft glow from candles and small windows. There is a chapel cut directly into the stone, with icons on the walls and a low ceiling above. Go slowly here. This is an active place of worship, not a museum. Speak quietly, and if a service is taking place, give it space.

Here is the second surprise. The monastery fell silent for a long period during the Soviet era, when religious life across the region was heavily restricted. After Moldova became independent in the early 1990s, monastic life slowly returned to the cave. So the candles you see burning are part of a living tradition that came back within recent memory. A tradition that some people thought had ended is active again inside this cliff.

From the cave, a terrace opens onto the gorge. Step out and look down. The river lies far below, with fields and trees following its curve. This balcony in the rock is one of the most memorable views in Moldova. Notice how the cliff sits between sky and valley, with very little in between. You can understand why monks felt that this place was close to heaven and far from the noise of ordinary life.

Above the cave, on top of the ridge, stand a stone bell tower and a white church with blue details, built in the early twentieth century. Near them, a carved stone cross marks the hilltop. These buildings are much younger than the caves beneath them, but together they form the picture most visitors remember: a church on the summit and a sanctuary hidden in the rock below. The contrast is worth noticing. One faith expressed in two directions — upward into the sky, and inward into the stone.

When you leave the ridge, walk into the village of Butuceni, at the foot of the hill. Here the human side of Old Orhei feels close and ordinary. Look for houses with painted window frames, wooden gates, and walls in soft blues and whites. Grapevines climb over courtyards. Some families welcome guests for homemade food, local wine, and simple rooms. Stone was taken from the cliffs for many of these buildings, so the village and the hill share the same material.

Old Orhei is protected as a national archaeological and natural complex, and it has been placed on UNESCO's tentative list, the step before possible World Heritage status. Much of the valley has not been fully excavated. That means the ground under the meadows may still hold walls, coins and graves that no one has yet seen. For archaeologists, the site is unfinished work. For you, it means the quiet fields are not empty. They are simply waiting.

A few practical thoughts as you explore. The paths on the ridge are uneven and can be slippery after rain, so sturdy shoes help. There is little shade on top in summer. Inside the monastery, modest dress is expected, and women are usually asked to cover their heads. Early morning and late afternoon bring softer light on the limestone and fewer groups on the narrow paths.

Before you go, look once more at the curve of the river. Thracian builders, Golden Horde merchants, Moldavian soldiers and generations of monks all looked at this same bend. The water still moves slowly between the cliffs. The bell tower still stands against the sky. As you walk back down, listen again for the wind in the grass and the sounds from the village below. These are the same quiet sounds that drew people here in the first place.
