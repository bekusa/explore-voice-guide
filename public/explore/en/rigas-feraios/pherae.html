The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Somewhere close, water is running. It is cool, steady and patient. In the modern town of Velestino, on the edge of the wide Thessalian plain, springs still rise from the ground. They rise where an ancient city once stood. That city was Pherae, and for a few dramatic decades it was one of the most powerful places in all of Greece.

Look out across the land around you. To one side, the flat fields of Thessaly stretch towards the horizon. To the other, the hills rise towards Mount Pelion and the Gulf of Volos. Pherae sat on the hinge between these two worlds. It had rich farmland behind it and a route to the sea in front of it. In ancient times, its harbour was the nearby town of Pagasae, on the gulf. That position, between plain and sea, explains much of the story you are about to hear.

The water you can hear has its own long memory. Ancient writers tell of a famous spring here called Hypereia. It appears in Homer's epics, which are among the oldest poems in European literature. Many scholars link it with the springs that still feed Velestino today. So when you hear that water, you may be hearing the same source that Homer's listeners knew by name, nearly three thousand years ago.

Homer also gives Pherae a place in the war against Troy. In his long list of the Greek forces, he names a leader called Eumelus, who came from this region. Homer praises his horses above all others in the Greek army. That detail fits the landscape. The Thessalian plain was horse country, famous across Greece for its riders and its cavalry. Imagine those fields filled with grazing herds instead of tractors.

Now for the oldest story of all, and this one is myth, not history. Legend says that Pherae was ruled by a king named Admetus. The god Apollo, as a punishment from Zeus, had to work for a year as a mortal herdsman. He chose to serve Admetus, and he found him a kind and generous master. In gratitude, Apollo won a strange gift for the king. When death came for Admetus, someone else could die in his place.

Here the tale turns dark. The king's parents refused. His friends refused. Only his wife, Alcestis, agreed to give her life for him. The playwright Euripides turned this story into a tragedy, which is still performed today. In the play, the hero Heracles arrives as a guest, learns the truth, and wrestles Death itself to bring Alcestis back. So this quiet town is the setting of one of the great love stories of ancient theatre.

From myth, we move to solid history — and to a man who nearly changed the shape of Greece. In the fourth century before Christ, Pherae was ruled by a tyrant called Jason. In ancient Greece, a tyrant simply meant a ruler who took power outside the usual rules. Jason built a large mercenary army. He brought most of Thessaly under his control, and other Greek cities watched him with real fear.

Ancient historians describe Jason as clever, energetic and very ambitious. Some believed he planned to lead the Greeks against the Persian Empire. That is the same dream that Alexander the Great would later achieve. But Jason never had the chance. Around 370 BC, he was assassinated. Here is the surprise that most visitors miss: for a short time, this small Thessalian city looked like the future centre of the Greek world.

The rulers who came after Jason were far less admired. The most notorious was Alexander of Pherae. Ancient writers remember him as cruel and violent. He fought for years against the city of Thebes. In one battle, the famous Theban general Pelopidas died while fighting him. In the end, Alexander was killed inside his own palace, and ancient sources say his wife's family organised the murder. Power at Pherae was dazzling, but it was rarely safe.

What survives of all this today? Archaeologists have uncovered remains of the ancient city within and around the modern town. The most important site is a sanctuary of Zeus, with foundations of a large temple. Finds from Pherae include pottery, metalwork and small offerings. Many of these objects are now kept in regional museums, including the museum in nearby Volos. Do not expect towering columns. Expect low stone foundations, quiet fenced areas and the outline of a city beneath the streets.

The story does not end in antiquity. Many centuries later, in 1757, a boy was born here who became one of the heroes of modern Greece. He is known as Rigas Feraios — his name means Rigas of Pherae. He was a writer and revolutionary. He dreamed of freeing the Balkan peoples from Ottoman rule. In 1798, he was executed in Belgrade. Today the whole municipality carries his name, and you will see his image around the town.

This region also saw fighting in more recent times. During the Greek-Turkish war of 1897, battles took place around Velestino, on the same strategic ground between plain and sea. Once again, the position that made ancient Pherae powerful also made it a place where armies met. Geography here has shaped history again and again, across almost three thousand years.

As you walk on, let your eyes move slowly. Notice the old stones reused in later walls. Notice the plane trees that grow where the water runs, and the cool shade they create. Notice how the plain opens out to the west. Think of Apollo with his herds, of Jason and his ambitions, of a young Rigas walking these same lanes. And listen, one more time, for the springs. They were here before every ruler of Pherae... and they are still here now.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the springs in summer for shade and cooler air.
Combine your visit with the archaeological museum in Volos.
Read Euripides' Alcestis beforehand to bring the myth alive.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground around excavated areas.
Ask locals for directions to the Zeus sanctuary remains.
