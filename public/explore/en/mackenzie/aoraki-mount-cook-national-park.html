The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Wind moves across the tussock, a dry rustle like paper. Somewhere up the valley, ice cracks with a low, heavy boom. The air is cold and clean, and it carries a faint mineral smell, like wet stone. Above you, a white summit rises out of grey rock. This is Aoraki, the highest mountain in New Zealand, and you are standing in the park that carries its name.

Before it had an English name, this mountain had a story. For Ngāi Tahu, the main Māori tribe of the South Island, Aoraki is an ancestor. According to their tradition, Aoraki was a son of Rakinui, the Sky Father. He and his brothers were sailing a great canoe when it ran aground and tilted. The brothers climbed onto the high side, and the cold south wind turned them to stone. The canoe became the South Island. Aoraki, the tallest brother, became the tallest peak.

The English name came much later. In 1851, a British naval officer named the mountain after Captain James Cook, the explorer who had charted these coasts nearly a century before. For a long time, that was the only official name. Then, in 1998, a settlement between the Crown and Ngāi Tahu changed it. The mountain's title became Aoraki forward slash Mount Cook, with the Māori name placed first. As part of that settlement, the peak was returned to Ngāi Tahu, who then gifted it back to the people of New Zealand.

Here is something many visitors miss. The mountain is getting shorter. In 1991, a huge rockslide broke away from the summit ridge. Millions of tonnes of rock and ice fell down the east face, and the peak lost roughly ten metres of height in a single night. Later surveys showed that the icy cap had kept wearing away. Today the official height is 3,724 metres. The mountain you see now is not quite the mountain your grandparents saw.

The park itself was created in 1953. It is a long, narrow world of rock, ice, and braided rivers. Glaciers cover a large share of the land, and many of the country's highest peaks stand inside its borders. In 1990, the park became part of a larger World Heritage Area in the southwest of the South Island. That status recognises its landscapes and its living story of ice shaping land.

The longest glacier in New Zealand runs down the valley just east of the village. This is the Tasman Glacier. For much of its lower length, it does not look white at all. It is covered in grey rubble, rock that has fallen from the slopes above and ridden down on the ice. At its end lies Tasman Lake, a body of water that did not exist a few decades ago. It began to form in the 1970s, as the glacier melted back, and it has grown quickly since.

Now look at the colour of the water, here or on the drive in past Lake Pukaki. That milky turquoise is not a trick of the light. Glaciers grind rock into a powder so fine it stays suspended in the water. Locals call it rock flour. Sunlight scatters off those tiny particles, and the lakes glow a pale, chalky blue. On a cloudy day the colour softens. In bright sun it can look almost unreal.

The most popular walk here is the Hooker Valley Track. It is fairly flat and follows the Hooker River up toward the mountain. Along the way you cross three swing bridges. They sway a little underfoot, and the river runs fast and grey below. At the end, you reach Hooker Lake. On many days, small icebergs float on its surface, broken off the glacier at the far shore. And on a clear day, Aoraki stands directly ahead, framed by the valley walls.

If you want height, there is a harder option. The track to Sealy Tarns climbs more than two thousand steps up the slope above the village. It is steep and tiring. But at the top, small alpine pools sit on a ledge, and on still days they hold a reflection of the peaks across the valley. From up there, you can often hear avalanches on the far side long before you see the snow move.

The mountain has drawn climbers for well over a century. The first recorded ascent was made on Christmas Day in 1894, by three New Zealanders: Tom Fyfe, Jack Clarke, and George Graham. Decades later, a young beekeeper from Auckland trained on these slopes. His name was Edmund Hillary. He went on to stand on the summit of Everest in 1953. Today, the alpine centre in the village tells his story, and a statue of him looks toward the peak.

Keep your eyes on the ground and the sky, too. In summer, look for the Mount Cook lily, a large white flower that is in fact a buttercup, the biggest of its kind. Above, you may hear a loud, rolling call. That is the kea, a green alpine parrot with orange under its wings. Kea are clever and curious. They will inspect a backpack or a car with real interest. Please do not feed them. Human food harms them.

When the sun goes down, the park changes again. This area sits inside an International Dark Sky Reserve, one of the largest in the world. There is very little artificial light for many kilometres. On a clear night, the Milky Way stretches overhead in a pale band, and the stars of the Southern Hemisphere fill the sky. Look for the Southern Cross, and for two faint, fuzzy clouds of light. Those are neighbouring galaxies, visible only from the southern half of the world.

The weather here moves fast. Moist air comes off the Tasman Sea to the west and hits these mountains first. A clear morning can turn to rain and low cloud within an hour. Many visitors arrive and never see the summit at all. Ngāi Tahu tradition has a gentle phrase for this. The cloud is said to be Aoraki covering himself. If the peak is hidden today, you are not unlucky. You are simply seeing the mountain as it often is.

So take one more look before you go. Notice the grey ice in the valley, the pale water, the line where the snow meets the rock. Listen again for the wind in the tussock, and perhaps a far-off crack from a glacier. The ice is moving, slowly, even now. The mountain is changing, slowly, even now. And for a short time, you are part of this place too.

Before you go

Practical tips

Start the Hooker Valley Track early for calmer air.
Pack warm layers and rain gear even in summer.
Return after dark for stargazing in the dark sky reserve.
Keep food sealed and never feed the kea.
Allow a flexible day in case cloud hides the summit.
