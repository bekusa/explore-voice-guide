The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the trees, the gravel shifts underfoot, and somewhere below the terraces the Dordogne moves slowly through its valley. The stone here is the pale, honey-coloured limestone of the Périgord. In the afternoon it takes on a warm gold that seems to hold the heat of the day. Above you rise steep slate roofs, slender turrets, and windows framed in carved stone. This is the Château des Milandes, and it has two stories to tell. One belongs to a medieval family. The other belongs to one of the most remarkable women of the twentieth century.

Start with the first story. At the end of the fifteenth century, this part of France belonged to powerful local lords. The Caumont family held the fortress of Castelnaud, a hard, defensive castle built for war. The usual account says that François de Caumont built Les Milandes for his wife, Claude de Cardaillac. She wanted a more comfortable home than the grim walls of Castelnaud. So instead of thick ramparts and arrow slits, this house was built for living.

You can see that intention in the architecture. The château sits at the meeting point of two styles. Late Gothic shows in the pointed details, the gargoyles, and the vertical lines of the towers. Early Renaissance, the new taste for elegance and comfort arriving from Italy, shows in the larger windows and the decorative stonework. It is a house that still looks like a castle, but it no longer needs to fight. That shift is the story of a whole century in a single building.

Now look more closely at the carvings and the coloured glass. Much of what you see today is not original to the fifteenth century. In the early twentieth century, a wealthy industrialist bought the property and restored it heavily. He added stained glass, decorative stonework, and modern comforts. Some purists frown at this kind of restoration. But it tells you something useful. Every owner of an old house reshapes it, and this château carries the marks of many hands.

And then comes the second story... the one most visitors really come to hear. In the 1930s, a young American performer discovered this valley. Her name was Josephine Baker. She was born into poverty in Saint Louis, Missouri, and she left the United States as a teenager. In Paris she became a sensation of the music hall, famous for her dancing, her humour, and her daring stage costumes. France gave her a freedom that America, with its racial segregation, had denied her.

Baker first rented Les Milandes in the late 1930s and later bought it. For her, this was not simply a country retreat. It became a stage for her ideals. Imagine the contrast for a moment. A woman born in a poor neighbourhood of a segregated American city now owned a Renaissance château in rural France. She called it her home, and she filled it with music, guests, and ambition.

Before that dream fully took shape, war arrived. During the Second World War, Baker worked for the French Resistance and for the Free French forces led by Charles de Gaulle. Her fame was her cover. A celebrated entertainer could travel, attend receptions, and cross borders without much suspicion. According to the accounts of her wartime work, she carried intelligence hidden in her sheet music, written in invisible ink. After the war, France honoured her with military decorations for her service.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Les Milandes was also the setting for a wedding. In 1947, Baker married the French bandleader Jo Bouillon, and the ceremony took place here at Les Milandes. The small village around the château became part of her life. Local people worked for her, and the quiet corner of the Périgord briefly found itself in newspapers far beyond France.

Then came her boldest project. Over the following years, Baker adopted twelve children from different countries, religions, and backgrounds. She called them her Rainbow Tribe. Her idea was simple and radical for its time. She wanted to show the world that children of every origin could grow up together as brothers and sisters. Around the château she built hotels, restaurants, a theatre, and attractions for visitors. She hoped tourism would pay for this living lesson in tolerance.

The dream, sadly, cost more than it earned. Baker spent generously, and the debts grew year after year. By the end of the 1960s, she lost the château, and she was forced to leave. Photographs from that time show her sitting on the steps outside, refusing to go quietly. Friends helped her find a new home, and she kept performing until the end of her life. She died in Paris in 1975, only days after a celebrated return to the stage.

Her reputation did not fade. In 2021, Josephine Baker entered the Panthéon in Paris, the monument where France honours its national heroes. She was the first Black woman to receive this honour. Today, much of the château works as a museum dedicated to her. Inside you can see rooms from her life here, costumes from her stage career, photographs, and personal objects. The bathrooms, decorated in bold Art Deco style, show her taste for glamour.

Outside, the house opens onto formal French gardens, laid out in clipped hedges and neat geometric beds. From the terraces, the land drops away towards the river and the wooded hills beyond. Birds of prey also live on the estate, and falconry demonstrations are often held in the grounds. Children especially love to watch the owls and hawks glide low over the lawns. It adds a living, moving element to a place otherwise full of memory.

Before you leave, stand still on the terrace once more. Look at the pale stone, the dark slate roofs, and the shape of the valley below. A fifteenth-century noblewoman wanted comfort here. A twentieth-century artist wanted a fairer world here. Neither story fully ended as planned, but both left something behind. As you walk back down the path, listen again for the gravel underfoot... and remember that this quiet house once held a very loud dream.
