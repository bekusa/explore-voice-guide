The guide

The story

Feel the stone under your feet first. It is pale gold limestone, worn smooth by centuries of boots and hooves, and in the afternoon it holds the warmth of the sun. Above you, the roofs are not tile but thick flat slabs of the same stone, laid like the scales of a fish. Below, far below, the Dordogne River moves slowly through its green valley. You are standing on a fortress built to watch that river... and to fight for it.

This is the Château de Castelnaud. It sits on a rocky spur where the little Céou River meets the Dordogne, in the region of south-west France known as the Périgord Noir. The name means, roughly, the new castle. That position was no accident. From here, a lord could see boats on the water, travellers on the roads, and armies long before they arrived. In medieval Europe, a view like this was power.

Now look across the valley. On the cliff on the far side of the river stands another castle — Beynac. The two fortresses face each other like players across a chessboard. For much of the Middle Ages, they were rivals. That rivalry is the thread that runs through almost every story told about this place, and it is worth keeping in mind as you walk.

The first great test came in the early thirteenth century, during the Albigensian Crusade. This was a war launched by the Church and northern French barons against the Cathars, a Christian movement in southern France that Rome called heretical. The lord of Castelnaud was accused of sympathising with them. In 1214, the crusade leader Simon de Montfort came to the Dordogne and took the castle. It was not the last time these walls changed hands.

The bigger drama came with the Hundred Years' War between England and France, which ran through the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Here the chessboard image becomes very real. Castelnaud often stood on the English side, while Beynac across the river stood with the French. Two garrisons, a short distance apart, watching each other day after day. Local tradition tells of raids, ambushes, and shifting loyalties as lords chose the side that best protected their land.

As you move through the castle, notice how it grew in layers. The oldest parts are the tall, plain square keep and the inner walls — built for an age of swords, arrows, and ladders. Later builders added thicker walls and a heavy round artillery tower. Why? Because warfare changed. Gunpowder arrived, and cannon could break walls that had stood for generations. Castelnaud shows that change in its stones. You can read the history of military technology just by looking at the shapes of the towers.

After the wars, the castle slowly lost its purpose. By the modern era, much of it was a ruin, its walls open to the weather and its roofs collapsing. Then, in the second half of the twentieth century, a long restoration began. Craftsmen rebuilt roofs, consolidated walls, and brought the fortress back to life. Much of what you see today is the result of that patient work — careful repair rather than a fantasy reconstruction.

Here is the first surprise for many visitors. Castelnaud is not furnished as a noble home. Instead, it houses a museum dedicated to warfare in the Middle Ages. Inside, you will find armour, swords, crossbows, helmets, and early firearms. The displays explain how a knight dressed for battle, how a crossbow was loaded, and how weapons evolved over several centuries. It is a rare chance to see these objects inside the kind of building they were designed to attack and defend.

Now step out onto the terraces and look at the great wooden machines. These are full-size working reconstructions of medieval siege engines. The largest is the trebuchet — a giant throwing arm powered by a heavy counterweight. When the weight drops, the long arm swings up and a sling hurls a stone through the air. Smaller machines stand nearby, each with its own design. Seeing them at full scale changes how you imagine a siege. They are enormous, heavy, and frighteningly simple.

The second surprise is how quiet a siege really was, most of the time. We imagine constant battle. In reality, a siege was mostly waiting. Attackers dug in, cut off supplies, and threw stones now and then to wear down walls and nerves. Defenders rationed food and water and watched from narrow windows. As you look out through the arrow slits here, you see exactly what a defender saw — a thin strip of valley, and whatever was moving in it.

Walk to the edge of the outer terrace and take in the panorama. The Dordogne bends below you through fields, walnut groves, and small farms. On a clear day, you can pick out other castles and villages on the hills. Swallows cut through the air beneath you. In the morning, mist often lies along the river, and the far castles rise out of it like islands. It is easy to understand why so many fortresses were built in this valley.

On your way down, take time in the village of Castelnaud-la-Chapelle. Its lanes are steep and narrow, and the houses are built from the same golden stone as the castle, with the same heavy stone roofs. Gardens spill over low walls. The village grew in the shadow of the fortress, and the two still feel like one place. Down by the river, canoes drift past, and you may hear voices carrying across the water.

Before you leave, stand still for one more moment and look across at Beynac. Centuries ago, a soldier stood near this spot and looked at the same cliff, wondering what the other side was planning. Today, the only movement between them is the river and the people paddling on it. Listen to the wind on the stone, feel the warm wall under your hand... and let the valley go quiet around you as you walk back down the hill.
