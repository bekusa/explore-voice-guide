The guide

The story

Before you see a single stone, you hear Tikal. A deep, rolling roar moves through the trees. It sounds like a large animal, or a storm coming in. It is neither. It is the howler monkey, a creature no bigger than a small dog, calling across the canopy. The air is thick and warm. It smells of wet leaves and soil. Under your feet, the path is soft and dark, and roots cross it like ropes.

You are standing in the forests of the Petén, in northern Guatemala. This is a low, flat land of limestone and jungle that runs north into Mexico and east toward Belize. Today it feels remote. But more than a thousand years ago, this was one of the most crowded places in the Americas. Tikal was a royal capital of the ancient Maya. Tens of thousands of people lived in and around it, farming, trading, building, and worshipping.

The Maya were not a single empire. They lived in many city-states, each with its own ruling family. These cities shared a language family, a calendar, and a writing system made of signs called glyphs. They also fought each other. Tikal grew over many centuries, but its greatest age came in what scholars call the Classic period, roughly from the third century to the ninth century of the common era. In those centuries, Tikal was one of the two great powers of the Maya world.

Its main rival was Calakmul, a city to the north, in what is now Mexico. The inscriptions carved on stone monuments here tell a long story of alliances and wars between the two. For a time, in the sixth and seventh centuries, Tikal suffered heavy defeats. Then it recovered. Much of what you see around the central plaza today belongs to that recovery... a city rebuilding its pride in stone.

Now walk toward the Great Plaza. This is the ceremonial heart of the city. On the east side rises Temple I, often called the Temple of the Great Jaguar. It is steep and narrow, with a small temple room on top and a tall stone crest above it. On the opposite side stands Temple II. The two pyramids face each other across the open grass, like two speakers in a conversation.

Temple I was built as a tomb. Inside it, archaeologists found the burial of a powerful king named Jasaw Chan K'awiil the First. He ruled in the late seventh and early eighth centuries, and he is often credited with Tikal's comeback. His tomb held jade, shell, and carved bones. Many scholars think Temple II was built for his queen. So the plaza is, in a quiet way, a meeting place for a husband and wife — facing each other forever.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Look at the grassy ground of the plaza. In Maya times, it was not grass at all. It was paved with lime plaster, smooth and pale. Many of the buildings were plastered too, and painted — often a deep red. The grey and green stone you see today is the bare skeleton. Imagine the plaza bright, hard, and almost shining in the sun.

On the north side of the plaza is the North Acropolis. It looks like a jumble of platforms and small temples. In fact, it is many layers of construction, one built over another across many centuries. Kings were buried here, generation after generation. In front of it stand rows of carved stone slabs called stelae, with round stone altars at their feet. The stelae record rulers, dates, and ceremonies in glyphs and portraits.

One of the most surprising stories at Tikal involves a city far away. In the late fourth century, inscriptions describe the arrival of a figure named Siyaj K'ak', linked to Teotihuacan, a huge city in central Mexico. Soon after, a new ruling line appears at Tikal. Scholars still debate what exactly happened — a conquest, a political alliance, or something in between. But you can see that foreign influence in some carvings, which show warriors in central Mexican dress.

If you walk west, you reach Temple IV, the tallest structure at Tikal. Its top rises above the forest canopy. From up there, you look out over a sea of green, with the crests of other temples poking through like islands. Here is a second surprise. In 1977, this view appeared in the first Star Wars film. It played the role of a rebel base on a jungle moon. Many visitors only learn this when they reach the top.

South of the main plaza lies the area called Mundo Perdido, which means the Lost World. Its great pyramid is one of the oldest major buildings at Tikal. It was in use centuries before the temples of the Great Plaza. Builders here watched the sky closely. Some Maya building groups were arranged to mark the movement of the sun through the year. It is a reminder that this city was old, even to the people who built Temple I.

And then, in the ninth and tenth centuries, Tikal emptied. The same happened across much of the southern Maya lowlands. Researchers point to several possible causes working together: long droughts, warfare, overpopulation, and the strain on forests and farmland. The kings stopped raising monuments. People moved away. Slowly, the jungle returned. Roots pushed into the stairways, and soil covered the plazas. The Maya people did not vanish — millions live in Guatemala today. But the great city fell silent.

Today the forest is part of the story. Tikal National Park protects both the ruins and the jungle around them. The whole site is a UNESCO World Heritage Site for its culture and its nature together. As you walk, watch for spider monkeys moving through the branches, and coatis — long-nosed, ringtailed animals — searching the ground. Look also for the ocellated turkey, with blue head and copper-green feathers. Near the entrance, you may see a huge ceiba tree, sacred to the Maya and Guatemala's national tree.

Before you leave, stop for a moment in the shade. Listen again. The insects buzz. A bird calls once, sharp and clear. Somewhere far off, the howlers begin again. Around you, under every small green hill, there may be a building no one has yet uncovered. As you walk back along the path, look at those low mounds in the forest. Each one may be part of the city that still sleeps here — waiting under the trees.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive at dawn for cooler air and active wildlife.
Carry plenty of water through the humid forest.
Wear sturdy shoes for roots and steep wooden stairs.
Bring insect repellent, especially in the rainy season.
Hire a licensed guide to spot animals and glyphs.
