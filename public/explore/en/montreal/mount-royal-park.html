Mount Royal Park

A forest rises in the middle of a city of millions.

⏱ 5 min
▶BeginOpen the Lokali app

The story

A forest rises in the middle of a city of millions. Gravel paths climb under maples and oaks. Somewhere below, traffic hums, but up here birdsong wins. Runners pass dog walkers, and cyclists ring their bells on the gravel. This is Mount Royal Park, the green heart of Montreal. The city took its name from this small mountain, and Montrealers simply call it the mountain.

The name is old. In 1535, the French explorer Jacques Cartier climbed these slopes, guided by people from Hochelaga, the village at the mountain's foot. He named the hill Mont Royal in honor of his king. Archaeologists still debate the exact location of Hochelaga, which vanished within a century. Over time, the name of the mountain slowly became the name of the city: Montreal.

The park itself opened in 1876. The city had purchased the land after citizens protested when an owner cut down trees for firewood. To design the park, Montreal hired Frederick Law Olmsted, the American landscape architect who had already created Central Park in New York. He was the most celebrated park maker of the nineteenth century. Instead of taming the mountain, he chose to reveal it.

Olmsted believed a park should heal city dwellers. He designed one long, gentle carriage road that circles upward at an easy grade, so the climb never exhausts the walker. He wanted the landscape to unfold slowly, view by view, like chapters in a calm story. Much of his plan survives in today's main path, Olmsted Road.

The summit rises 233 meters above sea level. That is modest for a mountain and generous for a view. The reward waits at the Kondiaronk Belvedere, a wide stone terrace named after a Wendat chief who helped negotiate a great peace treaty in 1701. From its edge, downtown Montreal spreads below, with the Saint Lawrence River shining behind the towers. On clear days, distant hills appear far beyond the water.

Behind the lookout stands the Mount Royal Chalet, completed in 1932. It looks like a grand mountain lodge, with a high wooden ceiling and paintings of scenes from Canadian history inside. In cold months, visitors warm their hands here before walking back out into the snow.

Higher still stands the cross. A steel tower about thirty meters tall, it was erected in 1924 and lights up every night. It recalls a promise from 1643, when the city's founder, Paul de Chomedey de Maisonneuve, is said to have carried a wooden cross up the slope after the young settlement survived a flood. The light is visible from far across the city.

Water softens the park's eastern side. Beaver Lake, called Lac aux Castors in French, was created in 1938 on the site of a former marsh. It is shallow, round and calm. Families float toy boats in summer. In winter, the surface freezes, and skaters circle it for hours while a nearby slope fills with sleds after every snowfall.

Winter does not close the mountain; it transforms it. Cross-country ski tracks line the main road. Snowshoe paths thread between the trees. Children drag sleds up the hill near the lake, and their laughter carries far in the cold air. On the coldest nights, the city below steams like a pot.

On summer Sundays, the mountain's eastern edge turns into a festival. Around the monument to George-Étienne Cartier, drummers gather by the dozens for the Tam-Tams, an informal drum circle. Dancers, picnickers and vendors fill the lawns. The tradition started informally in the late twentieth century and simply never stopped. Nobody organizes it, and nobody needs to.

The park is also a working forest. Volunteers plant native trees each year. Red-tailed hawks and owls nest in the canopy, and foxes cross the paths at dawn. In October, the slopes turn red and gold, and the whole city climbs up to walk inside the color.

Walking remains the honest way to know the mountain. Long staircases climb the steep face above Peel Street. Quiet trails wander past the large cemeteries that share the mountain with the park. Every route ends somewhere with a view, a bench and a reason to sit still.

Olmsted never saw his park finished, and budget cuts trimmed his plans. Yet his central idea won. A city kept a mountain instead of covering it with houses. More than a century later, office workers, students and grandparents still climb the same slow road to breathe. Some gifts a city gives itself keep on giving.

Key facts

Designed by Frederick Law Olmsted, creator of Central Park
Officially opened as a public park in 1876
Summit reaches 233 meters above sea level
Illuminated summit cross erected in 1924
Jacques Cartier named the mountain in 1535

What to look for

The white chalet with painted historical scenes inside
Beaver Lake ringed by lawns and picnic blankets
Red-tailed hawks circling above the forest canopy
Long wooden staircases climbing the steep Peel Street face

Practical tips

Wear solid shoes for the climbing gravel paths
Join the Sunday afternoon drum circle in summer
Carry water; fountains are scarce along the trails
Come at dusk to watch the city lights appear
