The guide

The story

Step inside and let your eyes adjust. The air smells faintly of beeswax and old wood, and above you, a ceiling painted deep indigo glows with thousands of gold stars — a night sky frozen over the nave. This is Notre-Dame Basilica, and it does not look like any church you have seen in Europe.

The building opened in 1829, designed by an Irish-American architect named James O'Donnell. He had never built a church before this commission, and he gave Montreal something radical: a Gothic Revival interior that ignored the pale stone restraint of medieval cathedrals and instead exploded into colour. The walls are cobalt, azure, crimson, and gold. Every surface carries paint or gilding. O'Donnell converted to Catholicism late in life and asked to be buried here — his tomb lies beneath the floor near the altar.

Look up at the twin towers flanking the façade outside. They rise seventy metres and bear different names: the western tower is called Perseverance, the eastern one Temperance. When they were completed, they were the tallest structures in Montreal, visible from the river and the mountain. The bells inside them once regulated daily life across the old town — work shifts, curfews, weddings, funerals.

The basilica replaced a smaller French colonial church that had stood on this site since the sixteen-fifties. By the early eighteen-hundreds, the old building could no longer hold Montreal's growing Catholic population, so the parish launched a competition for a new design. O'Donnell's proposal won, and construction took five years. The interior decoration continued for decades after, with local craftsmen carving the wooden screens, painting the vaults, and installing the stained glass.

Move toward the apse and you will see the altarpiece — a carved wooden reredos covered in gold leaf, depicting scenes from Montreal's religious history. It was completed in the eighteen-thirties by Victor Bourgeau, a Québécois architect who later renovated much of the interior. Bourgeau also designed the pulpit, a spiraling wooden staircase that rises like a nautilus shell. Preachers once climbed it to deliver sermons that echoed through the nave.

The pipe organ behind you is one of the largest in North America. Built by the Casavant Frères workshop in Quebec, it contains nearly seven thousand pipes. The instrument has been played at state funerals, royal visits, and countless Sunday masses. Céline Dion married here in 1994, and the organ filled the basilica with sound that spilled out onto Place d'Armes.

The stained glass windows do not depict biblical scenes — they illustrate the history of Montreal itself. You will see Jeanne Mance founding the Hôtel-Dieu hospital, Paul de Chomedey de Maisonneuve planting a cross on Mount Royal, and Marguerite Bourgeoys teaching children in a stable. The windows were installed in the eighteen-seventies, replacing earlier clear glass, and they turn the interior into a chronicle of the city's founding.

The chapel behind the main altar is called the Sacred Heart Chapel, and it tells a darker story. The original chapel burned in 1978, set alight by an arsonist. The fire destroyed the wooden vault and the nineteenth-century altarpiece. The rebuilt chapel, completed in 1982, is modern — bronze panels, abstract forms, a reredos that looks like a folded ribbon of metal. It is the only part of the basilica that does not lean into Gothic ornament, and the contrast is deliberate.

Notice the wooden pews as you walk back toward the entrance. They are made of butternut, a pale hardwood native to Quebec, and each one is carved with floral motifs. The wood has darkened over nearly two centuries of use, polished smooth by the hands and coats of parishioners. On winter mornings, the pews still creak in the cold.

The basilica sits on Place d'Armes, the old parade ground of French Montreal. Across the square stands a monument to Maisonneuve, the city's founder, and beyond that, the first skyscraper in Montreal — a reminder that this building has watched the city transform from a fur-trading outpost into a modern metropolis. But inside, under the blue vault, the light stays soft and the silence holds.

As you leave, pause in the narthex and look back at the nave one more time. The gold stars overhead catch the afternoon sun slanting through the western windows, and for a moment, the whole interior seems to lift. This is what O'Donnell built — not a copy of Europe, but a new world vision of the sacred, painted in colours the old stone cathedrals never dared to use.
