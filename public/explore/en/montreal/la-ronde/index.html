The story

Screams drift across the water before the rides come into view. La Ronde covers the northern tip of Île Sainte-Hélène, an island in the middle of the Saint Lawrence River. Steel tracks twist above the treetops. A white mountain of wooden beams rumbles under racing cars. Across the water, the office towers of downtown Montreal watch over it all. This is the largest amusement park in Quebec, and it began as a birthday party for an entire country.

The park opened in 1967 as part of Expo 67, the world's fair that marked the one hundredth anniversary of Canada. The fair transformed these river islands. Pavilions from dozens of nations rose beside new lagoons and monorail lines. La Ronde was the fair's pleasure ground. Visitors ended their day of exhibits here, among rides, games and music. When the fair closed, most pavilions emptied, but the amusement park never stopped.

The name is French, and it carries a gentle meaning. A ronde is a round dance, the kind that children perform in a circle while holding hands. The word suggests turning, repetition and play. It fits a park built on spinning wheels, looping tracks and rides that always return to where they started. French remains present everywhere here, on signs and in the announcements between the screams.

For its first decades, the city of Montreal operated the park. In 2001, the American company Six Flags purchased La Ronde and brought new investment. Larger and faster machines arrived year after year. Today about forty rides run in a typical season, from a classic carousel and gentle children's attractions to coasters that hold riders upside down high above the island.

The most famous structure is Le Monstre, French for The Monster. This wooden roller coaster opened in 1985. Two separate tracks race side by side through its frame of white-painted lumber. It is recognized as the tallest two-track wooden roller coaster in the world. Wood flexes where steel does not, so the cars produce a deep rattle and roar that steel coasters cannot imitate.

Goliath is the park's giant in steel. It opened in 2006 with a first drop of about 53 meters and a top speed near 110 kilometers per hour. At its debut, it ranked among the tallest and fastest roller coasters in Canada. From the crest of the first hill, riders see the river, the bridge and the whole island for one second. Then the floor of the world falls away.

La Ronde is also a stage for fire. Since 1985, the park has hosted an international fireworks competition, known today as L'International des Feux Loto-Québec. On selected summer evenings, teams from different countries launch synchronized shows over the water, set to music. Thousands of people watch without a ticket from the Jacques Cartier Bridge, which closes to cars on show nights.

The setting quietly shapes the whole experience. The park sits in the river, so a breeze crosses the grounds even on hot afternoons. Between rides, walkways open onto wide views of the water. Elsewhere on the island stands the Biosphere, the giant geodesic dome that also survives from Expo 67. The memory of the fair and the noise of the present share the same ground.

Families built this place as much as engineers did. Generations of children in Quebec measured their courage here, first on the small rides, then on the tall ones. Teenagers arrive in loud groups when school ends in June. Grandparents point at machines they first rode in 1967. The park works like a shared family album that keeps adding new pages.

A visit has its own rhythm. The season runs from spring to autumn, and summer brings the longest opening hours. Lines grow on hot weekend afternoons. Evening changes the mood completely. The air cools, thousands of lights come on, and the coasters run their final circuits against a darkening sky. Many visitors plan their whole day backward from the fireworks.

Listen to the park as much as you watch it. Every ride has a voice. The wooden coaster thunders like a passing train. The steel ones hiss and whistle. Chains clank as cars climb the first hill. Then comes a short silence at the top, just before the drop and the screams. That pause is the purest sound in any amusement park.

When you leave, look back from the bridge or from the metro platform. The loops and towers stand black and still against the sky. A machine built for a single summer in 1967 is still turning after more than half a century. Some celebrations simply refuse to end.

Key facts

Opened in 1967 as the entertainment zone of Expo 67
Largest amusement park in the province of Quebec
Purchased by the American operator Six Flags in 2001
Le Monstre of 1985 is the tallest two-track wooden coaster worldwide
International fireworks competition held at the park since 1985

What to look for

White-painted lumber mountain carrying two racing tracks
The downtown skyline framed between coaster loops
The Biosphere dome visible beyond the park
The Jacques Cartier Bridge spanning the river beside the island

Practical tips

Take the yellow metro line to Jean-Drapeau station
Visit on weekday mornings to avoid the longest lines
Stay for evening rides when crowds thin and lights glow
Check the fireworks show calendar before choosing your date
