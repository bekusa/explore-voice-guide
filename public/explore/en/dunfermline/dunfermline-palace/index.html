The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and listen. Below the tall ruined wall in front of you, the ground falls steeply away into a wooded glen. You can hear the trees moving and, on a quiet day, water running somewhere far beneath. The stone here is cool to the touch and rough under your hand, worn soft at the edges. The roof is gone. The floors are gone. What remains is a long, high wall of empty windows, open to the Fife sky.

This is Dunfermline Palace. Today it is a ruin, but for a short and important stretch of Scottish history it was a royal home. Kings and queens slept behind these walls. At least one future king was born here. To understand why a palace stands on this exact spot, though, you have to start with the abbey next door, whose great church rises just a short walk from where you are standing.

The story begins in the eleventh century. King Malcolm the Third married an English-born princess named Margaret, and the couple founded a religious house here in Dunfermline. Margaret was deeply devout. After her death she was made a saint, and pilgrims came to visit her shrine. In the twelfth century her son, King David the First, raised the community to the rank of an abbey. It became one of the wealthiest and most important religious houses in medieval Scotland.

A rich abbey on a pilgrim route needed somewhere to put important guests. So the monks built a large guest house, and that guest house is the origin of the building you see now. Royal visitors stayed here often. Over time the line between a guest house for kings and a house belonging to kings became thin. The building grew and changed, and eventually it served as a palace in its own right.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Dunfermline was not only a place where Scottish monarchs lived — it was also where many of them were laid to rest. The abbey became a royal burial ground. Robert the Bruce, the king who won Scotland's independence wars, is buried in the abbey church. His heart, according to his own wishes, was taken elsewhere, but his body lies here. So the palace sat beside a church full of royal graves.

The medieval centuries were not always peaceful. During the wars with England, King Edward the First of England stayed at Dunfermline in the early fourteenth century. When his army left, many of the abbey buildings were set on fire and badly damaged. The monks rebuilt, slowly and at great cost. Some of the stone you see around you belongs to that long recovery, layered on top of older work and later changes.

Now look along the length of the great south wall. Notice how tall it is, and how it seems to cling to the edge of the slope. The builders used the steep ground on purpose. On the glen side the building drops several storeys, while on the town side it sits much lower. This gave the rooms at the top wide views and a strong position, and it made the most of a narrow site.

The palace's best-known chapter came in the late sixteenth century. King James the Sixth of Scotland married Anna of Denmark, and he gave her Dunfermline as a gift. Anna took a real interest in the place. She had work carried out to make the palace more comfortable and more fitting for a queen. For a time, this was one of the homes where the royal family actually lived and raised their children.

And here is the second surprise. In 1600, Anna gave birth to a son here at Dunfermline. That child grew up to become King Charles the First, the king whose reign ended in civil war and his execution in 1649. He is often described as the last British monarch born in Scotland. It is a strange thought — such a troubled reign began in a room inside these quiet walls.

Then everything changed. In 1603, Queen Elizabeth the First of England died, and James inherited her throne. This joining of the two crowns is known as the Union of the Crowns. James moved his court south to London, and the royal family went with him. Dunfermline suddenly had no regular royal household. Without the people who used it and paid for its upkeep, the palace began its long, slow decline.

As you walk, look for the stone archway that joins the palace area to the old abbey buildings. Locals call this passage the Pends. The road runs right under it, as it has for centuries. Nearby stand the ruins of the monks' dining hall, with a large and beautifully traced window at one end. Together these pieces help you picture how palace, abbey, and town once fitted tightly together.

Beyond the palace wall lies Pittencrieff Park, the green glen you can hear below. Andrew Carnegie, the steel magnate born in Dunfermline in the nineteenth century, later bought this estate and gave it to the people of the town. Today families walk their dogs there and children run along the paths. Within the park stand the remains of a tower linked by tradition to Malcolm the Third, though how much is history and how much is local legend is hard to say.

Before you leave, step back and look up at those empty windows one more time. Imagine them glazed, lit from within, with smoke rising from the chimneys and voices on the stairs. Then let the picture fade, and notice what is really there: grass, birdsong, and the long shadow of the wall across the ground. As you walk away towards the abbey or down into the glen, listen for the water below. It was running long before the first stone went up here.

Before you go

Practical tips

Visit the palace and abbey church together in one trip.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven ground and steep slopes.
Walk down into Pittencrieff Park for views of the wall.
Bring a waterproof layer; the site is fully open-air.
Check in advance whether booking is needed for entry.
