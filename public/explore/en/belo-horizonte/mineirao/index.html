The guide

The story

Feel the concrete underfoot first. It is warm, grey, and slightly rough, and it runs out in a wide plaza around a ring of tall pillars. Above you, the stadium's curved edge cuts a clean line against the Minas Gerais sky. On a quiet weekday you might hear only wind, a distant car, and birds over the lake nearby. On match day the same ground shakes. This is Mineirão, and it was built to hold a city's voice.

The official name is longer: Estádio Governador Magalhães Pinto, after the state governor of the time. Almost nobody uses it. The nickname comes from "mineiro", the word for a person from Minas Gerais, so Mineirão means something like "the big one from Minas". It is a friendly, slightly boastful name. That fits a state whose people are known in Brazil for being reserved... until football starts.

The stadium opened in 1965. Belo Horizonte was a young city then, planned and built at the end of the nineteenth century as a new state capital. By the 1960s it was growing fast, and its football clubs had outgrown their small grounds. A stadium on this scale was a statement. It said the capital of Minas Gerais now belonged in the same conversation as Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo.

Look at the structure itself. The design is a large oval of reinforced concrete, held up by a repeating rhythm of external pillars. Each pillar leans and tapers, and together they form a kind of colonnade around the bowl. This is Brazilian modernism — an architecture of raw concrete, bold curves, and honest structure. Nothing here hides how the building stands up. You can read the whole engineering idea just by walking around the outside.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Mineirão sits in Pampulha, the same district where a young Oscar Niemeyer designed a lakeside ensemble of modernist buildings in the 1940s. That group, including the curved Church of Saint Francis of Assisi, is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The stadium is not part of that listing. Still, it shares the same neighbourhood and the same confidence in concrete. Pampulha was Belo Horizonte's laboratory for the modern age.

For most of its life, Mineirão was a shared home. Cruzeiro, in blue, and Atlético Mineiro, in black and white, are the two great clubs of the city. Their rivalry divides families, offices, and streets. For decades their derby filled this bowl. In earlier eras, crowds here reached well over one hundred thousand people, many of them standing. Imagine that noise rolling off the concrete and across the lake.

The stadium also shaped Brazilian football legends. In the late 1960s, Cruzeiro built a team here around the forward Tostão, who later starred for Brazil at the 1970 World Cup. Atlético Mineiro had its own heroes on this pitch in the decades that followed. Local fans still argue about which club owns the stadium's soul. Ask a taxi driver which side he supports. You will probably get a long answer.

In the years before the 2014 World Cup, Mineirão went through a major renovation. The old standing terraces gave way to seating, and the capacity came down to roughly sixty thousand. The playing field was lowered to improve sightlines. A new roof was added inside the original concrete ring. Crucially, the outer pillars and the oval silhouette were kept. The renovation changed the inside but respected the face the city knew.

The plaza you are standing on came from that same renovation. Before, the area around the stadium was mostly parking and rough ground. Now there is a broad esplanade, open on most days. People jog here, ride bicycles, and walk dogs in the evening. Families come with children on scooters. It is one of the few places in Belo Horizonte where you can find this much open, flat space in one piece.

Now for the match that everyone in Brazil remembers, and most would prefer to forget. On 8 July 2014, Brazil played Germany here in the World Cup semi-final. Germany won seven goals to one. Several of those goals came within a few minutes in the first half. The result shocked the nation. In Brazil it is still known as the "Mineiraço" — a word that points straight back to this stadium.

That match sometimes overshadows everything else, but Mineirão has hosted much more. It welcomed games at the 2013 Confederations Cup and football at the 2016 Olympic Games. It also hosts concerts and large public events. In recent years Atlético Mineiro moved to its own new arena elsewhere in the city. Cruzeiro remains the main club here, so blue shirts are what you will most likely see on match days.

Inside, there is also a football museum, and guided tours often take visitors through areas fans rarely see. Tour routes can include the changing rooms, the tunnel, and the edge of the pitch. Standing at pitch level gives you a very different sense of scale. The stands rise steeply, and the roof frames a wide oval of sky. Check locally for what is open, because access depends on the event calendar.

Before you leave, walk a short distance toward the lake. From the right angle, you can see the stadium and the water of Pampulha in the same view. The neighbourhood also holds the large campus of the Federal University of Minas Gerais, and a smaller indoor arena called Mineirinho sits close by. Together they show how Belo Horizonte gathered sport, study, and leisure in one corner of the city.

Now turn back and look at the pillars once more. Count a few of them as you walk, and notice how the light changes on the concrete as you move. Perhaps a runner passes you, or a child chases a ball across the plaza. On a quiet day, this place feels calm and almost empty. But the concrete remembers the crowds. Listen for a moment... then carry that silence with you as you go.
