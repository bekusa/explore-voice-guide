The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Under the canopy, sound behaves differently. Footsteps soften on a deep carpet of leaves and moss. A woodpecker knocks somewhere far off, and the knock seems to travel for a long time. The air smells of damp bark, mushrooms, and pine resin. The light comes down in narrow green columns. You are standing in one of the last large fragments of the primeval lowland forest that once covered much of the European plain.

This is Belovezhskaya Pushcha, on the western edge of Belarus. The forest does not stop at the border. It continues into Poland, where it is called Białowieża Forest. Together the two halves form a single living system, split by a line on a map. UNESCO first listed the Polish side as a World Heritage Site in 1979. The listing later grew to include the Belarusian side, and the two now share one transboundary inscription.

What makes this place unusual is not only its size. It is its age and its structure. In most European woodland, people have cleared, replanted, and managed the trees for centuries. Here, large areas have grown with far less human interference. Old trees are allowed to die where they stand. Fallen trunks lie on the ground and slowly rot. That decaying wood is not untidiness — it is food and shelter for fungi, beetles, mosses, and birds.

So as you walk, look down as well as up. A fallen oak or spruce may carry a whole community on its back. Bracket fungi grow out from the bark like small shelves. Young saplings sometimes root directly in the soft, rotting wood. Woodpeckers find insects there and carve holes that other birds later use. In a managed forest, much of this would be cleared away. Here, the cycle of growth and decay runs on its own schedule.

The forest survived partly because of who owned it. For centuries, it was a royal hunting ground. The grand dukes of Lithuania and the kings of Poland hunted here. Later, after the region became part of the Russian Empire, the Russian tsars took it over as their private reserve. Strict hunting rules protected the large game. In an odd way, the pleasures of a few powerful people helped keep the forest standing while woodland elsewhere disappeared.

Now for the animal most visitors hope to see. The European bison is the heaviest land animal in Europe. It is a massive, dark brown creature with a high hump over its shoulders. In Belarusian it is called zubr. This forest was one of its final strongholds in the wild. Then war, hunger, and poaching in the early twentieth century took a heavy toll. Around the end of the First World War, the last wild bison of Białowieża were killed.

Here is the surprise. The species did not vanish completely. A small number of bison survived in zoos and private collections across Europe. Scientists and breeders worked with those few animals to rebuild the population. In time, bison were brought back to this forest and released. Today, herds roam the Pushcha again. Every bison you see here descends from that tiny surviving group — a living lesson in how close a species can come to the edge.

The bison share the forest with many other animals. Red deer, roe deer, wild boar, elk, wolves, lynx, and beavers all live here. Most of them are shy, and the woodland is dense. You may not see a wolf, but you may notice tracks in soft mud near a stream. You may see trees gnawed to a point by beavers. If you want a closer look, the enclosures near the park's visitor area hold bison and other species where you can watch them calmly.

The park also has a quieter, more surprising place in modern history. Deep in the forest, at a government residence called Viskuli, the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus met in December 1991. There they signed the agreement that declared the Soviet Union had ceased to exist. It is a strange thought. One of the largest states of the twentieth century was formally ended in a hunting lodge, surrounded by these same trees.

For families, the park has a lighter side. Since the early 2000s, it has been home to the residence of Ded Moroz, the Belarusian Father Frost. He is the winter gift-bringer in this part of the world, similar to Santa Claus. The estate has wooden buildings, carved figures, and a festive atmosphere that children enjoy. In winter, with snow on the roofs and the forest silent around it, the scene feels like a picture from an old storybook.

The nature museum near the park entrance is a good place to begin or end your visit. Its displays introduce the animals and plants of the forest. They help you put names to what you have seen and heard along the trails. Many visitors also explore by bicycle, because the forest roads are long, flat, and quiet. Moving slowly, on foot or on two wheels, lets you notice small changes in the trees and the light.

The forest changes strongly with the seasons. In spring, the ground fills with wildflowers before the leaves close overhead, and birdsong is at its loudest. Summer brings thick green shade and plenty of mosquitoes near the wetlands. Autumn turns the oaks, birches, and maples gold and copper, and mushrooms appear everywhere. Winter is the most silent season of all. Snow makes animal tracks easy to read, and bison sometimes gather where rangers leave extra food.

Before you leave, stop once more and stand still. Look at the trunk nearest to you. Notice the texture of its bark, the moss on its north side, the insects moving in its cracks. Some of the oaks around you began growing long before anyone living today was born. They will likely be here long after you have gone home. As you walk back toward the road, listen again for the woodpecker. The forest keeps its own time — and for a short while, you have been part of it.
