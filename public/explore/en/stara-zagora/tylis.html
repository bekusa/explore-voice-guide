The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the ground under your feet. Here in Stara Zagora the earth is warm, dark and fertile. To the north, the low hills of the Sredna Gora rise in soft, rounded lines. To the south, the Upper Thracian Plain opens wide and flat. In early summer, the streets of this city smell of linden blossom. That sweet, honeyed scent has drifted across this land for a very long time. And somewhere in this wider landscape lies one of the great puzzles of Balkan archaeology.

The name you came here for is Tylis. It is not one building or one ruin. It is the name of a kingdom, and of its lost capital. In the third century before Christ, a group of Celts settled in Thrace and ruled a state from a city called Tylis. Ancient writers mention it. Coins carry the names of its kings. Yet nobody has ever proven where the capital stood. Think of it as a city that exists in the written record but not yet on the map.

To understand Tylis, start with the people who lived here first. This region was the heartland of the Thracians. They were tribal peoples who spoke an Indo-European language and left no long written texts of their own. Across modern Bulgaria, they built fortified hilltops and royal tombs. They made fine work in gold and silver. When Greek and later Roman writers described the Balkans, they described Thracian land. So when strangers arrived from the north, they came into a country with deep roots of its own.

Those strangers were Celts. In the early third century before Christ, large Celtic war bands moved south through the Balkans. They are best remembered for their famous raid into Greece, which reached as far as Delphi, the great sanctuary of Apollo. After that campaign broke apart, the groups scattered. Some crossed into Asia Minor, modern Turkey, where they became known as the Galatians. Another group stayed behind in Thrace. Ancient sources name their leader as Comontorius, and say they founded their kingdom here.

The main source for this story is Polybius, a Greek historian who wrote in the second century before Christ. He describes how the Celts of Tylis pressed hard on the Greek city of Byzantium, the town that later became Constantinople and then Istanbul. According to his account, Byzantium had to pay these Celts a heavy tribute every year. That detail surprises many visitors. A small Celtic state somewhere in inner Thrace was strong enough to make a wealthy Greek port on the Bosphorus pay for peace.

The kingdom did not last forever. Polybius names its last king as Cavarus. He seems to have been an unusual ruler. The historian praises him as a man of royal character, and says he helped to settle disputes between Byzantium and its neighbours. Coins bearing the name of Cavarus have been found in Bulgaria. Holding one is a direct link to this vanished court. Then, near the end of the third century before Christ, the Thracians rose up. The Celtic kingdom was destroyed, and Tylis fades from history.

So where was it? This is the question that keeps scholars arguing. Polybius places Tylis near the Haemus, the ancient name for the Balkan Mountains. That is a large area. Over the years, researchers have suggested several locations across central and eastern Bulgaria. The lands around Stara Zagora, Kazanlak and the Tundzha valley sit inside this zone of possibility. Some sites have produced Celtic weapons, jewellery and coins. But no excavation has yet produced a sign that says, clearly, this was the capital.

It helps to be honest about that uncertainty while you stand here. You are not looking at a confirmed royal city. You are standing in a region that may have belonged to the kingdom of Tylis. That is still remarkable. The Celts did not live in empty space. Their power would have touched the farms, roads and river crossings of this plain. Thracian villages around them paid, traded, fought and married. History here is often a matter of layers, not a single label.

And Stara Zagora is a place made of layers. Beneath the modern city lie the remains of the Roman town of Augusta Traiana, with its forum and paved streets. On the edge of town, archaeologists have studied Neolithic dwellings, homes of some of the earliest farmers in Europe, thousands of years older than the Celts. Between those two worlds came the Thracians, then the Celts of Tylis, then Rome. A short walk through this city can carry you across many of these periods.

Now look around you with fresh eyes. Notice the shape of the land. The hills to the north offered shelter and natural lookouts. The plain to the south offered grain, pasture and easy movement. Any ruler who wanted to control trade between the Aegean coast and the inner Balkans would value this ground. When you see why a place matters geographically, you begin to see why a war band from the north might decide to stay rather than move on.

Here is a second detail most visitors miss. The Celts who settled in Thrace did not simply bring their own culture and keep it unchanged. Finds from Bulgaria show a blend of styles. Celtic swords and brooches appear beside Thracian and Greek objects. The coins of Cavarus follow Greek models, with Greek letters. In other words, these northern newcomers quickly adopted the language of money used around them. The kingdom of Tylis was a meeting point, not a sealed foreign island.

If you want to see real objects from this long story, the regional museum in Stara Zagora is the best next step. Its collections cover the prehistory, Thracian and Roman periods of the area. Look for metalwork, coins and grave goods, and ask staff which pieces relate to the Celtic presence in Thrace. Museums often change their displays, so the items on view may differ from visit to visit. Still, the objects give shape to a name that otherwise lives only in old texts.

Before you move on, take one more slow breath. Listen to the city around you, the traffic, the voices, perhaps the wind in the linden trees. Somewhere under fields like the ones beyond this town, the walls of Tylis may still wait in the soil. Perhaps a farmer's plough will turn up the next clue. Perhaps an archaeologist will find it in your lifetime. For now, the capital stays hidden. And as you walk away, every low hill on the horizon becomes, quietly, a possible answer.
