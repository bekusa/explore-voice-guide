The guide

The story

Put your hand flat against the wall in front of you. The stone is dark, almost charcoal grey, and it feels rough and porous under your fingers. This is lava stone, the volcanic rock of Mount Etna. Catania is built from it, and this castle is one of the oldest things in the city made from it. Around you, scooters hum through the square, and pigeons settle on the battlements. The walls stay silent, heavy and square. They have stood here for nearly eight hundred years.

Welcome to Castello Ursino. The fortress was commissioned by Frederick the Second of Swabia in the thirteenth century. Frederick was Holy Roman Emperor and also King of Sicily, a ruler of German and Norman descent who grew up in Palermo. His contemporaries called him the wonder of the world. He was a scholar, a falconer, and a restless builder. Across southern Italy and Sicily, he raised a network of castles to hold his kingdom together. This one guarded Catania and its harbour.

The work is usually credited to Riccardo da Lentini, a military architect who served Frederick and supervised several of his Sicilian fortresses. Look at the plan of the building as you walk around it. It is an almost perfect square. A round tower stands at each of the four corners. Originally, smaller semicircular towers also rose at the middle of each side, and a few of them still survive. The design is strict and geometric. It reflects Frederick's taste for order and symmetry.

Now here is the first surprise. When this castle was built, it stood on a rocky promontory beside the sea. Waves broke against its foundations. A moat and the water itself protected it from attack. Today, the sea is hundreds of metres away, out of sight behind streets and buildings. So what happened? The answer lies on the mountain you can see on clear days to the north.

In 1669, Mount Etna produced one of the most destructive eruptions in its recorded history. Rivers of lava flowed for weeks toward Catania. They buried villages and reached the city walls. Around this castle, the molten rock surrounded the base and poured into the sea beyond it. When the lava cooled, it had created new land. The coastline moved outward, and the fortress suddenly found itself inland. A castle built to face the sea now faced a field of black rock.

You can still see traces of that event. Walk to the edges of the moat area and look down. In places, the cooled lava is visible at the foot of the walls, rough and dark, with a frozen, rippled surface. It is a strange thing to see. Volcanic rock built into the castle, and volcanic rock wrapped around it by nature. Stone from the same mountain, joined across four centuries.

Only a few decades later, in 1693, a powerful earthquake struck eastern Sicily. Much of Catania collapsed, and thousands of people died. The city you walk through today was largely rebuilt after that disaster, in the late Baroque style, often using the same dark lava stone with pale limestone trim. This castle survived the shaking. Its thick walls and low, solid shape helped it endure. In a city that is mostly rebuilt, this is one of the few medieval structures still standing.

The castle had a long life after Frederick. In the later Middle Ages, Sicily came under the rule of the Aragonese kings from Spain. They used the fortress as a royal residence, and important political gatherings were held inside its walls. Over the following centuries, its role changed again. It served as barracks and, for a long period, as a prison. Some visitors report seeing marks scratched into the stone by those held here, so keep an eye out inside.

Now, the name. Ursino sounds like the Italian word for a bear, and you may hear guides connect it to that. Scholars are not certain where the name comes from. One popular explanation links it to the Latin words for castle of the gulf, a reference to its original seaside position. Others suggest different origins. Treat every version as a possibility rather than settled fact. The mystery is part of the charm.

Before you go inside, look up at the main entrance. Above the doorway, a carved relief shows an eagle gripping a smaller animal in its talons, often described as a hare. The eagle was the emblem of Frederick's Swabian dynasty. Some interpret the scene as a symbol of imperial power over its enemies. It is a small carving, easy to miss as you hurry through the door, but it is a direct signature of the man who built this place.

Since the 1930s, the castle has housed the Civic Museum of Catania. The collections come largely from local noble families and religious institutions. A key source is the collection of the Prince of Biscari, an eighteenth century aristocrat with a passion for antiquities. Inside, you can find classical sculpture, Greek pottery, Roman fragments, medieval pieces, and paintings. Exhibitions change from time to time, so the rooms you see may differ from earlier visits. Take your time with the ancient objects. Many were found in and around this city.

Inside, notice the contrast between the rooms and the walls. The interior spaces feel cool and quiet, even on a hot Sicilian afternoon. The thick stone keeps out the heat and much of the street noise. Light enters through narrow openings and falls in pale bands across the floors. Climb wherever access allows, and look out toward the city. You can imagine where the shoreline once ran, close enough to hear the waves.

When you step back outside, pause for a moment in the square. Turn and look at the full shape of the castle again. A square of dark stone, four round towers, a carving of an eagle above the door, and black lava resting at its base. Around it, ordinary life continues. Children run across the paving, and cafés set out their tables. As you walk away toward the old centre, notice how much of the city shares this same dark stone. Catania was built from the mountain, and this castle is one of its oldest pieces.
