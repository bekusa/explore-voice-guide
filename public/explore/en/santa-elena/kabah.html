The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the stone underfoot. It is pale limestone, warm in the sun and rough at the edges, scattered with dry leaves from the low forest around you. Somewhere a bird calls. A lizard runs across a fallen block. And ahead of you rises a wall that seems to be made entirely of faces... hundreds of long, curling stone noses staring back across the plaza.

This is Kabah, an ancient Maya city in the hills of the Yucatán Peninsula in southern Mexico. It sits in the Puuc region, a band of low, rolling ridges whose name comes from the Maya word for hills. Kabah is quieter than its famous neighbour, Uxmal. Fewer buses stop here. That quiet is part of the experience. You can often stand alone in front of these walls and listen to the wind in the trees.

Kabah reached its height in what archaeologists call the Terminal Classic period, roughly the ninth and early tenth centuries. This was a time of growth across the Puuc hills. Cities here were wealthy, connected, and ambitious in their architecture. Then, like many Maya centres of that era, Kabah declined and was gradually taken back by the forest. The meaning of its name is debated. One popular interpretation links it to the idea of a strong or skilled hand, but scholars do not agree on this.

Now turn to the building that draws almost every visitor first. It is called the Codz Poop, a later Yucatec Maya name often translated as rolled mat. Many people connect that name to the curled stone noses on its face. It is also known simply as the Palace of the Masks. Look at the long western façade. Nearly every surface, from the base to the top, is covered with repeated masks, stacked side by side and row above row.

Most of these masks are usually identified with Chaac, the Maya god of rain. Each face has deep eye sockets, a wide mouth, and a long hooked nose that curls upward or downward. Here is something many visitors miss... a great number of those noses are broken. Time, weather, and earlier collectors took many of them. Look closely and you will see the stumps where the curls once projected from the wall.

Why so much attention to the rain god? The answer is under your feet. The Puuc hills have no rivers and very few natural wells, or cenotes. The limestone here swallows rainwater quickly. People depended on underground cisterns, called chultunes, that collected water during the wet season. A drought could threaten the whole city. A wall covered in rain masks was not only decoration. It was a constant appeal to the sky.

The building also shows the classic Puuc way of building. The Maya did not stack solid blocks here. They built a core of rubble and mortar, then covered it with thin, finely cut stone veneer. On the upper levels, small carved pieces fit together like a mosaic to form masks, frets, and geometric patterns. Stand close to the wall and you can see the individual pieces. Each one was shaped by hand and set into place to complete a larger face.

Walk around to the back of the Codz Poop. This side is easy to skip, but it holds a surprise. Here, archaeologists recovered carved stone door jambs showing human figures, often described as warriors or rulers in elaborate dress. These scenes are very different from the abstract masks on the front. They show real people, or at least the image that powerful people wanted to present. They remind us that this city had kings, ceremonies, and conflict, not only gods.

In the early 1840s, the American writer John Lloyd Stephens and the English artist Frederick Catherwood travelled through the Yucatán. Their books and drawings introduced Maya cities like Kabah to readers in Europe and the United States. Stephens removed a carved wooden lintel from Kabah and shipped it north. Sadly, it was later destroyed in a fire in New York. Catherwood's careful drawings now help researchers imagine how parts of the site looked before more collapse.

Kabah is larger than it first appears. Across the modern highway, which cuts straight through the ancient city, more ruins stand in the forest. There is a large building known as the Palace, with several levels of rooms, and a great mound often called the Great Pyramid, still mostly covered with earth and trees. Much of Kabah remains unexcavated. As you walk, notice the green hills around you. Some of them are not hills at all. They are buildings waiting under the soil.

The most famous structure on that side is a freestanding stone arch. It stands alone, plain and solid, with a corbelled shape. A corbelled arch is one where each stone layer steps inward until the two sides meet at the top. This arch marks the start of a sacbe, a raised white road built by the Maya. The road ran through the forest toward Uxmal, linking the two cities. Imagine walking that pale, straight path through the trees, under a hot sky.

That road tells us something important. Kabah and Uxmal were closely connected. They likely shared trade, alliances, and ceremony. Today the Puuc Route links the ruins of this region for travellers, including Sayil, Xlapak, and Labná. In a small way, you are following an old network. The paths have changed, but the idea is the same. People moved between these cities, carrying goods, news, and ideas across the hills.

Before you leave, return once more to the wall of masks. Look at it with the light coming from a low angle. The shadows deepen in every eye socket and under every curled nose. The faces seem sharper and more alive. Listen to the forest, the insects, and maybe the distant sound of a car on the highway. Then, as you walk back toward the entrance, look down at the scattered carved stones along the path. Each one was once part of a face that looked up at the sky, waiting for rain.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to avoid the strongest midday heat.
Bring plenty of water; shade is limited across the site.
Cross the highway carefully to see the arch and Palace.
Combine your visit with Uxmal, Sayil, and Labná.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven limestone paths.
