The guide

The story

Put your hand on the stone of the steps. It is basalt, dark grey and slightly rough, and on a sunny afternoon it holds the heat like a stove. Behind you there is a village and a few vendors' tables. In front of you a row of columns rises against the sky. Beyond them the ground simply stops, and the land falls away into a deep river gorge.

This is Garni Temple, in the Kotayk region of Armenia, a short drive east of Yerevan. It looks as if it belongs in Greece or southern Italy. It has a triangular pediment, columns on every side, and a staircase climbing to a single inner room. Here, in the highlands of the South Caucasus, it is a surprise. Garni is the only standing building of its kind in Armenia. It is often described as the only Greco-Roman colonnaded temple anywhere in the former Soviet Union.

Most historians date the temple to the first century of our era. It is traditionally linked to King Tiridates the First. He was an Armenian ruler of the Arsacid dynasty who travelled to Rome and received his crown from the emperor Nero. A Greek inscription found at the site names a king Tiridates. Many scholars connect that inscription to this building, but the exact date is still debated. Some researchers argue for a later period, so treat any single precise year with caution.

Why build a Roman-style temple here? Armenia sat between two great powers, Rome to the west and Parthia to the east. Its kings moved carefully between both. Classical architecture was a shared language of prestige across the region at that time. A temple like this showed that an Armenian king belonged to the wider Mediterranean world, while still ruling his own mountain kingdom.

The usual view is that the temple was dedicated to Mihr, the Armenian sun god. Mihr is related to the Persian deity Mithra. That explains why the building faces the open sky so boldly. However, not everyone agrees. One scholarly theory suggests the building was actually a tomb, perhaps for a royal figure, and only looks like a temple. The question is not fully settled, and that uncertainty is part of the place.

Now look at the staircase you are standing near. The steps are unusually tall, far taller than a comfortable human stride. People often say that this was intentional, so that worshippers would feel small as they approached the god. That is an interpretation, not a recorded fact. Either way, the climb makes you slow down. Notice how your pace changes as you go up.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Armenia adopted Christianity as a state religion in the early fourth century, and is widely considered the first kingdom to do so. In that period, pagan temples across the country were destroyed. Garni survived. A common explanation is that the site was also a royal summer residence, so the building may have been kept for other uses. Whatever the reason, it is one of very few pagan monuments in Armenia to outlast that change.

It did not survive the earth, though. In 1679 a powerful earthquake struck the region, and the temple collapsed. For nearly three centuries its stones lay scattered across the promontory. Travellers and scholars sketched the ruins. They measured the fallen blocks and wondered what the building had once looked like.

The temple you see today was rebuilt in the Soviet period, mainly in the late 1960s and early 1970s. The method is called anastylosis. It means that a ruin is put back together using its own original stones wherever possible. New stone is added only where pieces are missing. And here is the second surprise. If you look closely, you can tell old from new. The original blocks carry carving and weathering. The replacement blocks are plainer and smoother, and often lighter in tone.

Walk slowly around the colonnade. The capitals at the tops of the columns are in the Ionic style, with curling spiral scrolls. That is a classical Greek form. Above them, the frieze is carved with acanthus leaves and other plant motifs. Look for small lion heads along the cornice, too. In the original design they probably served as spouts for rainwater. The detail is fine and patient work, especially in a stone as hard as basalt.

Just beside the temple, look for the remains of a bathhouse. It is a low structure of walls and channels, and it is thought to be later than the temple. Inside is a mosaic floor made of small coloured stones. It shows sea creatures and figures from Greek mythology, along with a Greek inscription. A modern shelter protects it. Nearby are the foundations of a medieval church and the traces of the old fortress walls that once closed off this triangle of land.

Now go to the edge of the promontory and look down. Far below, the Azat River runs through the gorge. On its walls are tall basalt columns, grey and six-sided, packed together like organ pipes. Locals often call this stretch the Symphony of Stones. The columns formed naturally as ancient lava cooled and cracked. A road and footpaths lead down into the gorge, if you have the time and the legs for it.

The temple is not only a museum piece. Followers of a modern Armenian pagan revival gather here for ceremonies, especially around midsummer and other seasonal festivals. You may see people in white robes, or hear chanting at certain times of year. Musicians sometimes play the duduk, a soft, reedy Armenian woodwind instrument, near the steps. For many Armenians, Garni is a link to the world before Christianity.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment at the foot of the steps. Feel the warm stone again. Listen to the wind coming up from the gorge, and the low voices of other visitors. Look at the columns one more time, the darker old stones beside the paler new ones. They hold two thousand years in one frame... an earthquake, a long silence, and a patient rebuilding. As you walk back toward the village, turn around once. The temple looks small against the mountains, but it has stayed.

Before you go

Practical tips

Arrive early to see the colonnade before tour groups.
Wear sturdy shoes for the tall steps and gorge paths.
Allow extra time to walk down to the Symphony of Stones.
Bring water and sun protection on the exposed promontory.
Combine your visit with nearby Geghard Monastery up the valley.
