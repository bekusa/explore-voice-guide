The guide

The story

The ground here is flat and quiet. Rice fields and sugarcane run out toward a pale horizon, and the air smells of damp earth and woodsmoke. Somewhere a bicycle bell rings on a village lane. Then, rising out of the farmland, you see it: a low, rounded mound of old red brick. It does not look like much at first. But this modest hill of fired clay is one of the most debated and most moving places in the Buddhist world.

You are standing at Piprahwa, a village in the Siddharthnagar district of Uttar Pradesh, in northern India. The border with Nepal lies only a short distance to the north. Lumbini, the place where tradition says the Buddha was born, is not far away. This whole stretch of the Terai is the old homeland of the Shakyas. The Terai is the low, fertile plain at the foot of the Himalayas, and the Shakyas were the clan into which Siddhartha Gautama was born.

The structure in front of you is a stupa. A stupa is a solid, dome-shaped monument built to hold sacred relics. You cannot go inside one. Instead, it holds its treasure deep within, sealed in brick. Early Buddhists walked around stupas in a clockwise direction as an act of respect, and pilgrims still do the same today. As you look at the mound, imagine it once larger and smoother, perhaps plastered, rising above the fields as a beacon of memory.

For centuries, this mound was simply part of the landscape. Then, in 1898, a British estate manager named William Claxton Peppé decided to dig into it. Peppé managed land on the nearby Birdpur estate. He was not a trained archaeologist, and his methods were rough by modern standards. His workers cut a trench down through the brickwork, layer after layer. Deep inside, they struck something hard — a massive coffer carved from a single block of sandstone.

When the heavy lid was lifted, the box held several small vessels. There were urns and a delicate jar of soapstone, along with hundreds of tiny offerings. These included gems of crystal and amethyst, beads, pieces of gold leaf, and small cut stars and flowers. There were also fragments of bone. One vessel carried a short line of writing around its rim. It was written in Brahmi, one of the oldest scripts used in India.

That single inscription is the heart of the story. Many scholars read it as saying that these are relics of the Buddha, enshrined by the Shakyas, his own kinsmen, together with their families. If that reading is right, it would be one of the earliest written records connected to the Buddha himself. But the exact wording has been argued over for more than a century. Experts still debate the meaning of some words and the date of the writing. Both sides agree on one thing, though: the find is extraordinary.

What happened next carried the relics far from this field. The bone fragments were presented to King Chulalongkorn of Siam, the country we now call Thailand. He was one of the few Buddhist monarchs ruling at the time. Portions were later shared with Buddhist communities in other countries. Meanwhile, many of the jewels went to museums in India, and some remained with the Peppé family. So the contents of this one brick mound are now spread across several nations.

The story did not end in the nineteenth century. In the early 1970s, the Archaeological Survey of India returned to Piprahwa and dug deeper than Peppé had gone. Beneath the level of his discovery, the team found older chambers containing more caskets and bone fragments. This showed that the stupa had been built and enlarged in several phases. Each phase was a new layer of brick wrapped around an older core, like the rings of a tree.

The same excavations explored the ruins around the stupa, including a site called Ganwaria nearby. There, archaeologists found the brick foundations of monasteries. They also found clay seals that, according to the excavators, mention a monastery of Kapilavastu. Kapilavastu was the capital of the Shakya kingdom, the city where the young prince Siddhartha is said to have grown up. Based on these finds, Indian archaeologists proposed that Piprahwa and its surroundings mark the site of that ancient city.

Here comes the surprise that many visitors miss. Just across the border, in Nepal, another site called Tilaurakot makes the same claim. It has its own walls, gateways, and supporters among historians. So two countries, a short drive apart, each point to their own ruins as the Buddha's childhood home. The question is still open. As you stand here, you are inside one of archaeology's longest and most courteous arguments.

And there is one more twist. In 2025, a large collection of the Piprahwa gems held by the Peppé family appeared at an auction house in Hong Kong. The Indian government objected strongly, arguing that sacred relics should not be sold like ordinary art. The sale was stopped. Soon after, the gems were acquired and brought back to India. More than a century after they left this mound, these tiny crystals and gold flowers came home.

Now look again at the bricks themselves. They are wide and flat, much broader than modern bricks, and their colour runs from rust to deep orange. Notice how the courses are laid in neat horizontal lines. You can see the edges of the old trenches where the excavators cut into the core. Around the main mound, low brick outlines mark the plans of monastery rooms and walkways. Monks once lived, studied, and slept in those small cells.

Before you leave, take a slow walk around the stupa, clockwise, the way pilgrims have done for many centuries. Listen to the birds in the trees and the distant sounds of the village. Look out across the fields toward the north, where the mountains hide behind the haze. Whether or not this was Kapilavastu, people came here to remember a teacher. Their bricks are still under your feet, quiet and warm in the sun.

Before you go

Practical tips

Combine your visit with Lumbini across the nearby Nepal border.
Come early morning for softer light and cooler air.
Walk around the stupa clockwise, as pilgrims traditionally do.
Bring water and sun protection; shade near the mound is limited.
Visit the Ganwaria monastery ruins close to the stupa.
