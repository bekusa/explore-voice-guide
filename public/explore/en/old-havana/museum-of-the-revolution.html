The guide

The story

Stand for a moment at the foot of the front steps and listen. Traffic hums along the wide avenue behind you, and somewhere a vintage car coughs past. In front of you rises a pale stone palace, heavy with columns and carved ornament. Above it all sits a dome covered in glazed tiles that catch the Caribbean sun. It looks like a building made for ceremonies and grand receptions. For decades, that is exactly what it was.

This is the Museum of the Revolution, and the building came before the museum. It was built in the early twentieth century as the Presidential Palace of the Republic of Cuba. Construction ran through the 1910s, and the palace opened around 1920. A Belgian architect, Paul Belau, worked on the design with a Cuban partner, Carlos Maruri. Their goal was to give a young republic a seat of power that could stand beside the palaces of Europe.

Notice the style. It is eclectic, which means the builders mixed elements from several European traditions instead of following just one. You can see classical columns, decorative balconies and heavy cornices, the projecting ledges along the roofline. The interior was decorated with help from Tiffany Studios in New York, the firm known for its glass and luxury design. That detail surprises many visitors. A building now dedicated to the revolution was furnished by one of America's most fashionable design houses.

For almost four decades, Cuba's presidents governed from these rooms. They signed laws here, received ambassadors here, and gave speeches from the balconies to crowds below. The last president to use the palace this way was Fulgencio Batista. He first came to power in the 1930s, and later returned through a military coup in 1952. His second period in power was harsh. Opposition grew in the universities, in the cities, and finally in the mountains of eastern Cuba.

Now comes one of the most dramatic days in this building's life. On 13 March 1957, a group of armed students and young activists attacked the palace. They belonged to the Revolutionary Directorate, a student-led opposition movement. Their plan was to reach Batista inside and end his rule in one blow. They forced their way into the building and fought up the stairs. But Batista escaped, and the attack failed. Many of the attackers were killed that day, as was their leader, José Antonio Echeverría, in a separate action nearby.

Here is a detail many visitors walk straight past. When you go inside, look closely at the walls near the grand marble staircase. Bullet marks from that attack are still visible in the stone. They were left deliberately, as a reminder. It is a strange thing to see — polished marble, elegant railings, and then small, rough scars from gunfire. The palace carries its own wounds on display.

Less than two years later, the revolution led by Fidel Castro took power. Batista fled Cuba at the very start of 1959. The palace lost its role as the seat of government. In the years that followed, the new state turned the building into a museum about the struggle that had ended the old regime. So you are inside a double story. It is the house of the defeated government, now used to tell the story of the people who defeated it.

The exhibition rooms move through Cuban history with a clear point of view. You will see photographs, documents, weapons, uniforms and personal belongings of revolutionary fighters. Some objects are surprisingly ordinary — a worn shirt, a notebook, a pair of boots. Keep in mind that this is a state museum. It presents the official Cuban account of these events. Reading the displays is also a way to understand how the Cuban state wants its own history remembered.

Watch for the grand ceremonial rooms as you walk. One of them is known as the Hall of Mirrors, and its design looks back to the famous hall at Versailles, the royal palace outside Paris. Gilded details, tall mirrors and painted ceilings remain from the presidential years. The contrast is part of the experience. Glass cases full of rifles and battle maps stand inside rooms built for formal dinners and diplomatic dances.

There is one more surprise, and it sits behind the building. In a large glass pavilion stands the Granma, the small motor yacht that carried Fidel Castro and his men from Mexico to Cuba in late 1956. Around eighty men crowded aboard a boat built for far fewer people. The crossing was rough, and the landing went badly. Yet the expedition became the starting point of the guerrilla war in the mountains. Today the yacht has become one of the most powerful symbols of the revolution.

Around the pavilion you will also find military vehicles and aircraft linked to the revolution and its aftermath, including events like the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961. Some of these machines look almost improvised up close. Near the front of the palace, look for a fragment of the old city wall of Havana. The Spanish colonial authorities built the walls to protect the port, and most of them were later taken down as the city grew.

Inside, one small corner tends to make visitors laugh. Locals know it as the Corner of the Cretins. It shows caricatures of figures the revolution saw as its enemies, including Batista and several American presidents. The humour is sharp and very political. Whatever you think of the message, it tells you something real. In Cuba, history is not only written in heavy official language. It can also be mocked, cartooned and argued over.

When you step back outside, pause again at the top of the steps. Look up at the tiled dome once more and then across the avenue toward the old town. The light here changes quickly, from white midday glare to warm gold in the late afternoon. Behind you are the marble stairs with their bullet marks. Ahead of you are the streets where ordinary Havana life carries on. Take that contrast with you as you walk away — a palace of presidents, a museum of rebels, and a city still living with both stories.

Before you go

Practical tips

Check current access before visiting; restoration work has closed sections.
Visit the Granma pavilion behind the palace after the main rooms.
Bring water and shade; the outdoor pavilion area gets very hot.
Allow extra time to read the displays as official history.
Combine your visit with the nearby Cuban fine arts museum.
