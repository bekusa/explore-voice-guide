The guide

The story

Listen first. On a quiet morning on Wenceslas Hill, the loudest sound is often pigeons on the stone ledges, then the low hum of the city below. The cobbles here are uneven and worn smooth. Above you, three dark spires rise into the sky. The tallest one is so high that you have to lean back to see where it ends. This is Saint Wenceslas Cathedral, the mother church of Moravia. It is older than it looks... and in some places, much younger.

That contradiction is the key to this building. Almost everything you see on the outside comes from the late nineteenth century. Its pointed arches, sharp pinnacles and stone tracery are Gothic Revival, a style that copied medieval Gothic churches several hundred years after the originals. But the hill under your feet has held a church for about nine hundred years. So this cathedral is a newer coat on a very old body. As you walk around it, you can find places where the old body still shows through.

Let us begin with the hill itself. This rise above the river Morava was the site of Olomouc's castle in the early Middle Ages. Princes and bishops lived side by side up here. In the eleventh century, a bishopric was established in Olomouc. In the twelfth century, under Bishop Jindřich Zdík, the bishop's seat moved to this hill. A Romanesque basilica was built here, and it was dedicated to Saint Wenceslas. Romanesque means the older style of round arches and thick walls that came before Gothic.

Saint Wenceslas himself was a tenth century Duke of Bohemia. He was killed by his own brother, and he soon became a saint and a national symbol for the Czech lands. You may know his name from the English Christmas carol about "Good King Wenceslas". The carol is a later invention, but the man was real. Dedicating Moravia's main church to him was a clear statement. This cathedral belonged to the wider Czech story, not only to the local one.

Over the following centuries, the Romanesque church did not stay the same. Fires, wars and changing tastes brought rebuilding again and again. Gothic parts were added. Later, Baroque decoration arrived, with its curves and gilding. By the nineteenth century, the building was a mix of many periods. Then the archbishops decided to give it one unified appearance. Between the 1880s and early 1890s, the cathedral was rebuilt in the Gothic Revival style. The architect most associated with this work is Gustav Meretta.

Now look up at the main tower on the south side. It rises to roughly one hundred metres. It is the tallest church tower in Moravia, and one of the tallest in the whole Czech Republic. Here is a detail that many visitors miss. From much of the old town, this tower is easy to see above the roofs. It works almost like a compass. If you ever lose your way in Olomouc's lanes, look for this spire, and you will know where the eastern edge of the old centre is.

Before you go inside, turn to the buildings next to the cathedral. Olomouc's darkest royal story happened here. In 1306, the young King Wenceslas the Third was murdered in Olomouc. He was the last male ruler of the Přemyslid dynasty, the family that had ruled Bohemia for centuries. The killing took place in the deanery beside the cathedral, while the king was preparing a military campaign. Nobody knows for certain who planned it. His death ended a royal line and opened the way for new dynasties.

Step through the doors, and the light changes completely. Outside, the stone is grey and sharp. Inside, the long nave pulls your eye forward towards the altar. Coloured light falls from the stained glass and lies across the floor in soft patches. The air is cooler, and sounds become slower. A cough or a footstep travels a long way. Stand still for a moment near the entrance. Let your eyes adjust before you start reading the details.

Now the second surprise. Under the bright nineteenth century surfaces, older layers survive. The crypt beneath the church is the place where this is easiest to feel. It is lower, darker, and plainer than the space above. Here you are closer to the medieval building, in both age and atmosphere. The crypt has also been used to display religious treasures and to remember the bishops of Olomouc. When it is open, it is worth the walk down the stairs.

The cathedral is also linked to a more recent event. In 1995, Pope John Paul the Second came to Olomouc. During that visit, he declared Saint John Sarkander a saint. Sarkander was a priest from this region who died in the early seventeenth century, in a time of religious conflict. Locals still see the visit as an important moment after the end of communist rule. For many older people in the city, it was a sign that faith could again be practised openly.

When you come back outside, take a slow walk around the whole hill. Right beside the cathedral stands a building with very old Romanesque windows, small paired openings with slim columns between them. It is often called the Zdík Palace, after the twelfth century bishop. The neighbouring complex also houses the Archdiocesan Museum, which shows art and objects from the church's long history. It is a separate visit, but it helps explain what this hill once meant.

The square around you is Wenceslas Square, quieter and greener than the busy main squares in the centre. Students walk through here, because part of the university is close by. Olomouc is an old university town, and you will hear many young voices in the cafés nearby. This mix is part of the character of the hill. A place of bishops and kings is now also a short cut between lectures.

Before you leave, stand back far enough to see the full front of the cathedral. Notice how every line points upward: the spires, the pinnacles, the pointed windows. Then look down at the old cobbles under your shoes, and the low medieval walls beside you. Up and down, new and old, they sit together on this one small hill. As you walk back towards the town, turn around once more. The tall tower will still be there above the roofs... showing you the way you came.

Practical tips
