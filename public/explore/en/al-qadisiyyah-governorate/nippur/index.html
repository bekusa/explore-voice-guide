The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Out here, the wind moves across the mounds with a dry, papery sound. It lifts fine grey dust and drops it again. Under your feet the ground crunches with broken pottery, small curved pieces of fired clay scattered everywhere. Each one was once part of a jar, a bowl, or a lamp. You are standing on a city made almost entirely of earth, and the earth here is full of the people who lived on it.

This is Nippur. Local people have long called the mounds Nuffar. To the casual eye, it looks like a group of low, eroded hills rising out of the flat plain of southern Iraq. But for thousands of years this was one of the most sacred places in the ancient Near East. It was not the largest city in Sumer, and it was rarely a political capital. Its power was different. It was the home of a god.

That god was Enlil. In Sumerian belief, Enlil was the lord of the air and wind, and one of the most powerful deities in the pantheon. His great temple here was called the Ekur, a name often translated as the Mountain House. Kings from rival cities across Mesopotamia looked to Nippur for approval. A ruler who controlled other cities still wanted Enlil's blessing here. In a sense, this city gave kingship its stamp of legitimacy.

Look for the tallest mound on the site. That is the remains of the ziggurat, the stepped temple tower that rose beside the Ekur. A ziggurat was a solid, terraced platform of brick, climbing in stages toward a shrine at the top. The version whose core still stands was built by Ur-Nammu, a king of the Third Dynasty of Ur, around the end of the third millennium before Christ. Later rulers repaired and rebuilt it many times.

Here is something many visitors miss. The ziggurat's inner core is sun-dried mud brick. That kind of brick is cheap and easy to make, but rain and wind slowly wear it down. For the outer skin, builders often used baked brick, which is much harder. Some of these baked bricks were stamped with the name of the king who paid for the work. Kings wanted the god, and later generations, to know who had honoured Enlil's house.

The city had a river running through its life. A branch of the Euphrates once flowed past Nippur, and a canal ran right through the middle of the settlement, splitting it into two halves. Today the water has moved away and the land is dry. But try to picture boats, reeds, date palms, and the smell of wet mud along the banks. Without that water, none of this would have been possible.

Nippur was also a city of scribes. Excavators have recovered tens of thousands of clay tablets written in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script pressed into wet clay with a reed stylus. Many of them came from schools and houses where students copied out hymns, myths, proverbs, and laments. A large share of the Sumerian literature known today survives because young scribes here practised their lessons on clay. Their homework became our library.

One tablet from Nippur is especially remarkable. It is a map of the city itself, drawn on clay more than three thousand years ago. It marks the temple, the walls, the gates, the canals, and the river. When modern archaeologists compared it with the mounds, the layout matched in important places. Imagine holding a city plan made by someone who walked these same streets, and finding that it still works.

The modern story of Nippur begins in the late nineteenth century. An expedition from the University of Pennsylvania began digging here in 1889 and worked through several seasons in very hard conditions. They faced heat, disease, and conflict with local tribes. Much later, from 1948, the University of Chicago's Oriental Institute returned with Pennsylvania and continued the work over many decades. Much of what we know about Nippur comes from these long campaigns.

The city did not end with the Sumerians. It lived on under Babylonian, Assyrian, and Persian rule. From the Persian period, archaeologists found the records of a business family known as the Murashu. Their tablets show loans, leases, and land deals in the fifth century before Christ. They give a rare, detailed picture of ordinary economic life. Long after the great hymns were written, people here still argued about rent and harvests.

Later still, the ziggurat itself changed purpose. In the Parthian period, when a dynasty from Iran ruled Mesopotamia, a fortress was built on top of the old temple mound. A holy mountain became a military stronghold. The site was also home to a Jewish community in late antiquity. Excavators found clay bowls inscribed with Aramaic spells, placed in houses to protect families from harm. People kept living here into the early Islamic centuries.

Eventually, the water moved, the farms failed, and the city was abandoned. The mounds slowly took the shape you see now. Rain carved gullies into the brick. Sand settled in the low places. Because so much of Nippur was built of earth, it returned to earth. Yet underneath, layer upon layer, the city remains. Each mound holds floors, walls, and objects stacked over thousands of years of continuous life.

As you walk, move slowly and watch the ground. You may notice lines of brick at the edge of an old trench, a change in soil colour, or a patch of pottery that is thicker than the rest. These small clues are how archaeologists read a site like this. Please leave everything where you find it. Even a tiny sherd tells part of the story only in the place where it lies.

Before you leave, stand still and look toward the ziggurat once more. Feel the wind on your face, the same wind the Sumerians believed belonged to Enlil. Notice how quiet it is now, in a place once filled with priests, scribes, and pilgrims. Listen to the dust moving across the mounds. And as you walk back across the broken pottery, remember that every step crosses the floor of someone's home.

What to look for

The tallest mound marking the ruined ziggurat.
Broken pottery sherds scattered across the ground surface.
Mud-brick layers exposed along old excavation trench walls.
Harder baked bricks on the ziggurat's outer faces.
Rain-cut gullies running down the sides of mounds.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning to avoid the fierce midday heat.
Bring plenty of water and sun protection for the mounds.
Wear sturdy closed shoes for crumbling, uneven ground.
Arrange a local guide or driver before travelling out.
Never take pottery sherds or bricks from the site.
