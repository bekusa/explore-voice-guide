The guide

The story

Listen first. Up here, the wind moves steadily across the road. It carries the dry smell of pine and warm rock from the slopes below. Under your feet, the deck has a faint, deep hum as trucks roll across. Look out past the railing and the ground simply disappears. It falls away into a green and grey canyon, so deep that the river at the bottom looks like a thin silver thread. You are standing on the Baluarte Bridge, high above the mountains of western Mexico.

This bridge crosses the gorge of the Baluarte River. That river marks part of the boundary between two Mexican states. On one side is Sinaloa, the Pacific coastal state, where the municipality of Concordia sits. On the other side is Durango, a state of high plateaus and mining towns. So, as you cross the central span, you pass from one state into the other — suspended far above the water that divides them.

The numbers here are hard to picture, so let us take them slowly. The deck sits roughly four hundred metres above the river below. That is taller than most skyscrapers on Earth. The bridge runs for more than one kilometre from end to end. Its main span — the open stretch between the two great towers — is about five hundred metres long. Nothing touches the canyon floor along that central section. It is held entirely from above.

And that brings us to the first surprise. When it opened in 2012, the Baluarte Bridge was recognised by Guinness World Records as the highest cable-stayed bridge in the world. A cable-stayed bridge is one where straight steel cables run directly from tall towers down to the road deck. Since then, newer bridges in China have climbed even higher. But for a time, this quiet canyon in the Sierra Madre held a world record.

Now look up at the towers. Two tall concrete pylons rise from the slopes of the gorge. From each one, long fans of steel cables spread outward and downward. Each cable carries part of the weight of the road. Together they work like the strings of a huge instrument, pulling the deck up and keeping it balanced. On a clear day, the cables catch the sun and shine against the dark green hills.

To understand why anyone would build something this ambitious, you need to know about the mountains around you. This is the Sierra Madre Occidental, a long and rugged range that runs down western Mexico. For generations, it stood as a wall between the port city of Mazatlán on the Pacific and the city of Durango in the interior. Crossing it was slow, tiring, and often dangerous.

The older route across these mountains has a famous nickname. Locals call it the Espinazo del Diablo — the Devil's Backbone. The name comes from a narrow stretch where the road follows a sharp ridge, with steep drops on both sides. That road twists through curve after curve. Drivers sometimes spent many hours on the journey, and fog, rain, and heavy trucks made it harder still. The Devil's Backbone road still exists, and some travellers drive it for the views.

The Baluarte Bridge is part of the newer Mazatlán to Durango highway, which was built to replace that slow mountain crossing. The engineers did not try to follow the ridges. Instead, they cut straight through. The highway uses dozens of tunnels bored into the mountains, and many bridges that leap across the valleys. This bridge is the largest and most dramatic of them all. The result is a much shorter, safer trip between the coast and the interior.

Here is the second surprise, and it is easy to miss. This bridge was not built only for sightseers or for national pride. It was built for trade. The new highway links the Pacific port of Mazatlán with northern Mexico and beyond, toward the border with the United States. So many of the trucks rolling past you are carrying goods across the continent. The bridge is a piece of infrastructure first — and a landmark second.

Building at this height was a serious challenge. The canyon walls are steep, and the wind in the gorge can be strong. Workers had to raise the towers and then extend the deck outward, piece by piece, over open air. Each new section was held by fresh cables before the next one could be added. From both sides of the canyon, the two halves of the road slowly reached toward each other until they finally met in the middle.

Take a moment now to look down into the gorge itself. The slopes are covered with pine and oak forest, broken by bare rock and pale cliffs. In the rainy season, the hills turn deep green, and clouds sometimes drift below the level of the deck. In the dry months, the colours fade to brown and gold. Birds of prey often circle in the open air of the canyon, riding the rising warm air.

If you are travelling by car, please be careful. This is an active highway with fast traffic, and stopping on the deck is not safe. Follow the local signs, and only stop in places that are clearly marked for vehicles. The best way to see the whole structure is from a distance, where you can take in the towers, the cables, and the canyon in a single view. From the deck, you feel the height. From outside, you see the shape.

So... before you move on, pause for one more breath. Feel the wind again, and notice how it changes as you look from the towers down to the river. Listen to the hum of the road and the silence of the canyon beneath it. Somewhere far below, the Baluarte River keeps running, as it did long before any road came here. As you drive on into the next tunnel, the bridge will vanish behind you — but the mountains will stay.

Before you go

Practical tips

Never stop on the bridge deck; use marked pull-offs only.
Travel in clear morning weather before mountain clouds build.
Bring a jacket; wind across the gorge can feel cold.
Drive the old Devil's Backbone road for contrast.
Use a zoom lens to frame towers against the canyon.
