The guide

The story

Before you see the whole figure, you feel the climb. The stone steps rise steadily under your feet. The air in these hills of western Henan often carries a soft haze, and the light comes through it gently. Then you lift your eyes. A golden face, calm and enormous, looks out over the valley. The sound of the wind is the loudest thing here. The statue makes no sound at all, and somehow that silence feels large too.

You are standing below the Spring Temple Buddha, in Lushan County, part of the wider Pingdingshan region of Henan Province. The statue itself is about 128 metres tall. Add the lotus throne and the pedestal below it, and the whole monument reaches roughly 208 metres. For about a decade it was the tallest statue on Earth. It held that title until 2018, when India completed its Statue of Unity in Gujarat.

The figure shows Vairocana Buddha. In Mahayana Buddhism, the tradition followed across much of East Asia, Vairocana is often understood as a cosmic Buddha. He represents a kind of universal light, the idea that awakening shines everywhere at once. That meaning helps explain the scale. This is not a portrait of a single teacher walking a dusty road. It is an attempt to give shape to something boundless, and the builders chose height as their language.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. The name does not come from a temple called Spring. It comes from water. The nearby Foquan Temple takes its name from hot springs in this valley. Foquan roughly means Buddha Spring. Warm mineral water has risen from the ground here for a very long time, and the springs gave the area its reputation long before any modern statue stood on the hill.

Construction began in the late 1990s and the statue was completed in 2008. That is a long project, and the slow pace makes sense once you understand how it was built. The figure is not carved from a single cliff. It is assembled from more than a thousand cast copper pieces, fitted together over an inner structure. The surface was then covered with gold, which is why the face glows warmly on clear days.

Now the second surprise. The hill itself was reshaped. Around the time the statue was finished, the mountain beneath it was carved into tiers, so the landscape became part of the pedestal. When you look up from the lower levels, notice how the slope steps upward in broad platforms. Nature and construction meet so smoothly that it is hard to say where the hill ends and the monument begins.

Take a moment with the face. At this size, sculptors face a real problem. Proportions that look right at human height can look strange from far below. The features here are designed to be read from a great distance. The eyes look downward, toward the people climbing. The expression is quiet and settled. From the base, the gaze seems to meet yours, whichever side you stand on.

Look next at the hands. In Buddhist art, hand positions carry meaning. They are called mudras, a word for symbolic gestures. One raised open palm is widely read as a sign of reassurance, a gesture that says there is no need to fear. Even if you do not know the exact meaning intended here, the gesture is clear in any language. It is an open hand, held out over the valley.

The lotus throne under the figure is worth its own look. The lotus grows from muddy water yet opens clean and bright above the surface. For this reason it became one of the most common symbols in Buddhist art, a picture of the mind rising out of confusion. Here the petals are rendered at enormous size. Up close, they feel less like a flower and more like a curved golden wall.

The climb is part of the experience, and it is meant to be. Many pilgrims treat the ascent as an act of devotion rather than a chore. You may see people pausing on the steps, bowing, or lighting incense at the shrines along the way. The smell of incense drifts across the terraces on still days. Move gently around these moments. For some visitors this is a holiday stop, but for others it is a sacred journey.

The wider valley rewards a slower pace. Away from the main axis, the grounds open up into quieter corners, with temple halls, bells, and smaller images of the Buddha. The surrounding hills are green in the warmer months and paler in winter. From the higher terraces you can look back the way you came and see the scale of the whole complex laid out below, small figures moving up the long stairway like a thread.

It is fair to ask why such a project was built here, in a rural county rather than a famous city. Part of the answer is the old reputation of the springs and the temple tradition attached to them. Part of it is the modern wish, seen across China and beyond, to draw pilgrims and travellers through large religious landmarks. Both motives sit together on this hillside, and visitors often feel both at once.

Before you leave, stop at the bottom of the steps and turn around one last time. Notice how the gold changes with the light, warmer as the day moves on. Listen for the wind across the terraces, the shuffle of footsteps, perhaps a bell from one of the halls. The face is still looking down, calm and patient. As you walk away, the statue grows smaller behind you... but for a long while, it will still be watching the valley.

Before you go

Practical tips

Wear comfortable shoes for the long stairway climb.
Bring water, especially on warm summer afternoons.
Come later in the day for warmer light on the gold.
Keep quiet near pilgrims bowing on the steps.
Allow extra time to explore the quieter temple halls.
