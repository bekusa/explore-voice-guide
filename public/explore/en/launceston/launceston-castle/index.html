The guide

The story

Start with the climb. The path up the mound is steep, and your legs will feel it before your eyes have taken anything in. Grass rises on either side, cropped short and very green. Then the wind meets you near the top. It comes across open country, cool and steady, and it carries the faint smell of wet earth and distant fields. Up here, the town drops away below you. You are standing on one of the oldest seats of power in Cornwall.

This is Launceston Castle. Before the stone, before the towers, there was earth. What you climbed is a motte. That is a large artificial mound, piled up by hand to lift a fortress above the land around it. At its foot lay the bailey, an enclosed yard for soldiers, horses, and stores. This pairing, mound and yard together, was the classic Norman way to hold newly conquered ground. Here it still shapes everything you see.

The story begins soon after 1066. William the Conqueror had won England, and now he needed loyal men to keep it. One of the most powerful was his half-brother, Robert, Count of Mortain. Robert received huge estates in the south-west, including much of Cornwall. A castle at this spot gave him a firm grip on the region. The first fortress was almost certainly built of timber and earth. It was quick to raise, and it sent a clear message to the people below.

Why here? Look out toward the east. Not far away runs the River Tamar, the old boundary between Cornwall and Devon. For centuries, this crossing country was the main gateway into Cornwall from the rest of England. A castle on high ground near that border could watch the roads and control movement in and out. In medieval times, the settlement around the castle was known as Dunheved. Over time, the name of a nearby place, Launceston, came to cover the whole town.

Now turn to the stone above you. Over the following centuries, the wooden defences gave way to masonry. On top of the mound stands a shell keep. That is a ring of stone wall that wraps around the summit like a crown. And inside that ring rises a second structure — a tall round tower. A tower standing inside a shell keep is unusual in England. It gives the castle its strange, layered silhouette, one circle nested within another.

Much of this rebuilding is linked to Richard, Earl of Cornwall, in the thirteenth century. Richard was the younger brother of King Henry the Third. He was one of the richest men in Europe, and he spent freely on his Cornish castles. The high tower is generally credited to his time. Picture it newly finished. Fresh stone, sharp edges, and a view that let its keepers see riders long before they arrived. It was built to be noticed from miles away.

Here is something many visitors miss. Launceston Castle was not only a fortress. For a long period, it worked as the administrative heart of Cornwall. Courts sat here. Officials collected dues and kept records. Launceston served as the county town for centuries, and this castle was the reason. So when you imagine life inside these walls, picture clerks and judges as well as soldiers. Paperwork, arguments, and verdicts filled these spaces more often than battles did.

And where there were courts, there was a prison. Over the centuries, the castle gaol held many people, and its reputation was grim. Some of the cells were said to be filthy, cold, and cramped. As the military role faded, the jail remained. In fact, by the sixteenth century, writers described much of the castle as decaying. Yet the prison part kept working. A building that once projected royal power slowly became a place of confinement and fear.

Two prisoners from that history are still remembered today. The first is Cuthbert Mayne, a Catholic priest. He was arrested in Cornwall in the 1570s, during the reign of Queen Elizabeth the First, when Catholic priests were treated as enemies of the state. He was held at Launceston and executed in the town in 1577. He is often described as the first seminary priest to be executed in England. Centuries later, the Catholic Church declared him a saint.

The second prisoner is George Fox, the founder of the Quakers, a Christian movement known for plain speech and silent worship. In 1656, Fox and some companions were jailed here. They were held in a notorious part of the prison known as Doomsdale. Fox later wrote about the conditions, and his account is not easy reading. The name alone tells you something. Two men of very different faiths, both locked inside this same hill, both remembered long after their jailers were forgotten.

The castle also saw war. During the English Civil War in the seventeenth century, Cornwall was a stronghold for the King. Launceston, sitting right on the border, changed hands more than once as the fighting moved back and forth. After that conflict, the fortress never again played a serious military role. Over time, stone was taken away, roofs fell in, and the grass crept over the foundations. What remains today is a ruin, cared for by English Heritage.

As you walk the site, notice how the layers sit on top of one another. Down in the bailey, low outlines of walls mark where buildings once stood. Gateways still frame the approach from the town. Up on the motte, the stone of the shell keep feels rough under your hand, patched and weathered. Look closely at the walls. You will see differences in the stone and in the style of the work, the marks of repairs and changes over many centuries.

Now take a moment at the top. Turn slowly in a full circle. To one side, the rooftops and church towers of the town crowd close around the hill. To the other, the land opens into rolling fields and dark lines of hedgerow. On a clear day, the high moors sit on the horizon. This is the same view that Norman lords, royal officials, and frightened prisoners all looked out on. The roads have changed. The shape of the land has not.

Before you go down, listen for a moment. The wind is still moving across the summit. Below, you may hear traffic, a dog barking, voices from the green. Life in the town carries on at the foot of this hill, as it has for nearly a thousand years. As you walk back down the steep path, look back once over your shoulder. That ring of stone against the sky is how travellers have known they reached Cornwall for a very long time.
