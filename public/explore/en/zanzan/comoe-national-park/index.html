The guide

The story

Listen first. Before you look for anything, simply listen. There is the dry rustle of tall grass moving in the wind, the call of a bird you cannot yet see, and somewhere below the trees the slow sound of moving water. The air smells of warm earth and crushed leaves. The ground underfoot is red laterite, a hard iron-rich soil, packed and dusty in the dry months. This is Comoé National Park, in the far northeast of Côte d'Ivoire, and it is very large.

Comoé is one of the largest protected areas in all of West Africa. It covers more than eleven thousand square kilometres. That is an area bigger than some small countries. If you drove from one edge to the other, you would cross several kinds of landscape. Open savanna gives way to patches of dry woodland. Then come dark ribbons of forest that follow the rivers. Granite hills rise above it all here and there. This mix of landscapes is the reason the park matters so much.

The park takes its name from the Comoé River, and the river is its backbone. It enters from the north and runs through the park for a long distance. From here it continues south across the whole country, to the Atlantic coast near the old colonial town of Grand-Bassam. In the wet season the river swells and spreads. In the dry season it shrinks back into deep pools and sandy banks. Animals follow those changes. So do the people who study them.

Here is something many visitors miss. The forests along the river are called gallery forests. A gallery forest is a narrow band of dense, tall trees that grows along a waterway in otherwise open country. From above, it looks like a green corridor cut through golden grass. Inside, the light drops and the air feels cooler and damp. These corridors let forest species live much further north than they normally could. A monkey can find shade and fruit here, only a short walk from dry savanna.

This region was protected for a long time before it became a national park. Hunting reserves existed here in the colonial period. National park status came in the late 1960s, in the first decade after Ivorian independence. In 1983, UNESCO added Comoé to its World Heritage List. The same year it was recognised as a biosphere reserve. UNESCO pointed to the wide range of habitats along the river, because few places in the region hold so many in one landscape.

The story did not stay simple. In the early 2000s, Côte d'Ivoire went through years of political crisis and armed conflict. The north of the country was cut off from government control for a long period. Park patrols became difficult. Poaching rose, and cattle herds moved into protected land. In 2003, UNESCO placed Comoé on its List of World Heritage in Danger. Many observers feared that the large animals would not survive.

Then something unusual happened. After peace returned, rangers, the national parks authority, and international partners rebuilt protection across the park. Wildlife surveys began to show recovery. In 2017, UNESCO removed Comoé from the danger list. That is a rare outcome. Many sites stay on the list for decades. So the quiet you hear around you now is not only natural. It is also the result of years of hard and often dangerous work.

What might you see? The park is home to African elephants, although they are shy and not often seen. Buffalo graze in the open areas. You may see antelope such as kob and hartebeest, which have long faces and sloping backs. Hippos rest in the deeper river pools during the day. Watch the water closely, because two kinds of crocodile live here. One of them, the slender-snouted crocodile, has a long, thin jaw, and it is now rare across most of Africa.

Now for a second surprise. Chimpanzees live in Comoé. Most people imagine chimpanzees in thick rainforest, not in savanna country like this. Here they survive in the forest patches and move across open ground between them. Researchers have studied how these chimpanzees cope with heat, dry seasons, and long distances. If you hear a sudden burst of hooting and screaming from the trees, you may be close to a group. Stay still and quiet. Let them decide how close they want to be.

Look up as often as you look down. Comoé is excellent for birds. Hundreds of species have been recorded across its habitats. Along the river you may see kingfishers flashing blue and orange above the water. Storks and herons move slowly through the shallows. Birds of prey circle high over the grassland on warm air. In the early morning the noise from the trees can be very loud, as the forest birds call before the heat of the day.

The human landscape around the park matters too. Zanzan is a remote district, far from the busy southern coast. Towns such as Bouna lie near the park's edge. This area is home to several communities, including the Lobi people. The Lobi are known for their traditional farming compounds and their wooden carved figures. Farming, herding, and village life continue right up to the park boundary. Its future depends on good relations between conservation and these neighbours.

Come here with patience. Comoé is not a zoo, and it is not a place of easy sightings. The grass can grow taller than a person, and animals disappear into it in seconds. Roads are rough, and in the wet season some tracks become impassable. But that is part of the experience. You move slowly. You learn to notice a fresh footprint in the red dust, a bent branch, or a ripple in a still pool.

Before you move on, take a moment where you are. Notice the colour of the grass and how far you can see across it. Feel the heat in the ground or the cooler air near the trees. Listen again to the birds, the insects, and the river. Few places in West Africa still sound like this. As you leave, remember how close this park came to losing its animals. Everything you see here is still alive because people chose to protect it.

Before you go

Practical tips

Plan your visit for the dry season when tracks are passable.
Hire a local guide or ranger for game drives.
Head out at dawn when animals and birds are active.
Bring binoculars for distant river pools and treetops.
Carry plenty of water, sun protection, and insect repellent.
