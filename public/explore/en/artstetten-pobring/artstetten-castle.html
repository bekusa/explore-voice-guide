The guide

The story

Listen for a moment. Above the rooftops of this small Lower Austrian village, the wind moves through a cluster of towers. Their caps are rounded and pointed, shaped like onions, and they catch the light in a way that sets this castle apart from the hills around it. Beneath your feet the ground rises gently from the Danube valley. The air smells of cut grass and orchards. This is Artstetten Castle, and it holds one of the most moving stories of twentieth-century Europe.

Before the history, take in the setting. The castle sits on a rise in the region locals call the Nibelungengau. The name comes from the medieval Song of the Nibelungs, a German epic poem whose heroes travel along this stretch of the Danube. The river lies a short drive to the south. Around you are fields, woods and small farms. It is a quiet place. That quiet matters, because it is the reason the castle was chosen for what happened here.

The building did not begin as a royal residence. A castle has stood on this site for centuries, and it has passed through the hands of many noble families. Its shape changed with each generation of owners. Walls were added, wings were rebuilt, towers were raised. What you see today is the result of many rounds of building and repair, rather than a single grand design from one moment in time.

In the nineteenth century, Artstetten came into the possession of the Habsburgs, the dynasty that ruled the Austrian Empire for centuries. Emperor Franz the First acquired it in the 1820s. Later it passed to Archduke Karl Ludwig, a younger brother of Emperor Franz Joseph. And through Karl Ludwig, it came to the man whose name is now tied to this place forever — his eldest son, Archduke Franz Ferdinand.

Franz Ferdinand was heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne. He was known as a difficult, determined man, and a passionate hunter. But his life was shaped by one decision above all. He fell in love with Countess Sophie Chotek, a woman from an old Bohemian noble family. To the strict rules of the Habsburg court, her rank was not high enough. A future emperor was expected to marry a princess from a ruling house.

Franz Ferdinand refused to give her up. In 1900 the couple married, but only after he accepted harsh conditions. The marriage was declared morganatic — a legal term meaning that the wife and children of a royal husband do not share his titles or rights. Their children could never inherit the throne. At court ceremonies, Sophie often had to walk far behind her husband, sometimes alone, at the end of the line.

Here is the first detail many visitors miss. That rule of rank followed Sophie even into death. Habsburg rulers are traditionally buried in the Imperial Crypt in Vienna, beneath the Capuchin Church. Franz Ferdinand knew that Sophie would never be allowed to rest there beside him. So, years before his death, he made a choice. He would not be buried with his ancestors. He would be buried here, at Artstetten, with his wife.

On the 28th of June, 1914, the couple were shot in Sarajevo during an official visit. Their deaths set off the chain of events that led to the First World War. Within weeks, Europe was at war. Millions of people would die. And yet the story of that day often leaves out the couple themselves — a husband and wife who had fought hard to stay together.

After the funeral in Vienna, the two coffins travelled west toward Artstetten. According to accounts from the time, they crossed the Danube at night, and a violent storm broke over the river during the crossing. Whether or not every detail is exact, it is a scene people here still describe. The coffins were then carried up into these hills, to the crypt that had been prepared for them.

That crypt lies beneath the castle church. If you go down, you will find the two sarcophagi side by side. They rest at the same level. Nothing places one above the other. After a lifetime of protocol that kept Sophie a step behind, this is the second surprise of Artstetten — the simplest detail in the whole building is also the most deliberate one.

The family story did not end in 1914. The couple's children grew up as members of the Hohenberg family, the name given to Sophie and her descendants. Their sons Max and Ernst later opposed the Nazis, and after Austria was annexed in 1938, both were sent to the Dachau concentration camp. They survived. Descendants of the family have continued to look after Artstetten, and it remains connected to them today.

Inside the castle, a museum is dedicated to Franz Ferdinand. It does not tell only the story of Sarajevo. It also shows the private man — his travels, his interests, his family life with Sophie and their three children. Photographs, letters and personal objects fill the rooms. Take your time with the family pictures. They show a warmth that the stiff official portraits of the era rarely allow.

When you step back outside, look again at those onion-shaped towers against the sky. Walk a little way into the park and turn around. From there, the castle looks less like a monument and more like a country home, which is what it was for this family. The village continues its ordinary life around it. And somewhere below the church, a husband and wife rest exactly where they chose to be — together, and equal.
