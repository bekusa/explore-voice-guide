The guide

The story

The first thing you notice is the wind. It moves across the flat sand of the Cholistan Desert and carries a fine grit that settles on your sleeves. Then the walls appear, rising out of the plain like a cliff that someone placed here on purpose. The bricks are the colour of the desert itself, a warm, dusty pink-brown. In the afternoon heat, the whole structure seems to shimmer. Take a moment before you walk closer. Few places on Earth make a building look this large.

You are standing in front of Derawar Fort, in the Cholistan Desert of southern Punjab, in Pakistan. The nearest town of any size is Yazman, and beyond that lies the old princely city of Bahawalpur. Out here, there is very little to interrupt the eye. Low scrub, a few camel tracks, and the long horizon. That emptiness is part of the experience. The fort does not compete with anything around it. It simply stands, and the desert spreads away on every side.

Count the towers if you can. Derawar is famous for its forty bastions, the rounded towers that push out from the walls at regular intervals. They give the fort its rhythm, like the teeth of a giant comb. From a distance, the bastions create a pattern of light and shadow that changes through the day. In the morning, one side glows while the other stays dark. By evening, the pattern flips. The walls are tall enough to be seen from far across the desert.

The story of who first built a fort here is old and partly uncertain. Local tradition holds that a Rajput prince of the Bhati clan raised the first stronghold on this site many centuries ago. The Rajputs were warrior clans of northwestern India, and the Bhatis ruled lands that stretched into what is now the Thar and Cholistan deserts. Their capital was the golden city of Jaisalmer, to the southeast, across today's border with India. The earliest history of this fort is told more in oral memory than in written records.

The name itself carries a clue, though historians treat it carefully. One popular explanation says Derawar comes from "Dera Rawal", meaning the camp or seat of the Rawal. Rawal was a royal title used by the rulers of Jaisalmer. If that explanation is right, the name keeps a memory of the Bhati rulers long after their power here faded. It is a small surprise hidden in plain sight. Every time someone says the word Derawar, they may be repeating a title from a vanished court.

The fort you see today belongs mostly to a later chapter. In the early eighteenth century, the Abbasi family took control of this region. They were the Nawabs, or Muslim princes, who would go on to rule the state of Bahawalpur. They captured Derawar from its Rajput holders and rebuilt it in the form that survives now. For a time, the fort changed hands again, before the Abbasis took it back. Their family has remained connected to Derawar ever since, and the fort is still associated with their name.

Why build such a massive fortress in the middle of a desert? The answer lies in water and trade. This land was once fed by a river system that has long since dried up. Archaeologists have found traces of very ancient settlements scattered across Cholistan, some reaching back thousands of years. Later, caravan routes crossed this desert between Sindh, Punjab, and Rajasthan. A strong fort with reliable water controlled those routes. Whoever held Derawar held a key point on the desert roads.

Look closely at the walls as you walk along them. The construction is mainly brick, laid in thick courses that slope gently outward at the base. That slope gave the walls strength against both attackers and time. In many places, you can see patches where bricks have fallen away, and other places where later repairs used slightly different material. The desert is hard on buildings. Wind, heat, and occasional heavy rain all leave marks, and you can read that slow weathering on the surface of the bricks.

Here is something many visitors miss. Not far from the fort walls stands a white mosque, bright against the brown desert. It was built under the Nawabs in the nineteenth century, and it is often compared to the Pearl Mosque inside Lahore Fort, with its clean white domes. The contrast is striking. The fort is heavy, earthen, and defensive. The mosque is light, pale, and calm. Standing between them, you can see two sides of princely life here, power and prayer.

Nearby, you may also find the burial ground of the Abbasi family. The tombs carry decorated tilework and carved details, though some have suffered from age and weather. This is a quieter corner. Visitors often walk past it on the way to photograph the walls. It is worth slowing down here. These graves connect the fort to real people, rulers and their relatives, who chose to rest close to the stronghold that defined their family's history in the desert.

Derawar also has its share of stories that live outside the history books. Locals say that secret tunnels once ran from the fort to distant places, even all the way to Bahawalpur. There is no firm evidence for this, and it is best treated as folklore. But the tale tells you something real. For the people of this desert, the fort has always felt larger than its walls. It is a place where imagination fills the empty spaces.

Today, the fort draws visitors for different reasons. Some come for the architecture and the history. Others come for the desert itself, its silence and its wide skies. Each year, the area around Derawar hosts the Cholistan Desert Jeep Rally, when the quiet plain fills with engines, dust, and crowds. Access inside the fort walls has often been restricted, because the property is linked to the Abbasi family. Many visitors experience Derawar mainly from the outside, and the exterior alone rewards a long, slow walk.

Before you leave, stand still for a moment and listen. The wind is probably still moving across the sand. Perhaps a camel bell sounds somewhere in the distance, or a vehicle hums along the track. Look once more at the bastions and notice how the light has shifted since you arrived. These walls have watched the same sun rise and set over this desert for centuries. As you walk back to the road, turn around one last time. The fort will look smaller... and somehow, even more alone.

Decorated tilework on tombs in the Abbasi family graveyard.
Before you go

Practical tips

Visit early morning or late afternoon to avoid harsh desert heat.
Carry plenty of water and sun protection for the open desert.
Arrange transport from Bahawalpur or Yazman before setting out.
Ask locally whether interior access needs prior family permission.
Walk the full outer wall circuit to see every bastion angle.
