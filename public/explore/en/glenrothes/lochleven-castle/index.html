The guide

The story

Listen first. Water slaps against the hull of the boat, steady and patient, and somewhere across the loch a flock of geese is calling. The wind here comes off open water, cool and clean, and it carries the smell of reeds and wet grass. Ahead, on a small green island, a grey stone tower rises above a ring of old walls. This is Lochleven Castle. For centuries it was reached only like this, by boat, and that isolation is the whole point of the place.

We are a short drive west of Glenrothes, on the edge of the small town of Kinross. Loch Leven itself is one of the largest lowland lochs in Scotland. It is broad, shallow, and ringed by farmland, with the Lomond Hills rising to the east. On a clear day the hills look close enough to touch. On a grey day they fade into the cloud. Either way, the loch feels like a world set slightly apart from the roads and towns around it.

As the boat comes in, notice how small the island is. Today there is a lawn, a few trees, and space to wander. But the island was once much smaller. In the nineteenth century, engineers lowered the level of the loch, and the shoreline crept outward. So when you picture the castle in its working life, imagine the water lapping much closer to the walls. Back then, there was very little dry ground between the stone and the loch.

The oldest part of what you see is the curtain wall — the stone enclosure that wraps around the island. It is thought to date from the late Middle Ages, the era of Scotland's long wars for independence against England. The tower house inside came a little later. A tower house is a tall, compact Scottish fortified home, part castle and part family residence. This one stands several storeys high, square and plain, built for defence more than comfort.

Walk up to the tower and look for the door. It is not at ground level. The main entrance sits high up the wall, so in the past it was reached by a ladder or a wooden stair that could be pulled away. This was a common trick in Scottish towers. If trouble came, the family climbed inside, removed the steps, and left attackers staring at a blank stone face. It is a simple idea, and you can still see how well it works.

For much of its history, this castle belonged to the Douglases of Lochleven, a powerful landowning family. They were trusted by the crown, and that trust gave the castle its most famous role. An island fortress, surrounded by water, with one family in control of every boat — it made an ideal prison. And in 1567, it received the most important prisoner in the kingdom.

That prisoner was Mary, Queen of Scots. By the summer of 1567, her reign had collapsed. Her second husband, Lord Darnley, had been murdered. She had then married the Earl of Bothwell, a man many suspected of the killing. Scotland's nobles rose against her, and after a confrontation near Edinburgh she was taken north and brought across this water. She arrived as a queen. Within weeks, she was a queen in name only.

Here is a detail many visitors miss. Mary was not simply locked away — she was forced to give up her throne inside these walls. In July 1567, while held on this island, she signed papers of abdication in favour of her infant son, who became King James the Sixth. Years later, that same child would also inherit the English crown. So the line of kings that united Scotland and England passed through a moment of defeat on this small patch of ground.

There is a strange family twist, too. The castle's lady was Margaret Erskine, mother of the laird. Long before, she had been a mistress of Mary's father, King James the Fifth. Her son by the king was the Earl of Moray — Mary's own half-brother, and the man who became regent after her abdication. So the queen's jailers were, in a tangled way, connected to her own family. Politics and kinship were knotted tightly here.

Then comes the escape. On the second of May, 1568, Mary slipped out of the castle with the help of two members of the household. One was George Douglas, a younger brother of the laird. The other was a young man known as Willie Douglas, who worked in the household. Willie took the castle keys, let the queen out, and locked the gates behind them so no one could follow. Then they crossed the loch to waiting supporters on the shore.

Local tradition adds a final touch. According to the popular story, Willie threw the castle keys into the loch as they rowed away. Some accounts say a set of old keys was later pulled from the water and linked to that night. Treat this as a well-loved tale rather than proven fact. Still, standing by the water, it is easy to picture the splash in the dark, and the boat moving quietly toward freedom.

Mary's freedom did not last. Within days, her supporters were defeated in battle, and she fled south to England. She never saw Scotland again. The castle, meanwhile, slowly lost its purpose. Families moved to more comfortable houses on the mainland, and the island was left to the wind and the birds. Today the site is cared for by Historic Environment Scotland. The roofless tower and broken walls are quiet now.

Before you leave, turn away from the stone and look at the loch. Loch Leven is a national nature reserve, and in autumn and winter huge numbers of geese and ducks gather here. Listen for their calls drifting across the water. Watch the light change on the Lomond Hills. Then look back at the tower one more time — small, grey, and patient. A queen lost her crown here and found a way out. The water around you is the same water she crossed.

Before you go

Practical tips

Book the boat crossing ahead, especially on summer weekends.
Check before travelling, as high winds can cancel crossings.
Bring a warm layer for the windy open-water crossing.
Wear sturdy shoes for uneven grass and worn stone steps.
Walk part of the Loch Leven Heritage Trail afterwards.
