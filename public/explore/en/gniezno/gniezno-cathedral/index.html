The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment at the foot of the hill. Two brick towers rise above you, and their dark spires catch the light against the Wielkopolska sky. The brick is a deep, weathered red, the colour of baked earth. Pigeons settle on the ledges. Somewhere inside, the low murmur of a Mass or a tour group drifts out through the open doors. This is Gniezno Cathedral, and the ground you are standing on is one of the oldest places in the story of Poland.

The hill has a name, Lech Hill, and it carries a legend. Locals say that three brothers, Lech, Czech, and Rus, once travelled across the plains. Lech stopped here when he saw a white eagle in its nest against a red sunset. He decided to settle. The Polish word for nest is gniazdo, and according to the tale, that is where the name Gniezno comes from. The white eagle on red later became the emblem of Poland. It is folklore, of course, but you will see that eagle everywhere in this town.

The history is just as dramatic as the legend. In the late tenth century, a missionary bishop named Adalbert set out to preach to the pagan Prussians on the Baltic coast. In 997 he was killed there. Poland's ruler, Bolesław the Brave, brought his body back to Gniezno. A popular tradition says he paid for it with its weight in gold. Adalbert was soon declared a saint, and his tomb turned this hill into a place of pilgrimage.

Then, in the year 1000, something remarkable happened here. The Holy Roman Emperor, Otto the Third, travelled to Gniezno to pray at Adalbert's grave. This meeting is known as the Congress of Gniezno. During it, an archbishopric was created with its seat in this town. That gave the young Polish state its own church province, independent of German bishops. For a country only just entering European politics, it was a quiet but very large step.

Gniezno also became a place of coronations. Bolesław the Brave was crowned king in 1025, shortly before his death, and several of the early Polish rulers received their crowns in this church over the following centuries. Later, the coronations moved south to Kraków. But the Archbishop of Gniezno kept a special title — Primate of Poland, the senior churchman of the whole country. That title still belongs to this see today.

Now look at the building itself. What you see is not the church of Adalbert's time. Archaeologists have found traces of earlier pre-Romanesque and Romanesque churches beneath the floor. The present cathedral is Gothic, begun in the fourteenth century. Gothic is the style of pointed arches and tall windows that spread across Europe in the Middle Ages. Here it is built in brick rather than stone, which is typical of northern Poland and the Baltic lands, where good building stone was scarce.

The towers and the outer walls have changed many times. Fires, wars, and repairs left their marks. Baroque chapels were added around the sides, and the towers received new tops. In 1945, at the end of the Second World War, the cathedral was badly damaged by fire. After the war, restorers removed many later additions and brought the interior closer to its Gothic form. So what you are looking at is a mix — medieval bones with layers of later care.

Here is the first thing many visitors miss. Somewhere inside is one of the finest pieces of medieval art in Central Europe — the Gniezno Doors. They are a pair of bronze doors from the twelfth century, in the Romanesque style, the rounded, heavier style that came before Gothic. They are covered in small relief scenes. Read from bottom to top, the panels tell the life of Saint Adalbert, from his birth, through his mission, to his martyrdom and the return of his body.

If you can, take your time with them. The figures are small and stiff, but full of detail. You can see boats, swords, priests, and the moment the body is weighed against gold. Notice how the two doors are not identical. They were cast in different ways and show slightly different hands at work. Nine centuries of fingers and weather have polished parts of the bronze. Standing close, you are looking at a story told for people who could not read.

Inside the nave, let your eyes adjust to the dimmer light. The space is tall and narrow, with brick vaults overhead. Along the sides, the chapels hold tombs of archbishops and primates, many with carved stone figures lying on top. The air is cool, even in summer. Footsteps echo on the stone floor. This is still a working cathedral, so you may hear prayers, an organ, or simply the quiet shuffle of people lighting candles.

At the heart of the church is the shrine of Saint Adalbert. His relics rest in a silver reliquary, a decorated coffin made in the seventeenth century by a goldsmith from Gdańsk. On the lid, the saint lies in silver, and around the sides are scenes from his life. Here is the second surprise. The canopy above it is supported by columns, and the whole ensemble stands where pilgrims have gathered for more than a thousand years. In 1979, Pope John Paul the Second came here too.

When you leave, walk slowly around the outside of the hill. Look back at the towers from different angles. From some streets, they rise above low houses and trees like the masts of a ship. Notice the old buildings of the cathedral clergy nearby, and the gentle slope down toward the lake. This small town does not feel like a capital today. But for a short and important time, this hill was where Poland began to call itself a kingdom.

So before you go, stand once more facing the red brick. Listen to the bells if they ring, or to the wind across the hill. Think of the emperor arriving, the saint's body carried home, the crowns placed on early kings. Then look at the eagle on a nearby flag or sign. Legend or not, it brings you back to this nest on a hill — and to the quiet place where a country found its name.

Practical tips
