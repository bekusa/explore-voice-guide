The guide

The story

Stand still for a moment and feel the wind. It comes off the sea in a steady push, warm and heavy with salt. Below you the water shifts between pale green and deep blue, and small wooden fishing boats rock on it. Stretched across the strait in front of you is a long, low line of girders and piers. For over a century, that line has tied an island to a continent.

You are looking at the Pamban Bridge, which links Rameswaram on Pamban Island to the Indian mainland. The water beneath it is the Palk Strait, the narrow sea between India and Sri Lanka. To the north lies Palk Bay. To the south lies the Gulf of Mannar. The bridge crosses the meeting point of these two bodies of water, and on many days you can see their different moods side by side.

The original railway bridge opened in 1914, during the British colonial era. It ran for roughly two kilometres across open sea, and it became India's first sea bridge. Before it existed, pilgrims and traders reached the island by boat. The railway changed that. Trains could now carry people almost to the door of Rameswaram's great temple, and they could also connect with ferries bound for what was then Ceylon, today's Sri Lanka.

Now look towards the middle of the old bridge. Here is the detail that many visitors miss. The deck is not one continuous road of steel. At its centre sits a section that can open. Engineers called it a Scherzer rolling lift span. It is a pair of steel leaves that roll back and tilt upward, much like two hands lifting apart. When they rose, ships and boats could pass through the channel between the bays.

For decades, men worked this mechanism by hand. Crews turned gears and levers to raise the heavy leaves, and trains waited while vessels slipped through below. Think about that for a moment. A railway line in the middle of the sea had to pause for boats. It was a daily negotiation between land traffic and sea traffic, carried out in salt spray and strong sun.

This stretch of coast is not always calm. In December 1964, a powerful cyclone struck the area. It devastated the town of Dhanushkodi at the island's southern tip, and a passenger train on that line was lost with many lives. The cyclone also badly damaged the Pamban Bridge. Many of its girders were swept into the sea, and the island was cut off from the mainland.

The repair became a famous story in Indian engineering. A team led by the engineer E. Sreedharan restored the bridge in a remarkably short time — weeks rather than the months that officials first expected. Sreedharan later became well known across India for leading the Delhi Metro project. Many people say his reputation began here, on these windy piers above the strait.

Now turn your eyes to the road bridge that runs beside the railway. This is the Annai Indira Gandhi Road Bridge. It opened in 1988, and for the first time cars and buses could drive onto the island. Before then, the train was the main overland link. Today, the road bridge is also the best place to look at the railway bridges. From its height you can see the whole line of piers running across the water.

The sea is hard on steel. Salt air, high humidity, and strong winds attack metal every hour of every day. Engineers have long called this one of the most corrosive environments in the country. The old bridge needed constant painting and repair. In 2007, the line was converted to broad gauge, the wider standard track used across most of India. By the early 2020s, however, the old structure was showing its age, and railway services over it stopped in December 2022.

A new railway bridge now runs alongside the old one. It was inaugurated in April 2025. Instead of leaves that tilt upward, its central span rises straight up, lifted as one flat section between two towers. This is a vertical lift span, and it was described at its opening as India's first vertical lift sea bridge. Look for the tall towers near the middle of the new bridge. That is where the lift section sits, ready to raise itself for passing vessels.

So from here you can often see three bridges at once. There is the road bridge under your feet, the old railway bridge with its rolling lift span, and the new railway bridge with its lifting towers. Together they show more than a century of solutions to one problem: how to cross a short but stubborn stretch of sea. Each one carries the ideas and the tools of its own age.

Why did people work so hard to reach this island? The answer lies in Rameswaram itself. It is one of the most important pilgrimage towns in Hinduism. Its great temple, the Ramanathaswamy Temple, draws pilgrims from across India. According to the epic Ramayana, the god Rama crossed from this coast towards Lanka. Legend has it that a chain of shoals stretching towards Sri Lanka is the bridge his army built. Many pilgrims see Pamban as the first step of that sacred journey.

Before you leave, look down at the water once more. Watch the colour change as clouds pass over the sun. Listen for the engines of the fishing boats and the slap of waves against the piers. If a train is crossing, you will hear its low rumble carried over the sea. Then, as you head back towards the island or the mainland, notice how quickly the bridge shrinks behind you — a thin grey line across blue water, still holding the island close to the land.

Before you go

Practical tips

View all three bridges from the road bridge walkway.
Watch for traffic before stopping on the road bridge.
Bring sunglasses and a hat against strong coastal sun.
Come in early morning for softer light and calmer wind.
Combine your visit with Rameswaram's Ramanathaswamy Temple.
