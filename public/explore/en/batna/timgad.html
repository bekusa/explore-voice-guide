The guide

The story

Listen to your footsteps first. The great paving slabs underfoot are pale limestone, smoothed by centuries of wind. In places they carry long grooves cut by the iron rims of Roman cart wheels. Around you, columns rise from the high, dry plateau, and beyond them the Aurès Mountains lie low and blue on the horizon. The air up here is thin and bright. In summer it holds the smell of dust and wild herbs, and in winter it can turn sharply cold.

This is Timgad, known to the Romans as Thamugadi. It lies on the high plains east of the modern city of Batna, in northeastern Algeria. The Roman emperor Trajan founded it around the year 100 of the common era. Its official name honoured his family: Colonia Marciana Ulpia Traiana Thamugadi. Marcia was the name of his mother, and Ulpia was the name of his sister. Even the name of the city was a political statement, carved in stone.

Timgad was not a village that slowly grew into a town. Rome planned it and built it in one deliberate act. The first settlers were army veterans, very likely men who had served with the Third Augustan Legion. That legion kept its base at Lambaesis, not far to the west. A Roman soldier who finished his long service could receive land as a reward. Here, Rome gave that reward a shape — a ready-made town on the edge of its African frontier.

Now look at the layout around you. The original town was almost a perfect square, divided into a tight grid of streets that cross at right angles. Two main roads cut through the centre. The Romans called the north-south road the cardo and the east-west road the decumanus. The blocks between them are nearly identical in size. Historians often describe Timgad as one of the clearest surviving examples of a Roman planned town anywhere. Walk a few streets and you can feel the order of it — straight lines, repeated rhythms, nothing left to chance.

Here is something many visitors miss. That neat square did not last. Timgad became prosperous, and its people soon spilled beyond the first boundary. New quarters, markets, temples and bath houses grew outside the original grid. These later streets are less regular. So as you walk outward from the centre, you are walking forward in time — from the strict plan of the founders into the looser growth of a living, wealthy city.

At the western end of the main street stands the great arch, usually called the Arch of Trajan. It has three openings: a tall central one for wheeled traffic and two smaller ones on either side. Columns frame the openings, and the stone glows warm gold in low sunlight. Despite its name, many scholars believe the arch was built later than Trajan's own reign, perhaps in the late second century. It was a grand entrance, meant to impress anyone arriving from the road to Lambaesis.

Follow the stones toward the forum, the civic heart of the town. This open square was where people met, argued, traded news and worshipped. Around it stood a basilica, used for courts and public business, and other civic buildings. Look closely at the paving here. Romans sometimes scratched game boards into the stone to pass the time. And one inscription found at Timgad has become famous. In Latin, it says, roughly: to hunt, to bathe, to play, to laugh — that is living. It is a small, human line from people who lived here nearly two thousand years ago.

That line about bathing was not an empty boast. Archaeologists have found the remains of many public baths at Timgad — more than a dozen. For a Roman, the bath was far more than a place to wash. It was a club, a gym and a meeting room in one. In the larger bath complexes you can still trace the rooms for cold, warm and hot water, and the low brick pillars that held up heated floors. Hot air from furnaces flowed beneath them.

Not far from the centre, the theatre is cut into the side of a small hill. Its curved stone seats could hold a few thousand people. Climb a few rows and turn around. From here you see the city spread out in its grid, with the mountains behind. Timgad also had a public library, a rare survival in Roman Africa. An inscription records that a wealthy citizen paid for it. Its walls still show the niches where scrolls or books were once kept.

The city changed with the centuries. In the third and fourth centuries, Christianity spread across Roman Africa. Timgad became an important centre of the Donatists, a Christian movement that split from the wider church in a long and bitter dispute. One of its bishops, Optatus, was a powerful local figure. Churches and a later monastery appeared among the older pagan temples. The stones around you carry traces of both worlds.

Then came decline. In the fifth century, Roman rule in Africa collapsed, and the region passed to the Vandals, a Germanic people. Later, mountain tribes from the Aurès attacked the city. In the sixth century, the Byzantine Empire — the eastern continuation of Rome — returned and built a strong fortress just south of the old town. You can still see its thick walls. Some of its builders reused stones taken from older Roman buildings.

After that, the city slowly emptied. Over many centuries, windblown sand and soil covered much of it. This burial is one reason Timgad survives so well — the earth protected it. European travellers described the ruins in the eighteenth century, and serious excavations began under French colonial rule in the late nineteenth century. In 1982, UNESCO added Timgad to its World Heritage List. Today, Algerians also know the name from a summer music festival held nearby.

Before you leave, stand still on one of the main streets for a moment. Look down at the wheel ruts in the stone, and then up at the columns against the open sky. A veteran soldier once walked here with his family, heading for the baths or the market. The street plan he knew is still under your feet. As you walk back toward the arch, notice how the long shadows fall in straight lines across the grid — the same lines Trajan's surveyors drew, almost two thousand years ago.
